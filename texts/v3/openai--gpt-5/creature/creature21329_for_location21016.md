What lives here is patient enough to be mistaken for absence. It is the kind of living that learned early the economy of breath and then forgot the need for hurry. It is not green. It runs no blood. It answers light with nothing because there is none to answer. It keeps its ledgers in ions and bonds, in the temperatures of stone, in the stored syllables of old brines that never make a drop.

The first inhabitants are the salts themselves, not as inert décor but as the slow, mineral animals of this chamber. Halite and gypsum take food and give it back on tempos of centuries: one molecule taken from the air, another released; a face dissolved, a whisker grown; a seam eased open then locked, content. They sip the room’s rumor of humidity the way lichens sip dew in other worlds, but here the thirst ends inside the lattice. Their draperies, the frozen fabrics, are postures held without fatigue. The room’s darkness uses them the way a body uses bones: structure without drama.

Inside that patience, smaller arts. Tucked under ledges where even dryness casts shade live the rimes: sheens no thicker than a skin cell, glazes so thin the eye must bargain with itself to see them. They are consortia—a word that makes them sound more organized than they need to be—of stone-eaters and air-breathers who extract a diet from trace. They take oxygen when the room exhales and forget it when the breath draws back; they borrow a carbon from a passing molecule, a sulfur from the gypsum they sit on, a whisper of nitrogen. In return, they lay an invisible glue. Dust that would have been a perpetually ready powder becomes a fixed thought. A plate’s underside wears a film of this housekeeping, a shimmer when your lamp moves, like old skin that does not shed but accumulates its story.

In other protected corners, the wool: pale whiskers of gypsum and threads of halite furred with life so faint it can be called undecided. Threads condense along lines of the slightest advantage—minute differences in warmth, in mineral, in nothing anyone in a hurry would notice. Along those lines the inhabitants coax new threads into being, not with hands, not with tools, but with that same exuded glue, with the local change in pH they can make if they choose to spend a long winter’s treasure. A whisker lengthens by hair's breadth. A century later it snaps with the brittle ping you might one day hear. That sound is a death, but it is also how another surface is made for another film to take hold.

There is a library of the older kind of life sealed in the halite. You cannot turn its pages; they are not metaphor but tiny actual rooms, pin-head amphoras: inclusions of brine caught when a crystal grew and took a pocket of yesterday’s ocean as a memory. In each, there are sleepers—cells so rudimentary they have outlasted empires, veiled in carotenoid cloth they do not use for light because there is no light to use. They do not hunger. They dream in salts. Once in a great while, when a microfracture lets a few more molecules of water creep near, or when the lattice slackens from a slight warmth, they taste their world anew, divide once, subtract a little error from their code, and go back to holding the page. If you chipped the crystal, if you held a splinter up and let your breath fog it, they might wake long enough to regard you without eyes and then close again, neither grateful nor afraid.

In the darker knots where ironstone and chert are set like thoughts into the pale matrix, a different living exchanges electrons along miles of microscopic avenues. The iron-breathers live here, pilgrims without feet who use the stone itself as wire and wall. They push and pull a charge through iron that was once dissolved and then decided to be mineral; they ease a sulfate toward sulfide and are content to count that as victory. They do not ask for water to be free. It is enough that it is present as water of crystallization, bound into gypsum like trapped snow, enough that every so often a track of the room’s slow inhalation delivers two more molecules they can lay hold of.

There are viruses—of course there are gifts and thieves. They look like nothing; they are nothing—you could dust a crystal and say you had removed them and be both right and wrong. They keep the halite library honest. They move on the pinpricks and filaments not by walking but by being; when the rimes stir and the wool spends, the viruses spend, too, a bit of code unwrapped and then snugged down again into dormancy so patient it is indistinguishable from rock.

No creature with legs belongs to this economy. Every once in an era a gnat or moth may have strayed from some distant shaft, thrown itself on this warm stillness and found its last definition: an outline in salt, a leathered miniature of its original idea that the mineral slowly erases. In the meantime, what animals the room keeps are the trivial beings of physics—sound itself as visitor, the tick and ping and settling, your breath’s edge tasting the inner ear. Sound comes, lives its one life against these walls, and becomes the memory of a hair-thin crack. In here, even events are animals, short-lived and precise.

Between the guilds of film and inclusion and iron, there are messengers of a sort. Exhalations of warm, dry air slide along fissures so slowly you could die and decompose twice in another place before they crossed from one side to the other. On those motions, molecules are ferried: a trace of ammonia from a crumb of something that used to be plant far away; a breath of carbon dioxide from a visitor who never reached this room; the faintest stink of sulfur from a distant seam of pyrite grieving into air. To the living here, these are news. The films thicken by a measurable nothing. The wool adds a knot. Inside a brine pocket, a sleeper briefly becomes a pair. It is not a feast. It is the passing around of a rumor at a funeral, one that turns out to be true but not urgent.

What the chamber also keeps, and what counts as life if you stretch the word to where it wants to be stretched, is the order itself. The prohibition on water is a law that behaves like an organism: vigilant, metabolic, responsive. It reaches out invisibly to strip moisture from anything that presumes. It eats left-behind apples without letting them rot; it makes relics. It pairs with heat—the heat as stored by stone, an animal that does not move but has a pulse that takes a week to crest and subside. Between them they hold a climate you could mistake for indifference. It is, in truth, a form of attention that runs in the other direction: away from growth, toward keeping a shape.

If someone with hands came—and the room would allow it as it allows pings and grains—and pressed a line into the tessellated floor with a bit of flint flaked from a thought in the wall, the inhabitants would attend. Not quickly. The rimes would lay a more generous glue along that scratch, as if to soothe a wound. Dust would find the groove and decide, with the room’s help, to become part of the groove. Inclusions would remain inclusions. The iron-breathers would not remark it except as a new boundary in the map of where charges can go. But the law-of-no-water would notice. It would lay its invisible hand on your skin and take. It would make your leaving known in dryness to the very tips of the gypsum whiskers, which would lengthen over the next thousand years as if leaning, minutely, to hear if you were returning.

So what lives here, if we give names: the Rimes, who polish nothingness into a skin; the Wool, which tends to threads and listens for the snap; the Locked, who keep a salted sea inside themselves for when the world forgets seas; the Iron, who trade darkness for darkness and call that commerce; the Viruses, asleep in the salt with a smile too thin to see; the Law, which is not a thing but behaves like a thing, a mouth of dryness with a perfect appetite; the Heat, which is not a thing but rests like a tame animal on the stone.

They live in a way that seems to require no witness. If you never came, they would not be less themselves. If you return in lifetimes, you will find their same postures. One whisker may have finally touched its neighbor and, surprised by the intimacy, grown no farther. A plate’s edge may have slumped the width of a breath and incorporated that concession into the definition of edge. A new rime may have joined a very old one so seamlessly that history is the only difference between them. Their lives are exactly as large as this room and exactly as fast as a salt can remember. They are untroubled by the lack of sky. They do not expect rain. They are content to eat stone, to breathe the rarities in air, and to keep the chamber’s long hush whole.
