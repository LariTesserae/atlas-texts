Those who live on this rim have learned to be long in their listening and light in their touch. They think of themselves as people who keep a mouth from closing—those at the Lip—though if you asked for a single name they would hesitate and give you one you could carry on the wind: a twist of the fingers, a flag’s quick fold, an interval between two notes that the organ favors at dawn. They live here the way rope lives on a cleat: under tension, with room to slip and no wish to.

You can recognize them at a distance by the way they move. Bodies narrow from a life of climbing through wet. Calves braided hard from steps that end in air. Hands have the polish of woodworkers and the burrs of rope-burn, nails cut blunt so that net and bark are not snagged. Their ears are not larger than yours, but their attention sits in them like a second child on a lap. They do not carry much; they do not stand in groups where the planks are thin. A person with a burden is matched by a person with a hand at the small of their back, not to steady weight so much as to feel it, to listen through their bones to the same low envelope the bridge hums.

Noise is a hazard and a tutor. They speak—of course they speak—but when the organ is up they tuck words under their tongues and use their hands for sense. Their sign is exact and soft. It uses the palm as a grammar and the wrist as conjugation. Children here are fluent before they lose their milk teeth. Flags extend this language up and outward. A twist of patterned cloth announces that a cistern lip has cracked; a pair of stripes means the third terrace was gusted and a net is down; a square folded twice and pegged hard where it will snap and curl tells all eyes that weather is coming in a new key and work should come home. They do not shout across the bowl because across the bowl is never the way information likes to travel. They whisper into each other’s sleeves in the low rooms, and if there must be singing, it waits for a lull—their throats join the organ when the organ is tired.

The ring they’ve made does not pretend to anchor the world. Joinery is a philosophy before it’s a carpentry. They will not drive a nail to force a fit. They lay mortise against tenon and let the grain decide which path keeps the most memory of tree. Pegs are seasoned bayonet-straight; fiber is spun from bast and something they call lichen-silk, a draughty floss pulled and tempered with dexterity until it knots finer than hair. When the organ grows a new vowel because a lip of copper greened soft and fell, there is no panic. A child is sent to mark the change in the book of tones—stones in a pouch on a string, lengths and weights—and an elder nods because slow change is the only change they trust.

Water has to be coaxed to belong. They drink the air until it lies down. The nets—lacework with thought in every strand—are not hung and forgotten; they are combed like hair. Fingers walk them in the morning to free any snagged wing, any moth that slept too long in a fold. The cords are tuned, truly tuned, because a slack net throws its collected beads to the ground when a gust looks at it sidelong. Funnels clay-lipped and sand-smoothed are emptied in a rhythm that is part labor, part liturgy: first from the north terrace because the wind came there firmer; last from the south because the drips there are few and fine and the cistern tastes of rock. In high season, in fog-season, a person’s day can be nothing but walking the river they’ve summoned from nothing and tilting it into jars.

Food grows without clamor. Rope-gardens hold pockets of soil carried one back at a time from places where a spade will still bite. They grow what agrees to be grown in a place where roots live in cups and wind is never off them: ferns that can be eaten young, knobbly tubers that like to be wedged between two stones, sorrels that taste of clean rain and keep well in a basket. On slants where sun glances awhile, they train himalayan berries to crawl low and glisten; from their goats they take milk and hair and, rarely, meat. The goats are small and wise and live where ground will not argue if a hoof tests it. Cheese here tastes of resin and the day’s weather; it picks up flags you can read on the tongue. There is a fungus they prize, coaxed in damp shadow on bark and broken beams. Insects are not despised. A handful of midges netted over a cistern at noon is a spoon of flesh to be stirred into stews which are more steam than boil because steam leaves little ash.

They can make fire, but fire here is on a leash. Coals live in heavy clay that drinks their heat and lets none of it leap. Smoke goes out through broken paths, braided chimneys, so it joins the world as breath, not trumpet. There is a season when timber is quiet and a season when timber is contrary and will twist under a blade; they honor those seasons as other places honor the turn of the moon. Work that would argue with wind waits for the day when argument is not asked for.

They do not mine the bowl. This is law. They trim nothing from that new-made throat but cups of lichen and moss for medicine and bedding where it grows in too-deep comforts that will slip a boot. Copper is rare and is brought weathered from lower places by friends and kin who come with salt and seed and go away with lichen-silk and stories. When copper must be used, it is for lips—literal ones on the organ, a brightness that edges a slit to teach a note its beginning and its end. They love the stains metal leaves on their hands and hate the weight of it; both feelings are taught to the young.

The book of tones is a communal memory. There are no pages, for pages go pulpy here; there are ropes and stones and the bodies of children who become good at holding lengths between their thumbs without looking down. The book says: when the wind draws this vowel in frost-season, bring bracing to the fourth terrace because ice will follow the long note and make timbers fuller than they like; when a thin glass-thing cuts the hearing, look to the north lip for a hairline that will not have been there yesterday. It is ledger and hymn and weather-proof law. Elders keep it under their hands, but every person knows enough of it to tilt their day into the right groove.

The children are apprenticed first to balance. They learn how to stand over a thousand kinds of nothing and not imagine falling into each of them at once. Ropes under their palms teach more mathematics than abacus. They are taught to take sound into their bones: feel how the bridge goes from hum to cat-gut-ache when too many feet are on it; feel how the wind raises your skin a heartbeat before it rises your hair. There is a ritual of learning to drink the air. A bowl—a shallow, soft-fired dish—is hung from wet grass in the middle of the bridge; the novice walks there without looking down, waits with eyes closed while the fog earns them a mouthful, and carries that water back without spilling what they haven’t yet tasted. If they laugh with relief, the organ takes the laugh and makes it part of what the place is doing that day. If they cry, same.

Names belong to the day and to the person. There are sound-names, given the first time a child points, unbidden, to the right flag for a change in wind and then shares that look with an elder who understands that this gaze is the child’s measure. There are rope-names—knots that are theirs to tie, a signature they can leave on a beam or a net. There are also leave-names for when a person must go down into thicker world to fetch salt, to marry, to bring back a friend. Those are chosen to travel well in mouths unused to vowels that sit for a long time on the back of the tongue. It is not scandal to go. It is unthinkable to leave slammed doors behind; a leaving here is a quieting of a room.

Death, too, belongs to air. Bodies are not flung or burned, not set to pollute the bowl with a fall or a smoke. The dead are unwound. They are washed warm with mist-water and combed of all the snarls of a life, and then they are laid in a place of lull, under nets, at night or in cloud, where their weight becomes a way of measuring time as it leaves. Bones later are taken up white and simple. They are not made into the organ; they do not let kin sing as instruments, because that confuses the listener who is the mountain. Goat-bone makes chisels; human-bone makes memory. It lives as toggles on important ropes, as counters in the book of tones, as a little weight put on a sapling that must learn to bend properly toward the work it has before it.

There is no chief with a loud voice because loudness has too much already. There are circles of keepers: keepers of tone, of joinery, of water, of way. The keepers do not meet at night; night belongs to listening and to sleep which is here an instrument too—the best ear is rested. Disputes are solved with a game anyone can win: two people take a length of line and hold it taut between them, step up onto it with bare feet, and feel. Whoever lies with their hands while their balance runs off will be seen by all to be wrong. The rule is not infallible, but it is instructive. Balance is a better liar than the mouth.

Trade binds them to a world that runs instead of seeps. People from woodier houses with rivers that hurry bring salt and grain. People from the old copper places bring green-shy sheets and hammered slivers. In return the rim-folk send down nets that drink fog and ropes that last in wet. There is a kind of reed-grown here that, treated with sap and smoke, becomes both leaf and leather; it is beautiful where rain is constant and goats nibble everything else bare. Those who come up do not come very far; they do not enter the ring; they do not walk the bridge. There are woven gates on the path that are not bolted but feel that way. Trade is done with fingers and eyebrows and the calm back and forth of baskets slid across boards you can trust.

They have fears, but they’ve taught them to be specific. They are not terrified of edges. They are frightened of weight gathering without notice. They watch for the slow emergencies: a crack turned petty tyrant, a net that sags with a neighbor’s grief and is not retensioned, a child who sings in storms because the storm will sing back and split wood wrong for years to come. They have a superstition about shadows in the bowl and they enforce it without speeches: if you, without thinking, stand so your scissored self leaps below the lip, someone’s fingers will straighten your shoulders and their hand will show you where to stand to be the lesson they mean to keep teaching water. They don’t expect outsiders to understand, and they don’t mind, because it works.

Laughter happens, often, and is private. You will see a mouth go wide and bright and hear only the organ returning, with wonder, the shape a human face makes when joy takes it. Children joke in sign under tables with the crisp clever motions of thieves in a fable. Lovers carry a mismatch of weights down a plank to test whose ankle speaks better; this is their flirting. On clear winter noons the calm is almost a ringing in itself, and that day there is a game of guessing what note frost will drop to after the first ten breaths of dusk. Somebody always wins; nobody wins the next day; everyone gets better at guessing.

Other lives share the ring and the wet. There are dogs, smaller than you expect, with coats that bead. Their feet have learned the planks and the way ropes stack when they are coiled, and they do not need to be told twice where not to go. Salamanders glue themselves to the day and unstitch at night to feed. A frog with humor, singular as if appointed, calls sometimes from a seam. There is a moth white enough to be a glance. There are birds who ride the air; the people do not count them because the birds are always arithmetic you can finish but can’t begin right. There are plants that have learned the ring’s habits and grow as if to please. The people talk to them with their hands in a way hands cannot put in words, thinning, coaxing, tying, letting alone.

If they have a history, it sounds like a weather report told generation by generation and stored in dry cords and stones. The oldest story is not how they came here but when they understood what here was doing. A person walked the ridge in a fog and heard a sound like a letter learning itself. Another climbed down on a rope where nobody sane would and tied the first lip. Another thought to catch the air to drink. The bridges have been remade enough that no one claims the first; several people imagine themselves the last to cross alone at center to fetch a dish of fog-water. When a new vowel comes and stays—a winter O that sits in the bones and will be that year’s sleep—they have a feast that doesn’t sound like one. They eat and sign with greasy fingers and then all of them go stand, arms loose, to listen in their skins to what was given. The old are good at crying without moving; the young are good at pretending they are not hearing what they know they are hearing.

They do not plan forever, because forever has too many grooves in it. They plan for this timber and the one it will brace, for this rope’s life through three cycles of frost, for the throat of this lip until copper greens thin again. That their bowl will become a different bowl is the first sentence of the book of tones—they have a version of faith that makes you better at counting. If you wished to hurt them, you would hurry them; if you wished to love them, you would learn their ratios and go quietly through their doors and let your own ears go wide until your jaw loosens and you can feel in your molars what the plank would tell you if you were standing on it.

What lives here is a people who build as if they are playing an instrument with the mountain. What lives here is the instrument they play together. What lives here is moss in a thousand dialects of green, air thickened until hands can carry it, and a vow shaped as a ring that keeps remaking itself while the rock finds the voice it meant to have.
