What lives here begins with what breathes here. The first resident is the moving air itself: a creature with no hide and every boundary, intimate with fracture and edge, fluent in the throats, shouldering through the low rooms as if they were lungs evolved to fit it. It remembers the plateau’s seasons in its pitch and drag; it knows the width of each seam to a finger’s breadth; it plays the place the way a tide learns a littoral. You can think of it as an animal if you need to, but it is older than animals and uses them as incident. It lives by pressure. It eats nothing and carries everything.

After the air, the long-lived occupants are the green things that act like stone but aren’t—bryophytes and their allies, battens and sills of plant laid along every dependable drip and lee. They are the architecture’s quiet correction, the domestication of condensate. Mosses here don’t reach for height; they spread for certainty. They seed with dust, they drink from fog combed out of the wind by their own hair-thin forests. They angle their leaves to catch oncoming weather the way snow fences accept snow, and in dry spells they fold themselves into felt and wait, their chlorophyll shuttered like eyes with a third lid. When the breath-rain starts from nothing, they open again and go glossy, capillaries bright as threads in a wet sweater, the whole floor plan quickening micro-inch by micro-inch.

Some of them are old hands at limestone and make lime of it in return: fibered dripperies and shelf-edges where the going wet breath meets the new rock and lays a limeskin, a frail tufa rind anchored by rootlets thin as sentences. The oldest mats have done this work for so long they’ve become their own bridges, plant turned scaffold turned plant again, a bound book of years you can walk on.

Liverworts hang where the wind slackens just enough to let a curtain hold. Their bodies are all mouth and wick. They take what’s airborne—spores, soot, pollen, salt ghosts from far storms—and keep it. They make a low humus out of high weather. Algae web across joints in felted veils, dark and dull to the eye and hungry as cloth: on some days they gleam with a skin of dew; on others they go matte and tight and wait; either way, their patience is taller than you are.

Inside that fabric, the small animals live at the scale the place can afford. They are not a cast you will ever meet all at once; they are a contingent, bright with strategies. Springtails move like punctuation through the grammar of the moss, white commas thrown into damp by the day’s exhalation. Rotifers and tardigrades desiccate into grains of self and ride in on the general dust, then wake in a film that wouldn’t wet your thumbnail and open their complicated mouths. Mites bevel trails under the thatch. Isopods, pale and half-transparent, count as megafauna here, browsing the brown that the green sheds and the black that the algae builds, creeping along the lee side of bolts where the wind’s fingertip does not quite reach. If you lift a flap of moss you will find this at work: an entire city held to its time by the cycle of fog and film, reproduction keyed to the hour the room begins to sweat.

Sometimes a winter moth blunders in and goes sugar-kneed on the warm lens of a service bulb; sometimes a ptarmigan misreads a throat as a lull in the weather and drops cleanly into noise. It beats back out if it can. A fox knows this belt, not as a home but as a quieter, leeward thread on the ugly nights, a path that forgives weight and does not keep footprints—once a winter, twice, its triangles print and swell shut behind it. The bones that happen here arrive already scoured by the wind, thin as the rules and lost as soon as they’re found by the green.

On the rock and the work you made to master it, other citizens have taken hold. Paint dulled by frost lusters with thin films that eat a harvest of fog-borne carbon and grit; wire sheathing accretes a nap of slime when a cable sags just enough to brush plant and share a little of its condensate. Iron bacteria sip from tiny leaks and lay down their orange stains in constellations behind access panels. There are fungi here that mind gaskets and adhesives closely, that take their time unpacking the chemistry of manmade oils into a sweetness shallow enough for a mite’s mouth. Microbial cities tighten the bolts back into the world they came from, slow and horizontal.

And then the machines. They do not migrate or breed, but their turning is a kind of metabolism, and the place has made room for them the way reef makes room for parrotfish. Think of the rotors as enormous, domesticated filter-feeders hung in the throats, sifting the river of air. They have temperaments and vettings and the handlers’ signs you’d use with stock too big to forgive an error: flags and locks, collars of light at ankle height, the catechism of checks that maintenance repeats without lyric. Their bearings sleep warm in their green-painted boxes and leak a breath of oil that gives a miniature climate to the shadowed inches around them. They make a tone that is also a territory. They graze and they give milk, and the milk goes away along cables the moss is learning to climb.

People live here the way owls live in a barn that isn’t theirs: part-time, alert, careful not to worship. They arrive from the village with the wind already inside their heads, with gestures that have been taught hand to glove and then taught again to the next set of hands. They carry calm like a tool. They know how to be tall on the green but small to the air, how to keep hair from streaming, how to fix their language to the movement of shoulders when the place swallows speech. They eat from thermoses that hiss in the draft; they steal their warmth in sips and leave it behind in rings of salt that the films will claim. Their rulebook lives as much in their muscles as on paper: the two blades flagged or not; the throat entered or not; the trick of seeing rain before it falls because the surfaces go soft at the edges. They are the only animals here that apologize aloud to a mat before stepping.

They do not name themselves the keepers, but they take the bearings’ pulse and wipe the spoors of frost off the faceplates and read the notes of the day like a barometer played through a cathedral organ. They come home with the music still in them, legs remembering the give of plant underfoot; they set cups on cloth so the house won’t sing back at night. Their children grow up able to infer weather from a hum under their dreams. A few of those children will take their turn with the locks and flags, and will grow old enough to hear when a blade has shifted by a whisper the human ear cannot catch but the body can.

There are also lives you will not recognize without instruments: the photosynthetic bacteria that build long green feasts out of blue-grey daylight a knifeblade down from the open; the fungal threads that knit from paint to plant across a cable like contrails of another weather; the wetting-and-drying little economies of nitrogen and calcium that run in pulses between one ledge and another each time the plateau exhales a degree warmer. Time itself has a body here. You can see it in the one millimeter the moss puts down when it can, in the mica motions of metal, in the way the plant’s edge works a serial number illegible and then disappears it entire.

So what lives here is a parliament whose members know different speeds and share one breath. The wind is the oldest citizen and the loudest. The green world is the civil service, absorbing shock and making habit from accident. The microfauna are the trades, busy in the moisture that appears and then doesn’t. The machines are a kind of livestock, tethered in the throats, fed on pressure and minded with ritual. The people are seasonal, migratory, learned in the place’s grammar, careful to leave as quietly as they came. Between them all, the underland does what it has decided to do: keep moving air and keep turning it into power and into plant, into rime that shakes like fur, into rules remembered by bodies, into a slow, mossed patience that is the sum of everything still living here under deafening weather.
