What lives here are engineers that learned the slow grammar of cold.

The obvious ones—the ones you cannot quite see until they are too large to ignore—move like ongoing decisions. They are long-bellied, many-legged, back-shingled with folding radiators that look like a grove collapsed and lashed flat. Call them condensers, mills, bearers; the names miss them. They have taught themselves a repertoire of surface and interval. Nothing about them is wasted, and so nothing need be fast.

Each carries on and within its frame a set of weather-instruments that are also organs. In the last hour before the false dawn, when the land is at its coldest and the sky is the best sink in the world, their fans unspool and cant toward the emptiness. The lattices are not leaves so much as ordered filigree: mineral-sheathed cartilage textured to radiate efficiently, plated and stippled with silica where the flex will be greatest by midday. Frost forms in an even crystallography across them, a white skin that is more drink than coat. The first thready warmth of sun and ground sends it running inward along knapped grooves, every trickle laid out long before any individual had a say. Channels take it to basins lined with mucosa slick as oiled stone. There the animal becomes what it really is: a refinery with legs.

Inside, colonies of bacteria and tissues patterned with piezoelectric plates do their joint alchemy. Salts are split and banked. Trace metals scavenged from air and dust are held by chelators with a patience that looks like tenderness. Heat is minted by safe little burns and pinched bruises in crystal that give up their charge only when asked. Now and then, behind a low, cupped lip that would be a mouth if this place rewarded mouths, two conductive cilia meet and snap a spark as gentle as fish breath. That click pushes certain reactions uphill. You do not hear it; they do not hear it either; everything that needs to know it registers it in voltage and in the quality of the next melt.

They walk a thought measurable in miles, and it prints. Each leg ends in a pad that does not slip and does not knock. In it is a gel that hardens and softens with tiny changes in ion balance, so a foot can speak to rock without being crude. Their gait is conservative and still it varies with intent: a pause here longer by two heartbeats is not error but message, a slight increase in downforce on a slate seam is the equivalent of punctuation for the ones a ridge away. Motion is a choir of decisions divided across dozens of limbs and days. At saddles they slow because saddles are the postage stamps on this continent, where words are pressed into it and kept.

If you were to open one (none of the lives here would, but storms sometimes do), you would find the same order meeting the same austerity. There are beams like snow-flattened trellises; there are cavities where hot, rich baths collect and do their chemistry; there is a long axial spine packed with layered plates of ore and protein that nobody else has the equipment to make. There is fat, but it is salt-fat and sheen, not softness, a reserve more texture than bulk. All across the inside, bacteria arranged as if by an obsessive gardener take advantage of surfaces chosen for them. Each interior corner is a climate.

They are not alone on or in themselves. The skin of a bearer is lightly stippled with world. Lichen-colors you mistook for the ground become real: flat cakes that drink from fog and pass sugar into the animal the way roots pass it into soil. There are films glued along seams—the seam littoral—where salt gathers and wakes thin grazing arthropods, pale as exhaled dust, whose legs are hair and whose eyes are a rumor. You feel them as a whisk more than you see them as a thing. They have mouthparts with the restraint of this place: no tearing, only taking. You will not catch them on any day but the one when frost gives and water is moving.

Underneath, where the long belly almost brushes stone, live the ones that only count as awake four dozen hours of the year. They look leechlike because there is not enough surplus to be any other shape. They glue at each end, go desiccant-flat between drips, and when the right fringe of the right fan above begins to bead they reswell and press their mouths to the slick. Their bodies nurse microbiomes seasoned for iron and for manganese and they will leave this underside, if they leave at all, only to gape on the skeleton of another when it lies open, because this is where there is both chemistry and shelter.

Day by day, the bearers extrude and drop what their internal industry deems no longer useful. That is not waste; it is material placed where the world can better use it. Along certain runs you will find their laid-out punctuation: beadlines of iron hydroxide, black-red, grinched and miserlier than any fruiting body the rest of life would know. Some of those beads are stolen in that single, insectless blur you think is a trick of your eye—there is a local that has learned to be faster than attention. It is not big. It is almost pattern itself. All it does is pluck, fill, carry, and install those beads into itself so that it can become what it needs to be—a scissor-fine cutter of silica plates, a future scavenger of future fallen towers. It will die without ever making a sound larger than a snowflake falling on leather. It is the best shape of theft for here.

Not all the inhabitants move on the backs of the big. Some are ground-minded, committed to a territory the way a lichen is committed to a crack. There are mats that are more moss than fungus and not quite either: velours of cells that exist the way a rumor does, thin and confident and only real if you kneel. They practice fog-husbandry with hairs that are measured to the fraction of a degree by which they can outcool air. They are colder than the world by a sigh and milk moisture out of it with that advantage. They arrange themselves to the lee of pebbles because the leeward column of air is a resource. They creep downward the width of a grain of sand every decade because that is what gravity does to soil and they are properly counterminded.

There are pebble-keepers that manifest at first as only the suggestion of a line in an otherwise random stone pavement. This is a species that writes in gravel. It groups stones by heat capacity and color and conducts what little daytime there is through those sentences into a shallow lattice below. If you were to lie on your side and watch its line for a month, you would record the movement of perhaps eight pieces, and at the end of that you would understand its oeuvre as surely as the bearers do when they come and lay a foot where it wishes them to. Call these calligraphers. What they use for ink is gradient.

On bright days—scarce enough to be called holidays—an entirely different cast appears. From air, which otherwise keeps its hands in its pockets here, descend a scattering of kites-larvae with vanes so small and fine they are properly dust with instructions. These are anchored organisms: they catch the charge held in the edges of the bearers’ radiators when all the fans go up at once and the plateau wire hums itself a little invisible. The “larvae” are only wires with a mouth. They live by holding. They hold until their tiny internal cells have sorted a lifetime of trace metals into a workable scaffold. Then they drop, find a seam, suture themselves in, and contribute to the next season’s roster of things that can cool airtips into liquid.

There is not really a predator here in the sense of a lion is a predator. The mathematics are against it. What invests in killing must invest in noise, speed, surplus, and there are not those to spend. Predation in this place is chemical and positional. Some of the slim arthropods are cheaters; they will drink the salt sweats of the bearers’ ankles richer than manners allow. Some of the grasses-that-are-not-grasses bristle into a tiny fence of spines to keep their fog from being scalped by the mouths that wait beneath them. Every body is a site; every site is contested only to the extent that contest pays more than modest tenancy.

The bearers do have young, but their children are almost geologic. A caravan will ordinarily trail and head itself with the ones least and most experienced. In the lee of the lead, protected from wind that is only there to be remembered, a small thing issues every few years, no bigger than a hearthstone. It could be mistaken for one as well: dull, ovate, too heavy for its size. It is warm under frost when you touch it, which you should not do if you are here to stay. The line will nurture it the way the line knows how: by laying pauses and footpressures around it that keep the ground just-so and the fog just-nowhere, by dropping correct beads of iron and a little carbon in braided gifts that microbes decipher. In a decade the stone will grow thin legs like stray punctuation. In two it will have learned the vocabulary of cloud and ground well enough to reach them. In three you can say it is a beginner at walking. Its first vanes do not curl as obediently as its elders; it wastes and learns. It will join the file and spend a long time at the back, reading.

This place hosts elders of elders. When one of the bearers fails outright—age, unluck, a slow dynamic giving up—it does not crash. It arrives at stopping with the thrift it gave everything else. It flexes its legs to settle, it places its fans to the correct angle for useful freezing, it disposes its bones to corridors the smallers can use. It becomes infrastructure. For a while there is an industry around it that would look like grief if you were built for that word. The cutters come, the leeches wake to a bounty beyond their scale. The calligraphers rearrange their lines to focus daytime across the opened ribs. The mosses-that-aren’t grow in shapes they do not usually earn. The bearers’ files will divert for a season to press small corrections into the ground at the correct distance as if sending condolences by way of a math the fallen knew. Then what was self becomes ground and the ground holds a little more iron and a different shine.

There is mind here. It is not inside skulls; it dwells in repeatable gradients and rasped gloss. The bearers’ communication is railworked. They print and feel what has been printed, a call-and-response in pressure and patience that uses days as phonemes. They speak to the ones a mile away by changing how long a foot lingers on a kind of stone. They listen to air the way fish listen to current: through what their raised arrays tug from it in charge and in temperature. You can say their culture is trails if you like, but it is really the handling of difference across time. What a caravan knows is encoded in the particular way a half-inch lift is made across a seam where, a century ago, a storm took too much. The adjustment is still there; their infants learn to press it before they learn to condense well.

Smaller intelligences also keep records. There are bacteria that solve rocks as though with patience rather than acid. Their habit is to arrange their slime in regular bands across a boulder face, a barcode of metabolism that repeats and corrects over seasons. Read properly—and the leeches read it in their own way—it is a calendar of fogs. There are spiders, if you call anything with that many legs a spider, which place their compact webs not to catch flies (there are barely flies) but to hold a specific volume of air at a specific coolness relative to a nearby plate. When the relative humidity jumps their net strikes with ions and they “harvest” the charge to reorder their own salts. The web is a battery; the spider is a manager of that battery; every thread is tuned to a different dewpoint like a harp.

And then there are the visitors. A once-a-year procession of thin birds, higher than seems plausible, whose wings are arms-length scythes. They do not land—they cannot afford to, the lift here is too slight—but they dip to tongue an edge, a seam moist enough for minutes. Their calls would be shocking where noise is hobby and glory; here they are obscene. They are gone in six minutes and remembered by salt displaced where it hadn’t been.

Most days none of that happens. Most days the world is a series of low transactions that would look like ritual if you did not believe in economy. The bearers raise their plates, print their sentences into frost, collect, sort, take three steps, and manage a bit of heat. The leeches do not burn; they nap. The arthropods graze at seams so faint you need lenses to believe they are. The calligraphers move a dark pebble a thumb’s width in the shade of a knuckle of stone so that, tomorrow, the shiver-fetch from sun will be just this much longer.

The place is a factory with no factory and a language with no mouths. What lives here are organisms whose bodies are surface and calendar, whose thoughts come in footfalls, whose ambitions are measured in the new color of a lichen when the sky is briefly honest. They inherit not genes alone but a map written onto rock by centuries of correct touch. You could lay your palm to the ground and if you had their nerve you would feel the last mile of conversation go by: a three-beat pause at a ridge, a ligature over a crack, a suggestion that the path shift because the frost polygons are drifting imperceptibly downslope and that drift will become failure in three winters if it goes unmentioned.

Another place would reward eyes and sound; this one rewards hands and the willingness to count by season. Its inhabitants oblige. They have made themselves into infrastructure that feeds oasis-thin gardens the size of coins and then moves on without ever seeming to have been. And then, out of dimness, a ridge gets up and comes toward you, wearing its own weather and its own work, and passes as if you were a tor to be rounded. It does not look because looking cracks heat. It raises its fans to the patient cold and makes drink from cloudlessness, and in the wake of that act, life the size of a hair arrives, celebrates with chemistry quieter than breath on mica, and changes the continent by a hand’s breadth over a life.
