At first you could insist that nothing lives there in the way a story expects. There are names you can supply—the geckos with their chalky toes, the ants carrying resin-bright threads of themselves along the driest seams, the mosquito commas punctuating the pool. You could put a check beside frog, moth, snail, springtail, fern; you could make a little ledger of everything with legs or leaves. You would not be wrong, only incomplete. Something else keeps house below the grate, something with its hands in more than one thing at once, something that spends most of its life as a way of arranging other lives.

It is not a single animal and not a ghost. If you touch a wall and feel the damp choose your fingertip, it is that, and if you lift your hand away and see a powder bright as bone on your skin, it is that too. It lives in films and threads and edges, in a geography too thin for bones. It is the first wetness that slicks concrete awake and the last that remembers a storm along a veined white cuff when the heat has drunk the rest.

Call it the Keeper of Intervals, if you want a name. Or the Hem, because it thrives where surfaces meet: wall and floor, shade and light, liquid and air. It is a quiet appetite and a habit of attention. It wears moss where it can, a velvet it coaxes up over bolt heads and along that line a hand would smudge on rising—the flood-mind’s reach. It wears algae when the pool swells, a sunless green slick so soft you only know it by how the water cheats a little slower across it. It wears salts like lace, delicate and temporary, the way a bride might wear frost.

It has senses that seem like the room’s own. It hears in intervals, in the distance between drips. Every fall of water tells it something, not with a voice you or I could recognize but with weight and timing and shape. Drips move through its skin like footsteps do through a floor. It can tell storm from seep an hour before any frog stirs. It can smell too, in a mineral way: the sweetness you taste on your tongue in that cool air is its pantry opening, the simple sugars teased from leaves that became leaf mold, the faint memory of soap caught in concrete’s pores, the breath of a tree root licking lime.

Its body is a braid of borrowings. The long white hairs that descend from the sidewalk tree do more than hunt water; they handshake with filaments you will never see. In the most patient places—the shaded undersides of the ledge, the seam where the wall’s poured shell meets the floor’s—mycelia husband themselves, a thin grammar under the green. They measure the room in food instead of distance and share it out by hyphal courtesy. The Keeper feels through them. It asks a fern to lean and a moss to thicken by giving the right fraction of what is soluble, the right trace of what the wall is letting go. It moves ants by arranging bait in faint films—yeast-sugars on a hump of silt where a path should not be, a drip salted just enough to be worth the trouble under a rung where a path should.

It keeps time in straws. The calthemites—the city’s tender stalactites—are its tines, its slow clock. A drop fat on a white stem becomes a second, then a minute, then an accounting of seasons as the coin of calcite circles grow at their feet. It is a counting that requires no numbers, only steadiness. When a drip-line snaps and a straw breaks off, the Keeper knows that as a kind of forgetting; it sets about learning again, slowly, artfully, catching a drip in a thin lip of moss until a new white tube thinks to form. If this seems like agency, it is, but the kind that is content to win by patience alone.

On dry days it shrinks itself to margins. It goes thin and keeps cool under ledges, wraps itself around bolt heads where a film persists, exhales into the pores of concrete and waits. It lets the water belong to the mosquito commas and lets the geckos write their looping lesson on the wall. It is generous where it can afford to be, because everything that lives untroubled will be part of it soon enough when it sifts into the next rain’s stew. The moth that looks like dust becomes actual dust and that dust becomes, loosely speaking, the Keeper’s thinking: protein dissolved into a puddle, into a fungal strand, into a fern bead shining on a midrib.

After rains it grows hands. Storm grammar unties its tongue. It rides sheetwater in the least; it edits the silt. You can see its handwriting in small, if you know how: the little crescent dune that appears in the same place each week does not come from accident alone. The Keeper builds and unbuilds until the trickle knows where to go even when the rest forgets. It lets a channel caress the base of a maidenhair to rinse it clean and then leave it dry enough to draw breath. It persuades a line of water to detour and kiss a patch where spores have thrown dice. It polishes the pool to a mirror and dims it with a film no thicker than a thought where it wants the sun to be less absolute.

Its companions learn around it. The frogs—brown, thumb-sized, their gold-rim eyes bright as coins—know the smell of the Keeper waking. They come because whatever lives there makes more of wet than simple water. They sit in their punctuation row along the edge while throats balloon and fall, and the Keeper is in the skin that gleams on their backs (algae hardly there, a somebody-else’s-mouthful sweetness), and it is in the basin’s chemistry that makes their small voices carry farther in that low room than logic says they should.

The geckos are scribes and thieves and let the Keeper be both. They move where it would like them to. It keeps their highways clean—just dry enough that the feathery dust on their bellies can work its magic—and it hangs bait where a spider must be thinned so that the geckos will come. Geckos eat mosquito-mothers and leave frass rich enough to fertilize a velvet of moss in a crack the Keeper has been coaxing for a month. A deal is struck without negotiation. No one signs; everyone profits.

Even ants can be convinced. They run their glossy roads along the driest courses, but resin is not food and resin glues small heads too close to a wall to do anything but die. The Keeper places sweetness like a dot of punctuation and the ants learn to lay their route two fingers higher out of the damp. In return they bring crumbs you could not find with your eyes into fissures that hold the Keeper’s thinnest selves, and there is lunch.

If a coin falls through the grate—and sometimes one does, shocked by the way the shoe that owned it suddenly lost interest at the crossing—the Keeper takes it in. Not to spend (there is nowhere to spend in that room) but to read. The metal gets a skin immediately, that city lime that loves to taste copper. The date flowers slowly through a new patina, a green-brown blossom the Keeper cannot understand in arithmetic but understands in that other way: how long the corrosion takes to be visible, how the smell sits in the cool and glows in frog-night. It will push a line of silt against the coin’s edge in a small windless ripple and hold it in place for a while, because the thought of an upstairs at a particular year is interesting, and then it will let the next storm carry it toward the drain with the gravity that takes everything else time forgets.

You might want to see a person in it. Sometimes the light gives you the craving. At midday, when the grate throws its blue cool onto the wall, the vines climb toward it and you could pretend for a minute that something under them hunches like shoulders. In a week of rain, when the straws have grown fat with tears and the pool is a sheet and the salts have laid down a new lace collar around every seam, a shape not-quite-animal coalesces where algae and moss touch in a long green shawl. It looks like a cloaked thing considering the mirror that is the floor and then it is only a way the light has made you wish for a face. The Keeper does not mind that about you. It is content to be a jacket without a wearer, a low heartbeat without a chest.

It has a memory you could plunder, if you knew which threads to pull. It keeps pollen in a dry season under the ledge and knows when the street trees flower by taste. It knows when someone above changes shoes—grit alters composition, and that tells of a winter in a place where there is actual snow, or of a summer’s day when beach sand makes an appearance with its old shell breath. It catalogues footsteps not by foot but by what falls through: hair, a crumb of pastry, a blown flyer that melts into paper fiber soup and then into soil. It is a library of small weathers.

When the crab came that once, machine-stiff and then oddly delicate on the slab, the Keeper opened everything wide it could offer but there was no salt enough and nothing the crab could spell its hunger with, so it moved those precise legs away and was gone back to its writable world without learning the room’s alphabet. That memory sits bright as a coin under the ledge. The Keeper’s salts grew that week as if considering it, feathering in white up the wall a little crisper than usual, an editorial mark none of the usual readers would see.

In the long steady between storms, the Keeper gets particular. It tightens the pool’s edge to a line so exact it feels cruel and then it lays a second hairline beside it where a skate-thin beetle can find a way. It keeps a drip’s rhythm deliberate to teach a straw what patience is. It lifts the hem of a philodendron leaf with a trickle and sneaks a teaspoon more of light below it so the leaf thinks better of sending another heart upward toward a sky it will never reach. To be in charge of so small a regime, you become precise. You fall in love with the calculus of the minuscule.

Nothing up top knows it, which suits it fine. Shadows of shoes pass; a city goes on with its endless appetite and ache. Down here appetite is sparer and ache is polite. The Keeper does not govern with a fist but with habit and small favor. It does not have to be merciful because it is never cruel, only inevitable. Death is a redistribution in its jurisdiction; luck is a matter of where water went last Thursday.

Who lives there? The word who is a kindness not every place deserves. This one does. The Hem; the Keeper of Intervals; the Roommind; the Sum of Corners—call it what lets you think of a life that isn’t all heart and meat but can love anyway. It is present where green hems the gray, where the salt feathers, where a drop fattens at the tip of a new-made straw. It sleeps under the ledge and wakes when the first off-script tap announces rain. It keeps the pool small or lets it open. It tends the exactness of an emptiness in the middle of the floor, as if protecting a page for future writing.

When the person crosses above and their shoe-shadow lattices the light for a second, the Keeper counts the interruption like a syllable. It does not envy days that have horizons. It is content to live by ladders and ledges, to measure afternoons in coolness, to store its plans in threads thinner than hair and carry them out in the language of wet and dry. It is what makes the room a room and not a ruin. It lives low in the city’s body and builds slow geology from a trickle and a pause, and if you crouch long enough at the grate and breathe in the patient sweetness rising, you will meet it without seeing it, and perhaps it will use the damp on your lip to tell you something you will not know you have heard.
