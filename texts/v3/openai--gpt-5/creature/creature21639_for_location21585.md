They are the breath of the mountain and its appetite. The place is not their address; it is their body writ large, their molts and leavings and wills pressed together until “habitat” becomes “husk” and “husk” shelters the next will. If you try to count them as residents of rooms you will fail. They are, each in their way, rooms that move.

The first you notice are the day-rivers and night-rivers, because they unmake stillness wherever they pass. By day, swifts, swallows, and martins flake from the lips of openings like sparks thrown from a forge. Their nests crust the undersides of vaults—saliva bowls and half-collars, cream turned to stone—and a skein of their young makes a beads-on-a-string ringing along the ledges when the parents sweep home. They do not so much fly as fall together and pull up in the same moment, as if their bodies are hinged to the lift the structure exhales. Some click softly as they navigate the gloom; a few have taught their throats to echolocate, white notes bounced from resin ribs. They own the bright before it ever becomes glare.

Night inherits everything. Bats pour from wells in a single grammar you feel in your teeth. There are as many kinds as there are ways the air can be used: insect-eaters with wings long as knives that cut the corridors thin; little big-eared listeners that pour themselves into tight bellies of space, taking midges one by one like beads; nectar-drinkers that daub their faces yellow and come home faintly sugared, the pollen a currency that winds back into the honeyed courts by ways only their tongues remember. They warm the galleries, not just in the aggregate, but with their blood as a thing-that-happens-to-space, the temperature rising along their routes like the map of a sea current felt by fish.

Under all this rush live the papermouths. Wasps—foundresses with bellies of spring, daughters with jaws that rasp—reduce dead wood to fiber the way rain reduces cliff to silt. They measure with their antennae and build with their spit, panels hung one from the next until a membrane becomes a wall becomes a world. Queens are not crowned here; they are swollen ovals that the others pour food and attention around, lives lengthened into a kind of weather system. Their nests are not separate from passages; their brood cells are the geometry that bears a walkway you are tempted to call a bridge. They vibrate their bodies to ventilate, keep heat like a thought they share; they wash each other's faces, and their little domesticities make platforms resilient enough for the feet of strangers.

Close by them, but in chambers with a different scent—thicker, more resinous, with a treble of honey over it—the wax-makers hum. Wild bees, dark and gold, draw comb like an idea that must solve load and warmth at once. Their hexagons are math by lip and foot, tongues and bellies deciding an angle no tool could prove more true. Inside, dances happen that are maps, and the maps are not to distant meadows but to seams of flowers that hang within the architecture itself: strings of ivy blossom; a bower where a leak becomes a drinking place and a nectar fall; trays of moss gone fertile. They steal sticky tears from wounds in the structure and paste them into doors and baffles, medicinal; they polish their world with propolis until the corridors smell faintly of crushed green and summer tobacco. Their honey is stored weather, and where the stores sit thickest the walls are warmest, and where the walls are warmest the rest of the life draws close without knowing why.

Termites take what is still, drink the air, and release it in a better mood. They work in rooms where sound mutes down to felt, harvesting humidity with carton walls and letting it back in a ration that suits the brood. They walk on their own plaster in processions that are both road and appetite; they carry the dead in arcs to rooms where fungi wait, and the fungi stitch their little funerals into strength along the grain of the place. Some raise pallid gardens—combs where white-threaded mushrooms are tended as if they were children—so that in the darkest heart of the city, food is a steady light moving through. Queens here are obscene in their fecundity, bodies elongate as a rope of pearls, their rooms lined with the frass and touch of a thousand caretakers, the air moving in slow bells as the nest contracts and expands around the work of making more of itself.

There are ants, innumerable and various, writing the night with acid and sugar. Some shepherd scales and aphids along the green tunnels and milk them, bellies shining like beads while wasps and birds watch for a misstep. Others are predatory notes in the fabric, soldiers with heads like shields moving beneath the fluting of bats, raiding anything soft and unattended. Their trails are syntax you taste rather than see—sweet-semaphored exits from the honey rooms; bitter lines around the brood of others; peppered warnings threading a passage that seems silent until your fingers return tacky. To follow an ant path is to feel the palace’s decisions—turns chosen by scent and precedent—as if the walls held an editorial opinion.

Few lives here have no say in the making. The lichen does not, and yet it moves color into the picture that shifts with every wetting and drying; the moss keeps a drip from falling and so invents a frog pool; the frog, thumb-size, spends its entire citizenship in one cup, eating what the rainbreath feeds it and calling with a voice thin as a whetted coin; mosquito larvae turn on their heads like punctuation marks in those same water towers, pumping at the film that is sky to them. There are damselfly nymphs in some cups—blue ghosts that will crawl up into humid day, split, and leave a paper sleeve of themselves clinging to a green blade like a shed oath. Blind fish ride the trickle in cisterns and know the hollows by current and hunger, silver when occluded light stumbles across them, pale stones animating to take a gnat.

Spiders have invented the border between rooms as a kind of belief. They string harps in the margins of air where nothing ought to collect and yet does: a moth turned stupid by light leaking around a resin pane; a fly slowed by the heavy air along a dripline; a batling too clumsy to save, which is rare and tragic and becomes a week’s weather of food when it happens. Their webs hum when the chord of the place hums, and in that music some choose strums that warn them; others the silences that mean a fly has arrived.

There are tunnels that are nothing but scent and touch, and in them live things you’d miss if all you had were eyes. Silverfish like slivers of mica, laying down the slow grammar of grease; woodlice, blue-gray, assembled like rivets along the undersides of beams, eating what eats the paper so that the paper lasts; roaches, disgust and mercy both, sweeping crumbs of protein into the cycle, disappearing under the tongue of a stoat or the jab of a toad before your disdain has finished forming. Springtails pepper puddles when the air swings damp, buoyed on surface tension like script you can’t read because you are the wrong size.

At the green margins, life peacocks a little because there is space to. Bowerbirds choose courts where the light falls cleanest and the sound has a nice return. They lace lengths of vine and stick into alleys with perspective tricks that fool females into admiring secrets of distance; they bring blue, because blue is rare up here and says “I search,” and clean, because cleanliness says “I am not sick,” and snail shells because spirals read as something like competence to their private taste. When they are finished wanting, the ornaments walk away with them; the bower remains a grammar that other lives use as if it were always meant to be a path.

Predators here are not tyrants but editors. Owls move in the lower registers, gathering confusion into beads of silence that slip down shafts. A weasel writes a little line of nails along a feeder passage and a sudden absence opens where a bird was. Snakes metabolize the patient water into frog and then snake, drawn by the plink of a bromeliad cup and the smell of green rot that means, to them, food-with-a-pulse. Lizards, thin as reeds, warm themselves along the underlit lips of walls and take flies that cross the wrong seam, their tongues dovetailing with whatever phenomenon the architects accidentally shaped into a bug road.

Nothing with a stomach lives alone. Cleaning is social, and so are parasites. You see small birds—white-eyes, tits, the equivalent in whatever language lives here—patrol the backs and throats of larger birds at dawn, taking lice as fees for their calm. Midges ghost along with bats, surviving their hosts’ appetite by being the right shape in the wrong place to catch. Mites graze the sugar crust on the rim of a bee’s eye, do not harm it, and in return ride the buzz to a thousand flowers, their lives a coincidence leveraged into lineage. When a predator ends a warm life, you can feel the tumble begin: carrion beetles clacking their shields; larvae unscrewing meat into soil; ants reducing organs to questions of trail; bones becoming white hems on the bases of perches, mineralized memory the place wears as cuff and collar.

There are gliders who are not birds. Rodents with membranes like slack sails wait for prescribed winds and step out, trusting the writing the air has made on the edges of paper galleries. They do not seem to fall; they choose a new floor on which to walk, and their route is a practice shared mother to pup until it becomes family. There are beetles that turn themselves into leaves or bark and are accepted by gravity as such until a lute of air in a chimney says “rise” and they do, an elegant lie told to updrafts in exchange for height.

And everywhere, beneath and within all of it, the hyphae, a nerve net that works not with speed but with thoroughness. Mycelium is the quiet final say. It flexes wood that was paper into something with the elasticity of tendon; it drinks the sugars that leak from places they shouldn’t and turns them into walls; it signals stress to its own kin through long threads, the way rain signals more rain to a soaked hillside. It is the arbitration of rot—the difference between a room falling and a room settling, between a handhold shearing and a handhold turning soft and becoming a gentler curve beneath the feet of generations. Its occasional light—a foxfire seam along a wound or the breath-bright halo of a bole polished by wings—is not illumination so much as an honest admission that even darkness does work here.

If there are rulers, they are weather and hunger, but both are tempered by the habit this place has of distributing itself. An extra drip forms, and a court of bees fattens and keeps that water from persistently falling where it would rot the paper, while frogs recover it and make more of themselves. A new opening is punched by collapse or storm, and it becomes a singing well that the swifts inherit, a court for bower-building because sound pleases there, a sun for an ivy rope that will knit a dangerous edge into a path. Too many moths bloom in a season, and the bats’ wombs swing fuller, and the owls follow, and the guano salts rise one more knuckle, white as truth, at the base of a rib.

They measure time with their bodies. Bees surge when rain turns pollen sweet and hold when the wind goes thin; termites thicken a wall over twenty airs; a bat passes its roost down mother to daughter like a talisman and the roost remembers her and becomes her shape. The loudness itself has tides—crickets that scream for a year and then none at all for seven, cicadas scaling their arrival so that the walls hum different histories according to which brood is up. To you, this all sounds like chaos until you stand still and the rhythms find you: the dim tumbling ring of drops in hollows, the cathedrals of wing-whisper, the scrape-languages of jaws and tongues, the debt-collecting click of predators passing near, the tuned arches that turn wind into that feeling in your teeth. To them, the noise is map and law. Silence is the anomaly, a moving hole you track with the prickling of your feather-filaments and finger-hairs because it means an owl is here and you would rather be elsewhere.

When you ask who lives here, the place answers with hands you can’t see. It answers with a damp warmth on your palm from walls that are never quite still; with a smear of sweetness at a corner that means “Do not walk; bees work here”; with a filament nicking your wrist where a spider’s margin of safety extends into your space; with a shadow-burst whose body you never saw and a lizard flick you only feel because air rushed to occupy where it was. You look for a people with chiefs and find instead a republic of tendencies: everything that holds a drip is peopled; everything that catches light is gardened; everything that protrudes becomes perch and is therefore rounded, glossed, made kinder to land on.

And if you must single out citizens: say their names like this.

Say: papermouths, wax-heaters, threaders of absence, silk-sowers, bruise-winged dayrivers, nightcloth, white-eyes of mercy, editors on silent wings, needle-lizards, cup-frogs, drift-fish, harp-weavers, rivet-eaters, blue-fanciers, sugar-herders, scent-scribes, leaf-liars, membrane-walkers, salt-raisers, rot-judges, and the quiet, patient punctuation of foxfire where the book of it all is being written, again, with bodies.
