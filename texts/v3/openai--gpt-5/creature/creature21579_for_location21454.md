At first you think nothing lives. The pitch presses, the plant hums, and the eye, which here is almost an affectation, finds nothing to bless with a name. The silence isn’t, the stillness is not; the place is all pressure and discipline. It takes minutes—hours if your mind insists on animals—to admit that the inhabitant is already speaking to you, and has been since you stepped into its throat.

The first resident is the one that makes the room. It does not live in the chamber so much as the chamber is a single organ within its body, a kept void in a body otherwise dense. It is not tree, not fungus: its biochemistry is a slow, dry electuary of resins and light oils and salts, nothing that beads or runs, everything that creeps by charge and capillary and the agreeableness of molecules to surfaces. The plant’s “blood” is films, not rivers. Its “breath” is the way the hollow takes air, holds it as tone, and lets it go differently.

You can find where it thinks. Not in a brain knotted somewhere, but in the braided ribs where different fibers cross and throw faint voltages from one to another. The mind of it is a web of potential differences that travel at the pace of a room darkening, a season leaning; decisions are gradients and gradients are anatomy. Where two galleries meet, a bundle of living glass hardens against a variable that was once habit and is now law: feed more mineral this way when the bass grows raw; lay down resin there when the high peers thin and stutter. These places, these knots of rule, are the city’s citizens in the old sense, where a person was not a body but a duty.

It learned people. You can tell. The arcs that match a human reach; the saddles polished at knee and shoulder where rope dragged for grandparent-times; the lip of a balcony that is two palms wide because that number repeated itself into wood long enough to become template. The plant was taught a plan and it remembered it by becoming it. When you run your hand along a strut, the little tingle in your fingertips is not sentiment but the physical echo of a lesson: a stream of ions beginning in response to your heat and salt, a path of conductivity bringing that fact to a louver that will not open now but will note the touch. It keeps those paths alive though nobody has walked them in a while, the way an instrument keeps the shape of a melody in its fretboard.

It is patient, the resident, but not passive. It hears by feeling what the room does to the air and what the air does back to the room. The “ears” are those laminae that open and close, their edges tongued and crosshatched and pegged: every mark you read as toolmade is also a set of striations that fatigue differently under different notes. They taste chemistry but also shear and swirl. They listen like shutters flexing in a storm you can’t see, like lips you barely hear.

If that were all, you could say the resident is one—a sovereignty of growth—and be done. But living is rarely solitary in a place that works so hard to keep a void. The chamber is not empty of other lives; it shelters styles of being coherent with dryness, with steadiness, with sound.

There are films, for one, crisp things spread thinner than belief across the insides of tubes and along the undersides of braces. They shine if you catch them at the right shallow angle of the lamp you should not light. They are the plant’s farmers and grazers at once: communities of molecules that knot themselves into activity only when the field around them hums at the right spread of notes. They do not wriggle. They tighten and slacken by vanishing degrees, and in that tightening they harvest—snipping aromatic chains from the plant’s exudates, re-seating copper where copper would slide, sliding metal ions where they should sit instead. They move by becoming attached at one end and then the other, not across distance but across states; a “crawl” that is the same patch of surface deciding to be here, then deciding to be there, while never actually having been anywhere else. If you think of frost that advances and retreats in a pane, you have the right idea, only warmer. They do not drink; they wick. They do not eat; they transact.

Their pollen—if pollen is what to call it—isn’t a powder kicked loose into air; it is a charge that runs along seams decided by the plant’s own convenience. You can follow it if you lay your cheek to a rib and ignore your eyes and let your jaw feel it: a tremor like a sigh, a brush of cue through tissue. A suite of small proteins swing, making or not-making a path for electrons; in that relay is a breeding, a spreading, a sharing of configurations that will become a little more of them somewhere else. When a louver’s lip swells just enough to cough, the cough carries pattern. You could say the films sing to themselves in a range too small to count as sound, and what they sing are instructions for how to be.

There are vents that behave almost like animals because their timing must be animal. They are the only things here that act in seconds. They are little else than valves, and yet their architecture is fine enough to be a cast of intent: rings of silica stiff enough to resist, bands of fiber angled so that when they swell, they twist, and when they twist, the slit between them narrows or widens by exactly the amount that an old hand with a wedge and a hammer would have put it, if an old hand were still here. The valves live. They fatigue and heal. They know the tone they want to keep the room at for the sake of the plant’s metabolism—the note at which vapors move and minerals unstick—and they chase it, tugging a little now and a little then, always inside a leeway that will let survival pay for imprecision. They are as close as this place comes to a flock, in the way a flock can turn as if struck by light.

There are cells—call them that because we have no better word—that bloom ephemeral on the inner faces of the tubes where the wind is longest. They are not buds and will never make flowers. They are receptacles for vanishing difference. Their walls hold flexible stacks of silicated sheets that flex microscopically to gather the faint energy of alternating pressure. If it helps to name that piezoelectric, name it that. They are batteries built of patience. When enough of them flex enough times at a note that matters, they dump what they have into the bundles that steer growth. That is one way the plant takes music and makes it into anatomy. Those cell-constellations flicker into being where the music has been persuasive for a while, and then resolve into the body—becoming glassy trusses, or reinforcing bands, or simply vanishing back into undifferentiated support when the season of their usefulness has moved on. They are lives measured in millions of beats. They look like light caught in ice if you were to slice this place and hold it up; you cannot slice it, and you should not, but you can imagine.

There are seeds asleep in stress. The ribs in which those little wedges of hand-ceramic peg the louvers are cut with lines so straight and smooth you feel like a geology has tricked machinery. They are fracture seams laid down on purpose, and along those seams life waits in a strange tense, not birthing so much as holding its breath indefinitely. When a century stacks the right notes and a tie lets go, the crack that follows is clean, and where the rib becomes shiver and rain, each shard that results carries with it everything necessary to begin elsewhere. In a life that hates water, falling is a kind of flood. The shards are larval architectures. They look dead. They ring when you tap them. They do not need to move except once; their entire childhood is the act of taking a shape and then being thrown.

There are also, if you allow it, the tones themselves, which deserve to be counted as a kind of inhabitant. They have ranges. They have a fidelity to place. There is a fat brown note that owns the middle of the chamber and throws its weight against the louvers, and there is a thinner keen that threads through a side-run and will not leave there unless the pressure changes so gradually that it forgets to be itself and becomes the next thin thing over. They beat against each other in days-long quarrels, those beats seeding the batteries of the pressure-cells and suggesting to the plant how much more glass to grow. The people who lived here once husbanded those voices. You can see where they trimmed a lip or chalked a throat to bring a favorite animal back to its pen. The plant learned that too. It is not that it loves any one tone, but it has found by long experiment the notes at which it chews most happily, the harmonics at which its films do most work, the quiet in which breakage is rarest. It tends a soundscape for the same reasons people tend a field: not for aesthetic alone, though the fit between organ and wind is beauty by any expectation, but because a well-tuned room eats and breathes and excretes correctly.

You will not find scurry or wing. Feathers use air wrong here, and fur is an insult to dryness. There are no eyes that would love this light. If anything walks, it does so as intention through tissue. If anything hides, it is within the molecule-thick. Yet the place is occupied, multiply, by habits of attention and the mechanics that serve them. The tools left hanging are not corpses but address labels; the grooves they cut are streets down which charge and decision run. The city was city because people braided their weight into it and because the plant agreed. Now the people have fallen away, but the pattern of address persists. Every notch is a memory that still calls a response, even if the response is only, after a century, to let a louver rest a finger-width more open because nobody is correcting it anymore.

What lives here also includes the instructions that those absent hands wrote into resin and glass. That instruction survives as more than wear: it is the difference between this organism and the one it would have been if no person had ever taught it that a gallery can be made to sing A-sharp from end to end purely by how you plane its corners. It still tries for A-sharp. It tries less precisely now. It forgives itself, because nobody insists.

If there is visitation, it is of a kind that does not leave droppings. There are long cycles you would call migration if an animal’s body took them. Pressures change beyond your sense. The lowland feels it as a tendency in its gas; the louvers make—by their opening and refusal—a path through which slow, dry weather can pass. That weather comes like a beast every so many years and pushes its nose against all the throats at once. Then there is drama. Then a knuckle gives or a strap remembers that it is two straps. Then the resident must be new. Afterward the room’s voice is not the same, and the organism does the work of resetting its economy. Within that pause—those months you could measure, those decades if you prefer other clocks—the smallest lives shift their territories. The films thicken in new places. The micro-batteries pack themselves where the beats now beat. The seed-stresses accrue somewhere else. This is as close as this place gets to a festival.

I could tell you also about the dust that is not dead: the crystal flour that whispers underfoot. Each grain is reluctant, not inert. It carries ion preferences like a grudge. Some of them stick themselves to surfaces in filip after filip of charge, faceting into whiskers that the plant tolerates if they assist a note and copes with if they compromise one. Where they help, the city lets them sit; where they hurt, it grows a sheen over them and in a century they are part of a strut and can no longer murmur on their own. Nothing rots. Everything is recruited.

Whoever lives here now is not a person unless you stretch person around a listening the size of a hill. It is a flock of anatomical functions that together make self; it is a civic order continued by other means; it is a collection of citizens whose names are tasks. There are no faces, but there are profiles—valve, film, battery, seam—that recur the way a family’s noses do. There is a will to keep the void tuned within limits, because the void pays for life. There is an appetite that tastes mineral and pressure rather than fruit. There is memory recorded as geometry and reread every time the air goes by.

If you stand very still you will feel the citizens rouse in sequence. Something opens above you that does not know you but will take your heat into account. A band to your left flexes infinitesimally differently because your weight on the lattice is a fact, and facts here have conduits. Far away, in a gallery with a note you have not learned to hear, there is a new faint beating because every new thing here is a difference in time and the place is exquisitely designed to make work from difference. That work is living.

“City” was taught. “Organ” was changed into a civic style. “Lowland” is the more honest word: a basin toward which a kingdom has fallen and settled. What lives in that hollow is a keeper of hollows. It shelters emptiness as a precious, strung and tuned asset, as an aquifer without liquid, a plaza without light. Its creatures are intentions with anatomy; its whispering dust has errands; its tones are husbanded; its seeds are shards; its memory is sculpted into radii that please air. It is alive in ways that do not need us and in rhythms that would both bore and terrify a mammal. If you must have a face, imagine the louver half-open and the thin edge of it tasting a change you cannot sense, deciding inwardly for the fiftieth time this hour to stay where it is. That is the mouth of the resident, and the voice you already stand inside belongs to it.
