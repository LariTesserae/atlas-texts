Light lives here first. Not as a painter, not as a window, but as a creature with a regular pulse and appetites. It is thin-bodied and columnar where it drops; broad-backed and diffuse where the stone itself glows. It knows its route. Each day it puts down its white feet in the same long steps, testing the basins, licking the moss, calling insects up from their film of sleep. When the air above is glass-dry, light arrives hard and spare; when the air is soft, it fattens, becomes milk, thickens until it can be seen catching itself and falling. Every cycle it draws water out of the rock the way a tide pulls a marsh inside-out. That drawing is a kind of feeding. Everything else that lives here arranges itself around it as if around a watering animal with a strict schedule.

Stone lives here too, the old resident with slow nerves. It doesn’t move in our time, but it does answer. It opens its pores at warmth and shuts them at dark. Along its edges where it once ran like honey, it holds strings you can only hear with your fingertips. Set your hand to a calcite rib and the day’s light will tremble through your bones. There is a memory under that—a broken-off river that wrote the first grammar on these walls. The stone keeps the rules; it is what the rules were discovered in. It lends out niches and takes them back by powder, by skin, by the smallest filings of itself. It breathes in chalk, breathes out water. It is not indifferent. It is a very slow host.

In the thin hours, before the largest beams wake, the room is the animals of damp. Filaments and films do most of the eating then. Bacteria as clear as their work coat the basins in skin; green threads of algae braid up wherever yesterday’s light stood in a way that didn’t hurt. Lichens do their lacework along the shaded walls, broadcasting the dust of themselves the way a baker flours a board—a living talc that takes your thumbprint and uses it, later, as a map. Fungi sit with their gray fans open under the pale-root’s dripping, as if listening. They are the archivists of this place: whenever a leaf scrap arrives from above or a moth body falls down the chimney of a beam, they take it, sort it, translate it into flavor for everything else green.

Roots are the most muscular of the plants here. They come down in curtains as if remembering rain so strongly they have to go meet it. One of them has become a person in its own right: the wrist-thick, barkless rope that bowed until its face touched the floor. Where it kisses stone it has found a way to flower in the underlight, a rain of pale tubes with no agenda but giving. The tubes pay fungi, and fungi pay back with sugar broken fine and fed upward. At the root’s foot is a ring of little derivative plants—leaf-ideas, attempts—drawn wrong in the best way, shaped by having light that is always there and never direct. They hold their palms open to water as if it were coins. If there is a queen in this hall, she hangs from above and doesn’t know she’s reigning.

The green that keeps its own counsel lives where light comes like a guest, not a judge. Coins of moss sit under the kind shafts and look illegal, as bright as emerald spilled into bone. They drink when the beams pull water up; they hold it all night like a thought. Two ferns have tried themselves there, each frond a ribcage learning to breathe. On the better-dry rim of another beam, the succulents sit like someone had melted marbles into the floor and forgotten about them. They are all reserve and patience. Each pearl-leaf is a lung for fog. When the uprush in the tall beam gets playful, they lean and shine like a little crowd leaning out of a balcony to watch the street.

In the air: the midge peoples. They are not a blur unless you look through them. They set up their tents in the bright pillars and lace them into something you only see when a single thread crosses your eye. Noon is their market, evening their exodus. The adult day-flies spend themselves in a minute and are replaced by sons and daughters you could fit into the letter O on a page. The midges know the drafts as you know the hallway to your kitchen. When the inversion flips and the hall exhales upward, they lift as if the floor had tilted. They mate in little cursive notes and let the winning letters fall into basins to wriggle and graze. They are as brief as any sparks and they run the room’s calendar all the same.

Where the midges are, the long ears circle. The glider-bats here are pale, almost edible-looking, their ears the translucency of a candle curled back. They never take the center of a beam—it has made predators of them before. They keep to the edgelight, cut lap after patient lap along the curved wall where insects hesitate. The bats have three rooms like this, and they understand the tempo of each. They arrive when the sun twists right in the throat-wells, skirt the tenor of the day, and leave as the hall’s breath turns. They speak in a metal ping you feel in your teeth more than you hear, dropping coins of sound and catching them with their faces.

There is a lizard whose job is to read heat. It is hammered flat by its own habits, a little god of thresholds. It basks a palm-width into the wash of light until its blood carves awake, then slides back into the room’s thought to cool, then out again. When a drip lands near its eye, it taps the floor with the very tip of its tail as if to say count that. It eats the up-drifting things—spent midge shells, seeds that have fallen upward as if by joke—and it has learned to hop just enough to pluck them out of their stream. Sometimes it climbs the root and leaves its suit there when the damp season trades skin for shine.

On the instruments and small inventors’ side of life: the beetle that collects rain that isn’t rain. A darkling, likely, with a back like slate. It knows precisely where the room sweats first on a good day. It stoops, drags a curled lichen chip under the drip, props it at the angle that makes a cup out of almost nothing. Later it will come back, set its palps at the rim like a child at a pond, and drink greedily. It will break the cup and eat that too, years from now, after the lichen has been paid down to taste.

A mouse works here midday. Stone colored, ink-eyed, a little smith. It brings snails from the notches and uses the ridge everyone uses as an anvil. It has a hammer it favors—a pebble with a good flat—held and struck with practice like someone who has been doing this since mothers and aunts taught the trick. It leaves nothing arranged. Piles grow where they will, record without ceremony, a calendar in shells. The mouse sleeps elsewhere, where the roof opens to stars and seeds, but this is its shop.

Edges hold the slow ones. In the cool clefts where you can smell coin and damp iron, salamanders keep their whole lives. Their skins are hardly there; their gills are small flags you’re not sure you’re meant to see. They are not blind, exactly, but their eyes are like searching questions. They come out of the creases only when the room remembers storm, when every seam sweats at once and tiny channels speak to each other. They take what swims in the black pans and go back into thought. If they have language, it is the pacing of drops.

In the bowls: clear shrimp with old expressions. They were washed here by a year that had enough water to carry glass children down a light shaft, and they took the basins as a city. They eat the film that prints itself new every day around noon. When the midge boom falls in, they are opportunists with whiskers. Some carry eggs; some do not. At their smallest they are almost not there; at their largest they are antiques. To watch them is to see what attention looks like underwater.

The room attracts visits the way a well does. Swiftlets come the way coins do to a fountain—quickly, with a wish. They do not live here. They are punctuation. Even the owl who sometimes takes the root as a wrist-sitting perch is only passing. The owl closes its eyes until they are wires and takes the room in through its feathers. It knows that half-light is safest and cuts its outline across a pillar’s edge like a math problem. It keeps track of the mouse. It will ignore the lizard. When it breathes in this chapel, the breath shows for a second in the light and is gone, a visible thread of private thought.

In the joints and under the ledges: always a spider. Two, three. Webs are difficult here because the air is not still anywhere for long, but a patient weaver strings a skewed ring where a beam and a draft don’t agree and harvests the conflict. There are also the floor’s shepherds—springtails that hop when the powder shifts, mites that herd yeast invisible to us, small isopods that live in the living film on humid days and roll inward when things go back to normal. They are custodians of interruption. When you kneel, they map your knee in a circle of avoidance and then return to their graphs.

Above all that, plants that never meant to be cave plants make a go of it because the rules allow a little audacity. A childlike creeper has figured out how to grow leaves that are mostly light with just enough green to be practice. Fernlets unwind and rewind with the season, writing notes that only the water reads. Succulents slim down in winter and bead up again when the beam sleeps elsewhere for a month. The lichens in the dim make slow fireworks—they do not so much grow as claim.

There are other lives you wouldn’t call lives until you sat with them. The inward and outward wind keeps time. It smells of sage sometime, sometimes of flint the second after it breaks. You can tell if above has rain in hand by how sweet the draft tastes in the sinuses you didn’t know could taste. The inversion is a species. The powder that erases your tracks while you are still making them is one too. It has a patience that embarrasses solidity.

Everything has learned the gradient. Even predators here are polite about it. They keep one foot in the dictionary of shadow and one in clarity so that their edges don’t betray them to talons on the sky. Nobody stages in the bright center except those digesting light. Hunts happen at margins as conversations happen at doorways. The room is always almost empty because all its fullness is in the way pieces meet—drip to cup, film to mouthpart, beam to frond.

On days when the shafts fog and the insiders’ rain begins, the hall’s population changes mood. The shrimp lift their bodies and feed without moving. The salamanders come to the lip of their clefts and hold their fringes out to be rinsed. The beetle walks bold across the open to check all its cups. The bats don’t come—that is someone else’s weather. The succulents sweat and take their own tears back in. Even the calcite sings audibly then, a kettled choir as the thin sheets grow and shrink like fish gills. If the owl happens to be present, it will turn its head very, very slightly, the way one turns toward a remembered tune.

Night is not blank here. The beams sigh and fold back into stone. The rock glows a little longer than you think it should, the way a page will hold light when you look away. The air grows more still and warmer than the desert above for a while, an inversion you can lean on. Everything steps inward—lizard to crack, mouse to above, bats to their other hall, swiftlets to their dust-smelling niches. What remains are the residents that were never leaving: lichens with their silent tatting, roots tasting the day out of the stone, fungi digesting the leaf and the moth as slowly as counted prayer. The chamber holds your last footfall for a minute and eats it. Powders begin their slow drift back down to firstness.

So who lives here? A colony of arrangements.

Light with its temper and its calendar. Stone with its strings and its memory of water. Water itself, most often a habit wearing beads. Roots that give and don’t know they are generous. Plants that learned to be coins and lenses. Midge-cities that knit noon into fabric, and bats that unknit the edges. A lizard that reads warmth like scripture and a gecko that harvests the gravity that isn’t. A mouse with a hammer and an anvil and a pile of shells that proves culture can weigh half an ounce. Beetles with cups that are also roofs. Shrimp that brought a prairie of taste into bowls of glass. Salamanders that are the room’s punctuation marks, appearing only when it speaks rain. Spiders that tax the air. Powders that are not quite animals but act like whole weather-systems in miniature.

No one owns it. No one announces themselves. Even the owl relinquishes its claim as soon as the draft changes. What lives here lives by not insisting. By knowing the edge of light and using it rather than testing its center. By moving at the room’s speeds: the snap of feeding, the sidestep of heat, the hour it takes a drop to find its lip, the year it takes a lichen to sign its name, the hundred years it takes the roof to go thin as a thought.

You could think of it as empty, seeing only bones and brightness. Stay long enough to let your pupils learn the lesson and emptiness will resolve into use. Then you’ll understand that the chamber itself is the final animal here—a big white lung working light and water into something with breath—and everything else is its flora and its fauna, and the day is what it grazes.
