They live low and laterally, as if the place itself taught the lesson: spread out, keep close to the skin, make your life in the film where one thing touches another.

The first citizens fit inside a droplet and leave their color in it. They milk the dim light with pigments older than leaves. Their bodies are scaffolds of slime and patience—halophiles pinking the quiet sumps, purple sulfur bacteria lying just beneath the day, green cousins lower still, sulfate-breathers shut in blackness where the water has put its oxygen away for other conversations. Iron-eaters coax orange and soot-black carpets from trickles, thread themselves into frills and whiskers on the undersides of plates. Manganese sheaths accrue grain by grain until a microscopic forest stands along a rivulet. Fungi with a taste for mineral and oil lay down black varnish on concrete seams; they look like shadow but they are appetite. In the brine itself float eggs, ovals translucent as mica, and nauplii the size of punctuation mark a grammar of thrashing and pause. The mats blink, not with eyes but with chemistry, whenever the valve upstream closes a little or opens a hair, and in that blink you can see an entire street of lives tuck their beads in and wait, then unfurl again.

Move up a millimeter and it’s crowded. Springtails pinwheel across a gleam of condensation that has dared the underside of a pipe. Mites drill into the froth left behind by a passing wave of backwash, leaving hieroglyphs legible only to things with eight legs. In a crack where a galled gasket has shed crumbs of rubber into a little scald of salt, ants unpack themselves like black punctuation and test everything with their faces, write routes with the flex of their bodies. They farm nothing here; they harvest schedule. They know that the new bead of sealant is a sweetness for one hour and a map for a week. They know that behind the second door in the pump house, the drain under the lathe leaks for four minutes after a shift break and you can drink that water if you are small enough and will not be missed.

Around any lamp there is the soft agriculture of midges and mosquitoes. They occur as weather: a density of being that thickens corners, teaches the moths to hammer their wings, sets a murmur along the elbows of steel. The mosquitoes here are plump and unhurried because everywhere offers a sip; the midges rise by the millions and braid themselves into veils like knitwork on the move. Long-jawed spiders throw their silver ladders across rails before dusk and catch entire minutes of that weather. Water spiders pace the gloss of a weir with their palms pressed to the skin of surface tension, taking the frail ones. By morning, what web is left is salted and brittle, and the spider replaces it with more of the same, because the world refills itself every evening as reliably as a tide.

Beneath the films, the larvae make use of the blind economy. They rasp at the purple colonies, not knowing a name for them, only the taste. They sew themselves to hypnotic threads and lift and lower like spun bobbins. Amphipods, thin as a wink, throw their little stiff crescents into the water and go jittering along in bursts. Transparent shrimp hang in the slow roses of filament and pluck at whatever drifts. In concrete seams where the warm water never lets the crack dry, planarians iron themselves back and forth, practicing the smooth swerve. There are worms whose houses are straws they’ve made from grit and spit, and when the valves breathe out they lean from their doors into the passing feast and then slam shut with the importance of monks at prayer.

Fish here look old not in years but in agreement. They hold their bodies level to a habit learned from making peace with gates. Killifish, mollies, gobies—whatever genus you want to call them if you were being precise—work in layered calms like elevators that refuse to speak up. They know the siren's long throat-sound and move back as if politely giving the machine its space, and when the outfall reverses they ride the first pulse forward the way a city commuter steps into the first car out of habit. Eels articulate themselves out of holes dark as muscle, smelling everything with surface more than nose, attend to events and then take those events inside the banks as if filing them. Sometimes at night a shoal the color of mercury will pass through the main channel and turn all the complicated light into one flat sliding sheet; you could believe a single mind owns them then.

Along the hard edges, crabs do a patient mechanic’s dance. They test a bolt head as if it were a dial, pick at a scrap of paint the taste of which means barnacle and algae and man. They duel with gestures that are almost theater but mean a great deal to them, and then fall to work again. Shore crabs come in on a dark tide and pay a brief visit to a sump lip and look ridiculous and holy, all at once, walking sideways into an opening that was made for a pump. Silvery isopods tilt their armor and run the little back lanes of salt just as confidently as rats run the big ones.

Rats are the proper citizens of the overhauls and undertunnels. They are slick with the place, breathing it in the way their coats drink its shine. They trace paths worn right through precipitated halite as if they had dragged chalk lines with their bodies. They keep their children in cable trays and their grandmothers behind thawing pipes and their dead under blue pallets, and at night the whole margin of the process hums with their errands. They are not desperate; hunger is a novelty here. They time their approaches to gull roosts, work the perimeters of spill pans as if they were gardens. They understand the calendar kept by human hands; the day the cold rooms exhale melt into runnels is a holiday, and the afternoon when grain cars click to a stop at the apron is feast.

Above them the air runs its own traffic. Dragonflies hold the lanes like sentries at checkpoints, their eyes done in mirrored beads, their abdomens fine as antennae that read invisible currents. They pause at midspan and rotate a degree, then ease forward, always half ordering, half auditing. Swallows and martins write zipping annotations right under the sentence of a bridge, and in the dimness their grace is almost indulgent compared to the pragmatic grace on the water. Bats hang in the cool lip beneath the deck through the day and then injure the dusk with sound alone; when they launch, all at once, the air seems to reset its clock. Night herons move in sideways, already dressed for this light. Their calls sound like somebody thumping a wooden key, a metronome kept for no one but themselves, but everything with legs hears it and knows what time it is.

The machines are not alive, but they have been taught and they teach. They are hospitable in the way cliffs are: narrow ledges, luck, and repetition produce culture. The gauge panels hold generations of moths like family portraits. The housings of motors bear graffiti of oil and dust that the lizards read; geckos blink wide and then pull their soft feet up the walls and leave the faintest polka-dot of touch in the fresh sealant without ever knowing they’ve signed anything. Wasps paper a comb under a junction box and use the warm hum of wire as a weather report. If there’s a keyboard in a control shed that never gets closed, its trenches hold silverfish mistaking code for canyon, and roaches with their lacquered shields walk at the edge as if reading.

People live here less as colonists than as keepers of a long animal. They walk a loop that is older than their boots. They are not rangers—there is nothing to forbid—but farmers of failure. Their eyes rest naturally on the spot where two dissimilar metals meet, on flange lips, on weld toes. They can listen to a pump through a wall and know how long it has left in the day. In their heads, the plant is a map of habits; they know which run will sweat first on a morning like this, which bearing will warm an extra line on the thermal camera because the weather turned to soup last night. They do not hurry unless there is a lesson to be learned. When they move a gate by inches, they watch the surface for what it says—there is a literacy to the rings that a textbook won’t teach. Their offerings are small and exact: a zinc hung from a threaded eye, a smear of graphite under a hinge, a gasket changed before the failure is dramatic enough to be a story. They leave chalk on spares as if chalk could hold back entropy by naming it.

Their pockets choose them. In a pocket: salt in colloids, grit of iron, one brass screw that came home instead of going into the bin, a petrified wad of thread tape, a stub of carpenter’s pencil with teeth marks of thinking in it. On their skin: a glaze of place, thin, carnivorous, salt rushing itself quietly into everything that offers water. They are loved by small, unasked-for retinues. A pair of gulls that recognize a certain lunchbox. An egret that has learned what a slow-turning valve means in the language of shrimp. The shyest bats that come back to the same lip above the door every dawn, sewing their tiny toes into the same line of shade. In the slack of afternoon, when the air presses even the thoughts, a stray dog sleeps under the elevated pipe between basins eight and nine, ribs but not argument, and wakes only for the train that comes once and only one way.

There are migrations here that no map will draw. After rain comes from nowhere and leaves the way it came, eels go across the causeway in the night and lie there, astonishing polish in a world of matte, and the people who know this lift them by the gullet back into a runnel with careful hands. The first bright days after a long dimness pull dragonfly nymphs from their cases in a sort of ragged festival under the railings, and by noon the service walkway is sequined with discarded skins. In a week when someone closes a brine line for a repair, a spill of fugitives—brine flies and mite flotillas and the rare, delicate shrimp who prefer a salinity that is not in fashion today—evacuates into a pan and writes a new, temporary book for the shorebirds, who turn the pages with pattering feet and needle their citations straight out of the margin.

There are things that visit with a bigger argument: the occasional osprey that builds a nest of stolen cable ties on the crossarm no one reaches, and who looks at everyone as if the plant were her sea; a cloud of termites breaking out after a rain with wings that evaporate into litter at your feet; jellyfish washing up a concrete throat when the estuary breathes deep enough and teaching the rats that there is softness you cannot chew without consequence. Once, on a high tide driven wrong by atmospheric mood, an octopus arrived in a trash rack and wrote hieratic gray across the bars with arms that had never imagined iron, then unlearned quickly and went back the way it came, all deliberation and no regret.

Others live here slowly and without show. Rust absolutely lives here; it breeds in crevice and under seam, consumes and is sloughed, a red animal that makes offspring by moisture and neglect. Salts reproduce themselves on every flat surface, crawling back out of night air into the day with crystals no more solid than good will. Heat is a resident, a landlord even—generous and punitive. It attends every transaction, collects its rent in speeded reactions, in water pulled thin across big spans, in the refusal of greases to stay. The laws live here and have faces; you can watch gravity wearing its favorite coat, can read capillarity as an ethic.

Things that pretend not to be alive get their say anyway. The power lines hum so gently that you doubt them, but when a certain wet heat comes they take on a tone like a long vowel, and dragonflies rest on them and accept the meaning. The gates think in inches and stick with their decisions; you can feel the conclusion in your soles. A pump that hates August becomes a friend again in March and everyone forgives it. The place has a memory, not as sentiment but as protocol. The cycles write the animals into themselves; the animals read them back.

All this companionship adds up to a civics. The rules are not printed, and everyone obeys them. Move wide. Take the hours as doctrines. Assume salt will arrive in the lungs of the air and bite; build thick and round; when you fail, fail in a way that someone else can live on. Every form here is an answer to a question you can feel but not phrase. Every morning, when the light wakes badly and the surfaces gleam like eyelids without sleep, the residents resume. The herons put down their exact feet on the unbeautiful. The bats make ellipses between sodium halos. The rats run their chalked streets. The engineers walk their catechism and feed the plant tiny doses of care. The archaea do their slow, magnificent photos without calling it that. And the city lifts and settles by inches, alive in the way patient systems are alive, full of breath and seep and appetite, surrounded on every side by water that is always thinking about coming home.
