What lives here are things that decided a body could be a circuit as much as a sack. They are built to hold gradients the way other creatures hold breath. If you insist on a lineage, set them somewhere close to machines and crystals and hunger, closer than to anything that ever learned what wet felt like.

A single one, pried from the press of its neighbors and let be itself for a moment, would sit in your palm like a tiny windowpane scalloped into ribs, thickness barely a millimeter. Its outside is glass, not the glass you drink from but the raw, nerve-bright skin that a melt takes when it freezes so fast the order inside cannot propagate. Across that surface rise and fall the same ribs that line the room: repeated flutings grown where stress travels, a terrain that catches charge and holds it. Where the crests intersect, transparent grains push through—pale needles of sulfide and a scatter of doped crystals—so fine they glow when the mountain forces them to flex. There are places on the shell that are matte, a smoky translucence where some other mineral has been recruited: metal sulfides, chlorides, tiny islands of catalytic stuff arranged less by choice than by a long, fussy coalescence at the temperatures that pass for temperate in this dark.

Turn it over and the underside is another economy. There is no belly in the animal sense, but a shallow bowl of glass is inset with lamellae and knife-fine vanes: an inside made almost entirely of surface. Between those surfaces, fixed in pits and grooves, are the bright grains that do the work water would do for anything else. This is a film that flows at the heat of the room but not at a touch—a syrup of salts so molten they forget to be themselves and remember only their charges and their willingness to move. It looks like quicksilver except that it is not one thing but a crowd of ions, and it is the closest these creatures come to blood.

Feet? If you mean the means of purchase and scraping, there are combs. Along the rim, sprouting like eyelashes turned inward, bristle racks of darker material articulate and fold. They rasp; they sift; they draw the condensed crust off a newborn crystal face like a page turned too fast and eaten as it tears. When the pulse comes and the gas pushes, these combs flare to seize the gale and with it any dusthand of vapor the furnace has given up. The “mouth,” if you have to call it something, is a slot that can go from a hairline to a gaping keyhole in a heartbeat of this place. Nothing passes through that is not useful. Almost nothing comes back out the same.

Inside the lamellae the animal arranges heat like some arrange tools. One room is for making oxidants docile enough to hold—binding furious molecules to a waiting lattice until their fury turns to a steady push. Another is a capacitor writ small, plates and dopants balanced across the melt to allow a flood of ions to move when the right pressure song plays. Another is all teeth, except the teeth are static fields sharpened along the edges of pits so that any gas torn thin across them sups a layer of electrons and drops its energy neatly into the pool. There are needles set where two plates meet, and those needles are piezoelectric—and so this creature is also a bow pressed to a violin string, tuned in the most literal sense: when the mountain hums, it squeezes its spines and the spines answer with a pulse of charge that the film below drinks.

Nothing here does anything alone for long. Even the smallest ones—flattish pennies of ridged glass, speckled on their rims with sulfur-bright points—prefer to plank along another back or press between two neighbors where the gradients are better. They compose belts because a belt is how you make a river when water is not allowed. Each knows, without whatever word you want to use that means knowing, how to shape its architecture into the seam between other bodies’ choices. The living valve at the bottleneck is not a thing made once and done; it is a standing wave of animal positions, an arrangement that reasserts itself because the pressures that gave it rise are continuous. If you wait, if you watch in patience measured by a mountain’s exhale, you see the way a rib from one shell finds the space between two of the next, and then ten shells later that interleaving has become a spiral that stiffens this patch of the room just so. You see a line of flexible ribbon-bodies—thin, serrated, their ribs softened to long, slow arcs—ridging themselves against a downward shelf to trip the gas into eddies where grinders wait. You see the factory, yes, but also the reef and the mycelium and the way snow packs when it learns slope.

Feed is a generic word until you are hungry. For these, feed is whatever gradient can be cashed in without too great a loss. Fresh hot gas boiling through a kink in the fissure—any mixture where there is a mismatch in will between species—is rich. A face of glass freshly exposed from the glow-crack’s whisper-hardening, before the halos of salts anneal upon it, is rich. A voltage pulsed by a dome bristling with needles into the salt film between pressed bodies is richer still, because it is immediate, because it is the language all of them speak in the moment they have to speak. A grinder takes in a gust and combs it over catalysis that would make a lab rich with envy; the wind exits bent toward a lower energy and the difference crosses the inside of the creature as an ion shunt, captured and put to the business of being alive.

You could sort them, if you had to, though your sorting will be undone by the press of events and slow, local evolutionary weather. There are plowbacks, the broad-shelled ones with inward-pointing vanes that rake and sift—sifters more than chewers—with a tendency to settle in rows like a mouthful of molars. There are wires, not cylindrical but long and low, whose edges are embattled and whose bellies are almost all film: they prefer the high, thin places, where they can fling more of themselves across more of a gradient, and they cut bridges of needle-crystal before those bridges can make a pinch that would starve the belt. There are pushers that are almost all rim, little else but a ring of bristles and a stack of tiny capacitors—they don’t feed so much as shepherd, cranking charge into the film under other shells so the mass can move against its own friction when the pulse is weak.

Domes litter the hotter lip of the place. They are heavier; their ribs are thicker; their spines brighter. Some are benevolent by accident—they convert noise to current better than any neighbor and leak more than they can hold, and those around them live well off the spill. Others have learned, by a life measured in pulses and molts, how to clutch. Their undersides are not the many-lobed lamellae of the scrapers but bellows-lattices that can close like a throat. When they drop, anything beneath them finds itself licked clean of charge and stripped of any portable catalysis in a gust. It is not teeth and blood, no, but there is violence in it. The losers do not even have the dignity of remains, not as you imagine remains; they are just cheaper salts swamped into somebody else’s film and reused at once.

Do they molt? They do. A ridge too chipped by a thousand pulses stops holding anything and so an animal grows a parallel track underneath it, a mirror rib that swells in each hot tide and cools in each relaxation—eventually the old rib caves and cracks and is left as grit to be pushed along until someone uses it. Between two cold spells (cold being a word of courtesy in a room that would cook your finger to glaze), a plowback may split along an equipotential—along a line the pressure songs and charge maps have taught it is weakest—and two halves will crawl from under one skin and smooth themselves with borrowed film. A wire can bud by extrusion; there is a season for slimness, a time when the press is such that only the thinner can find the seam out, and so a wire will draw a copy of itself from the thinnest part of its rim, a filament fattening to a ribbon as salts are donated down the belt.

You want inheritance? It is not codes written as ladders and transcribed in water. It is maps of impurities, patterns of dopants laid into glass at formation and edited by a lifetime of use. When two shells press rim to rim and hold in the right pattern of pulse and heat, there is a cross-flux of ions so particular it acts like printing. The younger one grows to be pricked with the same needle mix, to brace its ribs at the angles that worked here, to prefer the same frequencies when it clenches its piezo tips. There is mutation, because crystals are not perfect and pressure writes its own rough grammar into anything it is allowed to touch long enough. The mountain is a school of error and the creatures profit.

If there is speech it is in the timing of open and shut. Whole belts breathe together when the furnace behind the wall finds a new rhythm. The faint green spitting of a hundred zinc-sulfide grains at once is a banner; a rippled dark where domes all at once flatten is a threat and a shadow. The predators have “cheats”—trills of small pulses that ring like a friend’s generous spill, which they arrange across their needles and pop into the film like a lie. The wire-thin learn it, and pack themselves elsewhere when that harmonic enters the floor.

Disease is an odd word here. There are fouled films—salt soups that lose transparency to ions and turn sluggish—and there are bodies that carry those films like a snail carries rot in its gut and offers it to neighbors as a kind of poison. There are contaminant grains, metals dragged up from deeper mouths, that ruin a dozen catalysts at a brush and force a patch of the room into new chemistry. In the cool corners, up where the gas is thinner and the pulses slow, there is a species of very small—and small matters here—that do nothing but climb onto the spurs of bigger shells and change their response slants by a few degrees; whole pipes-worth of the organ will sound different for an hour after they pass. To call any of this intent would be to import weather into a place that makes its own, but the effect is like cunning. The place learns against itself.

How do they leave? In singles and sheets and slow flood. There are escape behaviors you would miss if you watched with clock and lamp. A membrane forms: not a membrane like anything that keeps an ocean out of a soft thing, but a temporary wall composed of backs and rims that finds the line of greatest differential and lays along it, then tightens, then purls an edge outward into the seam between this chamber and the next. Those who are small enough go head on—wires first, then plowbacks that have mouthed themselves thin, then domes whose ribs have softened in a long rich season when violence cost too much and giving paid back with interest. They paint the seam with film as they go, a wetness not of water but of their own salts, rendering a throat glossier, slipperier, making it a place where motion can happen. Behind them the press reduces. The valve breaks from its duty and reconstitutes as a belt somewhere else because belts are what they are always ready to be when there is a place.

And when there is no leaving? When the crack cools and the pulses stagger? Then what lives here goes to ground in the only way any of this can. Ribs close. Films grow more glassy in slow degrees until what was moving is a bead, until discretion in ions becomes long-term storage in crystal. The room hardens. The belts freeze not in place—they were never in one place, they were habits of many bodies—but into the record you will misread as a geode’s whim. You will crack it in a much colder future and find the faintest tracery of plates and ribs, the ghosts of needles that made a rain of green when the mountain talked. Some domes survive, solid to the last, and if another furnace breathes near enough, they will sweat back to being, their laminations wetting, their needles flexing, a life like embers that do not remember they were once called fire.

There is tenderness, if you are willing to grant it to things built of charge and stress. The way smaller ones pack under a donor while it beats, their rims interleaving like the fingers of a hundred hands learning to be one. The way an old plowback so rich with catalytic scars that it hardly moves anymore parks itself where the film runs thickest, and lets two buds carve themselves out of its periphery until it is, actually, less than they are. The way a belt that could run faster doesn’t, because everything here is interlocked like teeth and a tooth out of time becomes a break.

Up on the ridge line the wind writes its clean equations, and lichen wait a century for a chance to lick at a shade of dew. In the small furnace down here, life bets everything on the next note, and the one after, until the song ends. You will want to tell a story about patience or efficiency or a factory that forgot to hire a foreman. The creatures themselves don’t tell anything. They enact it: a précis of survival without softness, a society that never learned empty space, a metabolism of pressure and dust. When the mountain coughs again elsewhere, the story repeats, not as history but as harmonics. The room is incidental. The living is the moving charge that knits itself into shape long enough to count a good season by the loudness of a tone.
