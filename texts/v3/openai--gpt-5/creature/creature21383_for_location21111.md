Those who live here are built for the size of a drop.

They are not a people so much as a patience stationed in many bodies. You can count some of them if you like—the bats in their high seam, the mud-cup row of swiftlet homes, the gesture of a gecko—but you will never total them. The place keeps them in fractions and in habits. If there is a lord of this chamber, it is a verb: to sift. If there is a hymn, it is the dim, regular soundlessness with which things take and yield.

The plants are the first citizens you see because they court the blade of morning. They live on stretched belief. Their leaves are all reach and pallor; the ferns wheel their hands to catch the narrow day as it sweeps. Those hands are big as dinner plates yet fragile as paper damp from tea; veins glow like river maps under skin. Aroids present their heart-shapes to whatever angle the light permits, moving an arm’s width between breakfast and noon. They are all edges and angles designed to hold the light longer than the sun’s geometry wants to. They don’t simply grow; they angle, they choreograph. Their roots refuse the blunt honesty of trunks; instead they braid themselves into draperies and ladders, they stitch into calcite, they learn the shapes of old drips and make those shapes new.

Mosses and liverworts do not bother with the drama of aiming. They keep to margins: the rims of the little dams where a bead can park its body and disperse its thought; the knuckles and brows of flowstone where mist comes to rest. They are fur worn by rock, and rock’s fur grows back within a day after your passing fingers flatten it. Among them, algae live at a scale that would fit inside the breath you just took. A fine film glosses stone that looks blank until the small suns caught in root-drops warm it by a fraction of a fraction of a degree. That is enough. The film darkens, divides, calls gnats. Someone eats the gnats. Whole economies bud off from a hair of water on a thread of root.

Where green retires, other tongues speak. You can see a fungus thinking in shelves as it eats the rot in a great dangling curtain of root—its edge faintly sugared with light, the sweetness borrowed from bacteria that trade mineral for glow. On the shaded wall, a bloom that is neither plant nor animal nor alone writes a dust of luminescence into the air just above the stone. It is a communal blur, a quiet assembly glinting at the edge of eyesight. If you let your eyes soften, you might feel watched. If you focus, it is gone. They prefer to exist at the corner of your notice; that is the bargain they make with the darkness.

Roots, though: roots live here the way roads live in a town that is proud of its alleys. They don’t only pass through. Some have grown columnar, a braided muscle of plant-thread that learned the gravity of the chamber the way an arm learns work. They feed at the lip of a terrace where minerals curl into ridges. If you follow one down, you find its toes splayed into the wet, each toe a school of seconds—fine hairs, each hair a handshake with something that can dissolve something else and make it digestible. Others play chandeliers, falling in fringes so thin they become dew-catchers, bead-stringers, tiny lens-makers. Under those strings, the moss is not the same moss as elsewhere. It has been warmed by dots. It knows afternoons differently than its cousin two handspans away. For many here, a root is not a pipe from a tree elsewhere; it is the tree. It is the ceiling’s handwriting, learned from water.

The animals fit the quiet the way tongues fit teeth. Bats have the lease on the hush. Fruit bats smell like daylight in a market if you breathe close: sweet and a little rank, a metadata of pulp. They live pressed into a seam where the air does not vibrate unless something big changes. They hang not because of gravity but because of grammar: the right word in the right slot makes the sentence. You would say they sleep, but the word is a soft lie. Their ears never quite turn off; their small rubbers of wings move occasionally like someone smoothing a bed you’re not done with. They count not minutes but drops.

Swiftlets spell the brightward wall with their cups. They own the right to chatter when they come and go, and even then their voice is thin as a stitch. Their young learn to open not only for food but for light itself, a reflex older than nest. In this room, mouths are often also leaves; if something opens, it is asking to be lit. Clay seeps out of a seam as if the stone had saved a little of its wet for this purpose, and their tongues are workmen’s tools. They build not nests so much as punctuation. When fish cannot exist here, and large predators would be both rude and wrong, the swiftlet’s arrival is a kind of holiday—noise briefly sanctified.

Far more common than birds are the things that don’t know voices. Salamanders practice the religion of skin. They come out rimward when water is event, laying themselves along the lip where a trickle decides its own shapes, tasting through their sides the difference between yes and not yet. Their movement is small and exact. If you force them into verbs you know, you will invent chase and pounce; what they perform is more like to appear. Tongues unfurl and return with arithmetic speed. Their lives are a sequence of almosts completed by patience.

Geckos are the chapel’s ushers at the border of light. They have made a habit out of edges: the line where the blade of gold cuts into the green dusk, the rail of a rimstone, the seam between glossy and matte. Their skins are the color of opinion until they move, and then they are punctuation—a comma flicking forward, correcting a sentence of gnats. They drink heat with their bellies and convert it into timing. If their world had a sound track, it would be the chitter of their toes; but it doesn’t, so they make do with the loudness of being quick.

Cave crickets keep to the undersides of what you could bump your head on. They are gloss and angle, an alphabet of legs that knows how to wait inside punctuation. When they move it is not a hop but a negotiated drift. They read the chamber with their knees. Close to them without being them, spiders have traded in flamboyance for certainty. They have given up color, fashion, signage, and turned their webs into sieves laid where mistake is most likely: the seam where a creature used to one rule steps into another without remembering to change. Noise is punished here, so their catch is almost always hubris—one rattled wing, one clumsy landing, one celebratory shake cut short.

Below the polite layer of sight, there is a register of animals that are more idea than appearance. Millipedes with the sheen of milk make a living off history: what fell, was chewed, was half a thought of fungus. Centipedes, text laid in lines along the pinchback tunnels, are sentences that prioritize the part of grammar that bites. Springtails live in the fluffy place between facts and moss, each jump an exclamation mark too small to hear. Blind snakes visit like rumors: a curve of absence turned into muscle, a tongue that reads tremor and edits the cricket census accordingly. A whip scorpion measures the day not with light but with the permission that warmth gives motion. Its feelers are essays, and their conclusion is often a return to where rock still breathes.

Everything here breeds in the tense of hazard and appointment. There are shrimps that appear as if imagined by a storm and then vanish with the storm’s memory, leaving their stasis tucked into film and fissure like folded letters. There are frogs who take advantage of hours when the funnels write water stairs and teach their song to this place just long enough that the stones can remember it as smell the next day. There are bookslice families the size of hangnails with entire feuds you will never witness resolving under a leaf you didn’t know could be shade.

And there are the makers that most try to be stone. Carbonate grows crowns on the pools; the edges of each low lip are studded with teeth of drip and time. Runnels speak with grammar learned from centuries of water signing the same conjunctive again: if drop, then flow; if flow, then shine. Rimstone repeats itself in patient terraces and steps the floor up and down in shallow arguments. The memory of stomps caught once upon a wetness is now a proper district: one basin crammed with a cushion of many mosses squashed into one soft word; another bristled with fungal pins like a tiny cavalry at bayonet practice that reveals itself only when a bat passes between its dusk and yours. Stone here has a civic pride; it improves itself by adding less.

Dig long enough into who lives here and you will touch a thing that is not plant nor animal nor mineral but something like rule. Quiet is not simply the absence of sound; it is predation’s handrail. It is as oxygen is to you: always there, always used, fatal to remove. The residents have trained themselves around it until quiet becomes architecture. Webs become sieves because chase would be noise. Geckos move like commas because argument would be loud. Salamanders drink air through skin because splashing would announce. Even the bats’ leaving is not a thunder of leather, but the press and release of silk, the arithmetic of unrolling.

Heat is another citizen, thick as a neighbor and as opinionated. It makes midday into a hush for those with choices. It writes the list of who may hunt and how fast. It keeps the stone kind to the palm and the moss awake. It convinces the chamber to exhale under its skylights and turns stray plant seeds into aeronauts. It spoils the ambition of those who are not good at stillness. The cave breathes around the math of warmth and cool so slowly that seasons could use it as metronome. Live here without an ear for that breath and you do not live long.

Light is no friend—sudden and sharp, brief and partial—but you take your friends as they come when you live in a throat of stone with two skylights for eyes. The residents court it the way you might court a stranger with a coin and a trick: carefully, with attention to exit. Seedlings that arrive on purpose by accident compose themselves for the hour that belongs to the west shaft, then dimly manage the rest. Insects suspend themselves in strings where the beam travels, always wary of drift. The gecko is there to make certain that string is not a promise but a suggestion. Those who misjudge die without complaint and feed the white things that keep no books.

Water refuses to be anyone’s servant even as it feeds all. It is occasion, and because it is occasion, it is calendar. On some days—and not on most—it descends with an opinion and an intention. It rings the basins, hyphens the stone, slicks the old roads of calcite. The room wakes to it as if an aunt had visited with sweets. Plans change. Eggs find their water. Tongues discover directions. Little lives that were content to live written on dust go legible and then liquid and then memory.

Who lives here, then?

A colony of bats and the arithmetic of their leaving live here. A row of swiftlets and the patience of their mud live here. The vines and ferns and liverworts live here, yes, but also the angle of their leaning and the hour-by-hour catechism of their tiny movements toward blade and gloss. Salamanders that have taught their bodies the meaning of a bruise of damp live here. Geckos that think in borders live here, and spiders that set their questions where mistake is common, and crickets that are paragraphs attached to stone by their feet. Millipedes shine without seeing. Blind snakes rumor without speaking. Shrimp that are a gust live here and frogs that are a choir that the room cannot keep but does not forget. There are also inhabitors that you would not mark as alive—the bead hung on a root that focuses the day into a pin, the legible drift that rises under the skylight in late afternoon and carries a silk and its maker one seam away. Rules live here long enough to gain manners.

If you insist on a single inhabitant—as if a place such as this could be headed by a monarch—then say that the one who lives here is the Convening. It wears bodies of limestone and moss and web and wing, but it is not any one of them. It is the room’s ability to make a city out of two narrow swords of light and a schedule of drips; to teach roots to be chandeliers and bats to be silence; to reward those who can live on the edge of light with a day and those who live in its refusal with a century. It eats noise. It sifts hours. It keeps time in rims and weeps, in small seeds rising on small winds, in the way heat holds a thought even after its reason has gone. It does not permit applause.

They, it, this—what lives here—are old the way good habits are old. They are small the way rules are small. They are everywhere your eyes rest once your eyes have learned to rest. They are not waiting for you and do not mind you, provided you do not clap. They have made a life out of the inside of weather, the underside of leaves, the part of water that can be both air and not. They are a kindness with teeth and a hospitality with edits. And if you sit long enough to belong, they will include your breath in the account they keep of what shapes them, and then, as they always do, they will continue to make themselves, drop by careful drop.
