What lives there is drawn thin and quick by space and patient by time. It is not one thing. It is people who have learned to be the white of a moth’s wing without being fragile, and it is the many small creatures that graze and ripple under their hands, and it is the work of light itself, which the citizens treat as if it were another animal to be herded and watered.

The people do not call themselves anything when they can help it. Names have edges that carry in the domes. When you must refer to a household from a distance you leave a sliver of light to list on their doorframe in the wrist-shape of their sign, or you tap their click-name on the floorboards and pass it along. Outsiders call them sieveborn when they bother to call them anything at all. They accept it with the small shrug a stone gives to a drop.

Their bodies answer to their place. Skin tends toward pearl, the color set more by mineral diet than pigment. If there is hair it is the pale that dust has, or the amber of old calcite; it is grown longer than you might think to read wind with, and tied up high when a pour is coming so the chimneys do not catch it. Eyes are used to the middle distance and the half-light, lids a shade heavier and slower to lift than yours, pupils capable of swelling to a wide, unjudging dark that drinks the glow without complaint. Children are taught to look at light only aslant; there are copper-gauze veils that break the worst of a new beam into something the eye can drink without shaking. Their ears are not larger, but they are truer. They know which kinds of sound get away with living under the stone and which kind bring the ceiling down; they have learned to let their jaws go soft and open when air changes, to measure draft with the wet inside of the mouth. Faces do not carry loudness. The muscles most exaggerated are in the wrists and the shoulders and the sole of the foot. There is a vocabulary in the way a forearm presents its inner face to a light, a verb in how a heel pivots without lifting.

They speak, of course, but not often and not for far. A voice here is a fingertip along the larynx, a close murmur stitched to the throat of another who leans to catch it; a song is what the light does on a wall when ten households tilt ten mirrors together and then un-tilt them. Arguments are conducted in doubled tempo handwork, clamping and unclamping louvers. There is a language of glare that is precise, and almost all of it lives in tendons. From a long way off you would think them a people of constant prayer, wrists lifted again and again to the light; close to, you see the quick grammar. The child with the sliver on the wall is not playing at being a lighthouse; she is composing the words for “I need that sledge after you” and “watch for a pour in thirty breaths.”

They eat what the cave permits. They farm fungus more the way you would practice a good habit than the way you would tend a field: consistent attention, the right damp, soft praise. There is a right place for everything; if a rack wanders too far into light the whole bed sulks and you have to pet it alive again for days. They lay out pans under the walls where damp remembers to settle and gather what blooms. Some of those salt efflorescences are so minor you can write with them only on a tongue; others flop off the wall in soft plates that a child can carry in both hands—medicine, preservative, temper for metal and bone. They keep crabs not in pens but in arguments: a ring of slick stone the animal could cross if it cared to, and a stringing of bright damp to convince it it does not. Fish come and go in shallow troughs with high lids and thin stones for lids; you are not meant to see the fish until you are already at the rack with the ladle, eyes down, pouring evenly the water they will be promised again at the same hour tomorrow. Eels, like thoughts, are left to find you of their own accord. If one pushes its head into your palm you are allowed to take it.

The animals have long, exact lineages. White isopods with bellies that whisper busy as a loom along the stone are not pests; they are gleaners, and there are rules about when and how much to harvest from their rakes of bacteria. Springtails, a drift of insistence over any wet shine, have a season that nobody will name because everyone knows it will come when it comes. There are moths without color and crabs with too much social aspiration and salamanders that write themselves into the cave’s margins and out again; all of them have place-names that are not said aloud, only shown in light on wall-corners. The bats are neighbors who owe you nothing and gift you guano and some days a kind of visible wind. The blind pigeons are a story animal; they are the day after a cold rain and the tale every child is given of being fooled and then saved by an ancestor’s rule. People crack eggs when they can, yes, but more often they just stand and watch the dust on those wings become briefly a storm in one bright place and then recombine itself into calm.

Tools here look the way a careful hand feels. Stone and bone kiss to do what steel would do elsewhere: peck, pry, nibble, score. Copper is a note you hear in light more than weight in the hand. Their inventiveness goes to anything that moves without asking the walls for permission—sledges with bellies of greased felt, ladders that walk, scaffolds with the patience of card houses and the memory of bridges. They have a way of making joints that would make any joiner weep: tooth into groove, nothing sings or clacks, everything holds like prayer without shouting itself into the world. Rope is a blessing and a kin. It is spun from hyphae and gut and the will of a woman who has done this all her life; the cores are cores of the same. The ropes have names and their names are recorded not twice: in a bone tally and with a tasteful stripe of soot where it handles better. You do not coil rope carelessly; here it is a good friend and an ancestor, and sometimes the only way between an hour and the next.

What they build is distributed the way light is. Nobody sits in a house at the center and says where to go, not because they do not like centers but because there are none that last. A quarter is the faces of its mirrors and whoever serves them that month. There are guilds, but guild is too loud. There are clusters of practice with elders and apprentices: shield-tenders whose wrists hold the grammar of glare, cistern parents who never taste first, click-braiders who shelter by the tap-ways and send messages shin to shin, seam-listeners who sit with their heads turned just so and their soft mouths open to test when a blue whisper will go through the rock. There are the ladder-walkers with the quiet feet. There are the wind tasters who know, by a one-hair flag or by the curl of their own loose hair, when the upper air has changed. There are the counter-sayers, the ones who repeat all through the ordinary days: Do not count on the ceiling.

Law is a series of reminders about what remembers and what does not. Stone remembers in a way that people can learn from. A child can spend a whole morning rubbing a palm along the fossil of an old ripple until the ripple is as much in the hand as in the wall; later, if a walkway fails under her foot and the beam goes elsewhere, the habit in that hand will keep her upright. Crime exists. It has to. There are those who take light that does not belong to them by tilting a mirror too much, by claiming a right of beam-splash they haven’t earned. There are exchanges and debts everywhere; the city’s currency is not shells or salt—in truth salt is higher than any coin—but favorings of time and glow. If you hold a shield that pinches someone else’s garden of hyphae out of food for three days, the guild of fungus-keepers and the elders of the quarter and the click-braiders who heard all the tapping you tried not to make under that act will come find you and take you by the wrists and show you on the wall how that looked to everyone. There are fines measured in the time you must spend with a seam-listener without speaking. The worst penalty is that you are sent to sleep in the bleaker quadrants for a month, where there are no useful oculi, and have to keep a little light alive the careful way the uneager do not.

Love happens, of course. Quietly at first, and then it can move furniture. Lovers pick names for themselves in the glare-code that only two wrists know. Weddings are not events; they are accords revised. When a partnership can hold a span together without singing to the stone, when a pair of bodies can take a street apart and put it up a wall in an afternoon because the weather has become rude, everyone knows, and someone drops a rope with a ribbon of fungus-skin braided through and the knot of it is a kind of official. Children are harnessed all the time, not because they are less trusted but because the stone is to be believed, and small bodies are quick to learn where not to test it. Games are all wind and water and light: chasing the edge of a beam with shell-strings between their fingers, counting drips aloud until a parent looks up with a glare-hand and says the number you should learn to mean danger.

They remember things that do not make a portrait on the wall. There is the grandmother who went up and touched weather and came down with hair of old quartz. Her story is the one put into the ears of those too young to know why voices are dangerous: that above is loud in ways that make you feel heroic but simply mean you have not learned to dread certain notes yet. There is the day someone followed a seam of light through a crackwide enough for a sledge instead of a human and found a chamber where the light had pooled up and curdled in the crystals, and they brought it back in a bag and it soft-shone for two days under the table and everyone slept on the floor in its calm. There is the day the ceiling failed and a rim fell like a clipped nail and went to needles and it killed no one because there are click-lines now, because once there were not and that made enough families empty to teach an entire city how to listen.

They bury their dead into the time of the cave rather than into its ground. A body can be given to dry, if the family needs to keep the shape of that person’s face as a steadying for themselves. There are rooms for that, with veils and low stools, and it is not morbid; it is stern. More often bones are taken to a wet place chosen for its generosity and left long enough that salt begins its white hymn on them, and the softened crystal ate away from them in a manner you might almost call affectionate, and then the bones are milled and mixed with a certain paste and polished into slivers. Those slivers go into the walls of houses and the faces of shields. In the wrist-light you can sometimes see, in the pale-slow heart of a people’s code, the signature of an elder’s bone, a steadiness under the flicker that means your own mind will quiet if you let it.

They decorate with behavior more than with things. There are scarves of copper-gauze that catch dew and bead it to the wearer’s mouth as a walk goes on; there are harnesses that are beautiful because they make the inner elbow say “I know what I am doing.” There is a practice of arranging one’s hair with tiny mica chips for a festival that is not a festival at all but the day after a big pour, when all things are clean and awake and too bright. There is a beloved vanity among the young to stand under a mistfall and let the mineral wash paint their shoulders with a lace that will last two weeks if they do not scrub hard; this too has its elders who sigh and its tide that prevails because everything that can be woven through the body without cracking the stone is allowed here.

Their faith is neither hard nor soft. It is stone-shaped. They have no gods that you could carve; they would not set anything upright on a floor and say worship this to it. They have sayings that are warnings that were also once hopes—Do not count on the ceiling is every prayer they know. Light is loaned. Water is a guest that leaves when it wants. These truths could hollow you; they let them hold them up instead. They believe in attention. They believe in the way a hand can learn to know a knob of fossil and then later in the dark, when lanterns are scarce and the crystal is only thinking about giving back the day, the hand goes where it goes and the body is saved by that.

There are seasons even without green. Season is the shape of mist in the mouth, the habit of a particular oculus to gutter or flame, the way the blind pigeons always think they remember something and cannot and loop once more. They make a year out of that and out of the days when the seam in the dome gives and gives little electric whispers; they sit with the children then and let hair lift under the soft lightning and tell the story of the crystal that changed its mind, and a child’s fear gnaws and then sleeps.

They do not stay always to the bright. Nobody can. The bleaker quadrants exist—faces of the cave that have seen nothing and do not care to start now—and people go there because someone always must. There are mines where the wall sweats a medicine you need, there are experiments left long by the first drips; there are eggs of something nobody has seen open in memory. Those who tend those places are kept soft in voice and hard in patience. They sleep with one palm to the stone. They come back with noses full of old dust and mouths that want dew and do not ask for it with sound; they set their hands in the glare and tell you what the darkness has to teach that day.

They are not alone there even when you count only the people. There are always visitors from elsewhere who arrive in ways that are as much accident as will—a miner who found a tunnel that thought it led up and did not, a boy lost in a storm whose world turned to silver water and who woke on a floor of fossil ripples with a rope around his wrist. If you arrive like that they put you to work and teach you slowly the gravity of your lips. The city eats no one who does not insist, but it is not sentimental either. It will lend you a mirror and then take it back and give it to your neighbor without apology if it keeps the light handled better that day.

And in a sense the stone lives here. Not in the embarrassed way of a human, but in its firm, useful continuity. The ceiling is a sky that cannot be loved back and so must be treated. The walls carry their old river like a book everyone still reads. The floor wears every spill and every care. There are crystals that learn you; they carry away the day and let you have it again in a small, low way at night. They are not magic. They are a timetable and a kindness.

You would expect silence to be a muzzle. It is not. It is a braid of other things. The quickness of wrists across a span. The white bustle of isopods over a film of food. The tilt of ten shields like the petal of a pale machine. The gloss of a crab’s humiliation a floor below. The river of bats at the blue edge and the way their edges become mathematics without making a note another could count. The city moves constantly, edits itself, tests its own joints, lets things go and brings them back. Everything living in it, people and moths and the light itself, grows used to being a different shape in an hour.

And always there is the rule shaped like an animal in the house: do not count on the ceiling. The children start by saying it with their lips and hands together; by the time they are old they can think it with the soles of their feet placed on stone and then lift their heels and step into a new century as if it were only another beam crossing the café. They live light-handed under something that does not promise, and this makes them strong in the kind of way that is almost quiet enough to miss. If you stay there long enough, your own wrists will begin to learn a grammar, and when you take your leave you will have to stop yourself on the surface from raising your hand to tilt a stray windowpane toward someone else’s morning.
