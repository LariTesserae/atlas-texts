What lives here are bodies that have learned to hold onto a falling thing and eat the difference between one kind of push and another. They are not few. The place is as crowded as a mouth.

Begin with the velvet that passes for groundlife. The first mouths are flat and felted and never more than a breath thick. They call iron awake and reap the electrons that iron lets go, lay down orange skins and black hairs as slowly as frost draws lace on a window. They smell like wire gnawed with teeth. In cross section they are maps—a deck of gradients, each layer living on the slant of a neighboring one. Filamentous bacteria lay cable in the cracks, white hairlines that run for hand-lengths, carrying charge like rumor from a rich patch of waking stone to a poor one. Silica-sowers squat wherever the water rests longest and trickle mineral snow that makes the sharp edges gentler by an amount you’d swear you couldn’t see and then can when you look along it. Manganese-burrowers stack black sponge under overhangs and bristle their oxidized whiskers into the flow to trap trace metals as if combing a river’s hair. These are the city’s first factory floors and pantry shelves, and nothing above them has much to do unless those thin colors appear.

The impellers hold the windward sides. Each is a donut with a spine, a torus cut into movable sectors that can shingle open or pull shut, an iris widened to a wheel. The beef of their bodies is not meat, not like you’d know it: ceramic-protein composite set in slow-growing laminae, living porcelain with quartz dust in the mix. They are quarries that move. When the river’s edge strikes their rims, strain rises through piezo-bones and the charge bleeds along channels to internal stacks where their endosymbionts sit—eaters of bare electrons, of oxygen, of dissolved carbonate. The impellers breathe by shearing. They wear their lightning in little spalls along the edge fur of cilia: a blue comb that sparks and goes dead and sparks again on a rhythm that makes sense to their inside bacteria, not to you.

If you cut one through you’d see a cathedral of baffles and chambers that hum. Water is pulled, slowed, made laminar, made turbulent, offered to the microbe sheaths that fix carbon into sugars the way frost fixes breath to glass. The impellers are cold to the touch, and then if you held on and knew how to feel, you would feel warmth pulsing in the spiral stairs that wind down their spines. This warmth doesn’t come from light; it comes from friction domesticated into metabolism and from electrons taking a one-way path down a chemical slope. They shed skinned stones, little plates of silica-bone, that sink and make a scatter of teeth where others can grip.

They make more of themselves by making skins. A matured impeller will, when a banked surplus builds, slow, withdraw its sectors, unfurl from its underside a hoop of nacreous braid the color of hail. It is a crib. Into this hoop it extrudes a thin axle with cilia like wheat-beards. The hoop adheres to whatever basalt can be negotiated; the axle finds the flow and quivers. The microbes go first, slicking the brace with a new city. The little thing grows by law: if the sheet sings a high tone, the sectors thicken; if the pressure jumps, the piezo plates knit denser. The newborn’s first skill is not eating but tuning, and the parents nearby flash their rims in slow codes that amount to a map of tolerable beats.

Between their petaled faces, the belt-worms shoulder through. They are knots made of themselves. A belt-worm is a loop of loops and everything it has repeats around and around: tooth-plates like millstones, tongues like escalators, paws that are not paws but spring-loaded hooks that grip a crack edge and then let go on a timing only they trust. They feed by taking the city off the stone and running it through their insides until it is all but immaculate in the mouthprint and hardly diminished in total. When a belt-worm passes, it leaves glass polished to a starved sheen that flashes, then mists over again by evening with new gray film—a bookkeeping entry: cost, receipt.

They are musical in the way a careful machine is musical. Each click is a tooth falling into a notch, a pawl engaging, a small success kept. There are long, silent tumults when two belt-worms meet at an angle and both will not cede. They begin to sabotage: quick tongues flick flints into each other’s conveyors; a hook climbs not rock but rival and wedges on a tendon; a back-spring is stalled mid-coil with a pinch of grit that will not wash away until someone loses patience and rips the whole segment free. Pieces lost this way are not wasted—scale moths will come and comb the gummed grease that oozes, bubble ticks will gather the nectar of hemolymph, wire eels will smell the iron and come to shuttle a bead of blood toward oxygen. A belt-worm that cannot keep its timings dies quickly and cleanly; it is taken apart by teeth that have no interest in killing, only in removing the obstructions to their own work.

On the backs of the calm eddies, the drum shells do not hunt so much as accept. They plant, make weight, and learn the room by ear. Their plates are membranes that can quicken to a thousand tiny hammers or slow to a heavy breathing, and each one chooses a range it can hold without tearing. A good shell is a choir of its own making: every lamina of nacre is laid down under a slightly different beat, so the finished jar contains a vertical stack of tunings that can be chosen in a moment by flooding this seam, clamping that. When they court—and they do, in their fashion—they broaden. They unfix every limit, let more notes through and overfeed for hours while the plates blur into a fricative roar. Everything around tastes it as a fatness in the water. The young of the drum shells are chips, droplets of mother-laminate seeded with microbes. They settle by finding a piece of noise they can stand and refusing every other.

Hovering diagonally in the plunge lanes, the hinge-mantas work the high, cold energy. Their bodies are anvils split down the middle and taught to flex. Perforations along their wings open and close in ranks, and what looks like a flapping is in truth a metered loosening and tightening of vortices trapped in the wing’s meat like marbles in nets. Flow stutters, eddies are compressed and squeezed downstream across millipore beds, and food—protein scum, gas-riding cells, motes—smears itself slow enough to be stroked off. Their undersides are libraries of suckers and fingers that sense not pressure as we do but the changes in the changes. Young cling to these libraries and do nothing for months but learn the pattern of the music that keeps adults in place. They learn which hinge to slack when a neighbor’s resonance changes, how to square their frame to a filament’s shadow, how to move as if never there when a grinder-crab bumps the rock.

Grinder-crabs are broad and low and run warm as far as anybody here ever runs warm. They eat stones like men used to eat bread. Their stomachs are mills with at least three stones each: one to crack, one to rub, one to polish. Working rock against rock makes heat and the crabs leak it in threads that attract small lives in winter, such winter as there is when the uplands frost and the cataract takes a slightly different pitch and the sound of the room goes an eighth of a note grimmer. They build nothing and leave behind only barrels of finer sand and a faint, rich smell of flint.

Wire eels are threads made of threads, sentient braids of magnetite whiskers and muscle sheaths that can take a twist and keep it. They swim by changing their own stiffness and letting the field take them, and they sense by tasting current. Their hunger is for the places where an electron can be tempted off a stone and into a mouth. They do not graze; they bridge. Many of the bigger bodies wear them like jewelry at their seams, alive gaskets that seal in and leak out on schedule. A well-kept wire eel garden—a row along a column rib—is a status here, if status can be said to obtain among bodies that own nothing but leverage. They steal from each other endlessly, a quiet tugging day and night, argument by the length of a curl. When they breed it is a braid parted, each strand twisting free and going slack to be caught by a bracket of iron whiskers that knows the taste of its family’s alloy and will keep it long enough for the loose ends to stiffen by themselves.

Shoulder-shrimp are wedge-shaped because the world is. Hexagonal basalt makes hexagonal gaps; the shrimp have six different grips and they know which to put forward when the crack turns. Their claws are not symmetrical but complementary; they fit like male and female bolts, and their muscles are not arranged in antagonistic pairs but in ratchets that throw forward and lock. They cannot be pried out by anything short of rockfall. They tend nursery chambers that are simply quiet. A shoulder-shrimp will steal quiet from its neighbors for its children by placing not stones but angled plates of muscle that behave, to water, like stones. Its art is shadowing, making a hole inside a hole that the cataract cannot see.

Small things boil through the larger like seasoning. Valve spindles look like long-seamed beans, open and close along their length, and walk by squeezing water in jets at a bias. They sit in the soft eddies that collect under every overhang and harvest dust by drawing it into small multistage filters that never clog so much as shift: when a layer fouls, it becomes the next layer’s substrate. Bubble ticks shepherd bubbles the way old coast people once shepherded rafts, pinning a string of spheres under shelves by fanning them with plumes that keep the thin gas skins cool. When someone’s blue-white spark burns one bubble through, the tick flicks the ruptured sphere out like a housewife casting a spoiled grape and recaptures what can be recaptured. Scale moths do not swim or fly. They ride the skin of flows inside the water, the laminated slicks that roof off eddies into little rooms, holding themselves in that one frictionless place with fans that all by themselves would constitute a small field of blades.

Predators—you keep the word because there must be one—are not shapely and swift. There is no space for speed, only for precision. There are grit-sowers that wear a belt-worm’s pace like a cloak and shadow the jaw until it opens to spit and then hurl a single shaped pebble into the soft of a tongue so the next plate comes down on a burr and shears its own hinge. There are resonance leeches that stick to drum shells not for blood but for tuning; their bodies are curls of gelatin with iron grit in them, and they settle to lengths that lower a lid’s favored frequency by half a semitone, starving the beast of its work until a neighbor flickers its cilia in a decisive blue bar and the leech shudders free to escape being crisped. There are perforation plug-bearers—mats of algae-like library cards stacked in a playing deck—that drift to hinge-mantas and find by taste the holes at work that day and slide home, making one wing grab too much water, making the whole animal pitch and break its stance; the mantas counter by rolling and slamming the wing into a rib, popping the plugs out with a shock that rings the column dull. There are thieves you would not see if someone did not point—fine threads that creep along a wire eel’s braid and set up housekeeping between magnetite whiskers, living wires growing on wires, tapping the line like distant kin listening to a party wall.

Because nothing is built and everything behaves like a tool, you are tempted to name crafts. There are cleaners, and they have clients they will not abandon unless the current skives them away. There are fitters, lives that keep sockets smooth by living in them and rubbing tails and tongues around until the edges stop biting. There are versions of a midwife everywhere—bodies whose own reproduction is tied to someone else’s timing. When an impeller slows to throw its crib-hoop, shoulder-shrimp swarm its foot and hold it in a precise counterthrust, for which service they are allowed to strip the old saddle of parasites. Belt-worms about to tear a segment will do it only when a cloud of scale moth larvae sits ready to catch the fat that will loose from the wound; the larvae then spin iron whiskers into their bellies as if knitting hair. Hive is the wrong word; guild, perhaps. But there are no oaths and no heritages beyond what the parts make possible.

There is sex. There is always sex when bodies are more than chemistry. It is rarely an act you could watch and point to. It is patterned stickiness, kin finishes, familiar alloy: the way a wire eel favors a whisker that was grown on the back of a grinder-crab that fed in a certain rockfall ten years ago and carries that stone’s grain in its magnetite; the way drum shells built from nacre that grew under a particular pitch like to couple because the harmonics line up and the plates slip with less loss. The only obvious joining is among the mantas: a slow trembling, a stacking like pages, one under another, until there is a roll thick as a mattress and the whole roll flexes once and then shakes out flat and four new bodies lift, the size of spoons, from the crease.

They are not blind. They are not deaf. They see in pressure and hear in light, in the way that a dimming from cream to lead at the fall’s ceiling is a noise to them, a change in the tumble that announces an hour that matters. Their language, if you must, is the beat. Hinge-mantas stammer on purpose to warn that a rock is about to flake. Impellers share their inside weather by lengthening or shortening the arc-spurts of blue-white along their rims, tones of plenty or of a membrane gone sour. Belt-worms scold by skipping a click at intervals of thirteen, a superstition with a reason: it mimics a broken pawl and draws raiders looking for weakness while other kin move past. To listen here is to be held down and flooded, and still there are patterns you can nearly hum.

Infancy, in general, is a time of drifting as small function. A larval impeller is a piezo hair and a wish. A shoulder-shrimp young is a palate that looks for a fit and binds anything it can reach until its body grows into the angles it has chosen. A drum shell novice is a lid without a jar, dragged by its own quiver into quieter corners until a rim grows under it like a collar. Death is most often a syllable going out of a word—the tired pause when a wing comes up to catch and doesn’t, the skittering rush of everything around being faster because one body’s friction is removed.

Leave and you would call them sessile, dull, repetitive machines. Stay and you see the room work and the workers invent their work. A column’s face will split, a cold slow pop along a joint. A whole grammar of flow moves sideways with it. Over days, everyone that remains will retune. Wire eels will abandon a song they taught their daughters for five generations and find a quieter measure on the next rib. An old impeller will fold for hours, then open with its sectors thicker along one third of its ring, a new candence. A mantle of lichening microbe will appear in a pocket nobody visited last year and belt-worms will line up like clerks for a ration and take turns with an etiquette that looks like ritual and is just arithmetic under pressure.

There are seasons you would not guess from the light. Two or three times a life, a chocolate milk of snowmelt or ash-smooth flood will roar across the bowl, and the notes in the roar will drift and rumble and the drum shells will climb a quarter-inch up their own nacre to a more stable shoulder and wait the color out. Once and again, a lean comes, the cataract’s lip eating back so that the sheet thickens and the beat that runs the place goes down into a moist baritone for months. Births pour then: edges stressed harder will spark more; impellers will throw more hoops. Grinder-crabs will break more glass than they can grind, and their underbellies will be busy with lives too small to name.

On some intervals longer than a lifetime, the bowl fails. A rib snaps, a column slumps, the sheet draws a new line where air becomes water and what was a workshop is a hole. You would think it ends. It does not end. Almost everyone here has a seed, a hair, a plate, a braid that knows how to ride. Old bubble ticks can hold a bubble long enough to get to the next room; shoulder-shrimp are born with one memory only, and it is of a fit they have never felt that tells them where to wedge; wire eels have cousins further up the gorge, distant aunts that grew under different pitches with different grain in their whiskers, and the migrants braid kin to kin. The mill crawls without legs.

It is hard to keep the word animal in your mouth when you watch them. Each one is a shop floor and a ledger and a task, and still there are touches that are not simply utilitarian: a belt-worm pausing longer than it must on a grit-scar as if remembering; an impeller glowing, not in the soft studder of electricity but in a genuine pulse, a strobe on and off as if laughing with no reason; a hinge-manta keeping a single orphaned scale moth on the lee of its wing with little quick passes until it finds a layer it can ride.

So who lives here? Everybody who can keep a beat under stone. Bodies that have chosen pieces of noise and made them food. Mouths with grips, rings with guts, gaskets with patience. An animal architecture that never needed a hand to draw a plan, an empire of leverage that measures time in pressure and calls voices currents. They are engines that remember being animals and animals that discovered engines under their own skins. They have neighborhoods made from eddies and feuds written in grit and weddings pitched to the tenor of falling sky. They live in the lesson of the bowl: if you hold just right, pressure is kindness, and if you tune just right, silence is death.
