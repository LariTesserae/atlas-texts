They are small to your eye at first because the bowl asks everyone to be small. Not in stature—though most are light-boned and quick, grown to fit in the spaces between ropes and shelves—but in the way they keep themselves condensed: elbows tight, hair braided close, speech cut short. They live by compression. Their gestures fold instead of fling; their feet find rungs by memory; their tools tuck into corners as if corners had been invented for that purpose.

Their skin carries a finish that is part habit and part inheritance. They oil themselves each morning with a thin mix of sap and ground leaf, rubbed until it shines and then powdered with dust. The oil seals; the dust breaks glare and scent. Under it, their skin is pale as the cliff when new, or smokesilver, or lacquer-brown depending on ancestry and how the sun found their ancestors before the veil. Children are glossy, bright-edged; elders wear a patina of old oils, a deep scent that is almost music when the heat lifts it. Hair is kept like rope—braids within braids, threaded with quills and shell to hold a curve when a hand must grab. Their teeth are tidy, resin-polished; their tongues are robust against bitterness and sting. Tears, when they happen, leave no wet on their faces; grief here goes out through the voice and the hands, through a drumbeat, through the long ritual of sanding a name into the next platform’s edge.

Light has taught their eyes its grammar. The saffron filters have widened their pupils’ patience. Whites are less white, touched with amber; irises run to dark, so that eye looks like seed in fruit. Height has taught their balance its grammar. Ankles are wiry; toes know to curl and uncurl at the right moment to hook a rung without looking. They can wedge themselves sideways in a crack with one knee and the opposite shoulder, let go with both hands to work a knot, and never think of falling. When they laugh—and they do, often—it is brief and low so it won’t startle wings or drip.

They do not sweat as you expect; not much. Salt here is eaten and worn, not lost. Breath is disciplined. Children are taught a way to count heartbeats against the bowl’s long inhalation and to set their own pace to it so no one takes more air than the outside wind gives.

Their speech learned its shape from distance and echo. Over the hum, you speak with aim; words are sent like thrown grains. Names are short and stackable. A person might be Tis, who tends the third kiln; or Ree, who goes to the veil; or Jon, who sings to snakes. They also have long names that are events—spoken in full only in closeness or in ceremony—that begin in the body and end in the bowl: I-was-lifted-when-the-net-failed, I-breathed-in-the-storm-and-breathed-out, I-kept-the-fire-seated-in-its-hunger.

They mate and raise children the way they do everything else here: with attention to flow. Courtship smells like boiled sap cooled over a shared bead; it sounds like a drum muffled against a thigh. Kissing is brow to brow, oil to oil, pressure carrying more than mouths could. Birth comes with lamps all hooded and a chorus of lowing gourds keeping the pollinators out of the birthing-ledge. The umbilical is pinched with a clay clamp and sealed with warm resin that will later be cut free and worn as a bead until it returns to the bowl. Infants are powdered daily with salt and seed-dust to keep the skin sure; they learn ladders before they learn flat ground, because there is so little of it. When a child laughs for the first time, a cord of bells is shifted one knot along the railing above the resinfall; the old believe it wakes the flow to the new voice.

Their elders are light as birds when you lift them, all tensile strength and little waste. They sit by fires—those contained, disciplined tongues—and tell maintenance as story: “When you tie above the cut, you listen with your hands. The stone says where its old sea was. The secret of knots is leaving enough tail for whoever comes after.” Memory is stored in cords, in little cages of woven reed hung from the same hook year after year; each cage has a smell tied to a season. Grandmothers rub oil into rope as they talk, because that is the grammar of thought here.

Who else lives here? The bowl does. That is the first answer a child is taught. The bowl as a slow body, as old skin, as a hand cupped against the world. It breathes through the mouth, you can feel it if you put your palm there when the wind tightens; it eats dust and sun and what bones and old platforms you return to it; it gives back resin. When you die, you are part of the bowl in a literal way—ground and mixed, there in the mortar that binds the next square of living to the last—and in another way that sits in the twitches of bead-nets and in the way a certain drumbeat makes lamps breathe. They will show you the net that moves at evening, how it quivers without wind. They say those are the ones who already learned to keep dry inside, rustling in the net’s names.

There are the resin-farmers, who are not someone so much as a practice. They come in uncountable kinds. You saw the beetles mouthing drips into pellets. The people call them Brickers and guide their appetites with salts so that the pellets form lines exactly where a railing needs to be padded, exactly where a step must be less slippery. There are the needle-ants that pick up those bricks and ferry them in sedate processions. No one steps on a line of ants; to do so is to knock a shelf-plant crooked at some remove. If one does, the penance is to shoulder a ropeway basket for a day.

There are the predators who live by covenant. The mantis-fishers—hand-sized, green-bone and deliberate—keep the midges from blooming into fog. They can be persuaded to take a nap somewhere else if you hum into their shells and stroke the joint behind the long arm. If they break rule and strike into a kitchen or at a child, there is a song for them too: a dissonant, teeth-on-ridge sound that stops them cold, and then a basket comes down with a quick mouth and they are relocated. The tendril-snakes are not snakes as you would draw a snake but ropes with opinions and a seam of mind along the length. They go where the milk-sap flow is thickest to hunt mouse-blossoms and the small lizards that drink there. When too many come to one spill and the bowl’s rule of balance is strained, the snake-herders sing them away by touching each rope with a certain bone; the bone hums through the resin and the snakes roll themselves, delighted, toward the tone until they can be carried in coils like armfuls of sleeping cords.

There are the lantern-keepers, who are a flock and a guild. Half-creatures, half practice, all noise: soft bodies that light from within when fed the right scent. Children are assigned to keep them off the lamps with songs that turn their glow aside; the keepers in turn eat the mothish tuft-flowers that would otherwise clog the veil with dust. In festival, the guild catches them on purpose and braids them into hair; all night the bowl is full of moving stars.

There are beasts you will never see but whose work touches you. Under the platforms a thin dust slides in rivulets on days of the bowl’s long exhalation. It seems like powder hurrying downhill for no reason until you learn it is being shepherded by mites as small as seeds, following scent-channels laid in last season’s oils. They are the bowl’s internal rain, the rearrangement that keeps the resin from armoring over and making new pockets starve. The people call them the Poor, meaning not that they have little but that they live where little light goes; when a bead-string is strung low for the Poor, it bears a specific herb that tells them they are acknowledged.

There are the plants in their multiplicity, which are not passive in any way you remember from wetter places. They argue with each other by vapor and sugar; they are always moving, only in pulses too slow for you to trust as motion unless you watch for hours. The rope-stalks braid new anchorings at night; the shelf-plants lift their plates and lower them in rounds that keep time with the kilns. Hair-moss caches the day’s smell and lets it out when wind cools, a way of speaking the day backward for anything that comes later. People prune them the way you regulate a market—hands everywhere, sign and counter-sign—composting the cuts with old bones and cured spines and dust until everything is fitted into something else. In spring (which is a word that means a thinning in the long cycles of heat, not rain), little fat sacs swell on the stalks, full of pressurized milk that will leap into a child’s face if pinched. Children are smacked for laughing at people so splashed. The sting is not trivial; it burns and leaves a shiny scar if not dusted fast. If dusted properly and cooled with a chip of old stone, the scar lies in the skin like glass and is considered fine.

The people have named themselves a dozen names and none are wrong. Outsiders, when there were some, would call them resiners or clifffolk. They call themselves Keepers-of-Glow when they speak to the fire; they call themselves Bowl’s Nestlings when reminding a young one not to swagger near the mouth; in long speech they are Those-Who-Stay-Dry and Another-Rung and We-Who-Return-Bones. If you insist on a single word, they will give you the sound made by brush dipped in oil across a clay rim, one syllable short, one syllable long, which your mouth won’t find.

They are governed by the fact of the place. There are people with tasks so formal they are almost castes: the Flow-keepers who sit by the central spill and watch it the way sailors watch sky; the Fire-sitters whose whole education is appetite and its limits; the Veil-menders who climb like geckos and pin mineral to mineral with patient paste; the Knot-Mothers who can tie a thousand kinds of knot and have names for each, whose wrists are scarred where ropes have burned through in emergencies and been stopped by skin and will. Decisions are made where everyone can cling—at the bowl’s back curve, where a person above can call down, and a person below can throw up a bead with a knot for yes and a knot for no. They vote with beads and with labor: whoever signs up to carry laced baskets to the Veil has more say about the Veil that cycle. No one rules fire but fire; it receives an empty place at meetings and a saucer of oil. It is not fed unless it is answered.

Trade runs along ropes too fine to see unless you look for the twist in the air. What comes in is what the plateau will consent to give without asking the bowl to break itself to pay. Old wire. Crates of empty shells for lamps. Dust baked into bricks by the sun somewhere a cliff’s shoulder allows one person to lie belly-down and work alone all day. In return goes resin rendered and colored, beads ambered around things with small lives still in them—these are prized by people who live where wet is not a sin—and baskets strong enough to hold a child by the ankle and not tear. Sometimes stories cross. Once, a traveler came whose beard held the idea of dew. He was detained at the mouth with kindness edged like a blade. Salt was shaken over him until the smell went quiet. Children who were permitted to watch remember the light on dust motes and a glittering like rain and the taste of fear that is not theirs.

Intimacy with danger is a household god. The kilns sit like little hearts, warm and ticking; every woman and man can work one if the keeper collapses. The ropeways hum like long thoughts; everyone knows how to catch a basket that has started to swing into pendulum and return it to stillness with three touches and one word. Fires are all called by names, oftener than children. This one is Thumb—small, stubborn, good at licking the belly of a pot. This one is Aunt—fussy, will only take new oil after her own is nearly gone. This one is Strange—keens sometimes without moving when the outside wind presses, and has to be sung to or it will frighten someone into tipping a pot. People stroke the rims of these fires’ basins with a finger when they pass, like greeting.

Forbidden things are named softly. Wet is the worst of them. It is less an idea of water and more an idea of ruin. The oldest stories are about rot and slackness, about ropes coming unlaid and goods slumping and bones turning unreachable because they have gone to pulp. Little children, to be frightened into caution, are told of a wet place where even light feels like touch; they hiss and scrunch up their shoulders and groan with delight because it is gruesome. Adolescents dare each other to press their tongue to cold stone and name “river” and see if anyone hears. The punishments, when they come, are all cleansing: dusting, salting, a week wearing wool that does not wick and so itches, long service rubbing oil into ropes. Exile is not used; where to, and with what air?

When the saffron deepens and the bowl’s notes lower, the place that lives in the center makes itself known without announcement. The Flow never stops. It wears a skin at evening, a deepening gloss that looks like rest. Under it, those with palms trained for such things can feel patterns—push and lull, push and lull—that are neither pulse nor tide exactly. It is older than any of them, older than bones here, older than the veil; almost old as the cliff and perhaps the last whisper of a tree that once leant here when the cliff was lower, or of an ocean’s ghost distilled by ages into sap. They call it Mother by convenience and then correct themselves because Mother has hands and this has only time. This is not god as you know the word; this is neighbor, landlord, ancestor, chemical, low song. Those who tend it keep their hair cropped very close and their ears unadorned so the Flow can be heard better. At marriage, a couple leans their forearms on the basin lip and promises out loud not to ask more of it than it can give without anger.

Children here grow up with two kinds of fear and one of them is good. The good fear is of slackness: of knots not checked, lamps not seated, baskets swung without thought. It is domestic fear and it makes hands careful and voices listen. The other fear is of the drop, which is not good or bad but simply absolute. To learn it, a child stands with a hand in an elder’s hand and looks straight out from the bowl’s mouth until the edge of the world goes clean and empty in their mind, and then they close their eyes and are held, and the elder whispers the names of everything that holds them: rope, knot, nail, shelf, bone, bowl, breath. After that, the drop is an understood absence, honored daily with a glance.

On festival days, which happen when the wind’s breathing and the Flow’s gloss and the ripeness of certain milk-sacs agree, the bowl becomes itself plus intention. Nets are braided new; bead-strings are turned so different herbs look out; the small cages for spirits are emptied and set to spin so each says its name. Fire is invited by name, not with flint but with lamp to lamp, grandmother to child. Lantern-keepers are let into hair and then let go. Stories are told where the hero is always the problem solved: a song that turned a swarm, a knot invented when two ropes would not agree, a time when the bowl remembered falling and a hundred hands held it until the desire passed. Food is oil-cakes with ground spindle-pods, brittle sheets of sweet resin that crack between the teeth and go fizzing at the brain’s back door, meat if a tendril-snake gave up a coil of itself on purpose—yes, they do this, from time to time; it is not a bargain so much as a kind of trust.

There are those who leave. A handful each generation. They go out over the plain and vanish in the veil of air and distant salt. They go with skins full of resin drink and a pack of cords and a knife so sharp you can shave light with it. They go because they want to see a cliff face from the other side, because the bowl’s sound has climbed into their skull and they need a different drum. Sometimes they come back and hang their long names in the bead-net and tell of other bowls, other flows, other ways of saying dry. Sometimes they do not. Their absence is woven in, like a hole in a net that makes the whole stronger because it must compensate with more intention.

At night—if you call this shift night—many things hunt and many things rest. People sleep in hammocks like open hands, head toward the stone, feet toward the mouth, so dreams drain properly. They pack their ears with felt if the mantises have chosen the ladder near them to whisper along. Lovers lie back to back so oils mix slowly. Those who stay awake for the watch move little, because movement speaks here and can startle a snake. In the deepest hour, when the outside breath is slowest, the central skin lifts almost unseeably and settles again as if the bowl itself took, infinitesimally, a sigh. Those on watch nod to it. Sometimes one of them answers, out of long habit, because that is what lives here: oil and bone, pollen and pressure, people whose names are all doing, a place that is also a person, a set of agreements that make gravity something you can live beside.

You ask who lives here. The place does, foremost: its cup and its breath and its old desire to be sea-bed again. The flows do, plant and animal and fire and wind, each with elders and juveniles and tricks. And threaded through them, not lord but caretaker, not capitalized but proper, are the folk who have learned the bowl’s verbs and made themselves into pronouns that fit: we who tie, we who skim, we who burn without blaze, we who never let a thing fall without three lines across and a song. They are dry by choice and by law. They are warm. Their laughter is resin-bright and ends quick so it will not upset the small economies that keep everything alive. In the morning, they dust travelers at the mouth until memory itself feels clean, and in the evening they sit with their feet over the edge and talk in very short words while the center of their home breathes a very old breath and the world outside is, for a while, only a straight line.
