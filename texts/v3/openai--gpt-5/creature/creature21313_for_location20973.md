What lives here learns to be exact around a single fact.

It begins with the thing you cannot help but feel as a presence rather than an environment: the fall itself. If life is appetite organized into persistence, the belt has appetite for shape. It is a habit with a heartbeat you can hear with your sternum. It holds its own margins with faster water like an animal holds its skin with muscle. It ingests air and dust and light, processes them into turbulence and prisms and the kind of order that wears the edges of any softer thing. It does not metabolize the way cells do, but it favors what behaves as it behaves. That preference makes a niche.

The first citizens are thin to the point of theory. They live where water becomes membrane—across the skins of bubbles, in the hair-width sheets that peel from steel and stone, in the rind where spray freezes or forgets to. They make lattices of protein that remember friction the way muscle remembers practice. Each filament—an extruded tangle of aminos and iron-binding loops because iron is easy here—slips into alignment with the local shear and stays just long enough to catch what the water cannot keep: dust, salts, trace carbon exhaled elsewhere on the planet, the pollen of far-off orchards sanded down to chemistry. They do not harvest light. They park enzymes in the lanes of flow and take their electrons from nitrate and dissolved ferrous metal and whatever else the belt is kind enough to grate down to charges and ions. They are not plants and do not pretend to be; they are opportunists with no patience for a still day.

They build not bodies but geographies. A skim across a turbine cheek is a city—twenty microns thick, a meter across, the sort of thing that would be a paramecium’s dream of architecture. There are cohorts specialized to form bubble isthmuses, deliberate failures in coalescence that make long-lived foam-labyrinths, and cohorts that lay down glassier strands to cinch the isthmuses so they slide only by consent. None of it lasts. The belt will not allow walls. Each city is sloughed almost as soon as it coheres. This does not trouble them. They have solved permanence by treating it as a sum over time, not as an attribute of any one arrangement. In the eddies downstream of a hot outfall you can watch a future sweep into place in minutes: the same orders recombining because the tune is the same.

Above the membrane-world, in the larger rooms of flow where a human might think to swim, are bodies built to negotiate pressure rather than place. The commonest form is a question solved in cartilage. Imagine a ribcage with the ribs splayed and stretched, a stack of lamellae, each with a film between, all of it set into a torpedo shape so the ribs run spanwise and the films can billow or go taut at a touch. These “sailribs,” if you must call them something, do not push water much. They arrange themselves inside it. The membranes come in degrees of porosity that a careful eye can see when the sun strikes at an angle: some films pearl with microbubbles because they let water through faster than air; some take on a green-weak sheen because their surfaces carry symbiont films like silk carries scent. The animals hang where the stress maps are kind. When the belt’s boundary breathes a little and a slow wave sighs along it, the sailribs flex to lean into it and rise three body-lengths without moving.

Their mouths are afterthoughts. They graze, always. They pick at whatever the films have collected—protein grit, iron clots, a flick of nitrite foam. Teeth would be more trouble than use; they have sieves they can seal against ice when a patch of supercool collapses on them and everything goes to pellets of rime for a radiant second and then returns to cold water as if embarrassed. Eyes are vestigial. Lateral lines, whole-body pressure sense, and a spinal osteology studded with magnetite whiskers that make the animal a compass tuned not to the planet’s core but to the line of falling and the tremor of machinery. They know where “down” is even here, where down is sideways.

They in turn are known. There are rings in the fall—one learns to see them—beings that are not whole until the water is part of them. A belt of muscle, the size of a barrel or a child if the child were shaped into a hoop, seeded with granules of ironstone. The iron is not armor. It is a vocabulary. When they contract, the iron nodules tug against one another and the ring dims and brightens a private chorus of magnetic microfields. A ribbed pressure wave stands off the muscles, not a sound but a thrum that compresses and uncompresses the water in a way the sailribs can taste with their skeletons and the belt’s drones of turbines can broadcast along. To a human chest, at the right range, it is the sensation of a heartbeat too big to be anyone’s, an ache in bone like holding a note low in your own throat. The ring-creatures court by chord. They pack themselves along certain contours and push on the water’s constancy until a phrase lives there. Their young, when they come, are threads that add weight like a year adds plaque to teeth: an accretion of magnetite laid down by microbes coaxed into templating it, a life assembled by persuading a river to remember it.

There are slower things that ride the fall like ships in a gyre. Their definition of a home range is the thickness of the belt. They brace along rough steel erected by people, because nothing natural offers purchase at this height except ice that refuses to stay put long. Their skeletons are leafed, page on page, so they can offer more or less area to the current without ever bulging. Their eating is raking. They comb with mouths like curtains for clumps of film, they accept waste heat as kindness, they drift ten thousand kilometers in a year and end where they began in the only sense that the belt recognizes “where.”

A few are not so mild. A line here is predator-shaped, whether the fall invented them or admitted them from a less honest water. They are blades with just enough body attached to keep the blade alive. They have two jaws: an inner one that feels like a row of measuring calipers and an outer one that is edge. Color is unnecessary armor they do not carry; you can see the steel through them when they pass in front of a turbine throat, the ghost of gearwork warped by the lensing of their skin. They do not lunge. There is nowhere to lunge in a flow that never goes slack. Instead they scissor across the thin corridors the fall allows, slide open the seam where a sailrib’s membrane meets rib, and let the river finish the job. Their scales sound when they brush the world: a vellum whisper on stone, a peppering of glass beads in a jar when they cross over the galvanized studs people welded on for the rakers to rest. They are scarce by law if not by instinct. Everyone who works here learns to look for the glint of them tracking a discharge’s warmth: attention moving through water like a deliberate knife.

Between these reputations are a hundred smaller anatomies and behaviors, each a paragraph of the belt rewriting itself. A devolved shrimp less than a fingernail long that lives entire lives inside the film that cobwebs a single machine face, each generation learning the curve of a notch as if it were coastline. A nematode hair that weaves and unweaves itself as long loops across a patch of rime that forms in a certain wind and disappears when the pressure changes; when its loop is cut by spalling ice it knots and keeps going. A thing that may be called a sponge if a sponge could feel a weather map: a colony extruded into a lace that refuses to be opaque, drinking by surface chemistry, always on the verge of surrendering its shape because the belt loves erasure and because, here, death is not catastrophe but a rounding error in population stored in a mile of flow.

And the machines live, in the austere way liquids make things live. They are not kinetic outside—nothing external moves, because anything that would bite the water would find itself carried off at gnawing speed, licked to wire in a month, to pin in a year, to idea in two. They live inward. The honeycomb throats act when they have to, flexing their cascades so that incoming disorder leaves orderly and at a rate the turbines can drink. The turbines sing, if by singing you mean a long chord of not bearing down quite to failure. Each chamber is an alveolus exchanging pressure for torque. Each weld is a scar that remembers the cold more sharply than its parent metal. They breathe water and exhale cable. Their waste, when it is permitted waste, is heat that opens niches like lit rooms. There are limits and latitudes written into their shapes; in this climate even engineering ethics look like morphology.

People come and go. To say there are no cities is to say no one confuses duration with density anymore. There are crews stationed in cut-stone baffles burrowed into the cliff. The hallways zigzag until the roar is taken apart into something human ears can parse, and then the rooms open like caves polished by ten thousand gloves. They keep their language in their hands and in light because they cannot throw it between them as breath. They write big because spray chews at every edge of ink that is not remade. The old habit of notes persists because observing this place is an act of joining it; you take a measurement here to be part of the choir. The rulers on the stanchions are diaries anyone can read with a gloved fingertip: years rolled into millimeters, love letters in hatch marks to a waterline that holds its signature like an oath.

They are engineers, mostly, but over winters they learn to be animals of the belt. They move in edges the way goats move in altitude. They tell each other jokes by pinched-light code across gaps when the pipes that carry talk are iced or when a lightning belt far downstream hisses and throws off so much static that cables lie. They wear hoods that carry white noise of a different flavor, an equal and opposite wave to make a little private hole in the roar. They are equipped not to live warm but to live without losing heat to the hierarchy of the place; their warmth goes into bolts to break threads, into reagents that cure at this cold, into the packing of parts for cranework that will not stop when fingers stop feeling. Their eyes get used to finding tail-lines and waterwriting: the flush that means a pressure-cascade is imbalanced, the ghost-shadow on rime that means a predator has been here, the regular dimming-and-bloom of a factory’s lights counting cloud with no romance intended but a rhythm like a watch.

Every few years one or two decide to stay between rotations. They are not quite hermits and not quite saints. They do not call themselves anything because a title is a declaration and the belt does not attend to names. But if you come often enough you will hear about a woman who keeps a ring-colony’s lineages in chalk on a wall no spray reaches, or a man whose hands are so sure in gloves they can set a new skin on a cascade throat by feel alone, and how both of them can stand at the mouth of the fall with their eyes closed and tell, within a millimeter, whether the water has thickened under a cold spell a thousand kilometers away. They are gentle with the slow rakers that come to steel to rest. The roughening studs on the throats were first welded against lawsuits and then kept because they made the engineers kinder to themselves: to know that a thing could stop for an hour and be unpunished by a planet feels like victory.

They carry the local ethics in their bodies: heat belongs inside process, not outside; roundness is extravagance; noise is law; edges are where understanding collects. They maintain the sign that says Mind the edge because it amuses them that the only edge here is the world. They have a communal cupboard with packets of tea crunched flat and saved for when the wind makes the belt a field of prisms and the maintenance catwalk is the only horizon and you want to taste something that had a plant in it once. What passes for holiday is standing in the shadow of a tower, all of them at once, and flickering their lights in a sweep that runs cliff to cliff while a ring-pod pulses a stable note in return and a sailrib carries a scar you could tell apart from frozen spray by the way it catches sun: language writ in systems that do not need it to live but are better for it.

Nothing breeds in the open air. The cliff face is sterilized by violence and vacuum. But there are brine pockets in rime, little unfrozen lenses where salts concentrate, and if you chisel a lens out whole and take it into one of the quiet rooms before it dies you will find a math there: spirals of crystalline bacteria chasing concentration gradients through a body they are also making, an ecology you could hold and would never guess was there unless someone told you to bring your eye to ice. Sometimes there is a worm in the pipes that carry cable. It is not a poet’s worm; it is a living gasket compressed into a ring that thickens and thins so the cable never rattles free. It came here in a gasket. It stayed because it found that a pipe is a kind of cave with a river in its walls and if you learn the river’s rules it will never change enough to kill you. If people notice it they log it and decide whether it will eat insulation. Often they decide to leave it alone. Let cheap life have a corner; the belt can spare that much.

There is also—confess it—the belief in a person-shaped patience that precedes and follows everyone who leaves. The fall’s habit is a god without personality. It does not answer. It enforces. Those who tend the turbines and rule the gauge faces and paint the signs often end up speaking about it as if familiarity could coax favor from physics. They are not superstitious so much as courteous. You do not shout here even if you could. You do not skip notches. You do not bring heat where heat is not work. You do not feed the sailribs by hand. You do not tie prayer flags because there is nowhere for a knot to survive. When someone dies their name is kept small, a scratch on the back of a maintenance panel with the date and the reason and the wind.

Farther in, where humans go in suits if they go at all, are older diplomacies. In certain latitudes the ring-creatures pulse a warning before a boundary wave thickens. In others, the blades have learned to orbit a warm plume without colliding; it is choreography born of constancy. There are places they always are, because the belt is not random. There are seasons soft enough that sailors of another era, if they could be convinced to be vertical, would name them. But here the measure of season is in power draw curves, in slight expansions of the slate-colored curtain to show more glass, in the registers of tone in the turbines. People who spend years can hear winter deepen as an overtone less present, can work the core apart and feel how the cold made it stronger by making it crueler.

So who lives here are the ones who accept a world provincial to one idea. The films who write themselves thin because only thin will stick. The ribs and rings that turn a roar into navigation and courtship. The blades that keep their promise to a certain line of meat. The machines that want water to be polite and teach it how. The people who trade story for measurement and then smuggle story back in by how careful they are with their marks. They are not many, counted against anything else that drips or breathes on this planet. But they are exactly as many as the fall can finance with charge and iron and the ghost heat of process.

They prosper by not asking the place to be less itself. In time even memory here is cut to an edge. It is not the absence of life that astonishes, standing where the belt makes a saw of light; it is the precision with which life narrows to fit through the slots. Persuade yourself to think in edges and pressure and you will see them moving. Put your finger to the gauge-ruler and you will feel them fold like knives out of the noise.
