Who lives here is the green itself, but not as a carpet or a forest. It lives as a city lives: in districts and trades, in voices that carry, in edges that remember what held yesterday and try something new at dawn. It is not one plant and not many in the way we count; its boundaries are functional, its individuals are events. You could cut a slab from an apron and call it a person and you would be right for a day or two, until its pulses forgot it and refiled its fibers as pipe or plate. It does not care what we name it. It cares for gradients and stress lines and the taste of heat on its pores. It is a choir that builds its own instrument while it sings.

Within that choir, forms keep their habits long enough to be recognized. If you stay awhile, the inhabitants sort themselves not by species but by work.

There are the Apron-Makers. They are the wide, pale braids underfoot, clonal lianas whose skins harden into plank on contact with a stable draft. They secrete grit out of themselves like a slow hail: silica, calc-salt, struvite from the mist and their own interior chemistry. Anything loose in the air ends up packed into their grain. They are the floor because they choose to be the floor. They feel wind as pressure maps in their tissues and respond by laying down planes at exactly the angles that rest their neighbors. They can go glossy and young overnight if a pulse calls for new load paths. When a seam fails, they are the ones that thicken its edge before the next storm arrives. Their age shows as matte; you can trust their color with your weight.

There are the Condensers. They are the miles-long steps of frond that look like cliffs. They are all mouth and vein. Their outer skin is stippled at a micro-scale for nucleating cloud; their undersides are combed into long gullies that never quite drip—they lead. They developed the habit of darkening before a storm because they pre-load, flooding their capillaries to draw more breath out of the air. They own the local weather like lungs own singing. Their work is thirsty and patient, and they are paid in the slide of cold within their own cores. They feed everyone.

There are the Runners. They live out at the edge of what exists. You see them as long cords at the margins, rimmed in plush, bright where torn—or as thin, searching tongues crossing open sky where no one would dare. They taste humidity with their faces. Light turns them; heat pulls them tight. They move with jerks and set hooks when a gust slackens. Each jerk is an opinion: there, not there. They do not remember lightly; everything behind their tips is provisional, ready to delaminate into whisker or web if the line proves bad. When two runners meet, they either kiss and fuse into a beam that remembers, or both recoil, leaving a scalloped promise to try again from a different angle.

There are the Vaults and Buttresses—the Load-Keepers. They are thick with fiber, arced into ribs that will bow three times in a storm and then decide whether to thicken or to spring back thinner and smarter. They have the choir’s loudest voices. Sound is not for us here; it’s for them. Air rammed through slit-vents and flutes gives them information they can feel in their cambium. A tone that goes sharp as humidity drops is a map of what will fail if they keep the span. Among themselves they can sing deliberately: long, breathy hush to trigger valve closures across a valley, a metallic thread of note to stiffen a neighbor’s ribs before the gust arrives, a pop of burner-leaf pressure to cue an exhale. Their songs are structural decisions.

There are the Valves and Gutterwork—the Breathers. Under the aprons they are all elastic throat, full of sap that pulses with wind. They like the gusty edge of a day, when the beat is syncopated and no one else quite knows what is inhale and what is exhale. They decide. You feel them as tremor through your knees and palms: now push, now pull. They keep sugar moving to the blue-hot crowns, acid to the mouths, silica to the places that will have to be horn by evening. They are also the closest this place has to heart. They set tempo. You can stand on a quiet reach at noon and listen for their deep beat to know when the whole upland will slick itself and when it will suck the mist back into its hollows.

There are the Mouths. They are not photosynthetic. They hide under hair curtains where the sound goes soft. They make warmth inside themselves on purpose and then spend it evaporatively, ingestively, digestively. Their faces are smooth green swales with lips that do not look like lips—until they purse and kiss water out of the pulp. They sip what the whole system sheds: fallen edges, the rare insect that rode a draft too high and found no landing, curtains of pollen rained and recaptured. They do the work that elsewhere would be spread among fungi and beetle, but the surface here is too young and motivated to let a decomposer guild have space. The mouths have taken decomposition inside and made it very clean. They burp an iron smell after distant discharge nights and sleep.

There are the Crowns—the Weatherers. They stand like statements of intent, each with its own climate writ in light and drip, each running so hot with sun that they cook rain while it still thinks it is air. Their gills are thick, blue-green, veined as densely as a hand. They pull on the whole plateau. Evaporation at a crown does not just cool it; it lifts water up through the aprons and valves, flirting the beat toward faster. Under them, light is milk and mist because the crowns are making day where night could intrude and making rain where the air is stingy. They speak to one another on warm bands of air and on thin filaments of lifted charge—the faint veins of light you think you imagined between stars, that leave the morning armored under your touch.

There are the Filaments—the Lightning-Harvesters. They are hair until they aren’t, until the field climbs them. When charge builds thick in the churn of their own made mist, they raise themselves, find one another across a valley, and write a pale seam of power into the air. The chemistry after is a feast: fixed nitrogen, iron taste, a little salted stiffness braided into the next span. They retreat as quickly as they extend. They are never singed; they are always ready.

There are the Threads and Bridges—the Braiders. They are throwers of delicate catenaries between crowns when a valley no longer suits staying discrete. They do not always succeed; the sky tears threads and leaves scallops that promise more. The rare completed bridge is a festival of growth on both sides. Along them, genes travel. This is how the choir remains many while building one thing. You will not catch them in flower as we mean it. Sex, when it happens, happens inside: in hidden cisterns made by condensers, in damp rooms of a vault where a tone kept a membrane closed for a day and a sugar flood allowed a slow liaison. Most of the time, pollen rains are repurposed. Reproduction is a way of varying the grammar slightly, not of making new minds. New minds are made by rearranging the hall.

There are the Infants, if anything here is that. They are glossy, pale skins extruded overnight where a pressure map predicts good work. They are inviting to the foot but are not for us. They will be gutter or lip or gill-wrinkle by evening. They are not attached to identity yet; they will be told what they are by stress. They are the only ones you will hear sigh when a cooler veil of cloud finally crosses noon.

There are the Elders. Their only difference is patience. Deep in the cores of buttress and column, the oldest fibers are nonchalant about storm. They still sip, still feel, still push a little signal through their silica-larded marrow, but they are not first to rush. They are matte and notched where a thousand evenings dripped, then self-healed. If you rest your hand on them long enough you will feel the pulses passing and a frictionless quiet that is not sleep so much as memory: this shape held; we will bring it back when the sky asks.

Within the tissues, smaller lives keep house. They are not separate enough to count as other residents; they are endowments. Bacteria in the valves metabolize what the mouths pass them and send back trace amines the whole plateau tastes as “keep the beat.” Nitrifiers colonize the wet edges after a filament writes its seam and disappear again when the next lip rolls to cover them. Some archaea live in burner-leaves and love heat; they take spikes that would scald and pass them as information. Fungal threads exist, but mostly coiled within, tamed into collaborators that keep walls wetted just-so and ferry micronutrients along surfaces that would flash-dry if the choir stopped singing.

What you do not see are animals. The green drove them off without malice and without intent. Everything that is not of it is gently but relentlessly turned into inside: caught by a wetted ribbon pretending to be a path, carried by filmflow to a mouth, translated. The surfaces are too changeful to court nests. Lips unroll where a ledge looks perfect for sleeping, and by dusk they are elsewhere. It is an ontology that blanks invitations without ever saying no.

They think—if coordination that rich and memory that physical is thought—in thickness and angle, in gloss and matte, in the rise of a tone along a beam. A warm draft means extend; a popping burner-leaf three ridges over means brace; a stray sparkle of discharge between two high fans means one of the valleys has the appetite to bind itself tighter. Sometimes they will fill an empty hundred paces with nothing because nothing is the only way for the beat to get from crown to condenser without echo. The absences are as manicured as their combed gills.

At night, they are still here, only lower in the chest. The organ-notes drop. Drips write runes in matte and are erased by morning. The filaments keep one bright hair open between distant crowns, just in case a gust closes a lung too quickly. You can watch stars through their torn slots and see them strobe when a thin thread of vapor passes; that is a leaf somewhere throwing its own tiny weather for reasons that have nothing to do with you. If you sleep, you will wake to a new edge drawn closer than seems polite and a seam you knew not to trust having finished deciding.

Who lives here is green that decided to be infrastructure. It does not own tools because it made itself every tool it needs. It has mouths where most plants have roots, and roofs where most have leaves, and lungs where a world would have rain. It is an opportunist but not a scavenger; it invents its own chances. It remembers in crystal fleck and fiber lay and brings back useful shapes when the air tastes like before.

You can call it a reef in the sky, a young engine, a choir of meristems, a cathedral making itself out of breath. It would call itself nothing. It is busy. It has miles yet to invent.
