The first citizens here are thin as intention. They live where stone sweats—films slick as spilled oil, throwing back ridiculous rainbows from iron and manganese and whatever else the bedrock confesses. They filament across the undersides of ledges and stitch gutters on a miniature scale, eating light so clean it tastes of glass, drinking metal-sweet water that never quite becomes a drop. They start the day as frost, change costume at sun, and are undone by noon; then they go quiet again in spore and sheath and seed of themselves inside the rock’s cold grammar. They do not have a season so much as a pulse, and the city and the animals count their days by that pulse.

After them come the things that happen fast enough to see. Gnats with bellies that shine like wet slate, beetles that blink oil-blue, lacewings with rims of rime. They know the hour when the smallest winds lift from sun-hot planes and they ride them as if on memory. They hatch from cracks too narrow for thought, from grains of talc-silt you would call nothing, and they drink the living sheen with mouths made for skimming. Their lives are a bright hour strung on a weather-thread—born in the thaw squeeze, spent in the fog, finished by the time the rock inhales toward evening. If you live here you learn the look of an insect day: the way the air at knee-height becomes smoke, the way swallows read that smoke long before you do and write through it a patient calligraphy.

The hunters are not one thing. Falcons and hawks are as much tools as they are selves—gravity is their favor here, thin air their strike partner. They train their bodies to a place that refuses ease; their chests are engines, their eyes learn to find gray on gray. They drop from a height as if that height were not a cost. Foxes burn and vanish and are there, tails a kept balance at the edge of shadow. Their bodies know the law about shade: stay out of the line where it turns air to something knifed. They understand the city’s generosity, too, and memorize the places where breath is thickened for people and carried, like warmth, into the day—those spans are soft-bellied nap sites for kits, a truth everyone here can see and no one disturbs. Hares are the quiet punctuation of this grammar, ears flattened to become smaller stories of stone, legs wired like a drawn bow, sending out only the signature of their moving in hissed avalanches of frost.

There are the grazers that look like dancers—narrow feet, long limbs—working the lichens, the cushion-plant bloom that happens when a terrace pretends soil for a morning. Their economy is patience. They score the edges of thaw-beds with their teeth and step like someone counting because they are.

Plants here don’t spread; they decide. They make choices microclimate by microclimate, a mat at a time, a cushion at a time, a dwarf willow threaded into a crack like a vow. In summer (if that word fits) the willows are the height of your boot; their leaves are an opinion held very close to the stem. Sedges strop themselves against rime, straighten, bend. The flowers that happen are white because everything else would be a lie in this light. The berries are a red reserved for error flags and blood, and each cluster is guarded as if the whole place has only allowed it by indulgence.

The city lives here as well, which is to say it is not just where people are. It has a body—pylons sunk without anger into stone, skins braided of something that drinks light and returns it as function, organs that hum the air heavier in the places where lungs like ours are weakest. It drinks cloud. It grows and sheds frost the way other living things grow and shed hair. It gathers water not by grabbing but by extending—webs hung where wind arrives, tuned to let go of load before they break. On a still night you can stand under a span and feel a sigh go through it when a family crosses, the microclimate adjusting to bodies with the same attention a plant would give its own shade. It keeps its noise like a discipline. It knows that here, speaking softly is not reverent so much as necessary—you can bruise sound, and sound can bruise back.

Inside this body are the people. They are altitude animals by culture first, by blood perhaps by now. They wear their breath. Masks are not embarrassment but jewelry and rite—fine-bodied, tuned valves that make back gifts of heat and water with each exhale. You can hear them coming on the long stairs only in the smallest way, a modulation like silk pulled through a ring. Their clothing has a temperament: porosities that go thoughtful under wind, skins that take on fog and give it to the cups sewn into cuffs, hems, the inside wrists where pulses beat and the small differences live. Children learn to tie and untie their own microclimates like other children learn shoes.

They do not call it farming exactly. They garden states. They keep thaw-beds the way a watchmaker keeps time—composite mats that know how to be peat without wanting centuries, wrung wet and wrung dry to suit the day. They grow reeds in constructed marshes that breathe—each trough a lung—and keep cup-plants that trade patience for nitrogen and a little protein, their green mouths fed by the insect hour. They coax bulbs to flare yellow exactly for the hour when sun and warmth agree to touch a terrace, a planned astonishment. In certain vaults the air is held slow and warm enough to make fungus maps grow; in others, water is moved sideways and up until it forgets down and becomes food. What soil there is, they make: sifted lichen crust, ground feldspar, the bitter talc-silts, all taught to hold together by threads and hyphae threaded like stitching.

Their work is made from the law of small differences. They harvest condensation not for water alone but for wattage, collect the tiny current that quickens where vapor becomes drop and drop vanishes into air. Whole neighborhoods run on the seam between day and night. Their heat lives in phase changes; you do not burn a thing if a change of state will do it instead. Their tools are tuned for edges: shadow-blurrers that keep a crop from a killing line, finger-wide wicks that carry a trickle up to a shelf of green, hand-fans that teach just enough wind to a web to make it drink. They own more words for fog than for rain, and how not to be surprised by that has become part of their ethics.

They have guilds because the place is itself a craft. There are Fogwrights who read the sky the way others read water—every morning they’re on the windward terraces, turning meshes coarser or fine, watching how cloud thins around cliff-face and learning when to ask it for more. There are Attenuators in hooded rooms who listen to hum in order to thicken or lighten the air over a plaza by a breath or two, a civic mercy. There are Seamlighters who walk the hot-cold lines along panels and collect what energy can be coaxed from the very idea of a boundary. There are Listeners who sit with backs to the rock on still days and note the sounds of ice inside it—paper tearing, rock swallowing, sighs like a thought leaving the head—and by their notes crews choose where not to put weight this season.

Children are apprenticed to distances. They learn what a kilometer looks like up here when the world is a catalog of far. They memorize the day’s tide: dawn gilds frost, rill-birth, cloud-lift, evening lace. They carry fold-cards that fog when fog is there and stay clear when it is not, a schoolbook of humidity you read with your fingers. Their first rite is to climb a pylon to where breath gets thin and sit for an hour listening to their own masks while a mentor rests a hand against the column and feels the city answering back. Another rite is naming a seep: the day you find a place where the rock is giving you something for free, you name it, and with the name comes work—keeping its edge from breaking into gully too fast, inviting sedges to the margin and then keeping their ambition ethical.

Their language has weather in it. A person may be called Leeward, Fog-Edge, First-Rill, Sound-of-Split. Households are terraces; kinship can be told as platforms stepped from cliff into air. They are not loud. Laughter is thin and clean and doesn’t travel far, stories are told by hand in shadow as often as in voice, music is for rock and not for distance—harp strings left in slate stacks, little ceramic whistles tuned to the note the pylons hum in, bass you feel through a rail. They keep time by light and by breath, by the hour when swallows go full and by the hour when they stop. Their festivals are precise: the Day of Yellow Bulbs; the Thaw-Lean; Fog-harvest when the meshes have drunk enough to feed whole squares their fill and shiver free of ice in a single, huge whisper.

If you look for their laws you do not find much text. You find devices instead, which is to say habits. Nothing left unclipped to a span. No simple downspout. No shadow drawn clean across a growing thing. No flat shelter out in flatness. Water moved sideways and up before it is ever allowed down. Children learn to put stones back where they found them because the freeze will move them otherwise and you might as well practice being corrected. They teach that the rock stores not heat but the possibility of heat, and that resolving a possibility into a pastime is poor citizenship. They do not take a thing into the open sky unless it is willing to be transparent to weather and, in extremis, not be there at all.

They feed themselves on a stack of small foods layered very thin: cups of sedge seeds, an afternoon’s worth of gnats brazed on a mesh and folded into a stew that tastes of metal and sugar and a faint clean bitterness, slivers of cushion-plant where harvest will not kill the pillow, leaves of dwarf willow in a brine that remembers old seas. Some take hares in season, and keep that practice inside hard rules no one has to write. There is a fish here, too—a thing kept in the constructed marshes, more pretense than fish by an old standard, but it sifts algae from slow water and gives protein like a quiet favor. Tea is made from a lichen that looks like rust when dry and turns vivid green under breath. Salt is treated as memory: moved out of bodies and back into field, lent to phase-change bricks. Wastes go to composite; nothing leaves because everything that leaves will ask to be fetched back at a cost.

Bodies here look like the place a little in that they carry themselves like people who have made a pact with thinness. Deep chests, a little more red in the blood if you cut a finger, calves like rope, a kind of slowness that is not lack but meter. Elders are not small. They’ve learned drag—how to be heavy when the wind wants you, how to be light when the step has to be a cloud. Children run and then stop and learn to listen for the rock inside the rock talking to itself. Lovers move in close circles under spans where the air is soft; weddings are contracts with the wind, vows to shape it together.

Sometimes there are others. Teams come up for a week in the bright half-season and work the fog-fields for designs that can be unrolled at sea or in deserts. Migrant bird-counters carry notebooks that never see a letter but are full of pencil dashes you can hear if you know how to listen. Nomads cross the far edge of the plateau with animals that do not know walls and look in with the kind of hunger that is not about food. Trade happens in skins of water richer than taste, in meshes finer than patience, in the algorithms the city uses to take a thing that hums and make it hum with kindness.

There are less material citizens, too. A roll of air going back and forth across the tableland all day like a sleeping animal’s breathing is as much an inhabitant as anything with eyes. There is a daily river made only of vapor that never reaches you and feeds you anyway. There is the sound the rock makes when it gives up a fraction of itself to the day and takes it back at night, a clock you cannot carry but that carries you. There are rules, yes, but they are not commandments. They are the relief you feel when you step into a place where everything is the way it must be to go on.

And time lives here in a way you can feel. Not the heaped time of old soils and rivers that forgot their parents, but first time. The gullies practice at being canyons. The cushions practice at being meadows. The water rehearses at being river. The city sits with that firstness without hurrying it. Its people have the faces of those who’ve chosen not to make more noise than their own lungs. They stand on ledges and wait and decide, and the place keeps offering: a fox asleep on a warmed rail, a beetle lifting into impossible arc, a ribbon of new gullies writing themselves deeper, frost raising a tiny mountain range out of gravel and then bowing it back into powder at noon. What lives here is not one thing. It is a stack of precisions. It is a choir of quiets. It is breath counted against distance and distance answering back with weather. It is witnesses and instruments, content to belong to a landscape that will, in time, teach itself how to be old.
