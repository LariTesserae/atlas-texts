Water lives here first. It lives as film and thread and veil, as something with its own musculature, creeping forward over quartz like a patient animal that knows every ledge by heart. It keeps a schedule, punches in at dawn, lifts itself by heat, drops itself by gravity, and everywhere that it moves, other lives lace themselves to its back.

On the walls, the first citizens are thin. There are skins on the stone—lichens in the orange cast of iron-eating algae, black lawns of cyanobacteria that write glossy hieroglyphs where a new seep breaks through, diatom grease that makes old handholds feel like polished bone. Film-feeders rasp those micro-lands: snails the size of a shirt-button, millipedes with lacquered segments that gleam as if oiled. Spiders set short hems of web between droplets to fish the falling—an economy of meniscus and silk. In the wetter streaks, iron bacteria throw up little towers of ocher so small your fingertip would crush ten seasons’ work, and their towns slough into faint rinds that make the cliff look varnished. The rock itself shelters a pulse of other eaters: endolithic algae inhabiting a few translucent grains down, tucked into a crystal cathedral so minute only the sun knows it exists.

Behind the veils, there are dark-air beings. Swifts have borrowed the noise of the falls for their own cloister; you only ever see them as parenthesis, the half-curves of ink that fold and refold along the cliff. When the weather goes hard and the water fattens to sheets, bats change roosts, taking the old echo of a dry alcove and hanging there like a thought held too long. In the spray inlets, swifts’ nests are glued with spit and cobweb and hair; their young grow up on wind like a trade. Things you will never see share those rooms—the small translucent amphipods that comb the mineral film off dripping edges, mites that graze spores from fern undersides, a moth that has spent its life hearing the pitch of falling water and lays its eggs where the note is right.

Up through the karren, the plants are rainwrights. You can feel what they’re made for by touch alone: leaves cupped like palms in prayer, pitchers fluted with ridges that fit wind and drip, rosettes that thread themselves tight enough to be bowls. The stone gives almost nothing, so all of the making comes from above. Heliamphora pitchers hold their gardens of drowned flies and the ghost-leaf tea that leaches down off the heath, and at their mouths tiny ants traffic—sugar collectors one day, meat the next, depending on which way unlucky legs were pointed. Sundews lie with their spoons out, glossy red and green, catching light and anything else that shines. Butterworts go flat as stamps onto seep-lips, quiet, their stickiness doing a slow bright murder on gnats.

Small bodies are the common currency. Beetles wear dew like armor; before they eat they drink from themselves, cocking up to let the rim of elytra bead and run. There is a snake as thin as a cord that takes the same routes every morning—across one runner, down two rosettes, along the lip of three pitchers in a row—tasting the night’s deaths and the day’s luck in an index you cannot see. Geckos with toes like velvet follow nectar maps between blossoms that look like porcelain nightmares; they come away powdered yellow and don’t know the difference between hunger and pollination.

At the top, it is cups all the way down. Bromeliads make neighborhoods the size of basins. Every one is a city with a population you can count if you have the patience and the courage to put your face in close: mosquito wrigglers, gnat larvae, beetle boathulls nosing upside down along the meniscus, a copepod that jitters as if embarrassed to exist, wheeled rotifers turning like micromills where leaf-shadows end. Utricularia bladders set little vacuums in that broth and tug microfauna out of the water with a pop you can hear if you could ever get that small. Tadpoles taste tannin like a first language and wear their one sky as a hat, translating rain patterns into schedules—today eat, tomorrow grow, when thunder walk your back legs for the first time across two inches of tarp world to the next city down.

Frogs here are more note than body. They call in square inches, each coin of a lily with its own tenor. Some never learn to jump; they are pebble beings, made to fold and roll down a danger-face like loose ore, gathering no bruise because gravity is an old friend. Others live as punctuation, a bright chirr chopped into the day between swarms of midges and the long vowels of rain. There could be a hundred species on the same acre and you will only trust that once you’ve learned to hear them as colors.

The shrubs that hold the skin of the mesa on—the leathery-leafed, the low and stubborn, the ones that go red at the edges when the sky threatens lightning—have their compromises written into them. Their flowers are austere cups and brushes the size of a fingernail. Hummingbirds keep their train tables in memory and stick to them as if they had contracts. They know which rosette is due to open at noon and which will bare its nectar at three today because it did so yesterday and by long figuring it will tomorrow. At the hour before a storm you can watch a flight low to the surface, every dart on an invisible plane, because the plateau is throwing charge up like hair and the real sky is telling them where not to be. Bees and hoverflies carry their own lightning, too: they step into the field and their fur rises, and pollen grains that would not have let go any other way leap between bodies in little invisible zaps. Spiders sense that brush of charge and raise one leg—saint’s hour—then let out a silk thread. It stands up straight before it slides sideways, and then the spider is gone, a seed on an electric wind.

Ants are the law. They are transport, cleaners, thieves, border guards, and municipal workers. You see their roads in negative: the parts of the moss rubbed smooth, the gloss of used stone. In the sponge under your feet they have cut galleries the width of grass stems. When stormwater runs, they are bridges and boats; they knot themselves into boluses and skate them across cups, they throw a living ribbon across a finger-runnel, beads tightening while the march goes on. They farm, but not as farmers with fields—there isn’t the soil—so they milk. Scales on bromeliad leaves give honeydew; a soft-bodied parasite inside a pitcher gets protected because it drops its faecal pellets into the broth and sweetens it for the plant and the plant for the ant. They harvest flowerheads whole and bury them in the cool of a crack until the seeds separate. Every once in a while, at some signal you cannot chart, a line of them will stop as if struck and every third body will lift its abdomen. Maybe it’s thunder at a frequency only they find useful; maybe it is the plateau murmuring to them through their cuticles about charge. Whatever it is, it travels their world at the speed of touch.

Dragonflies write their names on air. They hunt like stained glass—gossamer panes that flip oil-colors even in the flat light of a brewing noon. Their young live in cups and puddles that can die between breakfast and lunch; so they have adapted to emergency as normal, eating everything soft and slow enough and growing by theft and miracle. Damselflies edge through moss in orthodontic blues and greens, a palette that makes the tannin water look luxurious. A falcon rides the wind that the cliff spills like a spilt sheet, a bit of weapon given lift and a hunger that is almost an element. Its shadow bites at swifts and sometimes swallows only air, but the bite is clean.

At night the manufacturers run on a different shift. Crickets ratchet the sound of the plateau down to a work rhythm. A moth coats itself in wax that beads water and baffles the slick faces of sundew stalks; it will still, sooner or later, stick to something. Some fungi take their portion of the wet with a green glow so faint you have to convince yourself and then you cannot unsee it: the smallest ribs of a rotten leaf lit from within like a map tracing the old veins of a city. Bats drink on the wing from bromeliad plazas, nicking the meniscus and rising with beads on their chins like a laughable mustache. Opossums, or something shaped close to them, ghost the margins, leaving news only in indentations, the width of a wrist, and the tidy surgery of fruit left skinned and cored beside a root.

Electricity counts as a resident. It is here as a taste before it is here as sound: coin-edge on the molars, a bloom on the gums. It moves through grasses as low blue ghosts and makes kings out of the tips of things. Once in a while it kills outright, makes carbon of a shrub or tattoos a fulgurite of fused sand where a seam is dry enough to let itself be scribbled on. More often it just passes and remakes everyone’s hair. Birds take heed and tighten their routes. Ants flatten. The plateau breathes differently.

There are little permanent vagabonds. Tardigrades trudge the flat green world between droplets as if there were continents to cross—there are, to them. Ostracods in the tannin saucers swim like someone who has remembered how to walk underwater. Planaria write and rewrite their bodies. Rotifers turn wheels. Bacteria colonize the oxygen edge in a bromeliad cup and lay down rainbow scums that crack and writhe if you poke them, a petroleum beauty made with iron and appetite. Even the quartz has tenants: microtubes bored into the faintest impurities by things whose bodies are mostly patience.

Rumor lives here too. The crabs under the mountain wear gardens the color of pennies and graze iron as if it were a vegetable. Fish without eyes go up and down in a single boulder all their lives, living in a trickle that to them is a river and to you is a damp. There are swifts that never leave the updraft, swirls of them that sleep on wing and only know cliff by the smell of it. You will not meet any of these except as a change in how the place feels around the idea of them, a thickening, a tilt.

No large thing makes a permanent claim. A vulture might cross, reading thermals as scripture and stepping, at its own gigantic scale, from table to table. A person might come, one, two, four a year, and leave the trace of a skid on a polished runnel that is gone by the next hard rain. Anything bigger than the mesh of this place gets edited out by wet and hunger and the tightness of the ledger.

So who lives here? The condensation guilds and the cup-keepers. The landlords of film and the tenants of drips. Plants that hunt to afford their greenness; animals that drink to afford their motion; insects that mine weather; microbes that write mathematics in oxidation and slime. The wall-huggers and the air-breathers and the quiet nations that thrive in an eye’s worth of water. Lightning, as a seasonal predator. Heat, as a sovereign with a soft hand that is always on your skin. Time, as the oldest resident of all, not a person you can meet but a pressure you can lean into: something that has been sitting here so long that it has acquired a shape.

They live by catching the sky and slowing it. They live by holding on in a place that is mostly release. They live shoulder to shoulder, vertical and horizontal, in a factory without a clock where the only product is more of itself: wet made into leaf, leaf made into syrup, syrup made into frogs, frogs into sound, and sound into rain again.
