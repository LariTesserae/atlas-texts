At the scale of the sheen, life begins as appetite and touch. Films grow films—gray, nacred, faintly granular under a fingertip if you save your breath and fog a window with it. Bacteria and fungi write the first script on the sweating rock. They do not hurry; they have made a grammar out of being damp. Their bodies are lace, net, sponge, peel. Some digest calcium, some exhale it; some tie iron to sugar; some let salt ride their rails and break it apart. They take what the air offers—proteins caught from oceanic haze, the oils of distant flowers atomized and ferried a world away, ammonias exhaled by ungulates on plains too far to imagine—and catch it in the nap of themselves. When a fog-burst passes and a pool dies into the stone, the mats drink as if eating weather can be a profession. It is, here.

On that first surface, then, another: grazers as small as breaths. Soft things you will never name unless you live here—scroll-bodied mites with combs of teeth that rake the mats for their living, glassy flickers that skate on a film four molecules deep and bend at the sound of a whistle, wasplike darts reduced by this thin economy to veils and hooks. Their colors are the colors of dampness: smoke, mushroom, shell. They are translucent with the plateau inside them. They die in drifts and are eaten before they quite are old enough to have learned not to fall.

Higher still, winged things that are not birds and not, always, bats. The plateau breeds membranes. Where there is always updraft, there will be bodies that set themselves to it. Some are pure paraglider, fingered and jointless, hung on elastic ribs; their mouths are strainers. Some are long with hinged arms, two small thumbs for clinging to slick cornices and two longer for rearranging nets. They secrete a saliva that stands if you whisk air through it; they cast it, harpstring it, dip it through flight as through soup, and lick clean what the day has brought. Their eyes are small, because here eyes are less to do with light than with the briefest flash of edge; their ears are plains. They live in the city’s hollows where the stone agrees, stacking in heated hum. On a summer night, a single roost breathes like a forge.

And down low move the slugs that are not slugs because nothing that size can be a simple anything. Call them bulls if you must because it will satisfy something old in you to give agency a neck. They are muscle under slick, armored with shutters that slide like gills in and out to manage the day’s long exhalation, shaded blue-black like a bruise made of graphite. They are pace embodied. They do not hurry because the foodscape hurries toward them: a world of mats recapitulating itself in front of mouths that are rasp and velvet both. They leave behind them not dung but polish, not smell but a rind of lick. You can trail your hand through a slug-bull’s wake the way someone else in another world will touch a lacquered table. If you push your ear against the glassed stone, you will hear a small shearing and a long breathing: that is the pasture compressed to noise.

The city is not something separate from these layers. It hangs between and inside them, which is different. It is buildings and bridges, yes, but also agreements about channels; it is rules about tone; it is certain animals in certain numbers and others not, and it is the constant maintenance of that arithmetic. The ones who call themselves people—because they talk and write and are the kind of animal that tells its children stories long enough for scaffolds to set—have made themselves out of this.

They are not green either. Their diet is the city inverted: trays of filamentous growth; cakes of combed mat dried to a savory crisp and soaked again; the egg and larval bodies of the flying ones in regulated quantities caught in the wide-mouthed nets that edge the gutters; broth skin, condensate cheese. They will eat a fish when the underground sends one up in a bubbling from a river chamber, pallid and grained like driftwood, but this is holiday food and argued over warmly at tables: whether the plateau meant it, whether the gift was accidental. For daily bread they press their mouths to the work of their hands and harvest what all things here harvest, atom by atom lifted from breath.

They are not hairless, not as their grandparents were. Dampness evens hair to velvet. They keep it short because long catches flight and dusts the listening. Some bind their hair in wire so it may hiss when the rails hum, a style both beautiful and a tool. Ears are elaborate—grown and trained, in childhood, the rims massaged and weighted and sung to so the conches widen and the helixes fold and become as much instrument as organ. A child’s first lesson is counting by drips; the second is pitch; the third is never blocking a fall. In place of tattoos they glaze their teeth a whisper thicker at rites of passage, lay a wafer of stone on the tongue to taste its chime. Their fingers are buffed not by vanity but by work; the last ridge of skin on a forefinger is often glossy as enamel from decades of setting chitin combs, from scraping a white fan clean, from pinching salts.

They dress in what can be made to keep damp outside and inside both. Fungus-leathers, oiled and sealed; plates of beetle shell; resins poured in thin films like sugar pulled hard in a confectioner’s shop and sewn into aprons and hoods. Their shoes are soles they grow and resole again, layered fabrics with the nap arranged across them like fur that knows the direction of rain. They carry combs. Not to mend their hair—hair is already decided—but to draw in the stone. A sleeve full of combs might be a neighborhood’s inheritance: a row of rakelets of chitin and copper with teeth graded like the rainfall, each one coaxing a slightly different laminar slide. An adult is measured not by owning but by the part of the map they can sing without looking, by the number of relievers they have cut that took, by how their gutters never stall even in the second dry month when the ocean below lies down and the plateau must sweat for both of them.

Families are not blood alone but watershed. There is a name for your mother and another for the woman who maintained the fall above your sleeping balcony through the year you were sick. At marriage, there is a ceremony that looks, to an outsider, like two neighbors just laying a fresh string of gutter wire between their roofs and pinning it into the cornices’ lips; in fact it takes a day because the angles must be right and because the two of you have to learn to hear, together, when a line sings healthy and when it frets. There is a divorce ceremony too, and it is quieter. Two Inspectors come at dawn and the four of you stand belly to cold stone and the wire is unpinned and the plateau is thanked and the drips are reassigned.

Inspectors: small, precise people, often soft-spoken because sound is work and you do not spend your instrument on gossip. They carry bells as much to remember what silence is as to judge. The clean note of a line that is open, the quiet warble of one just about to blind, the sick thud you hear when someone has made a private dam: these are a language. Tax is a sound, here. Not all levies are money. You may owe the city a re-scribing, and men will come with harnesses and lines and feet that cling like geckos and sing to the stone through combs for a week and leave a lip a breath deeper and a neighborhood safer and your name crossed from a book.

There is government, if you need to call it that, but it is not a council in a hall. It is a map with a thousand hands on it. It is the Sluice Books—volume after volume of handwriting that no one keeps as private property because they are manuals of common survival. Your part is small when you are young; you add notes about a rack that thrums harder this month, about a curtain of fog that always seems to pool and never spill and so must be coaxed. Old ones write less every year but what they write can be surgical: “Reliever at South Throat sang low last wind; widen ninth tooth; do not add chiral salts in spring.” People copy this advice to the backs of their palms and go do it. In a place where most resources are genres of weather, literacy is engineering.

Children climb. Their bodies learn the tilt of stone the way other young bodies learn roads. They are tethered to rails not by law—though there is that—but because rails hum and the hum is safety, and no one raised on mood and chime will willingly step where the wire is quiet. Play, here, is often breath: competitions to hold it; games where one child whistles a gutter and another names it with their eyes closed; a kind of hopscotch across weeping walls where you must place your feet only inside rivulets that make a major third with the fall sound. They fall sometimes, and the city has netted where it can, but it does not lie to itself about gravity. The plateau keeps count; so do parents.

There are professions you would not have invented elsewhere. Chordwrights string the harmonic fences that keep dusk from pinwheeling into the blades. Their rigs are mixtures of carpentry and mending sail and building harp: taut, damp-tolerant, tuned to bat-ear and human ear both. Flow-advocates argue to lay a new gutter here, not there, because the city is going to be hung with a different weight in six years if the rains keep turning this way and you cannot be stubborn in the face of that or the stone will tutor you more forcibly. Fog-keepers ascend to the condenser fields to comb the teeth and clear lichens that would change the drip into splatter; they know, in their shoulders, the weight of air. Saltwrights paint lines the slug-bulls will read and move toward; people joke that Saltwrights must be witches because to move a city wall with a bag of crystals and a brush is as close to witchcraft as an honest trade allows.

They keep animals by licensing their appetites. There are roost-rights carved into charters and passed down: this family may keep fifty square meters of night-creature, to be adjusted in dry years downward, in wet years upward by an elected Assessor of Wings. There are penalties for feeding at dusk because one glut and a roost will outrun a gutter’s patience and peel a district to limestone. There is a fine for dead air not because air has feelings but because stone does; it splits if you suffocate it. Much of the law sounds like etiquette if you have been taught that water has a mood.

The dead are returned with exactly the gravity they deserve. Bones are not burned; that would be an insult to breath. Bodies are weighted with glaze and seated in cups carved under overhangs where the drip is good and even. They are not left to rot; they are cleaned, politely, by well-timed mats and patient beetles. People visit. They do not speak to the name under the glaze because they are practical, but they will run their hands over the developing flowstone and judge, with a craftsman’s joy, whether a life has made a good gloss. Shells of slug-bulls are leaned beside them, and in five years, ten, when they are hard enough, they are cut for trumpets that sing the way you do when your mouth has been taught to carry fog.

Trade is rope and hum. From below, dreadnought baskets rise on winches full of ore, or weeds from a river delta, or the carcass of a drowned thing fat enough to feed a quarter of the plateau. In return go blocks of airy cement as light as bread and strong as good luck, battery-cases that will not corrode in wet air because they were practiced on here, resins that only grow well where the pressure swings are lazy and come with fog. The ones who go down to bargain have bodies trained not to panic when water is not at their skin. They say the lower world smells like a clean shirt dried in sunlight. They always come home oiled from wrists to collarbone as if ashamed.

Religion is humility that learned to make itself useful. There are gods here if you need them—little gods of driplines and a bigger one of weather—but you will mostly see people do what looks like faith because they must. A woman will set a cup under a minor weeper for a winter and drink whatever collects and add her spit and see if it sets into a bead that rings when you flick it: a talisman, a calendar, an engineering project, a prayer. There are festivals, but none that gamble on riot; dusk is dangerous enough. They will sing to the plateau. The song is low and full of vowel because mist likes that, and because you can carry a vowel across a sinkhole without teaching your own name to the returning echo. The very rich commission entire buildings to be tuned. You stand under the eaves in a damp wind and the mass murmurs. You can feel the work required in that hum, and you know you will pay for its upkeep in comb and time and the occasional argument with a cousin about whether the seventh overtone is rude.

Visitors mistake patience for passivity and tenderness for fragility. The plateau corrects them. Panic kills because it creates eddies and because it teaches your hands to grab in a place where grabbing blocks. Mistake kills because consequence is immediate here and thoughtful elsewhere. So the people move slowly unless running a child out of a gutter’s mouth. They speak softly unless calling to a roost. They will not tell you they are brave; there is no award here for what everyone must be to go to market and back.

You will see, if you stay through a season, how the bodies take on the place. Ankles become willowy and precise. The cartilage of the ear learns language. The thorax deepens subtly under oiled cloth, a long adaptation to air thin as suggestion that has been speeded up by training and diet and a youth spent breathing in workrooms where the walls sing about patience. Fingerpads shine. Teeth gloss. Joints slow a little in the morning and heat sweetly when the rails hum. People move with the city: swallow, pool, run, fog. Lovers lean their heads together not because faces like faces but because the hum resolves to a kind of privacy between skulls. The newborn smell like warm water over metal.

Who lives here are the mats and the things that answer them, animals that fly through a soup made of breath, cattle that are walls you can pet and that will turn at a painted line, human beings whose morality is not in a book first but in the books they keep of drips and debts of flow. The city is a choir made of appetite and discipline. It does not hurry; its residents teach themselves to be weather, and meanwhile they hammer and glaze and baffle and salt, they work, they listen, they sing their gutters true.

If you live long enough, you will feel it in your bones the way a violin makes a shoulder remember itself. One day you will put your ear to a wall—your wall, stone you coaxed into the right amount of gloss—and hear years where time sounds like water licking a lip. You will count back from the night a condenser field changed its hum. You will know who died in fog there and who married and the day a herd went by. You will make of that knowing something practical; you will carve notches; you will mend a wire. And when you leave a room, you will set your hand briefly on the cornice and you will feel it answer, and the plateau will have taught you, without cruelty, to be a thing that sings back.
