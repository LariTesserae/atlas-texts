They are the ones who learned to live by pressure. If you had to answer quickly—if someone shouted over the roar, Who lives here?—you would be tempted to say people, because they have children and markets and old houses, because they laugh, because they are complicated and kind and sometimes mean. But that is the least of it. What lives here are bodies tuned into instruments, skins that can read, minds paced by a long note. They are citizens of a throat.

They carry themselves differently than the slow-water folk you might know. Their mouths are small and tight-lipped when not in use, more to keep in their own softness than to keep out anything else. Their ears are not pierced by rings but by clever shells of woven whisker-fiber that cup bone and fold sound away, things they can twist open like a shutter when they need the room’s message and clamp shut when they must think unshaken. Their hair is often cropped close or braided into short, heavy cables laced with resin beads that darken to copper with salt—a way to keep weight low and chatter off. Some have shaved brows, because nothing that catches the wind is casual. Some have extra studs of cartilage along the ridge of ear and jaw, a natural bracing that the wind has chosen for them.

They wear their eyes like tools, not ornaments. Eyelids are taught to blink with resin rather than water: a thin varnish scumbled from shade-kept plant saps is thumbed across the lashes at first light-peak and again after the Hollows. It does not glisten, exactly; it dims the glare to a bearable paper and seals the sting. Even their skin looks finished—wiped with a bloom that is not grease but some compromise they have made with the place, a faintly fragrant slickness rubbed thin over shins and knuckles and cheekbones, a thing that keeps the air from asking too many questions of them. Their hands are thickened along the outside edges where fingers brush bristle-screens all day; their fingernails are worn elliptical by the practice of making little cup-shadows for conversation. Their soles are book-pages. Calibrated callus grows there in whorls, trained since infancy by dancing on brackets and walking narrow ribs, and you can read a person by the script of their feet: how often they’ve crossed the main channel, whether they trust the lee of baffles or cut straight through eddies, what kind of garden they tend, where they stand during Surge Hours.

They don’t name themselves anything you can translate simply. If you asked and they decided you were worth the cost of a close exchange, they might press their laugh-lines to your temple, teeth barely touching your skull, and speak a sequence of chords through bone that means something like the Held or the Leeward, People of the Instrument, Those Who Remember the Right-Hand Knot. But mostly, if they have to give a word, they will tap three fingers on a rail and then one, a code that in their system is just home.

Their garments are noncommittal to wind. Tunics are short and cut from mats of fine bristle or from woven whisker-silk, a cloth that has weight like chain but moves like hair. Sleeves carry slats of ceramic sewn in at certain angles that throw little eddies of hush across the arms—useful if you want to draft a private place to think. Capes are not for warmth but for geometry: a panel of louvered vanes hung with pins so you can dial its effect on the air around you, make a blunted crescent of calm that follows you along a footbridge. The dressier among them wear bristle-fringe twisted into very narrow harps along hems and belts; you can hear them coming in the Hollows, a travel of small shivers.

They wear their language too. Everyone’s shoulders are busy. Every important message that is not pressed into your mouth is hung on you. Great collars of knots drape from clavicle to bicep, each knot a clause, each weight a tense. The heaviest cloaks are the archivists’—capes of woven cords hung with resin-lumps of old names and decisions, a bureaucracy you can literally feel. Lovers cut their hair into one cord and braid it together, and their pair-knot is a thing like a vow only more practical: it tells you where to anchor lines if one of them falls. Children’s backs are tied with play: bells that cannot ring, feather-panels that make friendly buffers, little vanes to teach them which way is lee.

They do not swap breath to talk unless it is very, very important. A plea, a prayer, a silly thing to say to someone you love—all of these are mouth-to-ear, cupped like a lamp, teeth resting like a spoon inside the curve of skull, voice pointed not to air but through bones. Everyone’s cheekbones are shiny from being touched. Everyone’s jaws know the measure of their friends. They have a hundred other conversations going at once, though: finger-alphabets they drum through rails, ankle-codes passed line-to-line across a bridge, palm-emphases on a post that say I will go, I am back, Wait for the Hollow. Their palms are tools of syntax.

There are lines among them, yes, but not lines of blood that trump everything else. Kinship follows sound. If your birth-breath rose during a tenor-thrum, your first sleep, your first quell of panic at the world’s noise happened under that frequency; the little flutes in the tenor quarter will always sound like home to you, and it is likely you will gravitate there. But you can be adopted by any tone, and many are. A person who works in the quenching-kilns might carry the weight of bass in their day, but sleep in the piccolo racket of the higher ledges because their spouse was born to that part. Clans have crests, but they are not banners, they are baffles: each group’s signature pattern of slats and lashes that throws just their kind of shade. They mark themselves not with colors but with calm.

Their work is the same everywhere in the city because the city is one work: to be present without breaking what the room wants to do. Some are growers of harmonics. They go into the thick forest and, with resin-combs and blunted chisel-knives, they braid the glassy plants into each other, train an elbow-high plate to catch a little more dust, shave a hoarfrost edge to narrow a tone. They don’t prune for beauty you or I would sense at first but for the room’s listening. They cut beads of sap when the light surges, pinch and coil and string them like fond sugar until they firm into sheets and rods, ceramics and varnishes and food. The best food is made on fog-weeps, those rare seasons when the plants gather a tang from the chamber’s breath and give it back as syrup—those harvests crack between your teeth, make your tongue taste the word electricity.

Some are quenchers. They live along the kilns where mineral dust is pressed not by hands but by wind. You can know them by the way they hook a calf behind a brace without thinking, by the dry, fine powder that glows in the seams of their clothes. They heat without fire; their furnaces are voices arranged in stone. They cast rods and pin-plates by wedging molds in the lee of strongest beats and letting pressure do what gravity does in another world. Their tools are hammers with heads of compressed resin, scrapers of chipped ceramic worn smooth by years of hush. Metal they do not trust. If you show them a gleaming bar from outside, they will set it down like a live thing and weight it with a stone. Metal sings not at your will but at its own, and here that is a species of treason.

Some tend the boundaries. The grate-end has its own people, jaw-muscled and serious, who call themselves Tooth-Wardens with a humor they do not show lightly. They keep the mouth-net clear of things that would rattle and catch. They teach children to be unafraid of the ribs of black teeth, to step between and touch the big wind with a flat hand for a second, to feel old fear go out of them. The slit-end has the Edgekeepers, a quieter lot, guardians of brightness. They replace louvers with unpitying patience, clean the slots with feather-stalks, mend the panels that throw light in sheets across the chamber. They are the ones most likely to have seen outside—a fact that folds their faces into rumor. Everyone goes to one of them at some hour of their life and asks what is beyond, and the Edgekeeper takes your face in their hands and says, More brightness and what might be a moving mirror. Most are not satisfied with that. A few are.

There are the Arp-Takers too, a kind of hunter who does not kill. If an arp—the pale, jointed creature that moves like a hand through hair—goes still and its plates stop shifting their song, the Arp-Takers track what the creature fed on to see if a wrong quiet has been laid somewhere. Then they free the plates from the shell, thread them with whisker-silk, hang them in corners that need calming. Everybody knows not to take arp-skin hot; it will play you wide. Everybody knows to leave something shaped like gratitude nearby anyway.

Children here are not raised to be still; they are raised to be exact. Their cradles are hammocks of thick cords shock-hung to rhythms their parents choose—soft, low beats that make the baby’s organs settle into the communal tempo. There is as much affection as in any place, but it is more head-pressed and hip-rest than kiss-loud. At three, a child will be allowed to run out into a Hollow for the first time and lie down in a near-silence so good it feels like falling into a story; at seven they learn the left-hand tether-knot and are allowed to help in the harvest along the garden’s lip during surge; at thirteen, during a high calm, they are blindfolded and walked the length of a footbridge by their own soles, every shift read through callus, every misstep arrested by an elder’s thumb. Adults remember the day they were taught the right-hand knot, the day the tether-length was cut to fit their stride and no longer the cautious standard. It is more than a day of competence. It is the city saying you are real to us now; your pull on a line is weight we count.

There are games, because there is always light enough and the roar, though honest, does not forbid delight. Young people stack baffles in delicate wrong ways to see how close to a bad note they can come without calling disaster, then unstack them and laugh. Racing is a matter of timing more than speed: four or five sleds on their bellies in the channel, each held by two lines from either side, each released and hauled slack on a beat, surfing the dull emptiness, twisting like fish, slaloming through invisible ridges of force. You will see a grandparent on a ledge during the Hollows, hands folded, whole shoulders shaking with the memory of when their knees used to let them do that. You will also see them smile as a glider worm hums on their wrist, a kind of bracelet-child they mind when their bones ache. They call the worm by name, and it curves into the hollow of their arm and makes a room there.

The law is not a set of commandments posted anywhere; it’s four lines repeated to toddlers until the lines feel like bones:

Sound is weight. Liquids are rare and hot. Erosion is appetite. Brightness is a breath.

The breaking of law is not policed by badges but by the room. Still, they have their troubles. Some people want too much private calm and will hang baffles that make dead spots in places not meant for that. Quiet in the wrong corner kills an arp and fouls a harvest. Others, especially the newer Right-Hand knots for whom every day is the day after a near-accident, will lash too tight and break a shared terrace’s give. There are arguments about whether a beam that fell should be allowed to be swallowed by the thicket or hauled out by twenty tuckered bodies to be repurposed. There are families who hoard Hollows and others who give them away. Nothing here can smolder for long; the light is too honest. They settle matters the only ways they can: by load and by knot. If you have wronged a corner, you carry weight for a season. If you have broken a rule you did not understand, they press your face to a post, and the oldest person touches your jaw, and they tell you the reason through your bones, a secret only because it can’t be heard any other way.

Their old age is not long, but it is deep. Elders turn from fast work to the long tasks: plaiting the great shoulder-cloaks of record, setting up the long pendulums that let ladders be climbed in tune, weighing each new bristle-vaned idea in their fingers until they can say, This shape will be pried loose or, This will find a lee. They nap face-down with their hands under their cheeks and wake up full of certain histories you can only learn by sleeping in a place that hums. When they die, they do not rot. They are unfastened with care and laid in a moderated garden, the one tended for this very kind of singing. The plants do not eat them like animals, not chewing, but lean and flex across them for a long time, trade salts with them, pull water from them that would be lost anyway, and raise on their ribs and wrists amber beads like little moons. When the beads harden and stop brightening, they are cut like any harvest and strung as weights on cloaks or on baffles that keep a street safe. A grandmother’s bead can hold shut a rattling shutter for a generation. A love’s bead can hold closed a mouth that would say something it cannot take back during a Surge Hour. It is not morbid, it is what the place believes in instead of graves.

They think about outside the way everyone everywhere thinks about outside. In great quiet times, two heads will lean together and one will say a story into the other’s skull about a place where the air is heavy with water and it falls from the top without being coaxed and you can drink it and it stays in you, about a place where the brightest thing moves on a schedule you did not negotiate with your roof. The other will press their hand to the first’s sternum and drum a laugh. There are long travelers, of course. Some have taken sleds and powders and brave little baffles to other rooms the pipe may connect to. They come back with soft, pitted metal and very straight or very round stories. They bring back shells beaten into weights, new whisker-seeds that grow into stiff-stalked plants that can be taught to throw better hush. Mostly they bring back the knowledge that there is not only the one chamber. But that was always true. The city is full of rooms within rooms within rooms made of air and listening.

In among them live the precise few forms that passed whatever test the place gives. The arps, yes: ambulatory instruments that comb static out of air and make it into food. The glider worms that unfurl like silk and ride pressure like a person rides a thought. A few tiny creatures that are not seen so much as felt, a tickle of collected dust along a heel, little white mites that gnaw at the sharp hardened edge of resin and drop crumb the gardeners sweep into their mouths because it’s good salt. The predator here is silence, embodied sometimes in a dome-eater found later, smooth and starving, a misreading that the room corrects by indifference.

They have holidays. Not by the roll of any planet but by the pattern of the place. There is a day, once in a long while, when the brightness goes lean and older folks remember, and everyone tethers every loose thing and the gardens are left uncut because the pressure changes are odd, and the entire city stands barefoot and rests their palms to the floor. It is very loud then, louder than a person alone could bear, but not one of them is alone. They stand like a braid. The roar swells and passes and, afterward, someone always says with a shoulder-squeeze, You felt that note? and someone else says, I knew it when I was born. Children climb a ladder with a pendulum and sit up in its rhythm and look down at their parents and grandparents shimmering in the doorway of calm their baffles made.

Ask, then, who lives here, and you will be told this: the people who stopped yearning for walls and asked air to hold them. The ones who put down their voice because there was a better one around them, and picked it up again to put in one another’s bones. The city that takes its own measure every day because it must. The citizens of a long, bright, hungry throat that plays them, and that they can play a little back, with care and with callus and with love, provided no one insists on quiet.
