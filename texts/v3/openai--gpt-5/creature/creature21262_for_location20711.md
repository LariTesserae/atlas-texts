They live by the hum. Before sight there is pressure: a gentle pry in the jaw and a measured thrum in the ribs that says the lungs of the basin are turning, that currents are abridging the distance to elsewhere. The ones who have grown up inside this cadence carry it the way gulls carry wind—an unexamined constant. Newcomers flinch at the reversals until they learn the descending chime and the taste-change that means let go, drift, make for the eddies. Then they cease to be visitors. They belong.

The largest lives wear sonar like heraldry. They were born in wilder waters but their calves learned their first syllables here, tuned around engine fundamentals so that the mesh of listeners scattered through the ribwork will hear and slow the world. They have schedules—God, do they have schedules—and those polyrhythms of pump and song have crept into their culture. Mothers mark the hour in long chords that find at last a kind note with a turbine stack three valleys over, and calves learn that when the floor brightens just a touch and a second chime shivers the lateral line, you bank left and a corridor opens. They ferry, and if you could ask them what they ferry it would not be romance or myth but saturations, clouds of cold-fed life carried on their backs and in their wakes from airbrights to dim vaults so those vaults do not starve. Ship-whales, tenders in a network, they travel the same blue routes so often that partial maps form in their heads: a comb-tooth wall with a generous lee becomes a grandmother; a warmth spill off a heat exchanger becomes a kind uncle, to be visited when hunger is not otherwise met. They know the names we have for junctions—Eddysix, The Cusp—without knowing the alphabet, because at Eddysix the pumps say soh to the whales’ me and the long flows intersect with a taste like iron in the mouth.

They are scarred not so much by teeth as by history. Flat white lines from fishing gear the basin will never allow in; a faint puckered pattern from propellers that will never enter these volumes because propeller wakes are not in policy. Their new marks are less violent: paint rubbed to shine where a curious flank touched a panel patterned to invite pecks, a dusting of phosphor grains on calloused lips from nosing hazard bands that only they can see properly. They gather during the programmed nights and sing a register reserved for this place, low as an anchor dropped in slow motion. Some of them have learned the maintenance tags—simple chords that mean stand by, proceed, wait—and fly them in mimicry as a kind of joke. Once, a matriarch masqueraded as a cleaning swarm for an afternoon, stringing together the four notes that mean small mouths incoming; she watched through amused monochrome eyes as seals stiffened in the lee and made a show of being perfectly unsurprised.

Seals live here like boys in a machine shop. The noise is nothing to them. They hang in the thermal stillness just above the deep courses, one flipper cocked for minimal drag, masquerading as lint until something interesting happens. They are connoisseurs of wake, braiding ladders of light with ladders of pressure the way children run the backs of theater seats. They know every rung on the big intake’s lip where a rivulet of current peels off just so and will fling you if you tuck and roll. They surf the valve-songs: when a distant gate clanks into seating, a microfront of pressure sweeps down a gallery, and there is an instant of joy available in which a seal straightens and simply rides. Their coughs are dry and comic, registering skepticism at glass and the habit of false sky, but they have made this sea’s grammar into games. They can fake the top-note of a reversal chime well enough to watch mackerel pour out of a slot mouth like silver weather into a seal’s mouth like weather’s purpose. A human operator in a little pill with hands will sometimes find an old dog or a short-faced rookie lying flat against a hull window to feel the microvibrations of the LIDAR spray in its whiskers, eyes half-lidded in bliss.

Between these two sizes ripple citizens built of compromise. Schools slip and whiten, breaking from gauze into arrowheads and back again when the chimes descend. They have schedules the way commuters have schedules and gossip the way commuters gossip: here is a slot that chews, a mast that blooms green around the fifth hour of second light, a crack in a comb wall where a spill of hatchery shrimp comes through slightly off-time because a bearing squeaks somewhere upriver. Mackerel queue, jaws easy-opened, to drink a stream blown from an elbow of ductwork. Jacks and long-jawed things—wolves and knives by other names—learn the mouthfeel of the flowlines and stand to one side of the bait machines like old men near an elevator, ready to step in. Some predators have invented new kinds of patience for a basin that holds so much airless distance; sharks of the cooler families fall into long, energy-slim swimming that reads like thoughtfulness even if it is only math, mirroring the pumps’ low-gear on the weeks when grids above grow fat with sun and wind. They ride fields as much as flows, yawning their heads to feel the skinny prickle of cathodic lines through the jelly of their faces; magnetoreception turns the basin into a map of taste.

Octopuses live here like thieves who have taken a house under renovation as their own. They do not pretend the girders are rock. They prize the detachable in the way clever hands prize wallets and maybe also in the way honest hands prize tools. Snap-rings and duffel clips, washers, lengths of thread—they stuff them into den mouths fashioned in the protectively eddied backs of fins and braces, and build doorways that clatter-dance at the pressure whisper of anything larger than a pollock. They are students of appetite management. They know not to taste the glistening beads of filter media when it rains glitter through the blue; they have watched their neighbors eat that snow and pass untroubled because calcium phosphate is not calcium from bones. Some of the squid have taught one another maps of quiet: a grove of microtextured cups strung in a village on a cable four hundred meters from an intake, reachable only when the big pumps lag by exactly this much after the second tone; a thermal slick marked by a row of what look like gold teeth, pressing warmth through steel from a maintenance node, good for brooding if you can time your rise to the moment the arrays go to night-mode and the chaff of hatchery shrimp falls like grace. A few have learned chords. They hoard sounds the way they hoard rings. An old female, with a mottled arm torn short and regrown at a funny angle, can reproduce the six-note signature of a survey drone well enough to make it hesitate near her den; she uses the pause to ghost upward and pluck a lace of ephyrae budding from a conduit.

Crustaceans make a forest’s worth of noise in the dark. The built here doesn’t creak and settle, but claws do. Barnacles are not permitted, but the urge to be a barnacle still lives in squat lobsters and amphipods, which upholster the seams of flow-shapers in beadwork lives, grazing the biofilm mats the program allows like teenagers hitting the salad bar after a fast-food childhood. Crabs big as dinner plates squat at slot mouths like toll collectors, feeling with hairs the subtle accelerations that mean protein will arrive in three, two, one. Shrimp rise in rains when the hatchery lines open and fall again in silver prayers. Their molts drift in corner-eddies like confetti. Their parasites and cleaners move with them, spectral and exact. There are isopods that ride aft of the bigger fish’s eyes, shaded from the light that comes in secondhand like canyon light and trailing like commas in a sentence the basin never finishes.

The smallest lives are everywhere the rules have made room for them. A green is braided to the exactness of a felt in only three addressed lawns per kilometer. It does not creep out of bounds because nothing is allowed to creep out of bounds for more than a day, but while it stands it blooms with the quiet industry of a thousand thousand factories, bubbles seeding its blades so that the microfoam looks like dust, so that light trembles just a hair by the panels and you can tell from sixty meters that this page of photosynthetic lesson is still being taught. Invisible middens of bacteria do the polishing work we used to assign to words like spring. They look, if you could see them, like night markets: nitrifiers hawking to denitrifiers across narrow alleys of gradient, shoppers with molecules to spend. The drippers above them time their alms with a clock no one notices until something goes wrong, and then the alms’ absence rings like a missing beat in the bassline, and an hour later out on the ribs a crawler beetle the size of a calf will plod to a node and change a filter with patient care, and the neighborhood will exhale.

Machines live here with the unselfconsciousness of goats. They graze. They swarm. They molt in designated pens. The maintenance fish that are not fish are tangible as rules: ovals with plush mouths that whisper over roughened patches like felt shoemakers, leaving behind no footprint but the sheen of compliance. They have a pastoral of their own. They sleep docked nose to nipple along a beam that hums, the way swallows line a wire, and when the chime descends they lift like a shoal, peel, roll, and arrow into the flowline to be carried to tomorrow’s grazing while their shared voice in the water—those tonal tags, those government chords—advertises their direction and intent. Smaller than them, pea-sized motes slip and settle and kiss sensors clean with acid that is gentle as breath to the skins they are allowed to touch and fatal to the skins they are told to find distasteful. They are corralled the way we corral bees by the taste of home. They come home smelling like nitrate. Their batteries are milked. They are loosed again.

Greater crawlers live like saints among the deep spines, jointed and slow, fond of duty. They carry with them tales in thick umbilicals that sing when pulled taut. They keep councils in shadowed bays while their oil warms to working viscosity, and their councils are about voltage and risk and the right sequence for the releasing of a seized bolt and they have nothing to do with predation or hunger or mating and yet those assemblies have a life of their own. The serpents long as trains pass unseen in their locked channels. They are resident ghosts, pressing their bodies of tanks and valves between walls like throats, carrying thought from one node to another in the medium of temperature. Their vespers hum is everywhere and nowhere, and fish have learned that where their harmonics flare, the water tastes of future warmth or future cold; migratory sheets of silver will dip a little when a serpent passes by overhead because the roof is about to leak heat into the night.

If humans can be said to live here, it is in the oblique tense, like an absentee landlord’s dream of tenancy. They appear in the way weather appears: punctual, slippery, their tracks readable for perhaps an hour in ridges of turbulence down an avenue while smaller fish play collect-souvenirs with their pressure sheds. The pills with hands have fingers that are kind, most days. They bear boredom, contentment, and a repetitive tenderness for gleaming beadlines and struck plates. They leave and the animals add their movement to the basin’s alphabet: a particular whirr; a chord that means something heavy but tractable is coming through; a smell like warmed electronics that means the ceiling dim will deepen twenty points in five minutes. Above the ceiling, humans are the sun and the moon. Below, they are episodic comets with manipulators, and some seals will ride the wake of a pill for a kilometer just to see if it will cough again.

Rules live here in the way weather lives in any place, and those who thrive have learned to be weatherwise. Every body in motion carries a voice for the water to hear: harmonic signatures that let the small choose open water or hole, that let the big claim right-of-way. Some of those voices have been actively taught. Sharks with nervous systems tuned to find dinner with fields have discovered that a particular repeating phrase in the background is the voice of a corridor where their gifts are best deployed. We have coaxed them to prefer these patrols, not to defang them but to harmonize appetite with pump economy. Seal mothers have learned to spank pups’ flanks when a low-note hazard band thrums along a wall; the children bolt and dwell in safe rounding. Jacks know edges are kind where the program thinks hanging will happen. Wrasses find their pecking panels the way pigeons find statues, and a year in you can read their little civil quarrels in the paint that has been abraded into satiny maps.

There are places famous the way watering holes and highway diners are famous, and fame here is a kind of gravity. Eddysix is a cone of leisure punched out of a working sea, the leeward side of a decision the basin makes every nine hours when the tide engines reverse into what we name with seasons we no longer consult: ebb, flood, slack. The Cusp is a cusp, moored to the idea of change: on the hour a pressure pulse from an appetites uptick in the east arrives as a cool kiss and a ripple in the pellet-rain and for the space of a held breath even the most predatory sensibilities quiet, and a leopard-bodied shadow hangs mid-blue like a held question, and amphipods tumble and right themselves with a patient practiced hilarity because what else is there to do when an entire world inhales? Things come to these places to be touched by change, because change here, though engineered, is also life’s breath. A squid that will not lay until the second reversal finds you there. A whale that ferries something more than plankton—call it a neighborhood’s mood—passes and throws your heart sideways in your chest without meaning any harm.

Not everyone is on the program. There is a category of resident the etiquette calls feral because it is easier to file than to understand. A cleaning swarm that found the seam between two subroutines and decided the seam was a road, now migrates every three weeks in an arc the designers did not intend, and in that arc there is now a tiny season, a little dry followed by a little green, and wrasses have written a feast day into their year. A patch of photosynthetic felt on a maintenance strut, misaddressed during a night when the ceiling dimmed wrong because of an above-storm’s peculiar ask, took root for thirty hours and drew in its wake a village of filter-feeders that only existed that once and ended up sloughed into a recycler, and the octopus with the crooked arm still keeps three of the little threaded cups from that brief village deep in her den and drapes an arm over them when the arrays sing in long chords.

If there is a single creature most at home in this engineered enormity, it is habit. Habit climbs ladders in light with seal bodies, habit takes the register of turbines into the cartilage of whale ears, habit drifts and eats and molts under drippers and knows the rain of numbered intakes is dinner. Habit learns the chords we play into the water and plays them back altered, for its own reasons, with its own jokes. The basin is young enough that pioneer temperaments predominate: generalists, opportunists, connoisseurs of edge effects and low-pressured lees. Some day there will be more beard on the world’s chin; the lizard-skin panels will all be slicked satiny by the administrative beaks of wrasses; someone will have died in a culvert, and the decomposers will have written that death into the water with a script as old as rain. Already the living and the built have blurred their seam. Internal waves ride isotherms with the inevitability of tide, and the tonal tags the trains sing gather small escort-fish as faithfully as any migrating herring ball once gathered gulls.

They live in the cold and are unbothered by it, in the loud and treat it as color, in the emptiness and make of it interval and rest. They live by schedules and chimes and make from these not strictures but weather to prefer or avoid. They live in laws written so that flow never strips fish, light never burns eyes, edges always round where a body would tarry. They live with us not as pets or subjects but as co-workers with very different job descriptions whose work makes our work work. They live in a sky that is not sky and do not miss stars they never saw. They live by taste and by touch and by a deeper sense we might call trust. They live in the water new every morning, not because a sun wakes it but because code and piston say it shall be new, and because life in its patience takes that newness and homesteads it until the homestead smells like sleep and home and always the hum.
