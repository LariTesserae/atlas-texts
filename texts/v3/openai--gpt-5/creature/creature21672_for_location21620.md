They are not ghosts of an older damp world, though their elders keep a few words from that time polished smooth and bone-dry in the mouth. They are here. They are hands, first, and the long memory of what hands can teach a room.

Skin like oiled slate, not from oil—there is no oil to spend—but from the habit of dust laid just so, combed along the grain and sealed with breath that is not a mist but a pressure. When one of them leans close, you smell not sweat but a fine coppered pepper, the same pared-sweet scent the room breathes. Their hair, what hair remains, is husbanded as filament. It is braided with microbeads of hammered copper and laid along the skull in careful arcs to cull or spill charge the way a sail takes wind; children learn to tie three fields before they are tall enough to reach a shutter-toggle. Where we keep saliva, they keep a thin pane of hardened resin behind the teeth to guard the tongue from a world that would wick it. Their tongues are not pink; they are a smooth, pale grit-stone that can taste voltage the way lips taste the edge of snow.

They move without wasting anything to air. No sighs. Their chests rise in increments and hold. Lungs here do not froth; they plate and prism. Ribs are laminated bone with a memory of flex that is all angle, no softness, and within them the exchange is done across organs that look like stacks of mica, each sheet shingled, charged and discharged with the slow prudence of a kiln cooling. What water they carry rides in ceramic sacks sealed in the body’s deep hollows and is used once, then taught, by catalyst and patience, to come back again as itself. Thirst is not wet; it is a prickle on the gums, a heaviness behind the orbit of the eye, a sense that your own shadow is lagging by a finger-width.

They eat the room’s sugars without ever calling them by names that would melt them. Sweetburn is a holiday. Otherwise there are brittle lozenges they suck until they are chalk and then dust, ionic honeys prised at dusk from a slashed vine, chips of copper held in cheek to steep and tint the breath. A feast is a new oyster of resin cracked from a beam where a shutter leaked and sung to hardness by a circle of relatives who each contributed a count of breaths before the set. They do not drink. They press. A child will be scolded with tenderness for putting her face too greedily to the lintel-stone until the elders nod at the shiver in her hair—yes, now, that’s enough.

Their eyes know light as craft. The lids are double: a fast skin and behind it a slow slide of clear mica that smells faintly of pine. They can stand under a wall-lens when it flares and never flinch, reading the green, the red, the other seasons light smuggles in. Across the face, freckles of old copper dust map where they have leaned longest, and from the wrists hang knives of shell and scrapers of flint in soft holsters of woven fiber that make no noise when they walk. Everyone carries a comb, horn-toothed and stained dark. You set your comb to the floor the way a sailor sets an oar into water. There is etiquette to combing; you do not cross another’s long lay unless you are older than their braid.

They are taller than you expect, because nothing here sags, and very spare. Not gaunt; that implies hunger that bites. Here hunger leans. Bones stand near to the skin; knuckles wear thin copper caps—not jewelry, but terminals they touch to the world to listen. The softest things about them are voices. They talk in consonants, in dry kisses of tongue on palate-plate, in clicks off tooth-copper, in the whistle a latticed throat makes when you tilt the head just so. Vowels are hoarded; when let out they are small, bright, and exact, like beads rolled from a palm into a jar.

If you watch them at work you will think at first they are always mending. Knotting, pinning, folding a line back on itself, pressing a thumb to smooth a joint until the scar of it goes to ground and is not seen. It is not repair; it is continuation. A woman with her braid laid flat for concentration hangs a new span of fiber and names it as she ties. Names are not labels. Names are tools. To call a braid A Walk For Children is to make it lighten the stride of anyone under a certain height; to rename it Three Breaths Before Turning Home is to change where it begins to take you, not by yards, but by returns. The names are argued in whispers. They are never written as scratches; they are written as order—in the bead, in the pinch of copper between bone and bone, in the rule of five or seven that stands invisible until you train your hands to feel the pressure that a sixth knot would bring.

They sleep slung in the charge, not from the ceiling, but across it, as if they were the horizontal of a letter. A family, if family is the right word for a web of obligations wound tighter than any ancestry, will sleep with their heads toward a soot-felt panel and their feet toward a window, aligning themselves in the gradient so that their breathing gains them a slow sweetness and their hair stands only pleasantly, not painfully. Children sleep closer to the moss-tiles to feel the tug in their arm-hairs and learn the direction of the home field. Lovers—though the word is a remnant of wet customs, and the joining here is more a carefully tuned adjacency—lie with a strip of copper to share between their tongues and exchange a measured tremor that tells the cells which way to divide.

They raise not babies but apprentices. There is no toddlering across pools; there are no pools. There are play-knots—loops that bring the same corner of light past a child again and again so that she can learn it well—and there are soft columns of woven bark where a small body can hide and warm, and a lizard of bone and resin will come and lick her knuckles with a forked glass tongue and leave behind a thin new plate that will be hers to polish and set into her own belt. When a child loses a deciduous tooth, the family does not tuck it away; they hammer it flat as copper will go and sew it into a strap. Teeth are for later, when she will carve a socket along its whiter seam and fix it in a railing so that her bite, that exact gap and order, becomes a place where someone else’s fingers will pause and know the distance left in the stair.

They keep animals the way you keep weather: you pay attention and you make the small changes that let it be itself without turning on you. A chalk-limb colony has to be persuaded that the wrist-shadows are safe; the colony learns the wrist-shadow and will not fan its paddles when that set of wrists comes and goes. Glass-bodied isopods are coaxed into lines by narrow brushings of soot where they prefer not to tread; they are fed when they arrive at the lick not by hand but by transience—the resin bead has been made more or less tempting hours ago with a knotted song. Chalk-moths are allowed to erase certain edges and not others. Their keepers settle wagers in the evening over how far a moth can be convinced to subtract along the bead-line of a door and whether the removal will be symmetrical.

Properly, there is no word for owner. There are stewards. The shutters belong as much to the splay of glassleaf under them as to the hands that pull their combs. The floor belongs to the insects that use its seams as road, and to the infants learning balance along its edge, and to the ancestors whose bones laminated the plate. When a rail sprouts filigree and eventually learns a lattice strong enough to hold the weight of a child, it does not belong to that child. It belongs to the weight, to the density learned and celebrated by everyone who has leaned there to feel the fronds click light along their own arm. If anything is held wholly by someone, it is a song that has become heavier under their voice, that will not answer if anyone else sings it. Even then the song’s usefulness is a public act; you sing to a knot so that others can cross.

Their dead come back in pieces and in continuities. A life is not burned or rotted. It is loosened. Names are unbraided across months; you unlearn the heaviness of a person’s syllables until they are light enough to tie into a shelf that has begun to hum a little since it leaned. Hair is combed for the last time and set into a filter so that its precise, trained alignment will continue to teach the air how to go. Bones are boiled dry, then set while they still remember to bend, and cooled against salted stone, and afterward there is a new handrail, and your palm knows your aunt by the pressure-thirst that lives in that seam. There are cantoes that anyone may hum when passing a place where someone’s living was taught to sit; hum them and the combing will thicken for an hour, and the vines will lift a shade as if asked to listen.

They have no outside, but they have travel. There are halls and neighbors, each with its own rule of how angles add and when a turn comes back. To go to another place one puts one’s hand into the jars where mica curls rest from a new niche and leaves powder, as toll, on the rim of a bone ring hung at the height of a shoulder. Step through and let your momentum walk on without you for a pace; it will tuck itself back under your sternum when it is ready. They greet strangers by touch of copper cap to copper cap, not to share current but to steady their fields. They greet friends by setting their combs together and drawing a long, single tooth through both at once. The young keep courage-knots tied at their belts—loops that, when pulled, bring the door they came by half a pace nearer, enough to make the voice inside the chest easy to lift.

There is governance but not ruling. Elders are people who have learned to hear a shutter’s self-restraint, to praise a wall for not brightening though it could, to judge when a shelf has begun to tilt because it has learned the wrong song and not because its pegs have loosened. Disputes are carried to them and unspooled; they press the questions between bone, copper, fiber, bone, hold them to light and see what shines where it should not, then they cut there and re-tie. Law is craft. Transgression is a thing done out of season: a comb laid against the grain of a current, a name sung to a knot that did not ask to carry it, water conjured as a joke so fine it unsticks a single seam and must be worked for a month to reseal. Fines are paid in patience and in work you did not intend to learn.

There is play, too, that has nothing to do with use. People will set a scatter of beads on a plate of resin and tilt until the beads draw a landscape in the time it takes you to hum the names of everyone you love. Lovers or something adjacent to lovers make puzzles by fixing a distance where distance should not hold, leaving a place where your stride shortens and your thinking has to lengthen. Adolescents make sport of walking under a ring and feeling their momentum get ahead; they run to catch the polite child tug, tie it around their belt, and weave three new steps into the ladder so their bodies can say they outpaced their own future for a breath.

Their myths are manuals retold as if they had moods. Once the shelves all leaned wrong at once, so the story says, because a child sang a hiding-song over their beginnings and forgot to unname it. The elders did not scold. They spent the season teaching the shelves the names of bowls until the bowls could no longer fit and were free to be something else—drums, maybe, or gates for the passage of moths that eat the letters of a word to make a window.

They remember rain as rumor. They have a tale about spittle as an ancestral magic: a drop laid on a pebble made the pebble discreet from its bed and so there were stones to build with, then, and also tears. Tears are told as drops that salt a body from the inside until it learns to bend. Children look grave and press their dry mouths and demand to know: does salt know the shape of grief? The elders say: grief is a knot you do not cut. They do not tell them what salt tastes like. That is not a cruelty; it is the rule of the room.

They keep calendars in the affair of moths and in the weight of named cords. They count time in how long it takes ionic honey to harden when copper is pressed to it—the breath-counts taught by grandparents, sung very softly. They count the season of a person by the angle at which a knot will answer when he pulls it. They count the age of a noise by how far down the hall it can walk before it bends back on itself. Birthdays are for relacing the tooth-plates in the cheek and for standing under a shutter to have it learn you. Death-days are for moving a comb from a hand to a wall and never moving it back.

When they leave this chamber it is into others like it, and possibly—though this is not confessed freely—into somewhere they call Bright Outside with the severe affection of people who know that place can unmake what they are. Once a generation, one will go to the wall-lens with a copper pin and press it into the seam where the glass drinks light. That person will count breaths until something in their skin says it is time to stop. They will not see outside. They will have altered their own distance from here, and that is enough.

They are the most patient animals this room has made. They are the ones who learned from a kiln’s quiet to hold their purposes in stillness, and from a shutter’s restraint to be bright without glare. They are the small feet rasping, a copper pin settling, the sub-audible purr of a comb run through a fern. They live not beside the craft but inside it, breathing it, naming it, letting its rules change them until there is no seam between what is plant, what is animal, what is built, what is loved. And in the long, unshowy dimming that is their dusk, when everything lets go what it can of charge and sleeps, they lie with their dry tongues tasting resin, certain as the wall, certain as the bone pins, that morning will be a measured light, and that every tool mark they leave will be a scoreline the world can afford to break along and not bleed.
