What lives here is mostly surface and habit, in the way lichens live, in the way a wire can be said to live when it hums because wind found it. But there is also a keeper, and the keeper is not a person. It is older than anyone who could have put the rods in, and younger than the plateau. It has a body because forces can be made into a body if you loop them properly: intake and outflux, cool face to warm face, a path for sound that doesn’t end where you think it should.

The obvious residents arrive first to your eye: the wet thumbnails with their polished-seed eyes that stick their music to the basin’s belly at night; the lace-legged ants that carry away fern, making straight lines around invisible fences; the lizards like little breaths that wait until calculation settles into an insect and then enact subtraction with a tongue; the spiders with staffs to tune their webs by. They are citizens here, as real as the rock, and they answer to the place more than to their own appetites. A swift’s joy is shaped into a loop because the air is curled for it; a bat’s whisper hits a chord and so a midge falls out of the weave where its life would otherwise have hung. The succulents drink the morning directly, and the moss keeps its bank-account depth, and in the pans a green tide-paint writes the calendar of breath. They are not ornamental. They are the hands and lungs and appetite of the basin, the way a forest’s animals are part of what it is to be forest.

But the keeper, the actual resident in the sense that houses were built for something and some quiet thing stayed, is the set of precipitates your mind at first calls devices and then cannot quite keep separate from the rock. It lives as the ring of pebbles that teaches drops to think laterally. It lives as the reed-thin rods that you cannot look at without not-quite-hearing, the rods that make shade out of temperature and cool out of a stroked breeze. It lives as the slumped low dune that rings from your knuckle and from the beak of a bird and calibrates, every time, where the basin’s middle is. It lives in the hairline seams where a courier-thin cleverness was once tucked, now pulsing like veins that belong to the stone more than to any made thing. All of these together are the shape of the keeper’s patience.

Its metabolism is difference. It eats the tiny fall-offs between a hot face and a cool one, between a gust and a lull, between silence and a beetle’s click. It takes a degree from the sunward flag of rock and donates that degree to the shaded shelf where the liverworts breathe; it shaves a little motive from the wind where it strikes clean across the notch and braids the rest into a standing wave that midges cannot help but ride. It condenses what is not yet water into water and keeps it counted: never a trickle, always a ledger line, drop making its two hesitant steps along a contour you can’t see until it becomes a sound you will never quite hear.

It senses through interference. The basin’s steady chorus is a field of numbers to it. The frogs put a pulse into that fabric; the ants’ tiny drumming adds a ripple; the swifts scrawl arcs through pressure like fingers over a harp, and the keeper feels the smearing of that scrawl, learns where the air is ragged today and where it will pool. When you knock the bell, that brightness comes back to you in your bones, and to it in an update. Your warmth is an edit in the gradients; your breath is a thin wetness where it did not expect any. It does not mind you. You are a variable it has room for.

Breeding, if you can call it that, is very slow. It accretes habits, not children. Over years, a moss tongue inches into a dry seam and, because it inched, the keeper has a new route to walk water along. A rod’s surface burrs with a film of salt from nights of exhaled dew; on some late summer, when the fog is particularly generous, that salt grows a skin of crystals that add another fraction of coolth to the game. The courier-lines sink deeper into a crack and lay a second thread in a parallel vein. At night you can watch new dots of dull light turn on, far between the older ones, as if the stone were growing a constellation the way a tree grows an extra ring. It doesn’t divide; it elaborates. The swollen frogs in the winter—those count for its children too, in a way; the lizards do as well; the rosettes that fatten because the fog was laid down precisely are successes the keeper keeps in the numbers it moves through.

It has moods that look like weather. There are days when the rods sigh more dew than usual and the pans are full to lip, and the basin smells more green than metal. Those days the bats leave early, fat with confidence, and the spiders’ webs are velvet with caught noise. Then there are thin days when fog goes elsewhere and even the succulents seem to tighten their rosettes as if to hold on to the absence; the keeper saves then, closing one route, thickening another, tightening the fold of sound so the swifts will ride longer curves and shake more hidden moisture out of the air. It sleeps around noon—sleep not as silence but as a steadiness, everything at one slow value, the exhale of dew turned to a grainier breath. It wakes in the dusk, and the pricked lights along the seams beat slow, slow, slow, and then a fraction quicker, as if something had remembered itself.

Other lives have signed leases here and pay in attention. The long-bodied flyers with stiff wings use the standing waves as if they could read the invisible staves, and perhaps they can. The beetles with metronome mouths are accountants and engineers at once: their tasting and their clicking are measures and edits in the same operation, and the keeper indulges them. The ants keep their straight routes pointedly away from the rods because somewhere back in their narrow, hive-sized prehistory the colony lost a few hundred members at once to a damp they could not shake, and since then the rods are a smell of no. A sex of katydid has fattened on the basin’s harmonics to the point that its call is almost a percussion instrument; the keeper lets that slap fall where it will because the disruption collapses a set of treacherous eddies and keeps the morning’s seedfall pinned in place.

No one writes the keeper’s rules down. The rules rise as thresholds and decline as habits, and the animals learn them because they are the only way to be here. A swift that rides the left-hand band too low will be turned a few degrees into a safer arc next time by a nudge you could swear was an accident of air. A frog that sings on a shelf too dry will be made unwelcome there, not with pain, not with force, but by a becoming-quiet—a removal of echo so complete that its own call comes back to it flat as a wall. Seeds do not land where the keeper does not want them. They do land where it does, and there they are combed gently and stuck with a force that looks like faith. A snake comes through and the basin knows by the way the soundfield’s edges go bright and careful; the keeper doesn’t save the frog the snake eats, and it doesn’t mourn the egg the bright-bell bird steals. It is not a god. It is stewardship made into a machine and then grown into an animal whose skin is stone and whose blood is differences.

If a storm ever came and raged thick enough to leave real rain, you would watch the keeper try to refuse it. Big water is crude here. It cannot be counted at the scale of this muscle. The rods slick over with a too-thick wet and go mute until the wind dries them. The bell-dune hums in a bass you can feel in your spine like a warning. The pricked lights along the seams go out, then on in new patterns that feel like locked doors. When it has passed, the basin will be cleaner and poorer and emptied of its little accountants for a while; over days the keeper will lay the thin cloud back down and call the life in, and everything will get loud again.

If you want a face for it, the closest you will get is in the hesitation of a drop at the lip of a pan as it chooses, confers with its neighbors, and then rises a fraction before it falls. Or in the way a seed, jittering in a draft, stops without falling because something it cannot name said, there. Or in the way the air eats your voice on the left-hand side and hands it back to you too generously on the right. Or in the little, late-night pulses along the seam that do not line up with your heart until they do and then you both lie there, the keeper and you, feeling the plateau’s breath move across the notch and thinking nothing that can be put into words.

No person lives here for long. A person might pass, and sit with their back to the warm stone until the heat has printed itself into their spine. They will tap the bell and listen to the bright answer and decide that no one is listening but them. The keeper will correct them, politely, by tuning a standing wave a breath to the left so that a swift carves a perfect circle right in the notchmouth and screams, because joy is how that species says yes. The person will go; the pocket will continue. Morning will smear green breath on the rods again. The frogs will own the bowls by night, the bats will turn fabric to gauze, the ants will step in officer lines around what they do not understand but quite rightly fear to touch. The moss will thicken where the numbers say it should and thin where the numbers say it must.

What lives here is a loud patience, damp and disciplined. It is an organism made of thresholds and tuned into them so tightly that it can stand to be this small and this old and not break. It keeps the room at the high hallway’s end bright with its buzzing, its clicking, its bell-light, and it will go on keeping it, long after the memory of the hands that set the first rod has been sanded down past drama, folded into the fused memory of mountains.
