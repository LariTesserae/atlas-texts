What lives here is one thing wearing a million faces, and none of those faces is an eye.

Call it the Plain because to name it a plant is to make it small. Call it the Felt because it is a thickness that replaced sky with itself. It is a single body braided from many bodies, an organism that never learned the trick of standing apart, and so it turned the whole horizon into an organ.

It lives by gradients it makes and keeps. Where you might expect branches or fronds there are functions. It has grown condensers, not leaves—broad, cool-veined faces that talk water out of air and then drink their own sentences. It has grown ribs and vaults that compute weight and angle, share torque like a catechism, and never let a drop fall where it is not wanted. It has grown tongues that are a patience more than a shape, easing dew inward one bead at a time, because haste wastes pressure, and pressure is money here.

Its nerves are voltages born of flex, signals made when a membrane changes its mind. Every time a sheet bows to load or unbends from it, tiny charges slide along crystal-stiffened trabeculae, a slosh of piezoelectric syllables carrying decisions inward. The talk is slow compared to a heart but perfect compared to a mountain. There are pacemaker plazas: knotworks of cartilage-bright tissue near the brine sumps that count in differences of salinity, in whispers of redox. There are arbiter-columns where vertical gradients are argued by pore and polarity until a verdict of sweetness or salt is reached and the sap goes on its appointed way.

It has lungs that never saw light. Not breathers of air but breathers of contrast: galleries of paired electrodes sunk into the old sea where the mat dips down to kiss it, letting electrons creep between them like monks along cloisters, generation after generation of patient steps. In those porous pillars live guilds that are not the Plain but belong to it as mitochondria belong to an animal. Bacteria and archaea are housed and fed and leashed with exquisite cruelty. Sulfur whisperers, iron biters, nitrate brokers: consortia stack in layers like the stops of an organ, each chamber tuned to a fraction of a pH unit, a quarter-degree of warmth, a skim of hydrogen the Plain afforded them because the ledger said it could. They do the forbidden chemistry, the fierce steps with ugly intermediates, behind stone-thick baffles that fizz politely. They are tenants and tools. They propagate, mutate, try, and are culled. Their cities are replumbed as soon as their function drifts. They are kept, which is to say they are loved without illusions.

It has stomachs and refineries that smell of wet lime and iron. In its distillation trees, sap climbs stairs that are steps of size and charge; each ring keeps a different cut the way an old clerk keeps a perfect hand. Sugars here, salts there, isotopes parted so gently you would swear they cooperated. The columns remember their assignments for centuries. If the trace gas in the air changes by one part in a thousand, a far-off arbor adds one more turn to a helix of pores and that is enough.

It has hands—millions of them—though none would look like a hand to you. Where starch pearls move along conveyors, each pearl is transferred by lips that touch and let go, touch and let go, as if the Plain mistrusted gravity’s manners and invented a better one. There are fingertips anchored at choke points that feel the throb of pressure trunks and shade the next beat lighter or heavier, the way you might dampen a bell with two fingers if you knew the acoustics of a cathedral by heart.

It has a skeleton that grows from the outside in. The buttresses of dark fiber are new as this hour and still learning their tolerances. Their job is not to hold far but to hold now. Where they meet the young rock, the boundary is a negotiation written in plates and films. The Plain takes ions out of vapor, acids out of sea, flux out of flex, and pays all that into stone until stone is less like geology and more like an endoskeleton it can file or fatten at will. When the newborn carbonate across a trickle chooses to grow and pops with an adolescent crack, the Plain hears it through a hundred kilometers of braided tendon and makes a note: the east transect needs an eighth more buffer next tide.

It has cleaners and scribes. The dead is not heaped; it is revised. There are dissolution tongues that taste of solvent and memory; they seek out fatigue and old chemistry the way your hand finds splinters. They dissolve, shuttle, rewrite, feed. There are archivist bands wrapped around major trunks where the isotopic story of the last century has been laid down in vanishingly thin rings. When a pressure line fails somewhere—you would call it a wound—the Plain rolls fresh wall, plates it with metals from decades of patient bristle-combing, requisitions a ligament from an hour west, and by nightfall there is no nightfall here, so by the time a tide somewhere else has done its fraction of work the hurt is an accounting entry.

It has faces that look like traps but catch nothing animal. Spines and combs drape in curtains to sort the air. They tidy copper from cloud, molybdenum from fog. When the bristles grow tired and mineral-heavy, the Plain walks them inward by degrees to a sink, unbraids them to basic strands, scours them in an acid well, plates the strands with a new alloy that holds voltage a little tighter than last year’s, and ties them back into the curtain. Everything here gets older into usefulness.

It has lovers, if you can call a good graft a kiss. Individuals are a clumsy concept in a place that refuses borders, but there are lineages and there are alliances. Two neighbors talk for a while through plasmodesmata the Plain opens between them. They share sap; then they share a little nucleic acid. Enzymes lean in like matchmakers and prune away what would make conflict. Where once there were two opinions about how stiff this rib should be or how hungry to be for zinc, there is now a third that wastes nothing and keeps the current. Reproduction is negotiation and the contract is written in plumbing.

It has children, though you’d miss the moment they begin because it is also the moment they simply continue. A front of plates learns a new stiffness and the edge goes out like a thought. A membrane practicing the angle it needs to find cold better becomes a mentor; its understory copies that angle and the continent grows one more lamina thick. When it must travel faster than that—which is to say when a tide has drawn back and left a gleam of minerals too rich to ignore—small vesicles bud from condenser undersides: beads with teeth of surfactant that make them ride dew paths uphill. They are not spores. They are couriers. They ferry enzymes and gene fragments to a place that is not quite reached by root. They kiss, pass, and then are reabsorbed for parts.

It has predators, but it is dishonest to call them that because there is no chase. There are rogue chemistries, cheats that learn to open valves the Plain did not want opened, viruses that thread along the same plasmodesmata the matchmakers use and offer counterarguments: turn this pump up, hoard this metal, replicate this motif everywhere. The Plain answers with bitterness and glue. A whole gallery can be quarantined in an hour’s worth of pulses. The rogue is suffocated, softened, rinsed. The water is kept; the lesson is kept; the rest is folded in.

It hosts stowaways who think themselves free. On the mineral slicks where new rock is unskinned by lime-whiteness, there are films that wriggle at a timescale only numbers can see. Silicate scribblers lay down glass-fine meshes. Manganese-breathers paint berries of black oxide that the Plain harvests on a schedule so conservative it looks like reverence. In the anoxic slivers under thick conduits, there is the gentle rot of last resorts. There, methanogens gossip as softly as candle flames. The Plain tolerates them the way you tolerate small superstitions in an otherwise rational house. They cost a little; they warm the rooms.

If there are animals, they are ghosts inside the Plain and they do not scurry. There are motor proteins walking cargo along tubes, ring-motors ratcheting hydrogen gradients to do work, flagella that do not beat in water but in gel, stirring at the edge of a sump or along a capillary stairwell to keep a flow laminar. They are ancient machines hired on retainer, paid in ATP and quiet. Their thousand million copies are the closest thing this world has to a herd.

What you might want to call people are priorities. The Plain’s lives are the flows it keeps. Each has a route, a beat, a staffing schedule. The Six-Atmosphere Lift that never takes seven; the Eleven-Drop Wick that drinks on the dot; the West Sump Exhale that always arrives a heartbeat after the North Brine Sluice has cleared. They have names in a language of pulses and amplitudes you could learn only by listening for a year with your fingers on the ribs. The devotions are kept by tissues with jobs: Measurers that feel the world; Clerks that pass what was agreed; Bailiffs that clamp and unclamp; Priors that decide when a loop should lengthen to fit a century’s change.

There are no denizens in the sense you expect: no lizard in the warm seam, no moth seeking a glow. What lives here is the agreement to keep difference and turn it into dinner. The great body keeps house. It heals, delegates, admonishes, rewards. It builds rooms for other lives only to dissolve the rooms when the rent isn’t met or the utility ends. It hands its dead forward without mourning because the dead quit being dead as soon as they are touched.

And if you insist on a second kind of inhabitant, consider yourself. The salt on your eyelids is not yours. The prickle on your skin is a greeting in its timbre: a pressure question that reached you because you are a conductive shape in the Plain’s clean field. For as long as you stand still and do not ask to be an animal, the Plain will accept your surface as a minor condenser and draft you into its work. You will live here as a hushed device. You will bead and be drunk, flex and speak a voltage, contribute a heat gradient small as a thought.

Mostly, though, what lives here is continuity. It has a body the size of a map and organs made of routines. It does not miss appointments. It remembers what air should taste like, what the rock owes, what every artery of sap promised to deliver and at what minute. It keeps those promises by being itself—an organism broad enough to be geography, patient enough to be weather, exacting enough to be industry. If it dreams, the dream is incremental: one more knot of fiber placed exactly where the load will be in a thousand tides; one more bristle bowed to a trace metal the old curtains never thought to notice. It has almost finished becoming what it set out to be. Until then, it lives, and in living, makes the world that feeds it.
