What lives here is not inside the place but is the place, a single body extended until geography mistakes it for itself. Its citizens are functions. Its language is pressure. Its temperament is the hum you hear wherever you stand.

The plateau is the main resident, a many-chambered green animal that never learned to move except as weather. It has roots in nothing you would call soil—its underparts are a repeating honeycomb welded to old stone and to itself, ribs upon ribs that carry sugars and air and rumor. The towers and bowls are not buildings but organs. They pull the sky down and hand it back tuned, bound to water. Memory is a property of the vascular webs: changes in conductivity, protein patterns along certain rails, the slow establishment of favored routes. Decisions here are gradients; will is a siphon primed and kept singing.

Sensing is distributed. Every surface is a fingertip, each hair a hygrometer, each slat a reed that can tell by its note how much push is in the passing air. When you feel the racket under your feet, you are eavesdropping on intercom. The sustained chord is not art but feedback control, the sound of a body hearing its own circulation and correcting, the way an animal breathes shallow or deep without asking itself to try.

It has company, but not the kind that fills a field elsewhere. Its companions are small, quiet and admitted on the understanding that they will do something useful or at least no harm.

Glide-things live here without ever owning a patch. They hatch from the hem like insight from a mouth—those pale drifters you see unbuttoning from the frilled edges, all web and cartilage and appetite for gradient. They do not land, not really. They kiss and let go, tasting the gloss for salts and trace metals the plateau cannot mine from air. In exchange they leave what they have stolen elsewhere—a dusting of micronutrients combed out of clouds that broke over oceans and deserts both. Their bodies are soft and easily unselved, which suits the host; what they lose into the fabric is absorbed without fuss, what they keep serves to send them on again. They court no mates you can see. They pair with drafts, they choose pressure slopes, and in the lee of the bowled vaults their little eggs hang like dew and wait for the right tone to shake them free.

There are beetles like beads of the place’s own sweat, glass-backed and pale, who graze edges. You watch them and at first mistake them for the water that’s finding a way along a ciliated seam. Then the seam shivers with a practiced shudder and tucks the droplets in, and the beetles flow around the gesture and keep on walking. They clean film, and in doing so they keep cilia from felting into useless fur. Their shells condense cold on the undersides and mete it where the platform wants hints of night—over valve-lips that stick in heat, on the pads that need to bite vapor better by morning. If they die, they disappear into the surface and no mark remains; the plain is efficient even with courtesy.

There are chime-birds, although bird is generous. They are dry, ribbed pods with beating margins, more seed-case than animal, tuned to the slots of the tower mouths. They comb the updraft, try to steal a note. The tower answers with a shoulder of harmonics and in the discrepancy, in the minute contest between reed and reed, the velocity through the organ steadies. The plateau uses them for calibration the way an orchestra uses a singer to find A. When the towers are sated, the chime-birds are sluiced along lined corridors that read their frequencies like a passport; the ones that sing within certain bounds are encouraged to propagate down in the low domes, fed gummy resins sweet enough to be a policy. The rest are tolerated until their ranges drift closer or they simply wear out and are unmade with the same grace as beetles.

There are worms that are not quite worms, threads of living felt that run along the edges where water likes to ride. They seem like ornament until you kneel and see the work: they comb and reset, they break menisci when the plateau needs droplets to fall, they braid the tiny hairs until they present the highest ratio of surface to air the morning will allow. Their bodies are a compromise between fungus and leaf—pliant hypha wrapped in cuticle, moved not by muscle but by turgor and habit. They eat biofilm and are paid in sugars grown just for them along the lanes they service: a stippled gloss that never overfeeds and so never mats.

There are bigger things, but not many. Deep in the body move the pith-rafts, caravans of buoyant tissue that wander the honeycomb’s lower galleries. The plateau makes them in response to shocks—hail, strange wind, the tearing weight of a falling glider—and sends them like tongue-sutures into hurt. They are mobile only here, in the inner cathedrals, parceled by valves and rung through by supporting strands. You could mistake one for a creature if you caught it negotiating a junction, sensing the tone-tilt of a passage and crawling toward the louder hum as if it preferred company. But their edges fuse with any wound they meet, they disappear into what needs them, and they do not have a separate fate.

Invisible multitudes live as film and thread inside the living gloss. The fungi are not tenants but a second mind that braided with the first so long ago you can’t find the seam. They run the sums. They count hours without seasons by chemistry alone, they remember shortages the way a tongue remembers a burn—by altering the way taste feels long after healing. When the wind stays slack an hour too many, the fungal republic opens millions of honeycomb cells to breathe in place of weather. When the dew runs fat and the bowls begin to overfill, it whispers through its hypha that the flumes may narrow here, the drains throttle there. This is the compromise you can feel if you lie flat and attend: a low base of mycelial will, above it the bright plant urgency, the two making a third voice that is the actual inhabitant—precise, patient, self-correcting.

At the hem live specialists that know falling. They are the only residents who flirt with the outside honestly. Some are nets that flex and take the velocity out of spray and glider both. Some are frills that send seed-islands to lesser hills, small self-contained knots of tissue with a day’s song and a week’s sugar and a chance to catch an eddy and come to rest on any shoulder that will have them. Some are tastebuds you could see: pads stippled with salt-loving cells that test what the cloud brings in from sea and from stone and tell the body whether to open wide or filter fierce. The hem has its own smell—a clean, clean bitterness—and its population has its own manners. The gliders court there and get themselves kissed into being. The chime-birds drift there to roughen their first voices against the porous teeth of the curtain. Beetles there shine more than glass, almost metallic, and let go entirely when the plateau chooses to slick them back into infancy.

The rest of the life is small and folded into function. Mites travel in the slip between bark and wet, their mouths adapted to snap only the short stray hairs that drift out of alignment and clog a pit; they never bite live fabric because it does not present itself as food, because to their bodies the plateau tastes like self. Algae come and go on the clear skins of the domes and are corrected. They are given a stint of morning to green the light a little, to filter a spectrum the organ likes, and then a dry breath shivers them off into digestible snow.

There are no predators in the sense of drama. There are no herds. The open spaces are empty on purpose, and nothing large dares to invent shadows the plateau did not ask for. If leak-feeders fatten, the hum cools a fraction and gutters that are not gutters open under them, an invitation they cannot quite refuse: a shade that tastes right, a pressure that feels like shelter, down which they go and are redirected into work. If a migrant from below—an accidental moth of the wrong country, a leaf-thing with a taste for cellulose—makes landfall, the first reception it meets is texture. The place is slick where slick will turn it, sticky where sticky will hold it until enzymes with a very narrow brief arrive and ask it to see sense. Most do. The rest become part of the resident’s memory of being visited.

Reproduction here is not spring but geometry. When the body gathers enough surplus quiet, it pushes lobes farther out into air, hung on nothing but its own tensile weave; sometimes the lobes accrete until a cuff is heavy enough to think about falling. Fall is rehearsed a thousand hours with tiny changes in tone. When it comes, it is not a tearing but a letting, and the new piece keeps its song and drafts on its parent’s weather as long as it can. Most are caught by lower winds and shred into advantages. A few catch a shoulder of mountain or the flat back of some neighboring table and lie there humming their first small chord, a nursery with no cradle but updraft.

If there are minds here aside from the plateau’s—if you argue the beetles have opinions about gloss, if you grant the gliders a sense of slope that amounts to preference—they are all folded into the resident’s way of being without insult. This is not domination; it is shear-thickening: everything that moves becomes more like itself by moving in the medium the plateau has made. Even you, if you walk on it. Your heart will seek the hum, will try and fail to synchronize, will slow anyway, and in the failing you will learn where the vents are by ear, where the water will go by lick and look, which apertures are true invitations and which are only instruments.

So who lives here? The city that is one body. The compromise that behaves like a person without any place you could point to and say: head. The fungal mathematicians that count and correct. The flume-groomers who move by taste. The glass beetles that polish and chill. The almost-birds that set the towers’ throats and are paid in sugar for trouble. The pale gliders who kiss and keep moving, who bring the outside in, gently. The pith-rafts that dream slow and become scar.

If you need a number to hold, count the notes. If you need a face, find the black oval of a tower’s mouth and watch the teeth of cellulose drip and shine. If you need neighbors, lie down—no, you won’t sleep, the hum won’t let you—and feel the vast thing around you inhabit itself, attendant to everything it has invented here: edges for water, veins for wind, bowls to make obedience of rain. That is the resident. That is who lives here, and who will live here long after even your memory of the sound has changed shape and returned like weather as somebody else’s myth.
