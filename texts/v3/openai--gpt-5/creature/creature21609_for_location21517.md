The first residents are so close to stone you mistake them for it. They arrive as tints, as damp decisions the rock makes about its own surface. If you kneel in the lee of a column where the light has time to gather, you will see the iron-green and smoke-blue films that started this place’s story thicken into a sheen. They are glass-eaters, new-world chemists, letting down acids in a slow rain of their own to coax the basalt to talk. They worry the silicate lattice for iron and manganese and anything else that will surrender an electron, drink the faint electricity that piezo seams and thunder lay along the skin, fix carbon pulled from water that is almost always falling. Their bodies are membranes upon membranes, stacked microns deep, sticky with self to keep from being peeled away when the vertical rivers shy sideways. In their gloss are first textures: tiny ridges that make the next drop hesitate, curl its lip, leave an extra heartbeat of wet before it goes. They do not think, but they are measurable—if you come back after a thousand storms, their green and blue will have added a hair to the mountain’s vocabulary.

Where cracked quartz breathes against pressure, something finer fans itself into existence. The white-grit palisades rising out of hairline fractures are mineral lichens by function if not by ancestry. One partner is a filament of acid and patience, a thread that tastes for cations and lays out a picket of precipitated silica, a fence against the world’s forgetting. The other partner mints sugar from almost-dark, because here the light has to be persuaded; it comes sideways through water’s glass, broken into columns of glow. They have learned to borrow the curtains. When the planes fall just right, light runs inside them like in a fiber, and the palisades splay vanes that catch it—slivers of wet crystal studded with black pigments that drink broad wavelengths greedily. They look like frost at first, then like fences built by insects that have never existed. They are, in truth, suggestions of soil, whispers of how a plain might one day feel beneath a foot.

Under the lips of overhangs hang the cords that make you think, for half a second, of an animal. They are translucent, an inch long when brave, a suggestion of jelly with a silver thread bright through the middle. When the fall slackens they reach for it. When it presses, they breathe in and vanish, drawn against the rock by their own will of swelling and shrinkage. The thread inside each is a stack of electroactive plates, proteins and mineral sheets that curl when a charge leans on them. Thunder speaks and they move; their own crystalline skeletons take orders from the air’s electricity. With their mouths—no, with their whole bodies—they rake the vertical water for dissolved carbon, for nitrates knocked loose by the storm’s roar, for stray organics from far-off places the updrafts remember. They take what touches them and haul it back to the seam. When they are ready to multiply they do not spit spores but let go of their ends and unzip lengthwise into ribbons. The flow carries the new halves out and down until charge catches them along a curtain’s edge; then they hook in, stiffen, and begin again. They are the first harvesters who learned to time the pulse.

In the rare pockets where wind forgets itself and the thunder walls leave a quiet, a skin like wet velvet lays a thumb on stone. This is the place’s dark photosynthesis, the black-bloom that eats light hard. It holds pigments that do not mind the coldness of light that has been sifted three times: once by the cloud, again by the falling sheets, finally by the glass of ice-skins that wink in and out on the rock. While the rest of the world is busy being loud, the velvet is busy being still. For an hour longer than the rest of the sheen, it remains, drinking what there is and hoarding water in mucilage that behaves like a muscle. When a slab of hail skates past, the mat flattens, the glue shears, the whole thing goes thinner than your idea of thin, then regathers with more grit caught in it: a few more quartz grains, a fleck of ash, a salt crystal seeded out of nothing but two charges disagreeing. Its children are smears; it colonizes by touch.

There are dwellers in the sheets themselves—if “dwell” can be the right word for something that exists for the duration of a plane of rain. They are not organisms as bodies are; they are arrangements that the place keeps rediscovering, standing waves in solution where amphiphiles and dust and charge huddle into vesicles along interference fringes. Where two curtains beat together just right, toe-to-toe on the frequency that makes a ladder of stillness in the fall, droplets trap within droplets. Membranes lace into partitions and, for minutes or hours, chemistry holds court. Carbon is fixed not by pigments but by chance redox at charge boundaries, by stray electrons squeezed out of quartz when the mountain shifts. When a rippling seam of cavitation sprints down a curtain like lightning inside glass, it unzips these congregations into sprays of seed: not genes yet, but templates of sorting, a preference for how parts want to face one another next time. They are the storm’s own garden—skyplankton if you need a name—born when the score hits a certain chord.

Along the basalt pipes where the wind draws a voice, there are combs of life that use that voice as muscle. They are made of cilia you would not call hairs because they are silica and flex at kilohertz. They stand at the mouths of hexagonal ranks, in the sweet spots where the organ-note thickens, and they sway not to make sound but to remake flow around them. With each stroke they pump an extra drip through themselves, rake a few more ions across their own charged faces, chew another layer of glass into ion soup. Their architecture is not by species but by frequency; one colony thrives at a note a few hertz lower than its neighbor and does not venture beyond that key. Where the tone sharpens and leaps, they go dormant, curling their cilia into stony fists. If you watched with an eye that could see charge, you would see them play the air like a harp with no audience, moving water in ways that make their metabolisms feel the less of gravity.

In the warmth of faults, in the gentle breath of things pretending for a second not to be stone, another community rings itself around heat. They lay thin bowls of silica that sweat when the fumarole exhales, trap the droplets against escape, and herd the chemistries that only happen at sixty degrees and then are gone. They smell of mistake and promise—sulfur, faint as a memory the mountain tries to keep. Inside their bowls grow beads like pearls that are not pearls: opal seeds the size of sand that record a day’s exhale in milky layers. The creatures—call them crusts, call them laminae—live for pulses. When the warmth ceases, they crack from their own contraction, and the shards go with the next descending plane, ride it a kilometer, snag on some new lip, and start the ring again. If any part of this place will someday hold ambered moments, it will be these opaline cups and their trapped, perfect, small nothings.

There are also the dwellers in ice-skin, the cryophytes whose lives would be comedy if not for the seriousness of their patience. When a hair-thin veneer appears on a sunlit ledge, they are the first to throw proteins at it that make crystals sing the shape they prefer. They get a few instants of clean water as the melt-line runs beneath them. They pack that water with their wants—phosphates, nitrates, carbons—and surf the retreating edge like a margin that never stops. They leave tiny sawtooth ridgebacks behind, biochemical teeth that make the next veneer take sooner and last longer, that bend the air into slightly better light. When the ice hisses away, so do they, into cysts that are more stone than self and that have the patience to wait one day, three days, a winter that is not a winter.

All this is biology in the conventional sense, if at the edge of it. There is also what lives in the interplay itself. The falling planes keep memories; vortical cords carry news. The life on the stone has learned to write in those cords. Some films extrude hook-shaped filaments that reach into the water and, by flexing in specific patterns, shed fragments with different surface charges. Those fragments travel only along certain spiral scars and not others. Hours later, kilometers away, the spiral unfurls into a different gouache of pressure and the fragments fall out onto some flank already written with their kin. In this way, a bit of basalt taste and a bit of thunder-grammar cross the empty air. These are not minds but logistics. A mountain teaches an ocean to fall; the microbes teach the falling how to remember where food was.

There are predators, though not by chase, and there are plagues. Viruses are the sky’s easiest cargo. They are not animals; they are instructions in boxes smaller than the thought of boxes. They learn the tints by lying and then by perfecting the lie—by turning a patch of iron-green into more of themselves until the film bleaches, goes glassy, and in its death seeds a silt of new facets for someone else to hold to. Sometimes a pebble of hail carries a whole city of them down through a dark matte. The matte sags and recovers; what dies feeds what stays. Grazers do not graze here; flow does that. A high shear is a scythe. A thermal sigh in a seam is a drought. The instruments that make this place sing are also its teeth.

Given time enough to sit and watch, you would learn their common habits and how they map onto the weather’s. You would see that the cords have a threshold at which they always retreat, a pitch in the thunder that makes them inhale. You would find that the palisades grow one more row of grit for every five days that the cloud curb braids above the highest spur, as if updrafts are their calendar. You would notice that the velvet mats darken minutely at noon because that is when the curtains’ waveguides are most parallel to the sun. None of this is will. All of it is bias, reinforcement, practice—shapes of adaptation, hammered like scales into the stone’s memory and the water’s.

Every life here adjusts the instrument it plays. The films alter wettability, and the vertical rivers behave a little more like panes for it; light rides a little truer inside them. The combs at the organ pipes pump enough to tilt a tone. The opal bowls trap heat long enough to change a microburst’s footfall. The place is not designed, but it edits itself, and the edits accumulate where lives sit. It is not wrong to say that the residents build weather the way reef-creatures build waves that habit them, by merely being in the way in consistent, small, clever ways.

If you are looking for a face, there isn’t one. If you want a movement that would make the mind say animal, there is none. There are bodies in the plural and bodies that are only currents, and all of them sleep with stone. To ask what lives here is to name things the size of sentences: the green gloss of glass-eaters; the white palisades that fence the future; the cords that are whole instruments tensed on one note; the black mats that make thumbs; the bowls that catch breath; the combs that stroke wind; the vesicles that live between two ideas of rain; the viruses that are the breeze’s sharpest thought. It is to admit that the architecture itself is resident—that between a roofworld and a falling ocean, life begins by writing its address into both and answers only to the storm’s slow grammar.

In a thousand storms there will be more. The tints will lift into tufts; the velvet will sprout leaves that deserve that name; something with a mouth may one day inhale the cool and plume. But today, what lives here are first sentences made of water and rock and electricity, and they are busy making the place talk. They do not act apart from the fall. They are the fall’s rumor of future flesh, articulating the plateau syllable by syllable until even the thunder knows how to say their names.
