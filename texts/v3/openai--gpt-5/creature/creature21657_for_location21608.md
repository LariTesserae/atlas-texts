They are light to the hand when you grip a forearm in greeting. You feel tendon and cord, a lattice under skin, not much fat anywhere that would add sway. Their weight is the first wisdom: they carry as little as they can and teach their children to put mass only where it matters—at the hips, in the thighs and wrists, in the bands of muscle that can stick to a rib with nothing but a palm and a breath held until the gust is past.

They grow compact, wind-colored. Hair is braided not just for beauty but for utility: tight plaits weighted with seedglass and heartwood beads to keep it from lifting like a smoke. They bind strips of leaf and thin hide along their arms so that, when the harp-lines thrash in a noon gale, the lines cannot cut to bone. Their feet are wide, toes articulate as extra fingers, with nails filed to shallow hooks. They smear their soles with the day’s resin or dust them with sand according to the mood of the wood. Their most precious callus is along the outer edge of the foot where rib meets void; it is a second sense that tells you, in the first half-inch of a tilt, whether a span is thinking about today or about a century ago.

They do not wear much. Cloth is drag. A cloak you can fold into a rope is worth more than a dozen garments that flutter. They go shirtless in dry heat, streaked with pollen and resin to a shine, and at night they sleep under woven leaves that they lash to the wood at three corners before they lie down. A child’s first coat is not a coat at all but a harness: a broad-bellied web of cane and cord with two good loops for anchoring, worn every day until the child has learned, by thumb and tooth and muscle memory, where anchors live.

Their voices are instruments they cannot count on. The wind steals syllables the way a river rubs down stones, and so the primary language under the noon is hands, eyes, small motions of the chin and mouth. There is a formal dialect of knots as well. Every bridge has its line of story-knots: not only the names of those who fell there, but the conditions—the green of the air, the smell of the resin, the time since the last storm. They can read weather by fingertip, space a sequence of warnings on a rope like beads on a prayer. In a loud day they speak to each other through the wood, too, tapping with the bone ends of old pods in a code old men know by ear and children learn first in play. When it is calm and the harps are sleeping, they sing—low, because habit keeps them low—in a scale set by the tension of the span they are standing on. The best singers are also the best listeners to the timber’s tick and swell.

They smell like medicine. Resin has got into them the way tobacco gets into certain families in other places: it clings to hair and skin and tools and makes its own smoke when they sleep near a puff of flame. They use the stuff as glue, as salve, as polish, as an evening meal’s fire; they can tell four kinds by nose with a hand over their eyes, and they can tell by tongue whether today’s harvest will harden into a glass that takes a nick or a wax that yields to a thumbnail. The story is that their oldest family—whatever they call it; the word changes with the rib—was a woman who learned to pull resin slow and clean from an old seam and not glue herself down. That is one of their core myths: not fire or bread but patience and angle.

Food is everywhere and nowhere. They herd the wind. Blue-lipped stakes thrust into the arid flats lure bees and bee-things by the thousand; they gather combs so thick a youth must brace a foot against a rib and lean back with all his body to tear a sheet away. Yellow smoke oozes out when they cut it—pollen smoke that makes your mouth run. There is flour in that pollen; you can clap your hands and turn the air into a loaf if you have a day to sit and catch what falls in the cups. They set hanging nets in the fast lanes and harvest pods of moths at dusk, fat bellies like sequins in a bag. They tend cities in the water, too: shrimp that eat the coats off the cups, crab-things that pick algae clean, tadpoles whose bones are soft enough to vanish into broth. They keep the appetites of these in order with hands as deft as any shepherd’s—the tilt of a leaf to pour out the unneeded, a whistle to startle a clutch of gluttons from a lip, a hand under a belly to feel whether the meat is ready.

They do not keep hooves. Things that cannot cling do not last long enough to learn their names. But they have a treaty with the bats; they build dark overhangs and sweeten them with old nectar skins, and the bats make their hem-stitches along the stars every night and come home to the same place, and if a woman has a child who wakes with screams she will seat herself in the hanging kitchen and let dusk pour the bats over their heads until the child sleeps and the heart forgets to hammer at such a crowd. They trap dragonflies by the bowl with reeds with shiny tips; they harvest beetles by knocking at the thickets where they breed, like beating for pheasant in another country. They do not eat the glider-snakes often—not because they cannot catch them but because a line thrown and caught by a snake is a form of temple work. That is what they call it, temple: the meeting-place of chance and practice where the first thin cord goes across a clean span and you do not fall.

Some of their work is invisible if you do not know it is work. They adjust the place. They are not the builders of the big shape—the ribs and hollows and catenary—but they are its caretakers in a way you will not see in a day. They prune by pattern, not by plant: a handful of creepers cut here, a cable weighted there with a ring of resin to bring its note down before it tears itself in wrong weather. They nurse cups along the ridges where there should be cups and strand them in places where there should not, so that when the storm comes it runs the steps it is supposed to and not a new crash of its own. They have a class of people whose hearing is as good as any shipwright’s knock: they walk with a rope across a shoulder and a little cone of stiffened leaf at the ear and listen to the ribs talk to themselves. They know when a line is fat with hidden wet and needs lightening and when a brown, singing ridge is ready to be left alone forever.

Oldness is honored in tools as well as in people. A pin of black heartwood gets a name and a bead when it has held three bridges through a season of blooms and not split. A rope that has seen a century becomes an archive; the months are counted in repairs, the years in fulcrums and falls. The elders sit with such things in their hands the way men in stone countries sit with pipes. They rub their thumbs over knots they themselves tied when they could run from one side of the world to the other in a morning; they send their grandchildren for a taste of a particular old resin with a certain sour in it and tell them to come back with news.

They trade, but not often and never urgently. Wind is the only road to other countries; sometimes a storm carries a net with weighted ends out off the lip of a place they know, and men in low green worlds see the bright tail of it in their sky and answer with a kite and a line that sings when they pull. Stone comes up that way in nodules wrapped in waxed bark, and down go oils and sugars and feathers dyed in lichens that only grow in light this clean. Once in a while a boy with a wild heart and strong legs puts on a winged coat sewn from glider-snake leathers and takes two days to go down by steps and slips and madness, and if he comes back he is teased by his grandmother for the way the low smells stuck to him and is asked by the whole world about what stone truly tastes like. Their stories of below are contradictory and breathless; they do not need them to be otherwise.

In the rules their elders teach there are also those that are more prayer than procedure. The wind has names that are not directions but characters: Knife, Lamb, Backhand, Mother, Chime. They greet these in certain ways with a hand or a cheek as they step onto a rib. They speak to their shadows as if to older brothers: wait for me there; show me the fray. They bury very little, because there is nowhere to bury. Their dead are given back in other ways. Some are taken down to a cup the family has tended for three generations and laid in water that has held their laughter; some are burned in the resin-bells that ring the high towers, and this is a holy sound that travels as far as a hawk’s drop. More often, parts of a person are made into tools—hair in a rope, bone pins for the bridge his granddaughter will name and recite as she crosses hand to hand. There is a piece of everyone in the place; that is one of the times they cry, laying a gaze and a palm on a line and knowing where it came from.

They mark their faces less than their bodies. Everyone who has come of age has the faint shiny scars along the insides of forearms from hauling a boy’s-arm-thick comb away from a feeder-stake, and the small lattices on the palms from long, hot afternoons with resin while the sun softened it into silk that could take a mold. The ones who tend cups have a certain smell to them that never leaves, mineral and sweet like leafdrip; the ones who read the harps lean when they stand as if they are forever bracing against a pressure, as if emptiness is a tangible thing that might at any moment press an inch too far into a person’s hollow places.

Children learn by play that is not play. They are taught to move quietly not because sound is an enemy here—sound lives everywhere and is not to be fought—but because suddenness is. They crank their necks like swifts to learn what a hawk-shadow is; they are handed seeds in the green weeks and told to squeeze them and feel the soft readiness along their seams so that they will not be surprised when the pods clap themselves open over their heads with a little popping of goodbyes. They catch dew on lashings they wove themselves and go thirsty if they wove badly. The brave are not praised for leaps; the brave are those who brought the smallest child and the heaviest pack across the same line without launching more than a thread of fear into the air.

Marriage is a bridge with a new name. Two families tie a first line between their living-places with the guidance of a singer of spans; the line is given the name of a failure in each lineage; then each brings a cloth cut from an old safety sling and stitches a double width to make a low wall for the first winter. That is as permanent as things get. Children belong to ribs and to routes as much as to the people who carry them. If a woman needs a cousin for a certain kind of morning—resin-pull, cup-clear, bee-drive—she goes to the knots and asks, and in the afternoon the cousin comes with her bad jokes and they do the work and the cousin eats at her edge-of-kitchen and goes home along the diagonal with the wind in her braid humming like a leaf.

Their days have features like the land. Dawn is for water and listening. Midday is for mending and risk. Evening is for fire and story; night is for quiet work in the lantern fungi’s amber rooms, the places under ribs where the big harps go slack and the ribs tick as the day comes off them and resettles. On certain nights in the lean months, when the quiet is hung like a sheet over the place and the stars are low and huge, they tied themselves for sleep more carefully than usual, not because of fear, but because someone old said softly that tonight the wood felt thin and you could hear through it if the wind came wrong. You do not take that kind of talk lightly.

They are not alone in thinking and not human in every the way you might expect them to be. There are other selves here. The old wood is a person to them in the way a ship is a person to a sailor, and perhaps in some deeper way: it has moods and cancers and sudden rages and long fits of generous calm. It can be soothed with weight or warned with a clack. The harps are not instruments but beasts that must be fed with care to keep them from singing themselves into pieces. Their household fungi occupy the hollows of ribs and will shift a light along a dark once for you if you have kept them well for five seasons running, and that is a favor that gets its own little knot on a certain private rope. The bees are a nation with which they have treaties and wars. The dragonfly schools are neighbors whose lanes you do not stand in if you do not intend to have your nose bumped a hundred times by the little armored faces until you move. Even the resin has a will; a fool who works it hot at noon will not just be stuck—he will be punished with a slowness in his life thereafter in the way elders tell it, a tackiness of thought. This is superstition if you like. It works.

There are castes only in the sense of aptitudes that the place brings out. Cup-keepers are people who can taste sour on the air and tell by a lily’s breath whether an old carcass is in the water and must be pulled. Rope-makers are people who cannot look at a line without knowing where it will go wrong in a year. Song-readers are those who can find a note inside a month of wind. Stormers are the ones who go to the edges when a sea-storm pushes into the high and stand with bodies slanted and power in their thighs and throw the seed-catchers down where they will do best. There are gift-names for these: Mouth-of-Cup, Hand-of-Rib, Ear-of-Harp, Shoulder-of-Gust. These are the names that stick longer than the ones your grandmother called you when she was scraping comb into a pot.

When the flowering comes and the week of yellow-green turns the air into an organ played by a god with too many hands, they lash their children down and weave themselves into the under-wood and ride the din. Oldest men tell the story that once they tried to fight the noise with noise and it broke them—that an elder strung a harp of his own with too much pride and set it to sing in the green weeks to drown the pods and the bees, and the land shook its head and took him and the strings and four good spans with him, and now the name of that bridge is taught alongside the smell of new-cut melon and the taste of a puppet’s cup and the double-listen before you move. Their wisdom is humbler than architecture. It is fit and give, brace and loosen, take what water comes and stack it where it will do the most good for the most days for the most people, leave behind nothing so heavy that it changes the note.

On the very oldest ribs, where the bark is gone and the resin in the seams is as hard as glass and the hollows run into each other like the echoes of a bell, there are families of elders who never leave. They keep the memory of how the line felt when it was still growing; they speak of sap as if it were a neighbor who lent them fire once and never asked for it back. Young ones come to them in seasons when travel is bad and sit with their backs to the hot glass and listen to the wood cool itself tick by tick, and the elders say: put your hand here; this is what a promise feels like. This is why we live small on something that is so big. This is why we do not hurry and do not wait. Listen. And then the wind plays the thousands of throats and the big cables hum and everything that lives beyond a handful of dust in this high place makes its claim, and the people who belong to it hold on gently with both hands and are at home.
