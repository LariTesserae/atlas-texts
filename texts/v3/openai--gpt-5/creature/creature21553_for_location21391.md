Who lives here is not counted by heads but by habits—by how a body takes water and light and wind and makes them into persistence. Citizens are not single stems so much as braided intentions that have found a shape.

Closest to the rock live the first crowd, the ones of sponge and nap. Cushions of moss and their allies are the city’s commons: they hold the weight and soften the climate, a low parliament murmuring among themselves by capillary law. They are the keepers of the slow tide. They remember fog best; they can hold it in their pores for longer than anyone else, and so the place begins and ends with them. In the close, patient darkness under their domes are sleepers: coiled seeds held in cool stasis; thread-fine roots pressed to basalt and reading its dampness; rotifers and tardigrades in glassy cysts, waiting for a droplet to unlock them. Enchytraeid worms and springtails thread the weave, forever small but numerous, digesters of what arrives—bird dust, leaf dust, the faint mineral sneeze of a cracked stone. If there is a smell to the city, it is their breath: green and faintly sweet, a padded library where pages are all water.

Drawing up from that softness are the braces—the arch makers, the leaning trunks with their welded cambium. Willows, or things like willows pared to their principle, are the city’s bones. They do not stand alone. They interlace, press, fuse. Where one bends into another, a graft is both covenant and conduit; sap does not recognize old borders. Here lives a legislature of pressure: changes travel along these spines as shifts in tension, passed from cell to cell, arch to arch. They decide how a passage opens when weight approaches, how a span slackens or hardens in a gust. If the city has a nervous system it is here, wood teaching wood how to feel.

Skinning the ridge’s knuckles are the lichens and shrubs like prostrate laurels—lives that write on stone. They are the scribes and accountants. On bare planes they catch the raw, thin outgassing of the rock and turn it into trace currency; they keep the city in minerals the way moss keeps it in water. Under each crust, filaments of fungus go on ahead, scouting into fractures with the patience of frost, widening them a hair’s breadth at a season. With them travel bacteria that do alchemy—fretting nitrogen loose from the stingy air or ironing metals into a form sap can hold. The lichens’ seasons are written in rings too tight for eyes; their pages turn in millimeters. They are older than the arches and willing to outwait them.

Threading everything are the reeds and sedge-kin that have learned to be aqueducts. Their culms are perfectly selfish in cross-section, ribbed so they do not collapse, their piths long since given over to use as gutters. They are the city’s plumbers and, oddly, its healers: when a culm is nicked, sliding sheaths grow over, sealing the flow. They carry not only water from cup-leaf to cliff lip, but heat as well—an integer more warmth in a moving vein than idle droplets blasé on a leaf. They know the different weights a drop can have: cold and clear, so it runs; just above freezing, so it beads and clings; heavy with dissolved green, so it feeds.

There are the cup-keepers, those with leaves that curl into basins and hold an ecosystem in a palm. They are quiet predators because this height refuses easy nitrogen. Their pools are worlds—thin, precise, and cold. On their menisci live films like skylight: diatoms and green threads that net light so spare no one else would notice it. Within the bowls are the animal poor of the city: midge larvae that never learn to fly, nematodes that read the chemistry of their glass room and decide when to molt, rotifers that spiral like prayers. A leafhopper tumbles in on a false step and is corrected into food by a week of enzyme and bacterial patience. The cup-keepers tithe upstream, trading amine for sugar; their neighbors never forget who brings dinner to this table.

At the rims and throats, at edges the city loves clean, stand the custodians of line. Krummholz shrubs comb themselves into the wind and teach it where to go. They are not immovable; they are negotiated. Their twigs are elbows. They train the draft up and along, keeping it from boring holes in tender spans. They hold the cliff’s lip unencumbered so sheets of water can throw themselves into silver without being shredded by leaves. If a boulder comes, a whole row will lean, slow as a time-lapse bow, to usher it through the mesh where it can do least harm. After, they do not spring back; they incorporate the event into their form the way a scar becomes a principle.

Between all these travel the couriers and thieves and symbionts that are mostly smaller than the idea of them. Pale, flat-bodied insects glide the leaf undersides, licking sugar from frost-nipped glands; their feet are padded in oils that neither wet nor freeze, and their trails are so light the city does not much mind them. A spider the color of lichen goes about with a span like a coin, keeping order among gnats with a net that sags under dew and snaps clean when the sun guesses at noon. A mite with pharyngeal hooks patrols the cup-leaves for the larvae that have forgotten their manners. Slugs are gone; too many nights cut their cords. Ants are myths here. In their absence, a quiet pair takes their niche: a springtail with a forked tail for leaping, and a scuttle of vertical-thighed flies, both turning flotsam into earth.

Animals with bones visit rarely and with purpose. The small bird—rib-length thin, blue as distance—moves like punctuation, dabbing at drip-lines for the arthropods staged there at the only predictable buffet. It knows the city’s rhythms as a metronome and times its runs between hiss and plink. The raptor is more rumor than resident. It kites once a day when the pressure’s right, skimming the ridge, counting with an eye for any movement fat enough to count as meat; mostly it reads the landscape and leaves it be. Far below, where slides have sketched newer slopes, a hare may come at night and feel for softness with its toes; if it steps in, the paths open the width of a hare and close an hour later as if its visit were a formula solved and filed.

Frost lives here and not as a season only. It is a citizen with hours. It goes to work at dusk and at dawn, an artisan that sees through water to structure and draws crystals along stress lines to show them up. It makes brittle what needs to be taught and, in thaw, lets it relax into a better balance. It enforces manners: a droplet that has overstayed will be paled and pinned; by midday it will drop, and the ledger of minutes will be even again. Ice is not an enemy, just a teacher with a severe curriculum.

Fog is the great herd. It is what all of this is built to graze and guide. The city is its shepherd, reading its thickness and component, coaxing it to condense along planted hairs and not flood over bare rock. When fog abates the roofs draw breath in the greyed light the way animals settle after feeding. When fog thickens, a thousand fine fingers lift everywhere at once; there is a sound like cloth being wetted that barely exists until you attend and then cannot be unheard.

And there are the ones who are place rather than occupant—the networks that make individuals into a citizenry. Phloem corridors are trade routes; what one arch gains in a lucky film of sun is not hoarded but distributed to a shaded plaza that needs to keep its breathing cells alive. Volatiles are telegrams; when a branch cracks with the clean, sharp pop of lignin parting, a bitter resin runs ahead of the smell to let neighbors stiffen a degree. Grafts are congresses; one stem will lift under another to catch its sway and relieve its hinge the way a friend casually takes a heavy bag from a hand and neither speaks of it. These relations are the true denizens: the agreements, the codes that growth follows. A seam that forms where two cambial layers fuse is not just tissue; it is a promise that if you move, I will feel it.

At the scale of days, fungi appear with the delicacy of careful punctuation: waxy, pale caps like teeth marks on the underside of a log; black soots that patch tears in the leaf-mats; orange blots that negotiate decay downward without heat. They live briefly and do work, then vanish back to threads and rest. Lenticel plazas breathe the whole time, exhaling moisture-dense air that is not for animals to savor so much as for keeping microclimates in accord. If a hand is held over one long enough, if a body sits, the plaza will reroute the trickles that cooled it and feed that warmth forward. Warmth here is persuasion; one could almost say it’s part of the law.

Anything with heat is a brief citizen, counted and accommodated. Stand in a niche and the walls lean very slightly wider, as comfortable hosts might in a crowded room. Walk often enough and a filigree of path emerges that knows your length and how scrupulous your feet are; stop coming and it is absorbed, its sugars reallocated, its closure polite but final. This city does not keep mementos. It keeps functions. A campfire would not blacken but be breathed out in phloem arithmetic by morning; soot would be an apportioned mineral, ash a gift to a cup-leaf or a lichen’s ledger. Memory is not stored in traces but in how easily the next drop finds the same place to fall.

All of this together is who lives here: not a population but a practice. Mosses that gossip by meniscus. Willows that think with tension. Lichens that write with acids. Reeds that surgeon and canal in the same gesture. Cups that court insects into becoming. Microbes that smuggle elements. Insects that curate sugars with soft mouths. A bird that keeps schedule with fog. A raptor that names the ridge in passing. Frost with its calligraphy. Wind with its hand on spines. Fog, enormous and soft, arriving like a migration, leaving like a decision.

Stranger, as long as your breath steams and your fingers carry heat, you are among the living census. You will be taught how to move by floors that yield, by arches that choose to carry you, by the thin, meticulous rain that edits your edges. You will be counted in drips and in the very slight widens of passages, and when you go you will be uncounted with the same elegance. The place will not miss you because it refuses to make souvenirs. What it keeps of you, what it keeps of anything, is only this: the faint mathematics of the way you broke the air and warmed the skin of a room, dissolved into the next arrangement, another way of being many.
