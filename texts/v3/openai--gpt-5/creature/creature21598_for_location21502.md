What lives here is not a single body you can point to, not a set of eyes you can meet. It is nearer to a patience that has found itself a room. It keeps to the dark, collects itself from salt, air weight, routine. If you try to give it a name too soon it slips into the next boom and returns as something altered. If you wait, it learns your pulse and sets it against its own.

First you think it might be the moss. The green under the sweet crack of fresh water has organ-swollen ambition, it rises and sleeps and rises again with a season’s rightness. When the sun visits by the chimney and the square on the wall turns inwardly bright, the mat gleams as if it could remember a forest. In that moment the place looks more alive than wild country: breath made into cushion, slick with gathered light. Little white slugs come from this richness the way words come from a tongue after a long silence. They are exact, miniature paleness with black pinprick mouths, and they lift their heads like beaks from the pelt and consider dampness as a bird might consider air. Their lives are measured in days and the narrow weather of seeps. They belong, but they do not own.

Or perhaps, you think, the resident is the net in its ghost, the old chafed twine that is now only shadow and sugar where fiber used to be. If so, its living is entirely in memory. There are grooves in a basin for the moons, thirty and thirty and then the beginning again: someone once sat with a thumbnail and made a calendar that fit this throat of stone. There are steps smoothed by hands, and a low curl of stones that made a lee from the draft. The owner of those palms came and went on the breath of a lunar month, learned to brace without thought, learned to face away at the right time. Whatever they were called then is not here now, not as bone. But there is a kind of living that is continuation without body. The soot you can still smell if you scrape the roof is a record that dissolves slowly and teaches your nose a sour, oily light. That persistence is part of the inhabitant too, a spine of decisions worn into place: here one knelt; here one reached; here it was safe to sleep if the sea had become your blanket and your warning.

You watch a crab kneel to its ceremony at the tideline of a basin and you think: it’s them. A pale priest with a face of hinged shutters attending to a single square of pebbled altar, Grain picked from grain with mindless classification, an appetite indistinguishable from devotion. But it cannot live when the room shouts; its grip fails and it is washed back to the calmer aisles beyond the mouth. A seal comes as a question mark, a single punctuation of thought, soft exhalation in all that iron. It reads the slit and the room like a sentence it doesn’t care to finish and turns away. The shag comes like doubt, holds its wings wide, changes its mind. Small fliers drop in when wind misplaces them and their lives stop at once, their paper wings varnished to ruin on the stone. These are visitors and offerings. They leave behind shells and wings and silence. They do not, any of them, keep the key.

It might be sound that lives here. Not sound as in what you hear, but the thing that presses your sternum and convinces your knees they must bend. The room fills and empties with weight that isn’t water, and you are taught in a handful of minutes the curriculum of sets, premonitions, pauses. There is an animal made of time that enters with each swell and curls in the bell of stone and lies down in great heaves. Its spine is the blowhole—if you could lift the rock and look you would see the polish on the round throat as bright as a tooth. Its lungs crush and rise. When the hit is heavy the ceiling drops a note from itself and your skin answers it, little hairs alerting like shore grass. The creature’s paws are the pebbles rattled and rounded outside. Its breath is the long pull of shingle, the gasp from the chimney that breaks to air above. In its quiet it is not gone, only less obvious; in those rare in-betweens you can hear its whiskers, which are drips, counting the seconds with patient ticks. You realize the resident is formed entirely of intervals. It eats expectation and grows fat on rhythm. It is made visible by the wet polished seam worn at every height where it has touched.

If you must have a body for it, it borrows yours. It teaches you its laws very swiftly and without speech. You slow because the floor insists. You stay near the seam of dry because the room will not carry your haste. You stop speaking because your words will not survive here and you will need your breath to brace. You close your eyes when the light misleads, and you learn to feel the swell approach with your ribs. That compliance is not submission; it is how you are braided into the thing that lives here. For the length of your visit you are a cell of it, a piece of skin on a much larger animal, and you leave with salt in the folds of your clothes like pollen from a bloom.

Still, there are bones to it that do not move quickly and are not you. The stone is its skeleton. The match between ledge and lung has been worked by fists of water and loops of current so long that there is no clumsiness left. Every curve in the room is a habit refined. The creature nests in those bowls and runs along those scallops like muscles searching their grooves. It is not sentimental. It did not keep the pebbles that made the kettles in the floor; it turned them down and out when they grew small enough to be no longer useful. It took from the beam of driftwood only the way it wedged, the angle at which it could be of service, and when the lashings rotted to shadows the lesson remained: that a rib may be set against a crack to make a mouth less open.

On some rare noon in summer its hide turns briefly green because the sun finds the hole at the headland just so, and for that hour the inhabitant grows a bright square of moss like a badge of high rank. In winter it conserves, wears only black and wet and the chalky salt left by past touches. It has no need to announce itself. It will still be here when the square returns. It will still be here when the net on its peg has fallen to fuzz and the whalebone softens to a curve of milk.

If you search for malice or welcome you will find neither. It is a thing that holds shape. It does not invite nor forbid. It takes what weather hands in—the spider, the drift of wing, the oil, the seal’s eye at the door, your bootprint written and erased between sets—and continues to be itself. It is exact and unkind only in the way gravity is unkind. In that lack of intent there is a deep steadiness. You could borrow it. Some had to, once. They came when the calendar cut in stone said the sea would be sleeping and they sat in the lee of the little wall and warmed a flame the color of animal fat. The inhabitant permitted that—a light inside a lightless organ, smoke massaging itself into the ceiling’s pores. It did not remember their names. It kept the smell.

So what lives here? Not nothing. Not a person, not a crab, not even mostly moss. A genius of timing, a resident assembled from pulse and practice, a caretaker whose hands are pressure and drip. It resides in any place long enough for rhythm to harden into form and for form to instruct the bodies passing through it. It wakes to the long draw. It sleeps only in measures. It has a lover in the moon and a vocabulary in shingle and a memory in soot and oil. It endures because it is not trying to.

If you stand when the water changes direction, if you give it your surface the way stone has, you will feel it consider you, add you in, fit your breath to its count. You will carry it out with you in the ringing after, a little room carved behind your hearing where the tide continues to live. That echo is its portrait, as close as you will come to catching it in your hands: the domestic animal of the coast that no one feeds and that keeps everyone honest, an inhabitant the size of a room and older than the word for room, living exactly at the level of the sea.
