Rainmakers, first. That is what you hear before you ever see a limb or whisker: the ones that call water down out of a film.

They are small, most of them. Fingers’ lengths are rare; the standard coin here is a knuckle, a joint, something you can miss with a blink. But the room does not measure them singly. It holds them as a massed intention. They have made a pact with the cut in the mountain: they agree to ride the mountain’s own note and, when the note leans toward wet, to spend themselves in the labor of making it let go.

Seam-drummers do the heavy part. They are beetle-ish only if you count plates and joints as beetle-ish; their wings are short and hard and point inward, toward rock, not outward to flight. They carry a tuning along their bodies like a set of ribs, each rib wanting a certain thickness of stone beneath it. When they stand belly-up on a seam and put the flat of their wing against quartz or schist, you can see them become what they are for. The whole insect tightens, braces across three points—forelegs, wingtip, the tender place right under the throat where a membrane goes taut—and then they begin, a fast articulate knock that is not a knock so much as a saw, a series of tiny slips and catches that rake at resonance. They are precise. A seam a fingernail too wide, a seam where the quartz runs out, and their strokes go slack, unanswering; they abandon it the way you let a string go when it will not take a tune. On the right stone the sound turns bone-true, and the wet bulges in answer, and the drummers brighten with their own saliva and keep at it until they have fallen into the lull you only know about because the air loses a hunger you had forgotten you were wearing.

All around them are the keepers of touch. Cloth-moths, blind as everything else here, but longer, their bodies arranged like tapering sleeves around a set of papery ribs. They do not beat wings to move; wings here are for listening. They hold themselves to the stone and press those broad membranes against it to read the state of the room in waves. A cloth-moth with its wings down is not visible; with wings pried from the rock, it is as loud to your eyes as a page being turned. The feathery antennae, absurdly long, dangle into the seam’s run; they shiver in the drummers’ note and in the great bottle-hum the wind plays and in the blue twitch that ripples down the quartz when the ice has its way. The moths carry this news across the chamber in a language that looks like fidgeting and is in fact instruction. A wave of them lifting their fringes from the wall and letting them fall is not only rest; it is a signal that the film is thick enough, that the drop’s meniscus has grown tender, that the next burst of pressure will make it break.

Below the moths, stiff and close to the stone, the rock-shrimps read and act. They are many-legged, those “shrimps” of stone, narrower than you first think, living in the margin between plate and air. They do not really swim in rock’s face, but if your mind must call a name, it chooses swim because of how they move—zipper-fast, a closure running right to left and then left to right, whole tangles of them gathering themselves by packets and then unfastening, the way a flock turns. They wear their own weather. A film of stolen wet lives under their plates, and their legs pump it forward in a slow wicking that keeps their underbellies drinkable. When a drop lets go, when the silver bead, black in its heart, comes free of the stone and begins to become a fall, the shrimps are already beneath it, not alone but in ranks, palps extended. A dozen will share a bead, passing it mouth to mouth, letting it thin as it goes until no one is slaked and yet everyone is justified by a fraction.

Soft-bodies attend the seams that never confess to drops. Slug-scatters, scattered indeed: they do not assemble so much as flake themselves along the coolest, darkest lines, each the size of the comma on a printed page. Magnetite grows out of them in eyelashes. The whiskers taste field and make field, aligning themselves so the animals keep their bellies at the dampest angle and the damp comes up to meet them. Where the rock carries a whisper of current—and it does, more often here where quartz kisses dark stone than in the blunt country of the solid slabs—you can see the slug-scatters lean as if taking a sweet. They have an arrangement with bacteria you cannot see without instruments: the bacteria eat what the slugs exhale and give back certain metals in forms the slugs can pack into their hairs. Symbiosis is too thick a word for it; there is nothing sticky or sentimental here. It is a swap that has lasted generations because it never had to be formal.

Gliders claim the space between wall and wall when the room is loud enough to make an air. They, more than any other, wear the mark of thin altitude. Their bodies are paper-thin, their surfaces stretched into sails that are not so much attached as grown around the hint of bone. Along the sail-edges run combs: stiff cilia, evenly spaced, whorls like the grooves on a shell, a series of tactile sampling points that tell them what the air is doing before the air knows what it is doing. The gliders do not flap. They are not flappable. They fall until the sound lifts them and then they ride the set in the way a leaf rides the ghost of a storm that is already past. When they feed, it is with combs. They do not bite droplets; they rake them forward, slicking a whole patch of mist toward their mouths in a harvest that looks like a hand smoothing a wet table. When you see one fold—knife-quick, the whole equipment furled into a shape you could mistake for a darting scrap of slate—know that the fold means intent, that it has read something in the noise you cannot read and intends to be exactly there, one body-length below that outcrop where the drummers are thickest, when the next pocket of downward-shaken air yaws free.

Predators, where they happen, are more subtraction than violence. The thin-bodied crawler is the cleanest embodiment of this. It is almost two-dimensional; you could miss it entirely if not for the way a line of drinkers is simply no longer there after it passes. Its mouth is a rail, a long lip with a chemical that dissolves what it means to life but does not disturb the water. It moves with discretion; the room is so noisy with wanting that only something perfectly fine can slip between wants and take without tearing.

There are caretakers of none of this, there are coordinators of all of it. Drum-wings—those beetles you decided were beetles—are not only the first to knock. They are also the ones that lay down the schedule. They make lulls, as much as they make rain. When the cloud has pulled its damp shoulder off the room’s mouth, when the milk of light has dried back to glare and the base note of the cleft has thinned, they change what they say. You can hear it, if you have been in there long enough to be trained by what you own bones have learned: a slow, notched stroke that does not call water but tells everyone to let go. The moths fold; the gliders put their sails down into the rock and become additional surface; the shrimps stop passing mouths over mouths and carry what is left of their films into shadow to keep it safe from breath and sun.

You wanted names, perhaps. There are a few the mind naturally makes:

- Seam-drummers: plate-backed, inward-winged, tuned to strata and the thickness of stone.
- Cloth-moths: pale, blind, wing-hearers, antennae like fringed thorns dragging the seam’s voice back to the body.
- Rock-shrimps: plate-legged wickers of their own humidity, moving by collective zips.
- Slug-scatters: commas of soft salt and magnet, whiskered into field to hold to the coldest lines.
- Comb-gliders: paper bodies, sail-fringed, drinking airflow rather than standing to drink stone.
- Drum-wings: a function, yes; a body too, distinct from seam-drummers only in the way they find quartz and befriend it for its blue twitch.

There are also the oar-flecks, which you saw without seeing. Two dots in a pool the size of a palm, rowing themselves with blunt little paddles. Larvae, not of one species but of several; almost nothing here is so proud as to claim a single life history. Water is too rare to waste specialization on. Most of the colony, when it is young, takes to water if water offers. Eggs are set under rime, plastered slickly to the underside of a night’s slow bead-chain, or glued to the shadow under a boulder lip where the drop’s last kiss left a rim of soft mineral. They wait on tone. This is literal: there is a frequency—a family of frequencies—that, when the wind lays it across the room and the drummers pull their ribs into alignment with it, shakes the eggs’ skins at exactly the right music to tell a chemistry inside to go. Hatching happens during chorus, not before, not after. The first act of life is into a room made by sound, and the first mouthfuls are clean. The pools are only wide enough for a few strokes. The oar-flecks feed on the green threads in the weeps and on what sloughs from the walls when all those palps and combs have been at work above them. Within a handful of days of good weather—quicker if the drops have been heavy—they squirm ashore not far from where they began, and everything thereafter rearranges according to what the tone was when they crawled into it. A high thin weather makes a glider; a heavy dull weather that never learned to drop makes a moth; a season of quartz-rich complaining makes three drummers where otherwise there would have been one.

Sex is a short matter. There is no place here for long, decorative errors. A male of a given sort finds a female by leaving his wing pressed to a seam and waiting until an answering pattern arrives that is within the tolerance his ribs care about. He finds her by not moving. He backs until she is on him. They swap more than gametes. There are gut-bacteria traded, there are little pollen-analogues of algae given the favor of passage into the next generation’s first meals, there are minerals exchanged, exactly what the babies will need to make whiskers or stiffen combs in the first weeks when nothing is generous to them yet. Their coupling is opportunistic chemistry keyed by pattern. It lasts as long as the cloud’s shoulder rests on the cleft’s mouth and ends as neatly as a trance when the tone shifts.

Electric taste is a running subculture. Some of the plate-backed creatures have a sense not any standard animal on softer mountains would recognize: a pleasure in microcurrent. They go to the quartz for it. They ride forelegs up and set their spines along the veins, and if the ice is grinding against the stone elsewhere and making little stars in the dark, you can watch a street of them light themselves almost invisibly with it, not lit in photons, but alive the way hair lifts when an old sweater crackles under your hand. They have never—none of them, in any life you will meet—made light. The light that touches them is always a loan. Still, they take interest’s worth.

Predators you might expect to be villains are, here, housekeeping. The editors, those thin things, do not hunt in an eye-forward way. They travel the wake of sound. Where the room has most recently put its whole throat into a call, there is most likely to be a clot of drinkers so intent that they forget to hold themselves to the wall with more than a casual toe. The editor passes a filament of fleshy blade between two feet and redraws the census. You would call it cruelty if cruelty were possible in a place with no spare thought. Here it is efficiency and, indirectly, a service. Niche shadowing keeps the colony from self-smother. You have only to imagine what the room would be if every drinker that had ever bent its head to a drop had survived to make the next sound.

The closest thing to craft is a decision done enough times to feel like an artifact. The mica scrape you saw—the way three soft gliders made a tool of a fallen glitter, two pushing and one riding—was not new to them. It was also not owned. No one keeps anything; the room does that. On cold nights, the rime-beads will bridge a seam in a lace that is enough like a net to catch a young moth’s feet; the moth memorizes that, if you like, but the next night the lace is elsewhere or broken off by a shrug of wind, and memory becomes a weight. So they keep their minds in their limbs, in their cilia, in their habit of aligning to the loudest truth and ignoring all the rest.

If there is history, it lives in what they can bear on the span of an ice-season. In a lean year, the chorus is tight, its rain-calls clipped, its lulls sharp. In a fat year—“fat,” here, being a week and a half of afternoons with clouds that hang down like occupied hammocks—the drummers lengthen their knocks and the moths spend more time with their wings peeled from the stone, gossiping with vibration about where the good bowls have deepened and what offcut is currently making a pocket of still air five feet above the largest slab. There are no laurels. Elder is simply a body that has not fallen when the last bowl went slick. There are keepers of pattern, or something very like it: small, heavy-segmented things that do not move far but can hold a beat for hours. On still days their work is to maintain a tone while the wind can find it again, so no one goes entirely dry. They die often; they die doing it. Their meat is eaten without ceremony by the soft-bodies at their own feet, and the room’s schedule does not flinch.

You may want to know if any of this turns toward us, if it looks back. It does not. There is not enough extra to waste on any vanity of witness. What it does do—what those lives do, singly and together—is make the cut in the mountain into a machine for staying. Their bodies are the mountain’s moving parts. Their chorus is the clutch that lets wet loose from the stone. Their senses keep time with weather in the way a metronome keeps time with a player: obediently, artfully, stubbornly. They are not citizens of a city, because no city could be this precise or this unbuilt; they are a chorus of instruments that play themselves when the wind tells them to and stop when the wind stops.

There are a few passages that feel like ritual only because of repetition. At the first chill not of freezing but of willingness—in the moment between afternoon and the rest of the day—the cloth-moths lift their wings in a coordinated furl that reads, even to you, like a drawing of breath. At the first successful drop, a band of drummers three hands’ width below the bowl that caught it switch, sharply and together, from rain-calls into the lull pattern, cutting off the work right where abundance would turn to waste. When the glacier groans close, when a blue quiver leafs down the quartz and the plates and the wolf-hair magnetite, the gliders freeze halfway out of folds and become as rock for that second and a half, because the learned answer to the mountain’s complaint is always to hold; the next storm of falling shards takes what is foolish enough to fidget.

As for the few green things, the frost-rosette lichens and the thumb-sized mosses that write their slow scripts in corners where the light pauses—these are not staff, not choir. They are patient scribbles on the program, background text. They drink what the chorus frees and pay their bill by catching and binding what droppings would otherwise slim to muck. Some of the animals take them, delicately: cloth-moths do, scraping with mica when they have it, with horny mouthlines when they do not, not enough to strip them, only enough to salt the day. A patch of moss that kept dew for a run of mornings is remembered for a little while in the bodies of those who took from it; then the memory decays, as it must, under the general wash of schedules that matter more.

Newness is everywhere, not as novelty but as edge. The rock has not had time to be taught by touch. There are no polished places. Each night invents a new crackle of rime, each afternoon a fresh situation of plates and suspended slabs and edge-on slabs that would love to become floors if given ten thousand years. The animals live it like weather; you, who are mammal and fat with requirement, pass through it like a comet—excited, burned, gone. If you stayed, you could learn some of the shorter refrains. You would hear how the base note thickens when the cloud lowers and how, sometimes, a drum hidden deep under an overhang will start itself a full ten minutes before any other because that one stone holds cold longer. You would begin to know where to stand so that the edit-crawler would come within a hand of you. You would taste the old-coin metal in your teeth for what it is: not age, not danger, only the mountain’s tiny blue generosity spread thin under your tongue.

Who lives here? The ones who took the wind’s bottle-note and taught it to make weather. The ones who press their nerves to rock and live by what the stone says. Chorus-animals. Seam-tasters. Comb-lipped riders of air made by noise. Magnet-whiskered wetsmiths. Editors thin as a thought. Oar-fleck babies that take their first strokes under a pale roof that flickers once and goes dark again. They do not build, they do not store, they do not keep. They inherit the tune and, for as long as their joints hold and their films do not dry, they sing it back into rain.
