The wind lives here first. Not as weather, but as an animal without edges: a thing with habits and corridors, a spine of pressure gradients, a ribcage of gullies and wells. It knows the throats and favors them. It comes with teeth and patience. It remembers which lips of cliff make what note when the sun has warmed the slab by a hand’s breadth. It has a daily route and a thousand exceptions. It breeds itself in the warmth of the flats and raises its own young in the lees.

Water lives here the way a rumor lives in a city—by adhesion, by cunning, by being everyone’s business. It clings to the mouths of leaves and into the wick of hair. It is caught and decanted endlessly, net to cup, cup to root, root to skin. It never hurries: seep, bead, meniscus, breath. It sleeps in bromeliad throats and wakes as fog again.

Plants live here like combs and cups and fingers. They are not plants that aspire; they lean. They present edge and velvet and hair to the wind the way a beggar presents bowl. Every frond keeps a posture calculus, a tiny theorem about how to receive. The dwarf trees learned lacquer, the mosses learned sponge, the cyphelloid fungi learned to be small enough to pass through the screaming and drink on the far side. Even the lichens have reputations: the black one that throws pale dust at the first hint of a changing pressure, the orange that reads the old cross-beds and inscribes them over again, the silver one that is brittle in the wrong light and shatters under a fingertip. They do not waste their hours on display. When they choose to flower, they do it at the seam of day when the place inhales; they flash their wet throats and let whoever is meant to find them find them by touch.

Animals live here by being particular. There is the rock-vole with its root-snarl paws and the gravity of a paperweight, able to survive the push that would peel other bodies up off the stone. It makes a career of the moss and almost never has to cross an exposure. There is the bromeliad salamander, monk of a green bowl, bone-pale and patient, grazing that single curved interior like a pilgrim circling a shrine. Ant lines write quiet on the stone by following cancellation nodes, sneaking from hush to hush, performing their geometry without caring for ours. The moth with the night-wide wings treats tone like a map; it rides isobars, hews to infrasonics the way other things hew to starlight. The long-faced glider—almost a leaf itself, a deliberate piece of membrane—uses the plateau as a field of soft bumps: it launches only in the held-breath mornings, touches down in the pockets, curls itself to drink as a leaf does, then is gone. There are raptors that arrive like foreign questions from below, break themselves into threads in the vents, knit again on the next turn. And twice a year the hollow-backed ungulates come up to taste the iron seam: they stand inside the roaring like a dream of steadiness and leave with their tongues new-colored.

People live here because somebody always did and because some kinds of knowing cannot be had in the lows. They are not many. Families rather than villages, alcoves rather than streets. They count their rooms by lee, their inheritance by tone. Their houses are folds in the rock helped along—backed with soot-polished clay and hair, throated toward the quiet like musical instruments that happen also to be walls. No doors: just returns sharp enough that the wind, which hates to turn, gives up and runs past. No metal: what holds is what binds. They live with cords more than with words. Bundles of astonishingly strong fiber are their archives and their common tools: knots are recipes, repairs, rules, flirtations. They fasten clothes with bone pins and obsidian shards carried carefully up from the low markets, each point wrapped in felted moss as if it were a child’s tooth. They keep their hair braided tight and hung with indicators—little flags and smalldrum leaves whose tremor will warn you a breath faster than your ear.

They do not whistle. Children are taught early that a mouth can be stolen by the wind. They grow a literate silence: talk by finger, by eyebrow, by shoulder, by the shudder of a hand-span fern held up at the edge of a gully. A lifted palm means Not yet; a leaf tapped twice against the thigh means Path spoiled—gusts braided; a thumb drawn across the lower lip means Save breath—hard tone coming. Love is written with cups and cords. In the days of low pressure there are toys that become instruments: reed cages turned wrong way to the blast make private notes only the two of you can feel thrumming in your collarbones. There are games of threading: two of you on opposite sides of a windbreak lowering a line just so to hear the cape of air pluck it into a shared rhythm.

Their bodies are not bred for the wind, not in the way a hawk is bred for thermals, but long habitation has taught them some things. Eardrums are grown broad and resilient, not easily ruptured; some families pinch their ears with strips of softened bark in the first years of life to teach the tissue the work of flexing. Sinuses are trained more than shaped, habitualized to the rhythm of equalization: the jaw as an under-skin valve that is never fully shut when they cross an exposure. They keep a stance that is almost a dance: knees loosened, toes seeking telltale harmonics in the slab, torso held just off the center of yourself as if you were waiting to fall and ready to step into it. They count the beats of the deep note in their thighs. The soles of their feet know the neighborhoods of the stone, every honeycomb and freshet of ripple rock, the rumors of tafoni under the skin.

They eat lee. That is to say: they domesticate microclimates rather than creatures. A grove is a made thing—aided and abetted, tuned by windbreaks nudging the gale a finger-width higher so it skims the crowns; fog-nets strung across the right fissures to turn air into drip, drip into ground, ground into edible leaf. They tend moss the way other people tend goats: combing it away from brittle thin patches and encouraging it up into sponges where it will not be peeled. They thicken liverwort beds and harvest them with blunt shell scrapers, press the slurry into cakes that will take smoke and not fall apart. There is a lichen jelly that tastes of iron and metal sun, boiled in pots that are themselves lashed to the wall. Bromeliad cups are their wells and their gardens: water, larvae, a scatter of spices they bring up folded in leaf—a little bit of below to bloom miniature cities in the cups. Fat is precious. It arrives as trade and is given back to the place in smears on cairn mouths not because anyone thinks the stone eats, but because better tones make better leaves and because gratitude is a practical virtue. Every function that can be symbiosis is symbiosis: the gliders take the fern fluff for nests; in return, people leave sweet-sour paste under the same ledges every dawn while the air is soft. The rock-voles will not be hunted in times of scarcity because to remove the rooters is to lose the breathability of the moss.

Their calendars are a noise-thing. Pattern-skins on leather show the winds of the season as somebody’s grandmother memorized them, counted in dots and bars like a music you read with your hands. The days are tracked by when the big under-tone wakes and sleeps; the year by the day the thunderhead bellies drag on the table and the whole plateau steams after. There are rites that involve touching your lips to a frond that moves to the infrasonic beat: not to hear it, but to be seen by it, to enter that cadence and know where you sit inside it. Children make small bargains with the plateau by learning where and when to be light, when to be heavy. Initiates must make one crossing of open slab without a bridge, at first light, carrying a brimmed cup unspilled, guided only by the cancellation paths where the scrubgrass refuses to list.

They do not sing with mouths. They do play the place. There are cairn-keepers who go out with pigment and fat to tune the mouths so the worst shrieks will be pulled a note lower. There are throat-minders who watch the wells for hours until a steady tone locks, then wind a little braid across the entrance to split it in two softer bands. There are fog-knitters whose work is visible as a hundred tiny drips from a line. There are bridge-setters who know how to read when a rope will land you in a null and when it will make you a flag. There are story-keepers whose stories are braids that only become themselves when held up to the gale in the right hollow—it is how they remember which aunt died in which season, and in what wind your parents were married. There are pollinators. In that seam of day when a hundred secret flowers open all together like a whispered yes, you will see them move with sticky brushes tied to the backs of their hands, fingers tasting resin and nectar, shouldered baskets shedding a perfume dense as honeyed wood. The moths are there, too, and the gliders, and for those brief minutes you can believe the place was made just so a dozen different lives could exchange what they need without shouting.

Trade is downward, always on the easy breathing mornings. People in the lows think the plateau-folk savage or godled, depending on the year, but what passes up and down the switchbacks is ordinary in its cleverness: obsidian and ochre coming up; fat and salt; a cloth like fishskin woven of some reed from the river; resins. Coming down: sponges of moss bound in leather for the travelers’ roofs; packets of dried liverwort cake that takes smoke well and tastes of pepper; small contraptions of leaf and cord that lowland children hold out their cart-windows to feel the river wind sing. Nobody up here wants a forge—metal pulls lightning and sings its own notes too strong to share. They take other people’s nails to use as fishhooks in the cisterns.

Other things live here that do not keep genomes where we do. The fog itself is practiced. It threads along lines as if it came with a lesson, ties itself into planes clean as glass, takes an hour to come loose and go ragged. People say that once in a while, after a thunderhead has bruised the day and left the stone sunswept, wells of air become so stable you can see seeds hang, catch, sprout tiny pale hairs in nothing, and then fall when they learn weight. It is not common and not provable, and the wise do not go looking for it, because any place that can hold a seed can hold a body. The orange lichen is rumored to count; its chevrons appear in the same order every time the big under-tone shifts a quarter-note, and somebody has surely made a religion of it in an alcove you will never see. There are tales, often told by people whose lives require such tales, about stone-eaters: microbes that feed on piezoelectric whisperings in stressed quartz, tiny nations powered by the translation of pressure to charge to appetite. If you want to see them, they say, watch where the quartz seams are blackened in a way that is not quite soot. If you ask why it matters, the story-keeper will make a gesture that means It pleases the mind.

What does not live long here is what cannot bend. Straight things are snapped, opinions smoothed. Predators are rare in the way that tyrants are rare in a village that has more exit than house. When something bigger than the place arrives—storm for instance—it is either eaten into it or exiled down.

This is what lives here, then:

- The wind, but more like a herd than a god, a domesticable wild that will never be owned.
- Water, redefined as a practice instead of a feature.
- Plants that mastered shape over stature.
- Animals that make a profession of exactness.
- People who have learned not to amplify themselves, who understand that in a loud world quietness is not humility but craft.
- And a scatter of intangibles—the harmonics that move seed and lichen and habit in lockstep with stone.

If you come and stay, you learn the taste of minutes in the air. You learn that the place’s language is not vowels and consonants but eddies and counter-eddies, that everything that lives has a lee and that a life is mostly the making of it. You will see children racing along a rib of rock, cups cradled full; lovers trading tones with a basket between them; old ones standing in a throat and laying a braid across its open mouth like a hand on a fevered brow. You will begin to keep your own small rules and find them sufficient. You will start to put your face in moss when you sleep. And one day without being told you will put your palm flat on the stone and hum back to it, a note that is yours and also not, and the plateau will let you pass.
