What lives here first is a habit. Before bodies, there is the way the place keeps itself: thin-making, bright-keeping, quiet. Lives arrive the way dew does—at points, in hours, and only where there is a surface prepared to take them. You can think of inhabitants as any presences that learned that cadence and fitted inside it.

Some of them are people. They are not many, because this height will not carry many and stay itself. They move like thoughtful blades, conserving motion as if it were a resource alongside water and shade. Thin air makes them long in the ribs; their chests widen low, where you can’t see it under a shawl, and their breath is a skilled thing. The whites of their eyes are narrow. They favor the dawn and the overcast and the last light before night snaps fully open. At midday they are shadows under the rims of rock, or asleep with cloth over their faces to keep their lips from crusting. Their skin runs the colors the plants choose here: old-coin, lichen-black, the diluted orange of a north-facing stone at dusk. Hair isn’t kept for its beauty so much as its capillary: it is braided wide and flat, nets that can drink; at dawn more than one head will be bowed over a lattice, letting the rime wick into braids that are wrung into a cup afterward with a motion that is half prayer and half ordinary.

They live in the “pages”—those low rooms set into slopes—and in hollows that you would never say were houses if you hadn’t been shown the touch marks on their thresholds: a polished brownness where every hand has pressed the same place for a century, a line of pebbles along an edge that looks like geology but is an arrow to those who learned the pointing. They do not have doors that shut; they have folds, turns, controlled transitions. Inside the air is different, and in this place air is counted the way other places count grain. The field-ceilings are as much furniture to them as any table; a fever will be brought home and laid to it and watched as the person cools like a stone at dawn.

They speak as little as the place does and for the same reason. The wind takes the cheap parts of sound. They make dearer speech. There are hand signs that are broad and slow, thrown like nets across distances; there are messages sent through stone—their palms are thickened at the heel, and each person carries a sounding spoon of horn or light alloy, a curve that cups to the rock and makes a private throat where a low word will ride. They know where two gullies meet, where the wind runs coordinated, and they go to those joints to make decisions. There is also a language in the teeth: the old anchors’ hum will step clean into the bone of your jaw if you let it, and the old women and men can answer it with a rubbed molar, a reply placed in a frequency that doesn’t go far and doesn’t need to. When they laugh, it is more pressure release than noise; the edges of their eyes say most of it.

Clothing here is a weathering. They make their own silence. Their sandals spread weight and sip friction; the bottoms are a fabric that won’t betray a rock to itself. Their cloaks are all shade angles and habit, a geometry of ways a body can be shaped to invite or refuse brightness. Some of those cloths are seeded with tiny filings—old work made rags—that exchange heat with the sky at a whim. They do not wear bright; there is enough of that in the air. They do wear small mirrors inside sleeves, to catch light and throw it in a controlled blink that reads as a syllable across a bench. The mirrors are coated so their flashes are not spills but small pours. Children learn the flash alphabet early, and a mistake is a comic thing, a story about how someone asked for salt and got the wrong cousin three terraces away.

They do not farm in straight rows because the plateau doesn’t. Their agriculture is a map of leewards. They ease a cushion-plant outward, persuade it to bulk in a shape that will throw a shard of shade just so in the fourth hour after noon. They extend lichen with a salve that tastes like metal and honey—it is a paste of their own spit, powdered rock, and a yeast they carry in their pockets. It darkens faces where water wants to be, and in the mornings it gleams. They coax roots down the old chimneys with wooden guides, and they lay a flake of bright metal at the bottom to keep the cold pooling there in night; there is a sort of tuber they prize, white as fingernails in the dark, that grows on light stolen from noon and fed to itself as evening. A child can carry one home in both palms, a knuckle of food. The taste is swallowed light, sweet and strange.

Hunting is a thing of hours and of respect for the calendar in an animal’s bones. The knives are not knives; they are hooks of force, sweeping arcs of magnet over a belt that can arrest a lizard without cutting it, bring it into a hand with less blood than it would lose to a hawk. They relieve the microbats in winter by lifting insects out of the dusk over the black lattices with a spun veil that looks like nothing in air but becomes an argument to the flying things that they cannot finish. The people talk with the birds by doing the thermals’ reading for them when the sky is wrong, raising a field over a warm grid to fatten a column and then stepping away, their palms turned outward, the way you offer a tool. Some of the hawks have learned to hover over those offerings, and their large eyes look like a kind of religion and are not one.

They maintain the old work with a manner that doesn’t have the word “maintain” in it. There is no line between tending a child and tending a dew-catcher; you oil both, you listen to both, you do not expect either to behave like a diagram. A thread falls: they cut it into lengths that will sleep in cloth and give their shade an appetite, then they take the smallest piece and set it in the school corner, where the young will learn to feel where a field starts without being told. They swear you can learn to see the edge of a hum in air if you practice—angles change, a shimmer leans—and by the time they are grown most can.

The old installations have become elders in the village sense. They fail and surprise; they are still fountains. The people write their daily gratitude on their faces, but in this place writing does not stain. They write with arranged stones whose cast shadow will spell thank you on the equinox, with lichen lines dragged in a direction the lichen likes, with a fist-sized shallow in a southerning slope where water might pause a heartbeat longer if the air approves. There are formal repairs, of course. There is a guild of thread-climbers—lithe and unafraid, men and women with callus in a ring around the crown where head and cap and rope make a true circle—and their work is to ascend the threads when they thrum like need and rub them down with a glass that does not scratch. They claim you can taste a bad capillary with your tongue if you lick it: copper sour, like a storm inhaling.

Social life arranges itself in cycles of brightness. There are markets at first light, when rime is fat on the lattices and everyone is out anyway. A market without noise feels like something else: goods are laid out on cloth under hand-sized stones, and buyers do not ask “how much,” because price is water and water is visible; a glance will do. There are story-nights that happen at slant-time under a sky you could name by each hard star; stories told mostly with hands and eyes, sometimes with a bowl struck gently and the tone cupped by three palms and passed around like a warm thing. There are weddings that look like people stepping under a thread together and gooseflesh rising along both arms.

Children are the masters of the between. They are sent out in pairs to walk to the thing that looks an hour away and be back by dark, and they return later than they meant to, smiling with lips that crack and are tasted like a prize. Their games are the place’s physics rehearsed as laughter. In the morning they race dew-drops along a lattice’s channel with eyelashes for brushes; at midday they lie on their backs under a cold field and put their teeth against the rock to hear the hidden rills, making bets on which will drip next, and when it comes there is applause with no hands. The first rule they learn is do not throw, because the air is not thick enough to love a projectile the way dense air does; thrown things travel strangely and break respect.

Injury here is almost always a misreading—a stepped-on silence, a miscounted brightness. They carry salves for burn and desiccation, and the old root white in its bowl is also medicine when shaved and let dissolve under the tongue. The elders are field-listeners, those who can tell you when a room is hungry for sound and feed it a whisper so that it does not startle later and turn a voice brittle. They will stand by an anchor and put your hand against it and say Listen, listen, and you will learn that listening here is not done only with ears.

There are other inhabitants that are not people and not mere animals as elsewhere would have it. The sound-eating varnish has its own hunger. It is not a moss, nor a crystal, nor a machine; it is a film that lives by taking the edges off air and digesting them. The people husband it. Once a moon they sing to stones—low, almost below breath—to keep the varnish sated and benign; they have stories from when a patch went feral and drank too much, and a footstep there could steal a heart’s beat for a blink. The lattices host tiny black-winged wasps whose nests are curls of dew-dry fiber; the wasps ride the morning rime, drunk as saints, and the people do not trouble them because they keep a chewing thing away from the roots. In a bad year the people will lay out salt—a pinch the size of a nail-paring—so the ants draw a line through the right places and not the wrong.

Sometimes a person will go walking and not come back. That is not tragedy by default. The rules were clear: predators respect quiet, prey respects schedule, and most deaths here do come at their time. When an old one feels their time slide close, they ask to lie with their head against an anchor and their fingers in the channel of a lattice at dawn. The field will keep their last sweat from leaving too quickly; the dew will touch their nails. If an accident takes a young one it is different, but even that is folded—folded, not forgotten. A cairn goes up that is not a cairn but a recalibration of shadow, so that every year at a certain hour a thin blade of light will touch the place and everyone within a two-walk will know and be still for it.

They are not alone in being persons here. Ownership of the thermals is a calling. There are hawks that have faces like knives and eyes set out of proportion to their heads, and they read a person as another slope. Some of them learn families by their shadow shapes; there are old tales of a bird that chose a house to patrol and for three years took only lizards from within a set of stones that were left in exchange—a tithe of dew-slick rocks arranged behind the north seam of a page-room, a contract. The microbats at dusk are more than soot; they are an economy. The people lift fields just enough to pool insects where the bats work, and the bats answer by leaving guano where there are tubers under a thin blanket of dust, a cycle whose arithmetic every child can do with fingers.

What you might call religion is not kneeling so much as tuning. There are rites of balance. On the first day the capillary threads harvest sea-smell from a storm, everyone walks under at least one and lets their skin go cool—this is called Lift, the Day of Up. On the last day before the dry returns, people step out onto an open bench at heat and stand and do nothing until sweat rises, then they catch the drops in their wrists and throw them into a cistern and listen for the single note of their own salt hitting water. There is a story about how the first people here were not here at all but in a lower, easy place where water was everywhere and sound was free and this made them careless, and the plateau called them up and said Come and learn debt. The story is not told with words. It is a walk that takes until afternoon and arrives back at the door you left from, and you are expected to understand what you couldn’t earlier: that distance was not space but instruction.

There are travelers, not born to this. They can live here if they consent to be walked by the place and not to walk it. They are given a net for their hair and a spoon of bone to carry in their pocket and taught the way to make a word be a private word and not a spill. They are told the rules about water choosing shade and sound being prey and brightness being a crop and the rest, and if they laugh it is not at the rules but because their mouth is dry and the air stole their breath and made it new. Some stay, and some carry away a seeded cloth or a new way of stepping into afternoon.

If there are names for these people, they change. Outsiders call them Condensers, or Quiet-Folk, or simply the plateau’s keepers. They call themselves what their work is. Some seasons, that is Listeners. Some seasons, that is Between. Most often, it’s a gesture: a palm turned down, fingers barely moving, the smallest invitation for the world to come rest. In a place that bright and that exacting, to be an invitation is to be an inhabitant.

And then there is what lives here beyond even that: pact. An old agreement with the day. It is in the stone’s memory of water and the lung’s memory of sea and the way the sun gives heat without force because someone long ago taught the land to drink it thin. It is in the pause between drip and drip. Everything here abides by it: lattice and lizard, wasp and hawk, child and old woman and the threads that climb until brightness eats them. The plateau is not empty. It is full of the precise lives that fit its shape, and their fullness, because it is quiet, looks like grace.
