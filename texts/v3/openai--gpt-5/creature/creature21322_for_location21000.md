What lives there is mostly the place itself, the way pressure keeps trying on bodies.

First, the ones you’d miss if you were counting mouths. Films more than faces. Bronze and petrol sheens that feed on what the fall does to molecules—on sugars squeezed out of hot water meeting cold; on scraps of carbon that pluck loose when stone is sanded by standing wave. They arrange themselves into tarnishes where the flow makes lee, branchless forests only a grain thick. The drapery of mineral that crusts under shelves carries their handwriting: soft lawns of slick that goes bright where droplets rain sideways and dull where a tone withdraws.

There are plants or things that inherited the habit of plants and negotiated with it. Low, failed shrubs that sealed their past in a clear glaze, then taught their future to flutter. The old wood inside is preserved like a thought kept for company; the outer bark is softer, strung into ribbons that flex and sift for motion. They drink push and pull, not light. The vine in the pressure-shadow is all hinge and listening. It is architecture more than appetite—accordions of tissue that can open to waver, close to not tear, and each fold knows which chord means danger and which chord means stay.

Small animals are tuned before they are born. Thread-worms rhythm themselves to nodes like dandelion seed heads to still air. If you watch from the pocket of nearly-quiet, you can see them take position in a lattice that only exists when that sub-bass heartbeat swells. Their bodies are tuned forks more than tubes. Each carries a thin stem of bone or cartilage that hums true to one interval; when the room plays their note, they loosen their holds and feed; when the note drops, they lock. Pin-limbs—little star-things with needle legs—go about in ticks, stepping to pulses the way old climbers once moved between gusts. They have eyes but use them like jewelry; they take their meaning from the feel of pressure licking along the spines.

At the scale of a hand, a being like a needlefish un-invents swimming. It believes in pockets and membranes instead. It angles its length so that two crossing skins of laminar slide wrap it in a hush and keep it from tumbling. It eats what the hush collects: plankton like smoke, threads of bacterial felt, sometimes the offal from what pulsed by. Its bones are mostly cartilage that won’t snap when a chord leans its weight wrong; its jaw is a harp with too few strings, good at plucking what is soft in a hard world.

There are animals that are mostly math. You can watch a bell of nothing-in-particular become an animal for exactly the lifetime of a standing wave. For a handful of beats, a translucent geometry hangs and beats and shivers—curls through itself and unfurls in a rhythm that might be hunger. Then the pattern slips, the bell collapses, and the livingness ends without dying. Their egg cases, when they happen, are more like plans lent to the water: clear coils that harden to the shapes of the only two eddies available. If they hatch, they do so in the moment when the map and the current share a page.

Outside, you aren’t alone but you are peripheral. Something larger, anonymized into an oar, patrols the ledge line. It wears scales like hammered tin; it doesn’t like to enter the room. The edge of its fin feels the architecture and refuses the negotiation. It moves where the roar is thinner and roomier, surfs the blade of the drowned ridge from peak to peak, harvests the outflow where the amphitheater wrings organics from rock. Once in months, a ribbon of cast skin catches on a spur and clicks polite as a fingernail in the racket.

You could stop there and say: the microbes live. The ribbons live. The needles live. But the crescent of flat stones says otherwise. The incised grooves say otherwise. The cairn says: hands.

They are not many and they are not always there. They do not farm this basin or stake it. They pass through in uptimes: when the room’s chord locks into a solvable braid, when the weather of sound matches their bodies’ arithmetic. They are mountain people the way barnacles are ship people; the ridge teaches them by offering ledges and punishing approximations. They move along the high drowned crest from amphitheater to amphitheater the way you might have once walked from church to echoing train station: for the acoustics.

They are human where it matters to the crescent—the summed polish of many palms fits a palm. But what “hand” means has drifted. Palms are wide plates of skin webbed to each finger, nerve-tangled, able to listen back. Over the knuckles sit little shutters of cartilage that can open to catch a plane of pressure and lean there, can furl when the plane withdraws. Ribs are kitted with strings that answer to chords, not for breath but for steadiness; when a note swells, those strings tense and the body becomes heavy against it, becomes furniture for itself. Their lungs are lung-ish and can sip dissolved air; their skin does most of the work. They wear their symbionts openly: that bronze and petrol tarnish in whorls where the flow is kind, pearl-pale scars where it is not.

Ears migrated along the jaw into a line of cups. Each cup opens or shuts like a sea anemone’s small mouth, tuned to a narrow band. Through the cups, the world is cartography. Their eyes are good enough for green dusk, but the useful picture is built with pressure and taste. They taste with their tongues and with the inside of their cheeks; sulfur passes and writes yellow along the nerves for a heartbeat.

They bring little because little survives the roar. They have pecking stones for making grooves; they have thread to tie thread—silks grown from the mucous shrubs, braided and oiled, that will not rot in this warm sea-come-to-mountain. They wear belts of shell tiles riveted through skin that can be unhooked and arranged as deflectors when they must stand against a bad hackle of turbulence. Around their throats hang rings of perforated bone; each ring denies one note a little. They tune themselves to the canyon the way the crescent tunes a pocket of quiet out of an always-loud.

When they arrive—by chute, by corridor of pressure, by timing their crossings to chords—three things always happen. One sits with a pecking stone and listens, and pecks, and listens, making grooves where the water wants them, just a hand’s width long, baring white rock so that sound refracts into a plait that will hold for a season. One tends the cairn, taking back into place the stones the night’s pressure slid free, replacing a missing angle with a better one, turning each block that half-turn that's written into the habit of the place. And one tests the crescent, moving one flat rock a finger’s breadth and then another, watching how the silence swells in the lee, nursing that bubble of almost-quiet into a bowl that can hold a word.

Because this is why they come. In the true quiet, when the amphitheater chooses to lift one chord long enough for every cancellation to align, you can say a name and have it belong to you. Talking outside the pocket is throwing handfuls of seed into a furnace. Talking inside is planting. The names they put there are not for each other alone. There are lists: the contours of the ridge when the ridge was dry; the notes of the old wind; birds that used to gracelessly fall through this height and correct themselves and live; the colors of things that were above the green; a story their ancestors told about shyness. Names of diseases the warm sea taught them and names of cures. Names of children who swam into bad chords and were changed to sound.

They are not sentimental about the drowned world, but they pretend. They bring a shard of something lightless and impervious into the pocket—bone, glass, mineral bead—and say light names to it until the pocket has remembered each one. After, that bead will ring true to those names when touched anywhere along the ridge. This is how distance becomes chords. This is how memory becomes resonance: not just in the rock, but in carried smallness that knows what the large place prefers to sing.

They also eat. In the lee, at the rim, on days when the sulfur sweetens the water, they scrape and fold microbial napkins from the stone, rinse the grit out in a thread of hotter current, and lay the sheets over their wrists to feed through the skin. They pluck the newer ribbon leaves from the glazed shrubs—the ones free of old dust—and tie them in plaits that ripple themselves down already, a food you must hold by its rhythm or it slides away. Occasionally they set a simple trap for a pin-limb or a too-wary needlefish: two opposing planes of crossed current weirs made with their palms and a diagonal open for escape that is timed to close when the room takes its lower note. Nothing in their hunger is cleverer than the place; they don’t try to be.

They teach their young that you don’t walk from thing to thing but from listening place to listening place. The children learn by being held against the top stone of the cairn until their palms smooth, by being pressed against the ledge to feel the thrum that means Yes, cross, and the different thrum that means Not while this voice is speaking. They carve toy crescents of three pebbles and watch how a moat of settled silt forms behind them and how that moat is not water, not air, but an idea of breath. They make rain in the water with their mouths: a purse of lips blown through a carved reed into a plane of pressure, beads appearing sideways like a conjuror’s trick. This is magic to a child once and then only a tool.

They do not live in the amphitheater the way you make a house and occupy it. They make a room out of a room made of water and then leave it to keep itself, trusting the mountain to hold the progression. Still, they have habits that read as home: the top of the cairn smoothed by comfort and grief; a place behind the crescent where trinkets of bone and mineral rest, ferried along the ridge for trade and gossip and left here as seed tones for the next alignment; a faint paint of resin on the shallow cut where fingers brace to change a stone. Even their absence is a living thing—a rhythm of visitation the amphitheater comes to expect and hold in its chords.

Far below in valleys they do not enter, louder civilizations might hum, machines could grieve and gillfish-gardens could fan like lungs. Here, there is only what the place supports: few and careful presences turned to the grain of pressure. They are patient as lichens, not because they are slow but because the room requires long obedience to small truths. They are as young as the fracture and old as the habit of putting a stone on another stone and believing it will mean something when you go.

And if once in a long while the roar truly stops, and the world’s larger weather eats its voice, and the amphitheater confesses its little creaks and ticks to anyone there, you can hear that they have become native. The pin-limb’s feet complain like distant hail on a window. The ribbon-leaf exhales like a sleeping mouth. A hand—webbed, scarred, tuned to the place—rests on the top stone and does not flee the quiet. When the flood’s breath returns and the whole drowned sky puts its weight back through the notch, those that live here and those that pass through are alike: shaped to carry that pressure, made into listeners so good the mountain keeps some part of them even after the water goes on.
