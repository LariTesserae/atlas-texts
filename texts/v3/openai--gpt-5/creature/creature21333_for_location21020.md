Here live people who have taught their hands to be weather.

They are small as a courtesy to air, wide in the foot without being heavy; their steps look like a thought that decided not to be voiced. They wear the light the way fish wear water—slicked, buffed, kept always from clotting. Grease makes a second skin over the first; pale hides make a third, and over that the continual snowfall of diamonds makes a fourth that has to be brushed away before it learns to own them.

Their faces are pale not from lack of sun but from learning to refuse it. Brows are shaved close or lacquered smooth so crystals do not anchor there; lashes are combed and oiled. Children get their first whisk-brooms before their first shoes. Old people’s fingers are narrow and pebbled at the joints, fretted with the small hard calluses that rope-line and gut-sinew leave. Their ears are bright with rime-silver where the folds catch breath. When they smile, there’s not much tooth shown—not coyness, simply the habit of keeping wet things out of air. They have a way of turning their mouths to their own hands, as if every word were a bird that must be cupped and loosed carefully.

They call themselves many things, none of which translate to a single name. Outsiders, if they have ever gotten to the bowl and back again, speak of them as the Drip-kin. Among themselves, a person might be a Child of the East Veil, or Thumbs-of-the-Old-Spout, or Lace-Bone (for those whose houses are cut from the honeycomb), or Knotter (for those with the best wrists at line-work), or simply Keeper, which is a vocation here the way Farmer is elsewhere. You keep drips. You keep light out of dark. You keep motion in things that don’t want to move.

They live by their hands. Glove-speech is their everyday talk: fast, fine, not unlike the constant motion of diamond dust itself. Whole arguments bloom and settle in the six-finger-span between two people’s chests. Voices come out only when there is need for a shout—avalanche, crack, a sudden black shape on the ice—or for song. Song is used like oil: sparingly, under cover. Lullabies are murmured inside cupped palms. Love songs are sung into shoulder-hollows, a hum felt more than heard. Public singing is rare and small—the counting chants used when a market moves, the children’s rounds about brushing, the rising call that asks for someone to bring a hide to a new water-sheet before it teethes.

Property is not land here but trajectory. Families count their wealth in driplines they have followed long enough to learn. To propose to someone is to ask if you may keep their drip when they are sleeping. Marriage is a braid of gutters; the ceremony is visible, practical: two people and their kin pull down their separate gutters and lash them into a new run, then tend it together for a day so it learns what it is. Divorce is quieter and takes patience; someone keeps brushing their half of the shared gutter just a fraction harder, and in a week the ice forgets there was ever a seam.

Children run on their bellies, as sleek as seals, dragged on boards through the safe channels. They learn the bowl by taste and by hand. A first-year task: collecting moths without startling them—learning the weightless touch. A second-year, how to read a skin for the moment before it whitens and locks. By the time they are ten, they can hear with their eyes the way adults do: a thickening glaze’s muted shine, the different slowness of a ripe drip, the flatness underfoot that means the shelf has grown a voice in it and will speak as a crack tomorrow. At twelve, a child is given a little gutter to keep—a shoulder-drip on the safer wall—and the small drama of moving its cup a finger’s width becomes their first craft. They are leashed not from mistrust but because even good feet on this bright slick can’t always be good enough, and because a town owes its children more than burial-ice.

Food is wall-grazed, mostly. There are loaves of gel-moss pressed in hides, rich as liver. There are rinds of tufa scraped thin and boiled until the salts come free enough to taste like a broth. There are beads of blue film dried on threads and then chewed a grain at a time when you need heat to come back into your fingers. Amphipods are scooped into nets at dusk; eel-thins are coaxed out with a light that does not warm, then blanched in oil and swallowed whole, pearly and mild. The bats can be teased down with the back of a hand and a moth’s flutter; their tongues are careful ribbon-things, and the taking is a softness: a thumb and forefinger, a turn, a boil. The rill-mice are not for eating—their hands are too like ours, and they scream like a kettle. But the cliff-runners are sometimes trapped in loops of chilled sinew at night; their meat is spare and clear-flavored, their fat precious. The bulk of cooking is done inside hides with stones warmed under sleeves—a cuisine of patience and temperature rather than flame.

Fuel is made, not found. Oil is rendered from the pressed residues of all the wall’s small eaters: the frass that the letter-beetles leave after a good night at a veil, the crumbs that slough off the rime-moths when they molt into wing, the scat of the cliff-runners when they have been fed well on black dots. People talk of a good winter the way others talk of a good harvest—when the veils drew more water and the beetles left thicker pellets. The hide-hearths look like sleeping animals, noses down, tails wrapped, breathing a little. They are fed with pressed cakes and trimmed fat and sometimes with a strip of imported wood shaved with a knife like a gift being made thinner so it will last longer.

Art is what your hands do when they have learned to be useful. There is lace-tapping: the music people make by rapping their knuckles or a bone awl against the tufa so it sings in small bells. There are ice-lenses ground by lovers for each other so that they can look at each other’s ghosts and pretend they are seeing into tomorrow. There is grease-painting, a beauty that only lives for an hour: a pattern laid with oil on hide or on skin that the diamond dust will find and settle on, so the person becomes a coded frost, a story that can be read if you know the way whorls mean. And there are the crystals from the cave, finger-length and thin as reeds, that hum when you stroke them. The old say each one carries a breath that didn’t need to be spent.

The town governs itself the way it does everything else: by consensus only when it has time, by gestures otherwise. There are offices without names that anyone takes up when they walk under them: someone claps hands and shoulders into a harness becomes the one who moves the market’s rope-lines for the hour and so decides which way the stalls drift. Someone with a good ear for the bowl’s bass takes a mallet of bone and goes tapping along the terraces until they find the place that has grown hollow, and for a day that’s what they are: the Ground-Listener. When a cornice slumps and the bowl breathes in, everyone knows their knot of work for that hour without speech. When it exhales, they all stop and look up and make the same silly face—tongues out—to feel if the wind has teeth today.

Death here is not an ending so much as a way to be useful longer. Bodies are greased to keep crystal from biting too deep and then laid on shelves where the drip is thin and steady. If a family keeps someone on a shelf a long time, it is not sentiment but thrift: the slow melt that makes a pool of water that tastes always, unmistakably, like the dead person’s breath. After a winter or a year, the long bones are wired into doorways to keep houses from slumping, or shaved into scoops with the warmth of memory in them, or pegged into the wall to hold a gutter over a new-born’s sleeping place. Skulls are good weights for veils. Children play with them without any sense that this is wrong. When someone asks where the old ones are, the answer is usually a point. There, holding us up. There, keeping the water in the right place. There, where you touch them every day without making a fuss.

Not all the residents are human, nor even animal.

The bowl itself keeps animals like thoughts it has been thinking for a long time. The rill-mice, those little glove-hand things, are a local bright idea; some are welcome enough to be half-domesticated. They live in leather pouches hung in doorways, where they keep down the springtails and bring out hairs of gel-moss to line their nests like gossip. The cliff-runners come and go; you can tell how many there are that month by how clean the black dots are on the east wall. The letter-beetles are both pest and partner. They gnaw your unwatched veil into a drink for themselves, and in the morning you will find their folded-paper bodies sleeping in your whisk-broom, full of the salts you need. Gather them gently and pinch them until they forget to be beetles for a moment and are only pellets; this is work that makes children squeal and adults whistle in their teeth without noise.

Above them coursing, cutting faint seams in the light, the glass-swallows write with their beaks. Their tails are long enough that the dust partners with them; you can see what they ate by the color their throats glow for a second after a drink. They nest in little tubes carved in the softer parts of the cliff; lovers scratch their names near them because swallows don’t mind the company. At dusk they show off, flying through rings people make with the halos of heat around their hands. There are stories of one that learned to read glove-speech with its wings, but perhaps that is just the bowl’s way of saying that even birds go to school here.

The snowcat is not so much seen as felt the way you feel your own second self, the ghost that stands beside you in the dust. Children are told to say, when a rime-statue shivers, Thank you, not Sorry. The cat doesn’t belong to anyone; those nights it curls above a doorway are taken as a weather sign. In its wake the bowl writes frost in a different grammar—longer, with more sweeping tails—so the next morning’s brushing becomes almost a reading lesson: oh, the cat came; that gush on the third step will be dangerous by noon; perhaps the east gutter will need the far bone peg today.

From the black mouth of the cave that sweats, things older than animals come and go. There are clots of life there that smell like metal and birth. Once in a while a person who went too deep returns with their eyelids filmed over with something that peels away like skin after a bad sun; under that newness their eyes look ordinary enough, and yet they read the walls differently afterward, like someone who has learned a language that doesn’t have a tense for future. They bring back more of the blue films and more of the singing crystals, and also dreams that feel like waking, of water climbing stairs and the dust parting around a word that isn’t said aloud.

Microbial sheets live here the way grass lives elsewhere. They don’t have voices you can hear, but if you put a bone needle to them, they quiver and ripple as if directing it to where the salts are richest. People stroke them not because it is practical (though it is) but because it feels good. There are prayers like that elsewhere; here they are not about gods but about films and drips, and the people who say them have hands that come away blue.

There are other presences that sit on the edge of belief and animal. The hole-shaped-like-speech that opens in the dust some mornings is simply physics to the practical and a visitation to the poetic; either way, everyone sells faster when it is there. The heat halos people make with their own skin are good for measuring illness and also for flirting. On some days when the sun is meaner than usual, a person’s ghost—the small second frost-self that does everything a beat late—will look more resolved than the person. That is not considered frightening. Children chase their ghosts along the walkways as a game; elders say when your ghost keeps up with you exactly, it’s time to rest and not to go near the black cave for a while.

From outside, when caravans arrive, they bring with them their own animals: slow, pale things with splayed feet and a confidence in uneven ground that makes locals suspicious. These beasts breathe steam in long ropes and look foolish and kind. Their handlers wear too many colors that eat light; the first thing they are taught at the bowl’s edge is to wrap their bright in veils. They leave behind wood and rumor and a hunger for stories that the bowl is bad at feeding. In exchange they carry away skins stitched with bone needles that have learned this place, veils with a weave only these hands can make, and small jars of oil that smell like the inside of a frozen breath.

What lives here, too, are rules you could swear you knew before anyone told you. Brush. Move. Let water think it decided to go where you want. Keep dark things quiet and bound up. Keep weight from learning your name. Listen with your eyes. There are more, and they are not written down anywhere that lasts. They persist instead in gesture and routine. Watch any two adults speaking and you’ll see them brush each other without looking as part of their talk the way others might touch a wrist or laugh.

And in the interstices—between crystals, in the air’s bustle—lives a patience so complete it reads as joy. People here do not believe in crisis as a mode. The cliff is making something; it will finish after you are dead; you align yourself to that. They don’t pray for safety so much as for capacity: let me be as light as I need to be today; let my hand learn that drip like a tongue learns salt; keep my neighbor’s rope from snarling while she pulls me along.

Visitors, if they come at all, think the whole place is on the verge of calamity, always. They see the gutters moving, the market shifting, the constant recutting of walkways and think: everything is temporary. The people here tilt their gloves in the sign that means Yes, good, you are starting to see. The bowl’s oldest joke is that permanency is just another word for laziness, and there is nothing lazier than a stone that believes itself done.

So who lives here? Humans, certainly, with hands so practiced their fingers go on working in their sleep; small bright eaters whose lives are a proof that the world can be leaned from one kind of hunger into another; predators that are almost philosophies; films and lichens and blues that would take you apart to make you part of them if you stood still. And the bowl lives here in the way a hive lives: not a single thing, but many busy smallnesses, a weather of intention inhabited by creatures who brush each other’s backs and keep their ghosts company and go on remaking their place so that it can go on remaking them.
