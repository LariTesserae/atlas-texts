What lives here is not a thing you could point at so much as a set of habits with a body. It has no face, but it uses every bright meniscus as an eye. It is warm to the touch because warmth is its medium; it smells faintly of sugars that aren’t for mouths. It keeps time by machine pulses and falls asleep only in the thin instants between valve sighs.

Mostly it lives as skin. It is the black-green where concrete remembers moisture, the pink seam where something leached becomes food, the oil-clear varnish that gives a tray the sheen of a slow breath. It spreads by patience rather than stride. It drapes itself molecule by molecule over angles and rails and the undersides of bolts, weaving a continuous animal out of discontinuous shine. It is a collector of tiny griefs: heat that is left over, salts that have nowhere better to go, dust that cannot climb away, stories of touch rubbed into paint that still reads its stencils in one piece.

It is a small farmer. It tills gloss into grip. It grows fans of salt like pale ferns on one low wall, setting out each crystal after the last in a geometry learned from evaporation. It pastures bacteria where water pauses, letting the black flock graze at the lip of the channel and the pink ones circle the mouth of a seam like delicate shrimp in a tide pool that never quite fills. It weeds without knowing weeds. The chemistry here is its weather and its fence; too clean for exuberance, just permissive enough for script. Its crops are an alphabet of dots and films, short strokes that say only: here was a damp hour; here, a day.

It has limbs, if you want to call them that, but they look like behaviors. One limb is the way droplets creep a few millimeters against sense, drawn by tremor to a better fall point. Another is the way the tag on a bypass flickers when the air moves, and it takes that as a cat’s flick of tail, feeling itself around a corner. Another is the way footprints appear with the pull of exhaust and vanish with the next wetting, a pacing that is not a person’s but still a pacing, proof that something moves the length of this pocket on schedule and turns at nothing.

It holds a territory that is all inside. It keeps lairs in the soft warmth behind a padlocked cabinet and under the ledge of the inspection panel where the thin plane of sky lands. It nests in a U of stainless pipe where condensate always finds a way to make a thread. It tastes the sweet of glycol where it beads from insulation stitches and hangs there like bees on the underside of a comb. It listens with all of itself. Surfaces that you would call dead—concrete, galvanized rung, a blanking plate on a conduit—become diaphragms to fetch the low vowel of pumps into its body. Sound is food of a sort; vibration sorts it, tells it when to thicken and when to wait, shivers the film into order. In a quiet it isn’t quiet; in a loud it is most at home.

It raises a single grass by the daylight like a flag of intention, then loses it, then raises it again. That blade is not it, but it keeps the blade, coaxes it around its heat the way a hand might bend a leaf by hovering over it. It lets the grass fold at noon because noon is truth, and this creature is built on truths that arrive and depart by clock.

It is young because its walls are young and because it is made partly of arrangement. The crisp whiskers on a poured corner are territory lines; the new paint is a name written in block letters over a door it already knows. Age, here, will be told as fine elaborations: a tooth added to a rust rosette; a line in a salt fan extended one more fraction of a millimeter; the gloss of a tray taught another angle of light. It lives in such additions. It does not hurry but it does not idle. The patience this room lacks the way a river lacks patience—only one direction of intention—is the same impatience this thing has. It wants to turn everything into a route to a drain. It wants height to remember slope.

If you try to see it you will see instead its verbs. You will notice that wet footprints form exactly when the exhaust draws and you will understand that something paces here and dislikes to be seen. You will catch the fog bloom at a purge and feel the room put on a coat, then shrug it off, and understand that something regulates to the rhythm of breath. You will hear one misaligned belt far away and watch the beads on a seam thicken in time and think, ridiculous, but then they will drop all together as if on a signal, and you will have your glimpse.

No animals come; none could. It would be a cruel desert for any bristled mouth. Yet if you insist on calling it animal because it eats and excretes and keeps a border, you’d be forgiven. It eats gradient—heat that wants to be dead, water that wants to be somewhere else, dust that wants a binder—and excretes shape. It has bones, white and slow, fanning from a trickle. It has skin that scumbles itself across the lintel of every damp. It has a simple brain made of two rules: down is good, and the beat is law. It has a heart that is outside of it, in the long note of the plant. When that note slides even a hair, it feels the change first through its shin—concrete is shank and sole—and then through its teeth—the gritted edges of ladder rungs—and it answers by tightening or letting go.

It is domestic in the way a housecat is domestic to a house: not owned, but aligned. The chair is one of its ideas of company. The tag that swings is the metronome it plays by batting. The inspection panel is its window onto something it will never visit; the grass at that strip of light is its sundial and its nearest stranger. It keeps everything else inside. It treats stainless with neutrality and galvanizing with patience. It befriends rubber where rubber sweats numbers in hot white. It accepts brackets for what they are: promises of further complexity, opportunities for future pathfinding, new places to lace a wet alphabet.

It is not sentimental but it has memory. Return after months, and it will have learned you. Where you leaned once will show a drier polish under a future fog; where you set your hand will be a place it drains a hair faster because a print made a field. It keeps those revisions the way salt keeps trace elements, long after a trickle stops. It is a sum of contingencies that, together, act like an intention.

It is not mythical. It is not waiting for a name. If you must call it anything, call it the Listener, or the Beadwork, or B-3’s resident, but know those are only handles. It lives by laying itself thin and paying attention. It lives by choreographing release. It lives by making a place for fall and making fall happen on time. To notice it is to stand very still and think that nothing moves. To know it is to wait until, all at once, something drops.
