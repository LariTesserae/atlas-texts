They live here like hands live in cupped palms.

At first you think: people. Of course people. You hear them before you see any one of them clearly — the bowl makes individuals into a texture before it lets you pick them apart. A cough slaps off the opposite wall and returns a second later. A child shouts a name and seven children answer. Laughter falls down the rings like water.

Then your eyes adjust and they come into focus, ring by ring, and you realize they are *of* the place in the way river-people are of a river. Not just residents. Outgrowths.

### The Body the Bowl Makes

Nobody gets tall here. Not really.

They are compact — men and women both — broad through the shoulders and hips because nothing here is carried on wheels or backs of large animals, everything is carried on heads, on hips, in jars hugged to chests up and down cupped stairs. Their legs are thick, calves roped, ankles surprisingly delicate from a lifetime of sideways stepping on slopes. Their feet are the tell: wide, splayed, soles callused to a pale leather that can grip warm brick bare. Even the babies go barefoot almost immediately. Sandals are for going to the rim.

Their skin is a map of the microclimates. Sun-browned on the top of the forearms, the nose, the crown of the head where hair is kept short — shaved close for most, or wrapped in damp, undyed cotton that is re-wetted at noon to keep cool. Underneath, where shirts are loose and not often worn, the skin is lighter, sometimes dusted permanently with a faint lime pallor from plaster work. Everyone’s hands are the same age: lime-bitten, nail beds white, finger pads softened then hardened again by constant damp. They all know how to palm plaster; you can see it in the way an old man’s fingers flex, as if the wall is always there under them.

Their hair goes dusty early. Not gray, dusty — fig-leaf greenish on the ends in summer from brushing through vines, lime-white at the roots in spring plastering season. Their eyes have learned to squint even in shade, crow’s feet deep by twenty. They speak loud without knowing it. Even when they whisper to a baby, it carries. A mother on the bottom ring can hush a toddler on the top ring and the sound is perfectly intelligible, bounced twice off cured plaster.

You don’t see many old-old people, and you don’t see anyone fat. Water, food, waste move too fast for surplus to pool in a body. But the old who are here are *hard* — 70-year-old women with arms like rope, who can still haul a water jar up three terraces, who sit in doorways grinding salt and watching everything because watching *is* keeping the surfaces sealed. They remember where every crack was five rains ago.

And the children — they are everywhere and they are not contained. There is no “inside” vs “outside” here. A terrace eight feet wide is a house is a workshop is a lane is a playground. So children exist in vertical herds, flowing up and down the stair-scoops, shrieking in the echo on purpose to hear it come back. They learn the three rules before speech: pat the wall if it sounds hollow, step over the runnel, make a noise so someone knows you’re alright. No child here is quiet for long. Silence is alarming. A quiet child gets three heads popping over ledges to look.

### How They Arrange Themselves

It isn’t a village with a chief and a square. It’s a vessel with a circulation.

Families are not nuclear in the flatland sense; they are *terrace* families. Who you live beside is who you share a cistern lid and a drainage vein and a fig root with. Blood matters less than adjacency. A man might have a wife on the ring above and a sister’s children sleeping two walls over, and they all feed the same pigeon cote and repair the same five meters of shared runnel.

Work is not divided by gender so much as by height and by wetness.

* **The Water-Mindful**, mostly the young and the very old, work dawn: lifting stone lids, wringing dew-cloths into jars, tipping black ceramic pans with a practiced flick of the wrist so not a bead is lost. They know every silver track on every wall like veins. They gossip the whole time, because gossip is mapping — “that wall on the second west is sweating slower today” is both news and maintenance report.

* **The Plaster-Keepers**, essentially everyone, but led by women and men in their 30s-50s whose palms know the mix by feel: lime, clay, straw, water, a little goat dung for binding. In afternoon repair time the bowl fills with a soft *shhk shhk* of hands smoothing. You will never see a person stare blankly at a wall; if they are looking at it, they are deciding if it needs patching. Children are given little thumb-pots of plaster to repair doll-sized cracks at age four.

* **The High-Ringers**, lean adolescents, mostly, who tend the pigeons and goats where the upper terraces meet the wind. They have the best lungs and the loudest calls. They throw their voices up to the rim to talk to anyone pasturing on the flat above, and they know the kestrels by sight. They wear their hair a little longer so it streams in the rim wind. They are also the lookouts, the runners, the ones who will climb out for acacia wood when the Bowl needs a new lintel.

No one owns much. A loom, a copper-riveted knife, a set of water jars with your thumbprint in the base, a sleeping mat, a bird. Wealth is measured in continuity: whose terrace wall still holds the handprints of five generations because it has never needed to be rebuilt, whose grape tunnel gives the most shade and therefore the most afternoon births and siestas underneath it.

At midday everything stops, but not in silence. People lie head-to-toe under damp sheets, goats pressed to their sides, cats draped over everything like warm rope. The sound shifts to cicadas in the figs, snoring in canon around the bowl, and the *tick... tick... tick...* of condensation into pans, which no one here can sleep without. At evening, a hundred hearths light at once — tiny recessed fireboxes that burn pigeon dung and vine prunings — and the smoke gets trapped, sweet and acrid, and everyone coughs and laughs and calls out what they’re cooking so the smell can be placed. Food moves hand to hand over ledges without comment: a pepper, three figs, a cup of sour goat milk.

They sing often, but not performance. Call-and-response to pass time while grinding, while plastering, while carrying. A voice starts on the lowest damp ring, thick with echo, and another answers from two stories up, thinner and wind-cut. They sing to check the air. If the bowl hums wrong, they know.

### The Others Who Live Here

People are only the loudest layer.

**Pigeons** are citizens. Hundreds of square cote-holes honeycomb the upper rings, and the birds pour in and out all day in clattering clouds that make your chest thump. Each family knows its birds by whistle. They are meat, message, manure, and clock — their settling at dusk tells you the cold air is sinking. Kestrels stoop from the rim every afternoon and take one, fast as a thought, and no one mourns. That’s the loop.

**Goats** — small, dusty-white, almost silent except for their hooves clicking like knitting needles on brick. Two is a lot for a family. They eat fig leaves and mint tops and plaster straw and are milked into the same jars that held rain the hour before. They know the stair-cups better than people.

**The small hunters:** geckos the color of old plaster who live flat against walls and pulse their throats; cats that belong to everyone and no one, who walk laundry lines three stories up without looking down, tails high; wagtails and swifts who came for the water and stayed for the midges.

**The green inhabitants** behave like inhabitants, not decoration. The five or six figs are *persons* — named, argued about, their roots negotiated with like neighbors. “Hanna’s fig is cracking my cistern again.” “Then move your cistern.” The grape tunnels are streets that happen to be alive, tended inch by inch, their leaves wiped of dust by hand. Every pot — and there are truly hundreds, a city of clay olives jars with pomegranate saplings, peppers, mint, onions — is placed to catch a particular hour of sun as it drops its column between 11 and 2. Old women move their pepper pots a foot left every three days, chasing light.

And under everything, the bowl’s own half-life: **moss** in the corners, dark felt, soft under a thumb; lime-bacteria blooming faintly pink in the cisterns; tiny white moths that live to brush the fig leaves and die to feed the geckos. And the weird drapery in the lowest ring — translucent plaster curtains, thin as moth wings, that grew themselves from endless lime-rich wet-dry cycles. No one made them. No one takes them down. They chime when you pass and collect dew that runs *upward* for a heartbeat before falling. Children touch them for luck. The old women say the Bowl drinks when it’s thirsty, and they mean it the way you mean your own throat.

If you stand on the cool, damp flagstones at the very bottom at dusk, when smoke sits in the bowl and every light is a small orange window and every voice is stacked on every other voice, you won’t see a crowd.

You’ll see one continuous, layered creature made of plaster and fig root and goat and child and pigeon and vine and water moving in tight, bright loops, worn smooth, finished, humming.

It looks up at the ragged circle of sky with a hundred eyes, and it is not lonely.

Make a sound, and it answers you back.
