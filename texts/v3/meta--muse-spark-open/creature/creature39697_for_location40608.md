They do not fill it.

That is the first thing to understand about the people of the hollow - before their faces, before their names, before their work.

Any other town would have grown to the walls. Any other people would have pressed row against row until the brick met the shale and there was no empty lot left to cut. These people leave the gaps on purpose. They mow them, in season. They keep them.

### The Keepers

There are not many of them. Two hundred, maybe three hundred if you count the children who are away on the plain for schooling and come back in summer. Few enough that you learn every hammer-tap by its author. Few enough that the square can be called a square even though it is mostly field.

They are maintenance people by temperament and by trade. You will not find a founder among them, no frantic builder, no one dreaming of a second shift. What you find are tenders, adjusters, oilers, menders.

The woman who runs the powerworks runs it at three-quarters. She could run it hotter - the stack was built to draw harder than it does - but she doesn't. She sits in a chair that looks out at the pressure gauges and knits between readings. When the steam needs letting off she does it by hand, at the hour, and the whole hollow hears it sigh.

The miller runs his wheel slow enough that moss grows on the paddles faster than it can be scraped. He scrapes it anyway, once a week, patiently, and throws the scrapings to his chickens who live in the old clay pit behind the mill. The foundry-man pours once a day, one heat, and spends the rest of the afternoon filing the same jig true. The carpenters build houses the way you would fill a bookshelf that is already mostly full - one every year or two, set precisely into its empty lot without disturbing the grass around it too much, as if not to wake it.

They walk everywhere. No one hurries even when the rain comes hard off the walls and everyone knows the gutters will overspill in six minutes. They just pick up their tools and move them to higher ground and wait with their hands in their pockets while the ponds behind the mill take the water.

Children grow up here learning two trades at once: whatever their parents do - winding, firing, fitting, sewing - and weeding. Because the green does not stop. Ivy will take a belt drive in a week if you let it. Ferns will split your back steps. Birch seedlings come up in every pile of shale dust and have to be pulled or transplanted to the scarp where they are wanted. The children carry little knives for it from the time they are six. They know the names of the sparrows before they know their letters.

Old people here do not retire. They become more like walls. An old man sits all day on the iron staircase that climbs the eastern scarp and watches the clay lip bow. He is the one who will tell you if it has moved this year. An old woman keeps the trackway up top clear of peat, even though no one uses it much anymore. They are anchors, ballast, the slow weight that keeps the place from deciding to fall in all at once.

They are not isolated, but they are unsociable in the way islanders are. Traders from the plain come down the cut-road with salt and wire and news, and leave with small, perfect batches of things: a crate of tools, a bolt of cloth, a set of hinges that will never warp. The hollow folk talk kindly, offer tea that tastes faintly of fern, and do not invite you to stay. At dusk they close no doors - there is no need - but everyone is inside their own lit square by the time the bats come out.

And they all, without discussion, honor the pillars.

When that midday light catches the dust and the steam and stands up in the cool air like glass, when the whole bowl goes quiet and the starlings sound miles away and the engines go cotton-muffled - they stop. Mid-stitch, mid-pour, mid-sentence. They look up at the size of the emptiness that is suddenly visible around their little oval of streets, the cathedral their town was meant to fill and never did. They wait until the wind on the plain shifts and the sound rushes back in, clack and trill and hiss at once. Then they go on.

No one has made a religion of it. No one needs to.

### The Tenants

But if you want to know who *really* lives in the hollow, count differently.

Count the three thousand sparrows that have learned that every eave is theirs and that no hollow child throws stones. They pack the gutters so dense the iron sags. Count the starlings that practice their decisions above the square every evening until the sky is a bruise of wings.

Count the pigeons on the stack, white as smoke against smoke. The wrens in the ivy, so loud for such small throats. The shrews that run the belt-drive housings like highways, warm from friction. The mice that live five to a wall.

In the four dark ponds: frogs. Not a few frogs. A parliament, a choir, a wet engine that on warm nights is louder than the powerworks ever was. And over them, dragonflies - long as your finger - hovering, hunting, mating, made entirely of light and hunger.

At dusk, the bats. They live by the thousand under the iron bridges, in the shale slips, behind the foundry's loose bricks. They pour out in a river you can feel on your face and they take every mosquito the ponds breed, every moth the town's lights call in. The hollow folk time their evening walks to them. Children lie on their backs in the empty lots and watch them until the stars come.

And the fox. Always the fox. She denned under the dead boiler three winters ago and found no one minded. Now she trots the empty side of High Street at noon with a mouse in her jaws while the machinist eats his lunch ten feet away and nods to her. Her kits play in the knee-high vetch of the lots that were left empty for them, though no human will say so.

Moss, too. Lichen. Ferns that have no business growing out of mortar and do it anyway. The birch and alder on the scarp that are slowly, root by root, taking the walls apart and making them into soil. All of them citizens, all of them counted in the census the hollow keeps without writing down.

What lives in the hollow, then, is not a town inside nature, nor nature invading a town. It is a compromise that has held long enough to become a new thing - an industrial meadow, a feral workshop, a single room where brick and fern have agreed to share the same cool air.

It does not hurry. It does not spread. It tends itself, it listens to itself in its bowl, and when the light stands up and the sound thins, it remembers, for a minute, how big its house really is.
