It is empty until you kneel down, and then it isn't. That's the first law of who lives here.

No one lives *across* the Cirque. No one covers it. Everything that lives here lives *on* something else — on a seep, on a shadow-line, on the lee side of a single stone that hasn't moved since the cliff above calved it. The room stays huge and clean and full of light and cold air, and life crowds into the exceptions.

Start at the bottom of that daily tide, because everything else hangs off it.

### The water-holders

The true residents, the ones who make all the others possible, don't move at all.

They are the moss. Not a covering, but cities of it. Foot-thick hummocks of emerald and bottle-green and black-green that have learned to be sponges. In the morning they are iron, rimed with needle-ice. By ten they are dripping, saturated, steaming faintly in the bright cold when the sun hits the black basalt behind them. By noon you can wring water out of them with your hand. By four they are freezing again.

Inside that pulse lives most of the Cirque.

Between the leaves — which are too small to see as leaves without bringing your face inches away — there are springtails, thousands upon thousands, charcoal-black specks the size of sesame seeds that unhinge themselves and flip into the air. They graze the moss itself, the algae on the moss, the rot of last year's moss. When you touch a hummock it seems to shiver sideways, not with wind.

Woven through and over it are the lichens. Orange, yolk-yellow, pale-bone crusts that look painted onto boulders and cliff seams where no water ever runs long enough for moss. They grow slower than almost anything alive — a coin's width in a decade — and they taste like stone dust. The pikas know which ones will make you sick.

And then, wherever a thread of meltwater has run the same way for a few summers, there are the flowers that act like heaters.

Alpine cushion plants — saxifrage, moss campion, starwort — that have given up on being tall. Up here height is death; wind strips heat. So they make themselves into domes, tight as fists, hard as upholstery, their tiny leaves packed together until the whole plant becomes a parabola. A pink burst of saxifrage no bigger than your palm can be twenty years old, its roots sunk into a crack smaller than your finger. Its flowers are absurdly large for it, white and pink satellite dishes, all canted sunward. Inside that dome it's 10 degrees warmer than the air. If you put your hand on it, it feels faintly, impossibly warm, like a breathing animal.

Inching between them, horizontal, is dwarf willow. It doesn't look like a tree unless someone tells you. It looks like a network of woody veins on the rock, each leaf the size of a fingernail, glossy. It lies flat to stay out of the wind and waits decades to grow a handspan.

At night, these are the ones who glow.

Not brightly. After the sun goes and the shadow of the western wall knifes across and the temperature drops like a stone, the water-tracks down the cliff keep a little of the day. A diffuse, phosphor-green afterglow along the edges of the moss mats and the wettest lichen lines, like veins under skin. Enough to see the outline of your own boot. The locals know: the glowing edge is concentrated chemistry, antifreeze and bitter compounds hoarded against ice crystal damage. Nothing eats it. Even the hungry bumblebee queens learn to avoid it after one try.

The light is spent by dawn.

### The workers in miniature

If the plants are the houses, the insects are the frantic tenants trying to get all their life done before noon.

Chief among them here is the bumblebee queen. You hear her before you see her — a low, drowsy *thrum* that vibrates in your sternum more than your ears, improbably loud in thin air. She is huge for this place, furry, orange-banded, slow in the cold. There are maybe a dozen in the whole Cirque, each one alone, each one the entire hope of a future hive down lower. She goes from cushion dome to cushion dome in straight, determined lines, spending minutes inside each flower, warming herself in its reflected heat while she drinks.

Everything smaller lives under her notice: midges that hatch in the flash-pools no bigger than a saucer and live for an afternoon, rove beetles under stones, mites orange as the lichen they mimic.

### The warm-blooded islands

Nothing large can stay here. That's the rule the Cirque enforces.

But small, dense, furious warm-blooded things can, if they cheat.

**On the ground:**

Pikas. You never see them first. You see their work — a haypile tucked perfectly under a lichen-crusted overhang, the size of a child's fist, composed of saxifrage stalks and willow leaves cut at exactly the same length and laid to freeze-dry in the sun. The pika itself is a squeak of grey-brown, ears too round, all metabolism. It darts, freezes, darts, stuffs its cheeks with green matter for winter, and whistles only rarely, a sharp *eek*. Its winter begins in August.

Marmots, in the scree where the young cliff has shed its knives. Fat as house cats by late summer, whistling once — one long, piercing note that bounces off three walls — and then vanishing. They spend nine months asleep in burrows below the frost line and the other three eating as if to make up for it. Their scat is full of half-digested moss.

Both of them borrow heat from the rock and from each other and never travel more than a few meters from cover. To cross ten meters of bare, sun-blasted stone in the middle of the Cirque is, for them, a desert crossing.

**In the air between:**

Rosy-finches. This is their air.

Flocks of twenty or thirty, grey-pink, the color of the granite at sunset, they lift off the central house-sized boulder all at once in a soft explosion, wheel over empty air where there is nothing to eat and nothing to perch on, and land again exactly where they started, chattering in thin, sweet peeps so high and quiet you think you're imagining them. They glean wind-blown seeds and dead midges off snow patches and cushion flowers and do not seem to mind the vast nothing between food sources. The nothing *is* their habitat — they specialize in distance.

And above them, always, the landlord.

A lammergeier, sometimes two, turning slow, endless circles in the updraft that pours over the open fourth wall and drops away into the world. Ten feet of shadow. Rust-stained chest, wedge tail. It does not flap much. Once every few minutes its shadow crosses the Cirque floor like the hand of a clock. It never calls. It is watching for what dies — a marmot taken by cold a week too early, a finch — and for bone. It can live off bone, dropping it from height onto the bare stones to crack it. In a place where nothing lasts that tries to take up too much space, it is perfect: a bird that eats what is left *over*.

You feel watched but not hunted. You are too big to eat and too small to matter.

### The visitors

No one stays.

That's not a metaphor. At eleven or twelve thousand feet, nightly freeze, searing UV, no standing water longer than an hour, you cannot keep a human body here. Whoever comes understands quickly.

They leave windbreaks — low crescents of dry-stone, stacked without mortar in the absolute lee of boulders, just high enough to hide a curled person from the worst wind. Inside, old fire stains: a darkening of the gravel where lichen-twigs and dwarf-willow roots were burned fast and hot and gone. No axe marks. No tent peg scars.

At the open edge, where the drop starts, a cairn balanced stone by stone, with a single white quartz cap picked because it catches the light. Further in, near the best, most reliable drip, the alcove: not quite art, not quite shrine. A marmot skull set facing the sunrise, a circle of tiny pink saxifrage heads pressed into clay that the daily freeze-drying has kept like pottery. Stones chosen for shape.

The message is legible without language: *we drank here, we were cold here, we added one small thing and took none.*

The Cirque keeps that too, like everything else, in islands. A little soot, a little worn hollow behind a windbreak, a little quartz that will still be there after all the saxifrage domes have dried to dust. It tolerates visits, not occupation.

If you go, you do what the finches and the queen and the pikas do: you move between the wet, green islands. You kneel at a dinner-plate puddle and find a jungle drinking. You cup the meltwater where it sheets off basalt, so cold it hurts your teeth. You listen to that surprising, indecent abundance ticking away — drip, bloom, feed, freeze — impossibly fast and completely silent except for a bee's thrum and a finch's peep, beating like a hummingbird's heart in the thin, bright glass of air before the night fills the cup again.
