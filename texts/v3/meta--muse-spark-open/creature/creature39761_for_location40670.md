Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing the basin teaches you, and you almost believe it.

No plant. No lichen, no moss, no grass blade in a crack. No insect to trouble the air, no bird to cut a shadow across the tarns. No fish in water so clear you can count the grains on the bottom. No animal track but your own, filling slowly with seep. The mineral films that blush the rocks are not alive, not as you were told alive means. They don't turn to the sun. They precipitate, flake, reform. The air is too thin and too bright and too loud with water to allow anything as soft as a lung.

That's the lie the place tells at first because you expect inhabitants to be warm and moving.

---

What lives here comes and goes. It was never meant to stay.

They were small. They are small. High-altitude people, or people become high-altitude by devotion. Not a city, not a village — a handful at a time, for a season, for a task. You can see their size in the shelters: dry-stacked slate walls to the height of a sitting man's shoulder, just enough to break the wind that comes over the serrated rim like a blade. A circle of uprights with an altar stone in the middle, worn smooth not by knees but by hands passing over it.

They didn't live *off* the basin. No one lives off stone and cold water. They lived *with* it, briefly, on what they carried in and what little else they could ask for. They drank the water that never freezes, though it made their teeth ache to the root. They came to stack and to divert and to mark.

You can follow their logic for a while and then you lose it. Here a groove cut with a chert flake to coax one tarn into another, for no irrigation, no mill, no purpose but leveling. There a cairn raised on a natural pillar until the pillar and the cairn are one continuous thing, new stone bleached and water-polished to the age of the old. Hundreds of them, three-four-five stones high, the human need to say *I balanced this, I was here, remember my hand.* Red oxide handprints under an overhang, fingers splayed, not art but signature, not paint but wet iron rubbed on with a palm that was cold at the time.

They are not here now. But they are not gone either, not in the way the basin counts gone. The rock is saturated and listening. It remembers pressure. Every stone added holds the heat of the hand that placed it a fraction longer than it should. When you stand still you can feel why they'd come: the low hum that lives under the wind and water, that chord held too long, resolves clearest when you are alone and very high and your ears are ringing with thin air. They came to be near the hum. To tend it. To keep the mirror from breaking.

They were caretakers, not residents. Pilgrims, perhaps. Or prisoners of a duty older than any name you want to give it — the Valley of a Thousand Mothers, the Sponge, the Wet Temple. All those names are true because none of them are the name.

---

That is what visited. What *lives* here never left.

The first citizen is water.

Not the tarns, but Water as a single, distributed animal. It does not flow downhill to the sea. It has other intentions. It arrives as mist wrung out of its own evaporation, condenses on basalt, beads, swells, rolls — sometimes up, sometimes sideways when gravity forgets to insist — and gathers in the fingerprint tarns. It slips over lips no wider than your fingers in bright braids, trying, endlessly, to cover everything evenly, to make one unbroken mirror the size of the valley and hold it. It is cold to a degree that should be ice and it moves too fast to become it. It breathes — that tug in your inner ear when you stand still is not wind, it is the basin inhaling toward its center and releasing. You hear its thousand small voices trickling, dripping, gurgling, and under them the splash that arrives before your boot disturbs the pool.

It lives by cycling. Cloud boils up in real time from its own skin, casts a hard shadow that races across the temple field of pillars, dissolves against the ridges, forms again. It has no predator. Only stone interrupting it, only sun drinking it, only cold preserving it.

The second citizen is stone that learned to be sponge.

Bedrock here is not bedrock as you know it. It holds water just under a translucent skin of silica that dimples under your boot and refills. The pillars — those low, close-set columns with waists and capitals, natural leftovers of harder knots of basalt standing in their own shallow pools — are not monuments. They are what remains when everything softer has been wept away. And they are dense the way a crowd is dense. From a distance the basin looks like a flooded cemetery where every grave grew its own menhir. Up close you understand the pillars are listening posts.

Stone here ticks and pings as cold sun hits cold water on its face. It sweats dark beads on vertical faces. It holds mineral pelts — rust-red, sulfur-yellow — thin as paint where water has wept for a thousand years and left its metals. The films are not alive but they grow, thicken, flake, reform, following waterlines with a patience no plant could afford. The green slate rings when tapped. If you tap it and wait, sometimes it rings a half-second too late, or a half-tone off, as if another stone somewhere else answered.

It remembers. That's its life. Every flake of chert left beside a diversion groove, every fallen tower, every palm pressed in oxide — the stone keeps a faint pressure map of it. Double shadows, pale second suns a few degrees off, are not tricks of light. They are the stone remembering where light *was*. The reflection that shows sky when you should see stone and stone when you should see sky in the tarns — that's not reflection. That's the basin showing you its other draft, the version of itself where it cooled just a little differently and the image wore thin.

The third citizen is the hum between them.

You hear it best when the wind drops. Under the roar over the ridges, under the whistle and moan through the pillars, under the drip — a low, electrical chord that the rock itself seems to make. It rises and falls, sometimes doubling. The air over the tarns shivers with it. It is not alive the way water and stone are alive. It is what happens when bright, thin light hits wet rock continuously in a place where the world was not finished being decided. It's the sound of reality fraying and being held, fraying and being held.

The builders came to tend that sound. The tiny towers on every pillar top are tuning forks, counterweights. A low wall become a dam holding a tarn a hand's breadth higher than it wants to be. All of it — walls, circles, grooves, stacks — is husbandry for the hum. Not worship, exactly. Maintenance.

And so: if you stay long enough with soaked boots and ringing ears, you will feel the fourth way the basin is inhabited. By the futures that didn't quite happen.

Mist that pours *downward* along an invisible slope, flowing uphill over a tarn lip without disturbing the surface. Beads that climb an inch before falling. Sound before its cause. Light flickering at the edge of vision as if frames are being skipped. These are not hauntings. They are occupants. Thin places where the consensus of gravity and time has thinned with the air. They live here the way fish live in deep pools — rarely seen, always present, moving according to rules that are not your rules.

That is why every cairn feels both newly built and impossibly ancient. If you add a stone to one, it will be there when you come back, even if you never do. The basin keeps what is given to it. Cold does not mean dead. Cold means preserved, means clear.

Nothing lives here, and it is crowded. Withdraw your hand from the altar stone and the mineral film will, over a thousand years, grow over the warmth. The tarns will keep trying to become one mirror. The pillars will keep listening. The hum will hold its chord until the next wind comes over the rim and tries to take it.

You do not live here. You pass through, bright as glass in the sun, loud with your own breathing, and then you climb back toward the ridge and leave them to their work.
