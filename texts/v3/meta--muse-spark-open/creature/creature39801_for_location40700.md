Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing you have to understand, and the last thing you stop believing.

Not nothing in the poetic sense — not *almost* nothing. *Nothing* with intent. The water was emptied. The air was scrubbed. Somewhere in the design of this place a decision was made that animals were noise, that evolution was messy, that mouths and eyes and wills would only get in the way of what the ocean was supposed to do.

So whatever lives here learned to live without being an animal at all.

### 1. The Ocean Itself

The first resident is the water.

Not *in* the water. The water.

You come up expecting an ecosystem and find a physics. But stay still long enough — let the Wall above you seal back into that luminous rain that is not rain — and you realize the turbulence is not random.

It is behavior.

In a normal sea, currents are rivers. Here, they are creatures that have not bothered to evolve bodies. A low roar builds to your left and you turn — too late — as two masses of water, each a different temperature by only a degree, each a slightly different clarity, shear past one another. They don't mix. They *refuse* each other. A cliff of refraction hangs vertical in open water for thirty seconds, a mirage made of pressure, and then collapses with a thump you feel in your molars. Cavitation bubbles are born in its wake, perfect spheres of vacuum, and die with a sharp crack like gunfire. That is not weather. That is respiration.

The High Water breathes by collapsing.

Vortices bloom a kilometer wide, slow as thought, trying to become hurricanes and forgetting how halfway through. Fronts chase each other and dissolve. Somewhere far above you — but there is no above in here, only deeper blue — a storm is living out its entire life cycle without ever touching a surface. It is born, rages, thunders with implosions, and dies in minutes, and is replaced.

Listen long enough and you hear the language. Not voices — intervals. The long grinding roar when shear layers drag. The hiss of the upper and lower Walls where the ocean constantly tries to evaporate and is pulled back. The deep bell-tone when the water transmits the groan of the embryonic mantle far below. The water is always being struck, and always striking itself.

It doesn't need food. It doesn't need prey. It is fed by the field that holds it up, which bleeds a little energy into it as heat, as motion, just to keep it from ever going still. Stillness would be death for this kind of life. So it churns.

It is not conscious the way you are. It is conscious the way a flame is conscious — it maintains a shape by constantly consuming itself. If you stop swimming, it leaves a pocket around you. Not out of kindness. A vortex, too, leaves a calm eye. You have become a temporary feature in its flow.

You are not visited by animals here. You are visited by currents that pause to examine you the way you might examine a mote of dust caught in an eddy.

### 2. The Gardens That Are Not Plants

After a few hours, you start to see them correctly. Not as sparse decoration, but as the only true inhabitants.

They hang alone. Always alone. Kilometers of empty, loud blue between one and the next — density 0.2, 0.9 of 3, numbers that felt abstract until you understood loneliness has a metric. Each one is a dark vertical smear at first, resolving as you drift closer into something that wants to be kelp and wants to be a burnt tree and fails at both in an interesting way.

They have no roots and no top. A central braid, perhaps twenty meters tall, sometimes more, made of what looks like black glass but flexes. From it, plates — fronds — unfold on invisible joints. They are translucent when new, veined with silica, pale as fingernails. Over an hour you can watch one pump itself full, stiffen, turn smoky amber, and angle itself to the cool noon-white light that comes from everywhere and nowhere. They are photosynthesizing, but not just light. They drink the field.

If you get close enough not to be afraid of being dashed against them — they don't sway, the water breaks around them like they have decided it should — you see the lower threads. Not roots. Filaments, finer than hair, dissolving outward until you can't tell where plant ends and field begins. They aren't anchored to stone — there is no bottom — they are anchored to the *holding*. To the invisible machine that says water shall stay here. They are plugged in.

They grow while being unfinished. That is their nature. The stone world below is embryonic, all sharp basalt and uncut valleys, a draft of a planet. The gardens share its ethic. You will see a plate half-formed, its edge still soft, weeping dissolved carbon back into the water. You will see a whole limb slough off, tumble slowly, and come apart into snow that never lands — it just dilutes, becomes rain inside the lower Wall, becomes air again. No one eats it. Nothing has ever evolved to eat it. It grows, breaks, dissolves, grows again. Reproduction is an accident of turbulence: a vortex shears a frond clean off and carries it a kilometer before dropping it, where it hangs and starts a new braid in open water, alone again.

They are not intelligent. They are tended.

You can tell because there are no dead ones. Or rather, there are, but they don't accumulate. When a braid finally exhausts its ability to hold — perhaps after centuries — it simply lets go all at once, in silence. A dark cloud of plates and threads that for a day looks like a drowned forest, and then the water takes it by degrees. No corpse hits a floor. No shell leaves a fossil. This is a garden that cleans its own beds.

If there is a purpose to them beyond themselves, it is this: they are lungs. Ballast. Regulators. Their translucent plates make oxygen that keeps the water breathable, take silica that would otherwise cloud it. They keep the impossible ocean clear enough that you can see for hundreds of meters into a blue that has no bottom. They are sparse because a few is enough. They are solitary because company would crowd the signal.

They do not know you are there. You could press your hand to a new plate — cool, 10 degrees, faintly slick — and feel a tremor run through it. Not flinching. Tuning.

### 3. The Builder That Stayed as a Field

And then there is whatever lives here that never left, because to leave it would have to stop being what it is.

There are no artifacts. That was the clue. No tower, no hull, no road on the raw stone below that points up and says WE MADE THIS. A civilization that could hold a sea in the sky would not need houses.

Look at the edge on a quiet day — quiet being relative, when the booms subside and you can hear your own breath half-water half-air — and you will see it: a large vortex brushes the underside of the lens, the absolute bottom where water pretends to become air. It hits nothing, and ripples spread across *nothing*. Concentric rings racing outward on an invisible surface like wind on glass. The field shimmers for a second with interference colors that have no source.

That's the resident. The field itself.

It has no voice and no shape. It does not speak to you. It does exactly what a living custodial system would do: it holds, it corrects, it invisibly flexes. It lets the turbulence run hot because turbulence is the point — a churning engine that stirs heat, that keeps the cool constant, that sings. And it holds the gardens at arm's length, feeding them power through their root-threads, telling them grow here, not there, alone, always alone.

Is it alive? The question stops meaning anything up here. It was built by something that decided biology was a bad substrate for longevity. They didn't upload themselves; they tuned a volume of physics until it behaved like an organism — self-repairing, self-gardening, self-sounding. The tech is not *in* the place. The place *is* the tech. And what is a technology that maintains itself for eons, that gardens itself, that sings to itself low and loud in the bright, cold, empty water?

In the old language, that word would be alive.

You came up here looking for inhabitants and found an ocean that behaves like an animal, plants that behave like architecture, and architecture that behaves like a god who got bored of being a person and decided to be weather instead.

Nothing lives here.

And that is why the water is still arguing with itself when you fall back down through the Wall, why the black braids still unfold their plates to a sun that shines through water, why the ripples still race across an invisible sky-floor when no wind blows.

They don't need you.

If you stayed, you would learn to hold still and be mistaken for a calm patch — for a while.
