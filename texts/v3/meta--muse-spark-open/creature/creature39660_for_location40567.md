Nothing lives here the way a house is lived in.

If you came looking for an inhabitant — a body curled in the corner, a nest, a watchful animal — you will not find one. That kind of life tried this place once and left. There is no water to drink, no dark to sleep in, no silence to hide in. Anything that breathes in and out quickly would go hoarse with the dryness, would go blind with the light.

What lives here lives slower, or not at all.

First, the lichen.

It is not on the walls so much as it is *of* them. A crust the color of old pennies and dried blood, so thin you would think it was a stain in the brick until you get your face close enough that your breath disturbs the chord in the niches and see its edges lift by less than a hair. It does not spread. It has mapped exactly the territory that will have it — the lower three courses of brick where the salt sweats up from the tiles — and then stopped. It does not grow. It holds. If you touched it, which you shouldn’t, it would be cool and as hard as the brick itself. It is the oldest resident because it learned how to need almost nothing: a little mineral, a little light filtered through alabaster, a century to do an inch of work. It is not alive the way a fern is alive. It is alive the way a patina is alive.

Then, the ferns.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, and you have to look for them because they are the same gray-green as the dust. They live in the failures — the three corners where a tile has cupped a millimeter deeper, where a brick has flaked and made a cup for dust to collect. No soil. Only wind-blown grit and the salt-fur that blooms on everything. From that they have made a body: wiry black stems, fronds curled so tightly inward they look like fists. They are not growing. They are enduring. Years ago they unfurled to this exact size and then decided, in the plant way, that any more would be extravagance. They do not sway in the draft. They shiver only if you brush them with your knee, and then they shiver once and stop, as if remembering that movement costs water.

These are the only things here that could be called biological and still be telling the truth. Everything else that lives here does not metabolize.

The sound lives here.

It is not an inhabitant that came and stayed; it is an inhabitant that is made, continuously, by the place itself. The pressure above the ceiling — the lungs of the districts on either side exhaling — drags air across the thousand little mouths pressed into the soft brick. Each niche is a flute with its lip polished by a hundred years of that same breath. Together they do not make music. They make a single note, low and organ-loud, that you feel in your molars before you hear it in your ears. It never starts. It never stops. It is the room holding its breath in reverse — always exhaling. If you stay long enough your own breathing tries to sync with it and fails, because it is not a breath. It is a permanence, like light. When you back out, you will hear it for an hour afterward in the quietest part of your skull.

And the offerings live here.

That is the wrong word and the only word. The niches are not decorated; they are inhabited by made things that have become indistinguishable from residents. Little envelopes folded around deliberate emptiness. Little pinch-pots made to hold a single seed that is long since gone, the pot still cupping its absence. Little grass coils tied with hair, little wax wicks that were never lit, little tablets where script has weathered into waves.

No one tends them. No one needs to. In this dry, bright, full-to-bursting throat, nothing rots, nothing falls. Paper bleaches to bone and then stays bone. Clay cracks and then stays cracked. What was placed to hold a prayer, a name, a debt, a dead child’s breath, has settled into its final form and learned to keep itself. They lean against each other, shoulder to shoulder, so dense there is no air between them, a crowd that has stood so long it has become architecture.

They are not alive, but they occupy aliveness’s seat. Each one marks a moment when a hand reached into this low, poured seam and left something because there was nowhere else to leave it. The human who left it did not live here. No human could. We crouch, we intrude, we back out on our hands. But the intention they left — *remember this, hold this empty, keep this safe where nothing changes* — that intention lives here. It has the same still, salt-furred permanence as the lichen.

That is the ecology of this pocket:

A lichen that lives by needing nothing.
A fern that lives by refusing to grow.
A chord that lives by never resolving.
And a thousand small, faithful absences, folded and coiled and potted, that live by being kept.

There are no animals. There are no keepers. There is only what has learned, over a long, bright, dry forever, how to persist without ever having to move.
