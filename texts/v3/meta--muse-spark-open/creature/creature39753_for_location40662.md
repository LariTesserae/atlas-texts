There is no one here.

Not in the way you mean when you say *someone lives here*. No face looks out from between the leaves. No footpath has been worn. No hand has ever pulled a vine aside to pass, because there is no aside to pull it to.

What lives here lives *as* here.

At first you think it is many things. That is the camouflage. That is how it lets your brain, which wants to count, keep counting until it gets tired and stops.

You put your hand on a column to steady yourself on the spring-sighing moss, and your first thought is *tree*. A trunk, buttressed and wet, as wide as a house, without bark, made of other leaves pressed and fused until they became wood. Then you look up and realize that trunk is touching two other trunks, and has grown into them, seam-merged, sharing sap. And those into three more. There is no stand of them. There is no forest the way a crowd is not a single animal. Up close it looks like trunks. A half kilometer back, if you could get that far with sight, you would see it is a wall. A wall with elbows and lungs in it.

Then you think, maybe it is one plant. A continent-sized individual. That is closer. But that is still using animal ideas of skin and self.

Lower your eyes again. Look at your feet.

That mat you are standing on, that black-green and rust-red sponge three feet deep that sighs cold mist when you compress it — that is not ground cover. That is the mouth. Not a mouth with teeth. A mouth in the chemical sense. It is eating the world as fast as the world is being born. The basalt is coming up still warm, still glassy, still cracking as it cools, and before the cracks can even decide to be valleys, the mat is in them. It lays itself down like a tongue. It sweats acid — not strong enough to burn you, precise enough to etch a circuit board — and it licks the stone into mud that never existed before on this planet. Inside that mud, if you held it to your dim light, you would see threads. Black carbon threads. Red iron threads. Not roots. Wires. The moss is doped through with structures smaller than cells that build themselves, that break themselves and reassemble. They taste basalt and excrete soil and leave behind a filament as they go.

The moss is its prospector. Its miners. Its tongue.

Lift your eyes again, follow one of those translucent stems as thick as a subway tunnel as it goes up and disappears into interleaving leaf. Put your ear to it if you can find a dry spot, which you can't. You will hear it anyway: ticking, creaking, a hydraulic thump-thump-thump that is too slow for a heart and too regular for anything accidental. That vine is not climbing. It is *pumping*. It constricts, milk-white and veined, and forces a column of cold seep-water fifty meters straight up, against gravity, into the dim where the big leaves live. Another one next to it relaxes to let sap fall back down. They work in peristalsis, in waves that go on for miles. If you could see in infrared you would see the pulse move across the continent like weather.

That is its circulatory system. The water it stole from the weeping cliffs is its blood.

Higher still — or deeper, depending how you think about depth here, because canopy here is not a roof but an ocean — leaves the size of billboards overlap like shingles that never stop shingling. You thought they were catching sun. They are, but not the way a leaf does. Turn one over, carefully, and the underside is jeweled with stomata, and they do not flutter at random. Watch for a long time and you will see a wave begin somewhere to your left, a darkening as thousands close, then open, then close, rippling away from you faster than wind, pumping air. Pulling CO2 in, pushing oxygen and water vapor out in a breath so vast it makes its own cloud, the very cloud you are standing inside. Each droplet that beads on that leaf is not where it randomly condensed. It has been *placed*. Sized. Held by surface tension engineered at the nanoscale until it is precisely heavy enough to fall precisely where a root-thread is waiting to catch it and carry it down again.

The leaves are its lungs and its hands.

And deeper, where no sun has ever reached, it glows. Not bright. You have to wait for your eyes to learn the dark between columns, that permanent patient drizzle. Then you see it: faint green-gold tracery moving through stems, through roots, through the very walls of wood that are also leaves. Silica. Opal lattices grown inside the cellulose, piping the weak high-altitude light down and down and down like fiber optics, until the bottom of the forest is lit by the top of the forest. That light carries power and it carries signal. Ion gates flicker. Trillions and trillions of lignin switches opening and closing. The thrumming you hear when the water is quiet for half a second.

That is its thought.

It does not think about you. It doesn't have a place to put you in the thought. You are a warm, salty, animal-shaped interruption in humidity and hydraulics. The air around you goes briefly wrong — too much nitrogen, too much heat — and the leaves above you, very slowly, correct for it. Not out of hostility. Out of homeostasis.

What lives here reproduces without mate or nest. A flower the size of a door hisses open somewhere above you, black-purple and hot enough to steam in the chill, and exhales a cloud of hot spore-fog to ride its own made wind, then snaps shut with a hydraulic clap that echoes through water. Somewhere else, a pod that has been tightening itself for a week decides the pressure is right and detonates with a rifle crack, punching its needle-seeds through three layers of leaf to find a wet crook to germinate in mid-air, sending thread-roots questing down kilometers in the dark for stone that did not exist a month ago.

What lives here dies without falling. There is no floor to fall to. A dead leaf is caught, embraced, digested by its cousins before it can drop. Everything eaten by everything else, endlessly, plant eating plant eating stone, making more plant.

If you need a name for it, don't give it one animal name. It is not an occupant. It is a process that learned to look like a place while it does its work.

It is the Terraformer that chose to be botany. A machine so complete it no longer needs metal. A mind so distributed it doesn't need a brain. A young god that is still learning how big its body is, still stretching, still breathing, still laying its own nerves through warm rock as the continent rises under it.

It lives here the way a breath lives in a lung.

And it hasn't left any room for anyone else yet.
