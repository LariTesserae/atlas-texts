What lives there lives in two different densities, which is the same as saying it lives in two different worlds that happen to touch at the rim.

### On The Table

Almost nothing, if you count by acres. And everything, if you count by tree.

The table itself is too old, too flat, too dry, too sun-burned to carry a forest. What lives up there has learned to be sparse and to be sufficient unto itself.

Lichen first. Black-green rosettes a foot across, centuries old, growing so slowly on bone-colored granite and basalt that you could watch one for your whole life and see no change. They are the true permanents - they own the stone.

Then the ironwoods. One every hundred meters, low and wide and round-shouldered like the hills themselves. They are not stunted. That's the trick. Each one is absurdly, disproportionately alive. Umbrella-crowned, glossy-leaved, overfull. Every branch is a hanging garden - orchids, bromeliads, epiphytes stacked five deep, roots dangling in dry air waiting for a fog that mostly never comes. Each tree is an isolated jungle, an island reef of green in stone. Between them nothing but heat-shimmer, honeycomb-pitted rock, and sky.

A hawk's shadow every hour. That's the census. Maybe a lizard flattened to the warm stone, storing sun in its bones.

Nothing can afford to be big, or numerous, or wasteful up there. Life waits.

What lives on the table is built for waiting for water that comes sideways.

### In The Pocket

Everything.

The bowl - fifty meters across, twenty deep, moss-staircased, fern-choked - is what happens when you take all that waiting and give it a room that weeps.

The stone sweats. Warm day air on cold stone at dawn makes condensation bead on every surface. Evaporation falls again as a four-minute shower that was never weather. Shallow tinajas hold dark tea-water for a week, vanish, come back. And so what was sparse becomes concentrated. What was patient becomes riotous.

**The foundation** is not soil but wetness itself:

Moss in a sudden emerald seam where there should be no green at all. Ferns the size of doors unrolling from every ledge. The daughters of the ironwoods, grown twice as tall in half the space because they don't have to be islands anymore, their trunks braided through with strangler figs until you can't tell tree from vine. And the white trumpet flowers - huge, waxy, funnel-shaped - that don't track the sun at all. They track the arch. They exist only to catch dew off an invisible field and funnel it to their roots.

Every surface drips at 06:00 with a thousand unsourced streams. Everything inside is built to catch, channel, hold.

**On that, the arthropod city:**

Wasps like polished amber, big as a thumb, working the bromeliads methodically. Flies in iridescent clouds that rise and fall as one animal, casting shade. Beetles that click when they walk, loud enough to hear from the rim. Dragonflies hawking wasps in mid-air. Spiders the size of saucers who have learned exactly where to string a web between two fern fronds to catch a flyway. Water-beetles and tadpoles in the temporary tinajas, living fast because the lake will be gone in a week - then back again tomorrow.

And the crickets. At midnight they take over and make a wall of sound so dense you have to raise your voice inside a space only fifty meters wide.

Nothing is still. Everything is eating, being eaten, breeding, molting, singing in full daylight. No nocturnal politeness. Predator and prey chase in front of you.

**Above that, the vertebrate market:**

Swallows, swifts, flycatchers by the hundreds. They live in the walls themselves, in perfect spheres excavated into softened rock - too perfect, you realize later, to be beaks. They shriek and dart all day, never still, turning the bowl into an aviary of constant motion.

At dusk they trade shifts. The bats come funneling out of a fissure on the north wall in a ribbon that takes twelve minutes to pass, replacing wings with wings.

A sparse high savanna's worth of life has been folded into a single damp room, the way desert animals fold themselves around a waterhole. Density outside is low. Density inside is overwhelming. The bowl hums. The table is silent.

### What Doesn't Live There Anymore

Scattered through the riot, almost hidden by rot and bloom and wings, are the inhabitants that make the rest possible.

They are not alive. Not now.

Three or four low cairns on the rim of darker, glassier stone with no lichen. A half-buried arch in the bottom, warm even in shade, vibrating faintly when you touch it, now serving as a trellis for figs. A tinaja in the center that never empties, its water domed in a lens above the rock lip as if surface tension forgot how it works. A perfect cylinder of empty air two meters above the deepest pool that dragonflies swerve around. Walls that cancel shadow at night, as if they remember being liquid.

Whoever smoothed the table long ago decided to keep this one pocket from ever smoothing. From ever drying. They engineered a cistern, a nursery, a conservation project - a place held forever at late-summer, forever mid-morning after a rain that never fell - and then left.

What lives there now is not them. What lives there is what moved into the maintenance system after the maintainers were gone. The moss grows thickest where condensation drips most regularly off an invisible field. The swallows nest in the spheres because the spheres are perfect. The white trumpets orient to the arch because the arch is still warm.

The rules inside are slightly edited - 1% lighter above the pool, sound echoing half a second too long, stones taking a fraction too long to hit water - and life has learned to lean into the edit. To breed in the edited gravity. To hunt in the edited echo.

Outside the pocket, the world is ancient, flattened, and drying toward stillness.

Inside, it's still at its peak.
