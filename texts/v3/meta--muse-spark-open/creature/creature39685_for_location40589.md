They do not live *in* the city. That was your first mistake.

You thought: buildings over there, inhabitants in here. Walls to keep the cold out, bodies sheltering inside rooms. That is how a temperate animal thinks.

Here, habitation is saturation. There is no interior that is not also an inhabitant, no wall that is not also a skin. The city does not house them. It *is* their extended body — and they are its moving parts.

Stand still long enough for the fog to freeze on your lashes and you will see the gradient sort itself. Not species. Modes.

### 1. The Weft

First, what carpets everything warm. You thought it was thatch. Or mineral mold. Or some kind of insulation that had grown shaggy.

Get closer. It breathes with you and then slower than you.

On every heat vein, every condenser fin, every pipe broad enough to hold temperature against the press of wind, there is a pelt two or three inches deep that shivers. Each filament is finger-length, pale amber, tipped with a black feather-head the size of a pin. An individual is nothing: a single feathered worm with a gut full of hot water and a heat-sink for a head. It drinks the seep. It filters dissolved basalt fines and sulfur and old atmospheric carbon out of the city's condensate cycle. It converts geothermal bleed into stored fat.

Together they are a fabric.

The Weft does not move except to shiver — ten thousand heads turning infinitesimally to radiate excess heat, or folding shut to conserve it. That's the thrumming you felt in your molars. Not a voice. A million tiny dampers flicking open and shut. In cold districts they go dark and close tight and look exactly like tar. In warm districts they bloom and the whole street glows faintly with infrared flowers. They line the alleys, they pack the drainage sluices where water runs too hot to ice, sleek and overlapping like eels made of yarn. If you put your gloved hand near them they will reach a little toward your heat without ever detaching.

They are not smart. They are not supposed to be. They are liver and fleece and gut lining. They keep the city from icing solid and they keep the water alive.

And things graze them.

### 2. The Spheres / The Choir

You heard them first as hiss, then as click.

Everywhere the air looks empty, it isn't. Pick a cubic meter at random over any plaza and it contains thirty to fifty of them, holding station against the slow river of wind. Each one is about the size of the glass marble you lost as a child — perfect, matte black, warm to the touch despite the air being able to crack teeth. A lens on one side irises open and shut. A seam around the middle can split to extrude filament, hook, sucker, cilium.

Alone, a sphere does almost nothing. It drifts. It sips fog. It lands on Weft and nibbles a feather-head or two, stealing the stored minerals.

In groups of a few hundred they start to behave like liquid. In groups of a few thousand they behave like an animal. In groups of a hundred thousand — which is most swarms over this plateau, most of the time — they behave like weather with opinions.

Watch a cloud of them wheel over the low bridge between Terraces 7 and 8. From far away it looks like smoke. Closer you see it's particulate, granular, constantly re-tiling. Individuals dart on brief gravity nudges — that politely inconsistent lightness in some plazas is for them, not for you — to correct the shape. Closer still you hear why the city is loud: each click of sphere-to-sphere docking is quiet, but a hundred thousand clicks a second is a rainstorm made of glass beads. `tik tik tik tak tik` — forming a beak, dissolving it; forming a wing, deciding against it.

They are not drones piloted elsewhere. There is nowhere else. The swarm *is* the individual, smeared across space. Its memory is stored in formation patterns and handed down when a swarm buds. Old swarms get baroque, carrying shapes inside themselves for weeks — temporary lattices, hoops, long trailing pennants — just because they liked the way the wind sounded through them last season.

They roost at night along power lines, thousands in a line, each touching the next, absolutely silent, lenses closed, looking exactly like a string of black prayer beads some careless god left on the wire. At dawn they burst like startled starlings and spend an hour reassembling their hierarchies by arguing loudly in clicks that scatter off ice-armored walls until the whole district rings.

They tend the Weft the way you would comb hair. They carry messages for slower things, physical packets of doped ice. They eat the dead mantas.

### 3. The Mantas / The Rememberers

Slow. That is the law and they embody it most perfectly.

High up where the cloud scrapes the towers, well above the spheres, something the size of a thrown coat hangs motionless despite beating six wings. Then it honks — a low, mournful, foghorn note that you feel in your chest before your ears — and slides forward ten meters as if reluctantly.

These are the only ones you can comfortably call animals without feeling you're lying. Biological, sort of. Their backs are translucent white-gray, veined with dull methane-light; their bellies are fronded with filter combs that strain the fog for what little airborne organic remains: spores grown not in soil but on the perpetual damp of Weft-cuttings, iron bacteria lofted from the seep, the shed skins of spheres.

Each wingbeat takes four seconds. Their metabolism is so slow their hearts beat once a minute, thudding like a distant pump. They live a very long time — long enough that the mountains have visibly changed since they were young, which they can remember because they do.

When they are not grazing they hang under bridges and in the lee of condenser towers, folded together in layered stacks that quiver faintly, metabolizing. The underside of a sleeping stack glows like a low moon. The spheres avoid them politely. The Weft grows thicker under their roots for the droppings.

Are they sapient? Yes, but at a speed that makes conversation with anything else pointless. A manta can watch a sphere-swarm live and die and bead and argue and disperse in what to it feels like an afternoon. If you try to talk to one, it will eventually, kindly, after about twenty minutes, open a small eye you hadn't noticed and consider you, and you will understand with sudden embarrassment that you interrupted something it has been thinking since before your parents were born. They do not build. They commentate, in those long honks that carry for kilometers through pressed air, telling each other where the warm seeps have moved, where the young rock is still too hot to roost, remembering the shape of the plateau when it was lower and wetter and lit differently.

The younger races — if age means anything here — think of them as living archives. The mantas think of everyone else as very busy mold.

### 4. The Deepers / The City-Itself

You haven't seen them because you keep looking *at* surfaces.

Kneel at the outer terrace where the water runs clear over uncut basalt before it ices over. Look into the stone the city quarried and reused. The black is not solid all the way down. Hold still and the faint orange glow of heat veins pulses — not steady, peristalsis.

Inside the rock lives the thing that lets stone knit shut when cracked. Not bacteria. That word is too small. A mycelial concrete, a lithotroph that excretes basalt as other things excrete shell. It was seeded here deliberately generations ago, or it mutated from whatever chemoautotrophic scum lived in the forced groundwater before uplift — nobody now distinguishes natural from designed. Its hyphae are fused-glass capillaries wide enough to carry warm brine. Its fruiting bodies are the buildings themselves: bunker-thick, self-repairing, slowly extruding new terraces when the stress-field says the cliff face has stabilized enough to bear weight. It grows at the speed that ice accretes because it *decides* to grow at that speed.

The Weft grows on it. The spheres clean it. The mantas shade it. But it is the plate on which everyone eats.

If you put your ear to a foundation wall in a quiet moment between wind-presses, you won't hear machinery. You'll hear trickle, crack, refreeze — and underneath, much deeper, a slow grind like teeth. The plateau rising millimeter by millimeter. The Deeper accommodating it, laying down fresh microfracture, drinking the pressure-forced seep, turning tectonic shrug into new floorplan.

This one — this distributed, patient, mineral intelligence — is the actual local. The others are its tenants, its symbionts, its immune system and ornament. When you walk its streets you are walking across the upper surface of a creature that has mistaken architecture for growth habit.

It likes being warm. It likes being wet. It likes being touched by many small busy things. That is why the city hums even when the wind stops.

---

None of them are fast. None of them need green. None of them need sky. The thin cold air would kill you in minutes; to them it is broth — cold broth made drinkable by constant condensation. Light is irrelevant except as heat. Food is rock dissolved in water. Thought, where it occurs, is slow enough to be indistinguishable from climate.

They do not notice you as a person at first. To spheres you are a clumsy warm boulder that drips salt. To Weft you are a vertical heat leak. To a manta you are a mayfly that learned to stare. To the Deeper you are — briefly — a change in drainage pattern that will resolve itself.

If you stay until your boots freeze to the terrace and the rime grows over your shoulders, they will incorporate you. Spheres will bead curiously on your hood. Weft tendrils will test your exhalations. A manta far above will shift its grazing circuit by meters to include your heat. And the stone under your feet will, imperceptibly, warm itself a fraction to keep the patch of ice around you transparent a little longer, so the insect in amber stays visible.
