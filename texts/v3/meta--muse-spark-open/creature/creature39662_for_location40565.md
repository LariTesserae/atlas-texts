Who lives there is a smaller list than should be possible, and that is the point.

### The People

Thousands — that is the right scale. Not a tribe clinging to an oasis, but a city dispersed until it barely looks like a city. You don't meet them in a crowd. You meet them singly, or in knots of three or four, and the quiet does the rest.

They are not stunted by altitude or lean from scarcity. This is a warm, wet, fed place two miles up. They are well-fed, broad-shouldered people made taller by the work of fitting basalt without mortar. Their skin stays damp most of the day and they don't mind it. They wear light, loose weave — cotton and pounded reed-fiber and a kind of softened bast from the marsh — clothes that can be soaked by mist at 10am and dry on the body by noon without ever really chilling. Most go barefoot. The stone is warm even in shadow, the stepping stones worn to a dark polish by soles. Shoes would fill with water anyway.

You can tell a plateau native by how quietly they move. In a place with no birdsong, no insects, no dogs to mask a sound, every footfall carries. Children learn early not to shout; a child's voice will carry half a mile over flat water. Adults speak low, and often not at all. The loom clacks. The pestle thuds. The weir trickles and stops. That is a conversation. 

There are no herders here. No hunters, no fishermen, no beekeepers, no poultrymen. There is no word for most of those things that isn't borrowed and half-mythical. Their economy is plant and stone and water.

* **The water-tenders.** The most respected work is not growing, but leveling. The terraces that step the eastern bowl are held to a tolerance you can see in the still water — each one a mirror held perfectly horizontal. The weirs of notched wood that let one terrace spill to the next are communal law made physical. A family that takes too much leaves a line on a cistern wall, visible to everyone. Old men and women whose backs can no longer lift stone sit by the log aqueducts on stone piers and watch the flow, clearing liverwort with a reed rake, listening for a change in pitch that means a joint has slipped.

* **The growers.** Taro and paddied rice in the terraces, starch-reed beaten into pale cakes, lilies whose tubers are roasted, ferns harvested young while still coiled. Nothing is slash-and-burned because nothing needs to be cleared aggressively. A fern as tall as a man will grow alone in a fissure and not crowd its neighbor. The growers don't weed so much as thin and guide. The yield is the same every month, because there are no seasons. Harvest is not an event. It's what you do on Tuesdays.

* **The builders.** With basalt everywhere and metal nowhere, stone is their literacy. You can read age in a wall — how tight the courses are, how pale the lime mortar has been washed. The plaza near the center, where three fissure-springs converge under tight-joined slabs, and the long hall with thirty-foot timber posts adzed smooth, were not built by a king. They were built the way the aqueducts were: by agreement, a little at a time, because there was room and time and no reason to stop. Every house has its one-foot plinth and its little gutter. Children are taught to lay a course before they are taught to read.

* **The keepers of emptiness.** For a city, what they value most is interval. Between any two houses a lot left open. Between quarters, five minutes of only reeds and sky-mirrored water. They don't fill it in. They could — the plateau is the size of a county — but they have learned the equilibrium depends on not pushing. A crowded terrace silts. A choked fissure floods. So they live sparsely on purpose, tending buffers of marsh and pavement as carefully as they tend fields. Privacy here isn't walls. It's distance left alone.

Psychologically, they are a people of long patience. Nothing rots, nothing erodes, nothing booms or crashes. The thatch stays thatch though soaked daily. A sweaty palm-print on warm basalt will still be there next year, faintly dark. You get the feeling if you ask a grandmother when the hall was built, she'll say, "Before," and mean ten years or a hundred — the difference has stopped mattering. They are not in a hurry to become something else because the place isn't. Their rituals, when you finally see them, are small water rituals: pouring a cup back into a cistern, unblocking a spring-sweat on a wall with a thumb, laying a white flowering vine over a threshold again after the mist has taken it.

They are healthy in a way that should be impossible. No animal vectors means no zoonoses, no swarms. No rot means no mold in lungs, though the moss on the north wall is an inch thick. Diet is entirely vegan by necessity — no milk, no eggs, no meat, no honey. They have learned to get fat from starch and oil pressed from lily nuts, to get salt from the deep-cut cisterns where minerals gather. Babies are few. Not because they are starving, but because they don't need to outbreed death. Death is quiet here too.

And they are alone in a way that presses on the ears. At night the town floats — stars above, stars reflected below in every paddy and fissure-pool, houses stitched between like dark paper cutouts. No owls. No crickets. A couple making love tries to be silent without knowing why. A grieving person learns that wailing just goes on and on over still water with nothing to answer it.

### The Plants — Lush Without Crowding

What lives besides people is almost only plant, and even the plants seem to have agreed to this truce.

On bare pavement: crusts of liverwort like bright green rafts, mats of moss so velvety you want to put your cheek to them, lichen pale as the stone itself, holding water in beads.

In cracks and fissures: single sovereign ferns, unfurled and beaded, each occupying its own slot; clumps of cotton-headed grasses; reeds in the marshes growing straight out of flooded rock with no soil, roots spidering into cracks.

In tended water: taro — broad, emerald, orderly; rice; the starch-reed they pound for cakes; lilies with waxy, sky-mirroring leaves.

On houses: white and pink flowering vines, scentless — there is nothing to perfume for. No bee will come. Wind does the pollination here, a slow, damp wind that carries pollen from one solitary fern to the next. Water carries seed along weirs and log aqueducts. The result is eerie perfection. Every leaf looks tended even where no hand has touched it, because nothing has to compete desperately for light or water. Abundance without jungle.

What doesn't live here is almost more important: no trees but those few whole trunks brought for the hall. No shrubs that sucker and tangle. And nothing that eats the plants except people. No caterpillars lace the taro. No borers in the thatch. No birds to take seed.

Which is why the thatch doesn't rot.

### The Almost-Nothing

That clean-to-emptiness in the water is not poetic. Hold a cup of it up. Six inches to a foot over flooded pavement, warm, still, perfectly clear. No wriggling. No tadpoles. No larvae stitching the surface. No algae bloom. The cisterns covered with slabs stay clean without scrubbing.

Decay itself is muted. In any normal warm, wet place, thatch thick and steep would blacken in a season, basalt would flake with frost and biofilm, plunge pools would be chewed under the hundred weeping waterfalls on the rim. Here waterfalls have no plunge pools. Fissures don't widen. The young, sharp, jointed escarpment stays young and sharp.

So the smallest inhabitants — bacteria, fungi, the decomposers that should be everywhere — are either absent, or throttled, or simply... not invited. The stone sweats pure water. The air is mist without spore. That is why sweat dries slowly but doesn't sicken, why moss can be an inch thick but doesn't advance, why reeds grow and are harvested and grow again at the same rate forever.

A city of several thousand shouldn't be able to live on plants alone on fresh rock with no soil. And yet.

What lives there, then, is this: a human city learned to live like a plant — rooted, spaced, patient — in a place where only plants and humans were ever meant to live, where the usual noisy, biting, blooming, rotting entourage of the world was left below when the plateau was lifted. Or removed. Or never made.

You stand at the edge where the cliff falls away in steps still square-shouldered, watch a hundred thin braids of silver slip over the lip and never gully the stone, and understand: the people aren't surviving this impossibility. They are part of it. They are the animal that belongs to a place with no animals, the keepers of intervals on a table of stone that shouldn't be wet and shouldn't be warm and will go on being both, exactly as it is, while they go on laying one more course of basalt, watching one more weir trickle, listening to the plink of a droplet into a pooled fissure that has never gotten any deeper.
