They do not live *on* the plain.

They live *as* the plain.

At first your mind wants a hierarchy — predator and prey, builder and dweller, something to call master of this table. That is a lowland idea. Up here, after a billion years of sanding down, everything has been pressed flat into a single layer of living varnish.

### The Ones Who Never Leave The Rock

Lift nothing. Just look long enough and you will see the stone is not stone.

Under every exfoliated flake of gneiss, where the underside stays iron-cool and damp, there is a country. The flake itself is the roof. The lichen that paints the north face of every dome — mustard, ash, rust — is not decoration. It is the pasture.

Grazing it are the pale moths. They are not moths. Their wings have lost any need for flight beyond a single, silent hop. They are the color of dry paper, as thin as ash, and they pack themselves edge-to-edge under the flakes, dozens to a square foot, breathing in unison. You will not see them until you see one sheet lift — a whole shingle of living tissue peeling off the rock at your footstep, hovering for a breath, and settling again exactly one foot away where another flake waits. They are lungs. They drink dew off the stone and exhale something that keeps the underside damp for everyone else.

Below them, in the grit of the pans, live the Processionals.

You called them pill-bugs. That is close enough for their shape — segmented, domed, the size of the joint of your thumb, with a slate-grey armor that is actually the same mineral varnish the domes grow, secreted and polished by generations. There are millions of them, but you will never see a swarm. You will see a line.

A single-file line, three or four animals wide, moving at the pace of a minute hand from one damp pan to the next, following a highway you cannot see until the light hits it right — a faint polish in the rock where a million feet have worn the quartz smooth. Another line crosses it at a right angle. They do not stop. They do not collide. At the intersection the two lines interleave, one-two, one-two, like fingers lacing, without losing step. Around your boot they part and rejoin on the other side as if your boot were not there, as if you were a temporary boulder that had always been there.

If you kneel and watch one individual, you see it is not carrying food. It is carrying building material. A grain of white quartz. A fleck of mica. A single, still-wriggling midge larva held gently in its mandibles, not to eat, but to deliver. They are not foragers. They are couriers. The blood of the plain moving through open veins.

Where do they go?

To the builders.

### The Architects

Every pan has a center. Every dome has a high point. And at almost every center, out of sight of the next until you crest the whale-back of the next dome, there is a structure.

You noticed three kinds. There are really one kind, seen at different ages.

The builders themselves you will almost never see. They are inside the third kind — the hive — and they only come out when you are not looking, or when the light has gone red and long. If you are very still for a very long time beside one of the cool-breathing sand domes, you might see the entrance darken.

They are not termites. They are not ants. They are soft, eyeless, the color of wet sand, with forelimbs turned into trowels. A dozen to a hive, no more. They never leave the pan they were born in. Their entire life is to take what the Processionals bring — one white pebble, one grain at a time — and arrange it.

First, they make the cairn. Three or four flat flakes balanced impossibly on edge, in a place where wind should have taken them. It is not a marker. It is a try. A plumb line to test if the pan is still breathing dampness evenly. If the cairn stands for a season without toppling from the stone's own daily expansion-tick, the pan is stable.

Then they rake the spiral. A meter across, perfect as a fingerprint, grooved into the grit with their own bodies, one slow circuit a day. The single white pebble the couriers brought is placed at the center. It is not decoration. It is condensation engine. At night the white stone cools faster than the pink-grey gneiss. Dew forms on it first, runs down the spiral grooves, and waters the whole pan for a foot around. The cushion plant at the edge of the spiral — that perfect, waxy disc a foot across — is not wild. It was planted there, or rather, allowed to remain, because its roots hold the spiral from erasing. Every cushion plant you saw is the same size because every builder keeps its garden pruned to exactly that size. No larger or it would shade the dew-pebble.

And finally, if the spiral holds water for a generation, they cement the hive. Sand grains glued with spit and lichen paste into a beehive dome, one round mouth facing east to catch the first sun and exhale the night's stored coolness. You felt that breath. Inside, in absolute dark, they tend a mat of translucent eggs that is also the mud itself — that writhing, mouth-open mat you saw in the damp pans. That is not a separate species. That is their young. And the young of the moths. And the young of the Processionals. Communal nursery. The plain pools its children in the same damp pans, whatever their parentage. Dragonfly nymphs share the puddle with builder grubs, mouth to mouth.

Weirdness is not an event here. It is husbandry.

### The Watchers

This brings us to the still ones.

On every cushion plant. On every quartz vein that rises a half-inch above the dome like a white scar. One per territory. Pale, long-limbed, mantis-like, the color of dry gneiss with a faint pink along the joints.

They are not lizards. They are not insects grown large. They are the adults.

Everything else you saw — moth, courier, midge, builder grub — is larval. This is what they become if they survive the pan, if they are not delivered as food, if they are chosen. One per dome. No more, ever. The spacing is exact because they can see each other, dome to dome, for a mile, and they will not tolerate a neighbor closer. They do not need to move. They have waited a hundred million years to achieve a hunting method that wastes zero energy.

They do not stalk. They do not pounce. They face outward and wait, utterly still, for days. Their heads do not appear to move because the movement is inside the great, faceted eyes that never blink.

Above them, the air has become an organ of the plain.

The pillars of midges, gnats, nameless hatching things — they are not swarms. They are livestock. They hatch from those communal nursery pans in vertical columns that hang motionless in the warm, heavy air, held by the synchrony of a million tiny wings beating just below hearing. That pressure you felt, not a buzz — that is the plain humming to keep its food aloft. The pillars do not drift with the wind because the insects are not flying. They are treading air, exactly countering it, the way a river fish holds in current. Each pillar belongs to a watcher. A territory marker made of meat.

When the watcher flicks its tongue — too fast for you to see — it does not hunt. It harvests. One missing gnat at a time, the pillar thinning imperceptibly over three days until it is gone and another hatches from the pan below to replace it. No struggle. No cry. The midges do not flee because flight would be wasteful. They were born to hang there until taken.

This is why there are no swallows here. The niche is filled, perfectly, politely, by something that never needs to fly anywhere.

### The Memory

You asked who lives here. The answer is: a single, distributed negotiation that has learned to look like many animals.

There is no queen. No brain. The lichen feeds the moth that keeps the flake damp for the courier that feeds the builder that waters the cushion plant that shades the nursery that hatches the pillar that feeds the watcher that watches the lichen so no one overgrazes.

If you stay until the long light, you will see what they have been doing with the mica. At a low sun angle, every fleck in every dome catches light at the same instant — a blink that runs from horizon to horizon, dome to dome, faster than any signal should travel over that much stone. It is not reflection. The watchers are adjusting their posture by a fraction of a degree, all at once, catching the sun and signaling *still here* to the next dome. The plain remembering, for a second, that it was once mountains standing shoulder to shoulder, passing pressure along a single range.

They are not building cairns and spirals and hives because they need shelter. The stone already gives them that. They are building because building is how this place thinks. A slow, patient mending of the varnish. A prayer wheel made by creatures with no gods that you would recognize, but with a very clear sense of maintenance.

Sound is unnecessary because everyone already knows where everyone else is.

If you leave a boot print in the cracked mud of a pan, by morning the spiral will have been re-raked around it. The couriers will have detoured. The watcher on that dome will have shifted its gaze two degrees to include you in its count.

You were not an intruder. You were briefly, and very gently, incorporated.

And when you surface back down off the plain, the ticking of the stone cooling behind you will sound, for a little while, exactly like breathing.
