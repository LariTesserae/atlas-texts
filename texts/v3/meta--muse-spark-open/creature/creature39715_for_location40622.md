No one lives here the way a village lives. 

That is the first thing you have to unlearn about a place like this. A house holds people. A field holds people part of the year. This does not hold them. It *uses* them for a few hours at a time, and then lets them go.

If you stayed long enough to see a full cycle — from the cold mist that beads at false dawn to the hard white noon to the bead again — you would see that what lives here is not a population. It is a maintenance.

### The Tenders

They do not live on the table. No one could, not for long. The air is too thin to sleep well in, the light too total to let skin rest, the stone too cold to hold heat after dark. There is nothing to eat here and nothing is allowed to grow that would be edible. Even if you brought food, the wind that makes the water would take it out of your mouth.

So they live below. Far below, where the unmade mountain softens and soil is allowed to exist. You can see the faint thread of their path if you know where to look at that outer lip — not a road, but a polish on the basalt where feet have gone for longer than any one life remembers.

They come up in singles, in twos, in a silent file when the wind has changed. Not every day. Often enough.

They are high people. You would know them by their chests before you saw their faces — broad, barrelled from childhood in thin air. By their feet most of all: bare or in soaked leather wraps that never dry, soles broad and cracked, toenails thick and ridged from kicking against stone, from standing for hours in water cold enough to ache. Their hands are not soft. Stone-on-stone work leaves a particular kind of hand, where the callus is not a pad but a shell.

They do not talk much up here. Sound carries too well over water and low walls, and talking would be a kind of silt.

What they do is simple enough a child can do it, and exacting enough an old person is still learning it:

Sweeping.

Not building. Building is finished. The mountain was made before it could be a mountain — hollowed, leveled, terraced into that close labyrinth of interlocking cells — and now there is nowhere left to add another basin without taking one away. The work that remains is negative work. Keeping empty.

A woman — it is often a woman, or a girl and a grandmother, though there is no law — walks the walls without stepping on the rims. She carries a broom of stiff alpine grass bound with sinew, its handle bleached white by sun and water. She sweeps the film of airborne dust that the wind has laid on a basin floor before it can become mud. She sweeps the thin, hopeful smear of green that tried to establish itself in a notch overnight before it can stitch itself down.

Another, behind her, carries a flat stone the size of a palm, ground smooth on one face. He checks each spillway — that thumb-wide notch cut in every rim — for a grain of grit, a flake of tuff, a fallen eyelash that could make the water hesitate. Rule 3 is the only prayer they have: *Nothing stops the water.*

A third, if there is a third, carries the scoop: a shallow gourd or a bent slab of wood on a long handle. When a section on the windward edge is drying because the air has shifted leeward, he lifts, not much, a double handful of water from a lower, fuller cell and lays it back into a higher, emptier one. Not to hoard. To keep the breathing even. So the whole plateau sighs at once, instead of choking.

They work at the speed of the spill. That is to say, agonizingly slowly to anyone from below. To them, it is the correct speed. To hurry would be to make a wave, and a wave would overtop a wall, and an overtop is a flood, and a flood would mean building again. They have been told — or they have decided — never to build again.

You will never see more than six or seven at a time, and often you will see none at all, only the footprints filling slowly with condensation, and a broom left leaning against a wall to mark that this row has been checked and will not need to be checked until the light changes.

They do not own the water. That is important. In the villages below, water is owned — a spring is someone's uncle, a stream belongs to a house. Up here, owning would be impossible. The water belongs to the stone for the ten seconds it rests in it, and then to the next stone. The tenders understand themselves the same way: they belong to the basin they are tending for the ten minutes they are in it.

When they are done they do not linger for the view. The view is the instrument. They turn their backs on the outer lip — that sheer fall where the made world simply ends and the real mountain resumes — and go down the polish in the rock, single file, feet finding holds without looking.

They rarely speak of what they do. If you asked one in the low market why they climb to sweep water that will only be mist again before it reaches the crops, he might not have an answer that sounds like an answer. He might say, *So it stays clear.* He might say, *So the sky can see itself.* He might just shrug. He was taken up as a child to learn not to pick the algae; the lesson stuck deeper than language.

### What Else Tries to Live Here

Up here, life is an intruder, but it keeps trying, because water calls it.

On the outer windward walls, where the tenders cannot sweep every hour, a faint rust-bloom forms — iron bacteria dreaming of a skin. In the deepest reservoirs, the house-sized cisterns where the final water waits, a microscopic dust tries to settle and become bottom. The tenders scrub it out with sand until their shoulders burn, until the stone is pale again.

Once a year a bird, lost or curious, will land on an outer wall to drink. The tenders will stand very still until it leaves. They will then empty the basin it drank from with their scoops, one lift at a time, and spread that water thinly across ten other basins so no trace of feather-oil remains. Not from unkindness. From protocol. This water is not for drinking. Not directly. It is for being exactly itself — cold, mineral, empty — until it becomes mist again and falls lower where it is allowed to be dirty, green, alive.

In that sense, you could say what lives here is the *refusal* to live here. A curated emptiness. A fastidiously kept absence that has to be tended like a garden, lest life rush back in.

And — if you are quiet, if you stand in the bright, shadowless noon with your breath making little clouds and listen to the thousand small tickings that refuse to become a loud sound — you might feel that something else does live here after all.

Not a person. Not an animal.

The water itself, distributed.

No single basin is alive. But the whole slow sheet-flow, the pulse that takes hours to cross the plateau from high edge to low, the way a spill in one cell shivers the next and the next without ever hurrying — that behaves like a creature. It breathes through stone lungs. It sweats. It remembers where the wind last touched.

The tenders know not to call it that. But they move as if they are grooming a vast, flat, cool animal that is too large to see and too slow to startle, an animal made of nothing but surface tension and patience, whose only need is to keep moving a hand's breadth at a time and never be stopped.

That is what lives on the mountain that was made before it could be a mountain: a large, slow, mineral animal of water, and the small, warm, barefoot people who have agreed to keep its bed clean.
