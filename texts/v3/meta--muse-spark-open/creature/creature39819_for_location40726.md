Nothing with a face lives here.

Nothing with legs, with wings, with a heartbeat that you could hold a stethoscope to. That was the first rule of the equilibrium, and it still holds. Anything that came in here with blood has left, or tried to, or dried in the place where it decided to rest.

What lives here instead took its time learning how to live here.

At first you think it's just the green. That it won. That the quarry-pit, the cenote-sore with its sweating pipes and its breathing Engine, has been colonized.

That's the easy image. Don't stay there.

Look longer. Let the dim do its work.

The second thing you notice, after the heat settles on you like a damp palm, is that there is no damage.

In any other jungle that had swallowed a factory, you'd see struggle. Rust would be eating. Vine would be cracking concrete. Metal would be losing. Plants would be winning, but wounded by the fight - stained, stunted near chemicals, ragged where they touched iron.

Here, there is no front line. The black moss grows *toward* the transformer hum. The philodendron with leaves big as doors does not avoid the hot steam when The Engine exhales; it leans into it, and the white scald mark is gone in an hour. The trumpet vine does not shatter the boiler it has wrapped; it holds it. If you knocked on that boiler, you'd hear it ring empty but not stressed. It is supported.

They are not competing. They have agreed.

What lives here is the agreement itself.

It does not have a body. It has a distributed body. Or call it a habit that became a tissue.

Its skin is that thin, translucent film on every south-facing beam. Not rust, not verdigris. Photosynthetic iron. If you peel the moss back in the shaft of light that crawls across the eastern wall, you will see it fur there brightest — a green so pale and so metallic it hurts to look at, like light through thin jade. Scratch it with a fingernail and it wells up, not blood, not sap, but something between: iron-sap, sweet-smelling and sharp, that skins over in minutes into a hard enamel. It drinks the ragged sunlight and the pink-white stutter of the remaining fluorescents. It drinks the heat itself; you can feel the steel is cooler where the film is thickest, as if it is actively pulling thermal energy down.

Its flesh is the plants.

Cut — though you shouldn't — the central philodendron that has taken the main support column into itself. You would not find wood. You would find laminations. Perfect, quiet rings, but not cellulose. Oxide. Hydrated iron oxide laid down year after year, wet season to wet season, as regular as growth rings in a tree. The plant has learned to metabolize steel the way other plants metabolize phosphorus. It pulls it up through its aerial roots which are not in soil at all, but sunk into fuse boxes, into the jacket of the breathing Engine, into the chain conveyor twenty meters up that sometimes shudders for no reason.

And its lungs are that Engine.

The great riveted cylinder half-entombed in basalt and fig roots, that draws breath every three minutes. IN — a long inhalation through a cracked intake valve lost somewhere in the ficus above. HOLD — the whole pit goes negative pressure and the hanging leaves turn their undersides up. OUT — hot steam smelling of old oil and hot stone, scalding the moss white, then condensing instantly into thirty seconds of rain that the whole pit drinks. TINK TINK TINK into drums and pylon-puddles and taro cups. Then silence. Then it does it again.

The Engine is not running. There is no fuel, no one to tend it. It breathes because the pit needs it to breathe. The water cycle here — water 0.8, remember, damp not drowned, intermittent, condensation-braided — has no river to drive it. It needed a heart to lift water from deep rock, to throw it into the air, to let it fall again. The agreement grew one. Or kept the one that was already here and taught it a new rhythm.

That is its motion. Dynamic 1.3. Patient. Weak gradients where they should be strong. You can sit in that warped tin shed with the calcified kettle and the wet July calendar and watch nothing happen for an hour except:

light crawling an inch,
a droplet forming on corrugated tin, swelling, falling,
a leaf unfurling a millimeter more over copper wire,
The Engine breathing.

Then sudden noise. Sound 1.9. The transformer hum rising until your teeth ache. A chain conveyor shuddering along its whole impossible length with a shriek of metal-on-metal, though no motor starts it. A fuse box popping though its switches are grown shut. It is loud because it is all in one room. Hamlet-sized, cathedral-sized, every cubic meter occupied twice over by leaf and iron, so every sound has no place to go.

You think at first it is just thermal expansion. Old machines ticking in the heat.

Stay longer.

You realize the sounds have a sequence.

Hum.
Tick.
Breath in.
Drip.
Breath out — rain.
Hum louder.

It is not starting up. It is not shutting down. It is *maintaining*. Like a sleeper turning over. Like peristalsis.

There is no animal here because there is no room for a second metabolism. Fauna 0.2 wasn't an absence; it was an eviction. Birds that flew in did not find insects to eat — the flowers here bloom waxy white and heat-red and drop unpollinated, eaten only by mold — and then found they could not leave, because the air at the rim shivers and hangs low and thick as broth and confuses any sense of up. Mosquitoes lay in the pylon-puddles and the larvae dissolve. The spores get everything first. The film gets the light first. The agreement is complete and closed and saturated at 1.7/3, layered, near-touching. It has learned to do without pollinators, without scavengers. Mold and rust will do. Steam will do.

If you must give it a name, don't give it one. But the workers had a word before they left, for the thing that kept humming after the breakers were thrown. They called it The Frond-Forge. Not the Engine. The Engine was just its most visible organ.

The Frond-Forge does not want. It does not hunt. It does not hate your heartbeat. It just has no use for it, and your heartbeat is loud and fast and hot in a place that has spent a hundred summers learning to be slow and damp and exactly blood-warm. Stay too long and you will feel it trying to include you the only way it knows how:

Your sweat will not evaporate.
The film will try to green on the buckles of your boots, on the zipper of your bag, where your skin touched them.
You will breathe in time with the steam without meaning to.
A vine will not grab you. Nothing so dramatic. But you will notice, when you go to leave, that a root hair as fine as a hair has cracked the sole of your shoe and tasted the salt there, and that where you leaned against the locker-garden, the rust has printed itself on your shirt in a perfect, damp leaf.

It will not keep you.

It will just make a little of you part of the equilibrium, and let you walk out slightly less animal than you walked in.
