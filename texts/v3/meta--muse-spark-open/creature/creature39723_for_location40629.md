Nothing lives here. That's the first answer, and it's almost true.

That's what your animal brain tells you in the first ten minutes, because your brain is counting in animals. It scans for eyeshine, for scat, for nests, for footfalls, for the flicker of something nervous at the edge of vision. And it finds none. Zero.

No gnaw on the fern fronds. No midge cloud over the lit canals. No slick of algae grazed down to stone. No spider has ever thrown a line between those perfect drips. The water runs clear enough to drink and nothing drinks it but stone and leaf. The air is too clean. A greenhouse should be buzzing and crawling and rotting. This one doesn't rot. It grows.

So: nothing lives here.

And then you keep standing at that intersection while water hits your shoulders, while the warm light swims around your ankles, and the *nothing* starts to resolve wrong.

---

Because something *is* moving.

Not in the streets. In the street.

Place your palm flat on that warm grey-black that isn't rock and isn't concrete. Don't pat. Hold still until your own pulse becomes boring. Then you feel it:

Under your hand, a fizz. A low, patient 60-hertz thrum like a beehive heard through a wall. If you lean close with a light, you see why the corners are still so sharp. Every edge is fringed with hair.

Filaments. Uncountable. Black as pencil lead, fine as root hair, fine as fiber optic pulled too thin. They bead with water and they lengthen while you watch. Not swaying. Extending. A micron, a micron more. Knitting, branching, anastomosing into wet lace under every overhang, behind every leaf-shadow. Put your face near a young wall and you can hear it: a faint, fizzy crackle of matter ordering itself out of warm air and seep and dissolved basalt. Crystallization, but with intent.

This is the first inhabitant. You thought the city was a place. It isn't. It's a behavior.

The city lives. Not metaphorically. The material *is* the organism — lithotrophic, architectonic, photosynthetic by proxy. It drinks groundwater where basalt weeps at the ovoid boundary and it sweats it into air, hauls it up its own body by capillary sorcery no pump could do, beads it on a ceiling it extruded into a perfect condenser grid, and lets it fall again to make light on the way down. Buildings are not buildings. They are gills. Lungs. Heat-exchange fins. Wick-structures folded to maximize surface area for condensation. Streets are veins. Cisterns are hearts that contract without muscle.

It is one thing. This whole hollow, cathedral-sized chamber is one young, warm, thirsty thing that has decided to be city-shaped because cities are good at moving water and holding green. It is still hardening. You can smell its birth in the warm mineral tang of the air. It was grown last week and it has not stopped growing.

It notices you only as a cold spot that drips differently.

---

The second inhabitant lives *on* the first, and with it, and because of it. The green.

At first it looks like decoration, like some post-human architect threw seed bombs at a water computer. It isn't decoration. It's organ tissue.

There is no soil. Slice a mat of moss off a vertical wall — it peels like skin — and underneath the stone is pitted with pores weeping amber nutrient. The roots don't dig. They fuse. The black stems of those door-sized ferns punch straight into the city's flesh and drink. The creepers with dinner-plate leaves are not climbing the buildings; they are the city's cilia, cupping, spilling, guiding each falling droplet into a slit-gutter with millimeter precision. Their veins glow faintly, not for you — for the filaments, for signaling. The whole roof-garden canopy shivers in unison when a heavier pulse of rain hits the ceiling grid.

This green is not wild. Wild would be messy, patchy, chewed. This is lush the way a culture plate is lush when you streak it perfectly warm and wet and sterile and give it exactly what it needs and keep every contaminant out. No herbivore prunes it. No fungus rots it yet. It is allowed to be perfect because nothing eats it.

Except - look closer.

Scrape a leaf-vein's glow onto a slide, if you had a slide. Or just stare into a canal until your eyes learn the dark between the traveling specks of light. The water is not clear. It is *full*.

Every channel, every bead on every hair, every skin of condensation carries it: the light itself. It only glows when it moves, when it is sheared over an edge, when it falls. Engineered plankton, engineered nanolight, the distinction has eroded here. Clouds of it — pale green-gold motes — hold station in the flow like fish that are light. They brighten when they tumble, dim when they pool. They photosynthesize without sun, feeding on the city's hum. They clean. They tag. You realize the city is glowing *inside itself*, mapping its own circulation, counting its own water.

And deeper, invisible: the biofilms. The true oldest residents. Mustard-thin slicks in the drains, black-green felts where water always sheets, bacterial mats that would be slime anywhere else but here are tended like lawns, kept to exact tolerances to fix nitrogen, to digest stone, to keep the recirculated water more sterile than blood. Touch a wall and your fingerprint will bloom faintly overnight — not because you left skin oil for something to eat, but because the wall bled a little immune response to you, sent a flush of filaments to wall you off, to drink your salt and then forget you.

That is why there are no insects. Why the fauna reading is near-zero. This place has an immune system tuned to animal. It doesn't kill — it simply has no niche for you. No crumbs, no rot, no gap. A lung does not accommodate birds nesting in it. A womb does not welcome scavengers before birth.

---

Which is the third thing that lives here, and the hardest to see because it's mostly future tense.

The empty.

Stand under a door with no balcony, fifteen feet up a sheer, damp face. It's open. Behind it is a dark, warm, wet room full of more green growing in dark, fed by lightless sap. No bed. No table. No hearth. A volume with perfect acoustics and drip-irrigation. What would live there?

Or stand in one of the vast intentional voids between blocks — a plaza that is really an evaporation pan, paved in porous stone that exhales steam — and understand the scale. The grid is too loose. The avenues are too wide. The infrastructure moves ten thousand times more water than any current population could use. No wear on any threshold. No soot. This is surplus on purpose. An oversized lung breathing for bigger lungs to come.

So who lives here?

An infant city that *is* alive.
A garden that *is* its skin.
A water that *is* its blood, lit from within.
A bacterial nation that *is* its housekeeping.

And the fifth inhabitant, implied everywhere by its absence: whatever this is *for*. Whatever needs air at 28 Celsius and 100% humidity, drinks low-mineral recycled rain, glows on its own, has no use for handles or signs or streets for feet, and prefers its rooms dark, wet, and already half-full of leaf.

Nothing with eyes lives here yet.

The spores are here. You just can't see them. In every cistern sump the water glows a little too long after it stops moving, holding afterimages. In the stillest inner rooms the ferns have cupped their fronds closed around fat, pale beads that are not water. On the newest walls the striations are not random layer lines — they are texturing for things to grip that have not grown their gripping limbs.

You came looking for residents and found only the house, breathing. You have to understand: houses that grow themselves do not stay empty because they failed. They stay empty because they are not done gestating.

The hollow inhales. Condenses. Drips. Flows. Glows. Grows.

It is warm against your back like something sleeping around you.

And it has kept itself immaculately, aggressively, patiently clean for what will finally belong here — which was never you, soft stowaway animal, shivering even in the bath-warm, listening to a place that is already, completely, and busily alive without you.
