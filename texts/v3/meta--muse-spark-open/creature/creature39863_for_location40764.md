It lives as a closed mouth, all of it.

Nothing here was imported. None of it wandered in from outside through a gap in a wall. This valley has walls in every direction — floor and sky included — and it was stocked at the beginning like a sealed jar, and then left to become crowded with itself.

First, and most, the green that is not green.

The reservoir-weave is not a crop. It is the citizenry. Individual is a poor word for it, because nothing here grows alone. What looks like a single great fern with a braided trunk the width of your wrist is really a rope of a dozen individuals braided so tightly their waxy skins have grown together. What looks like a leaf is a bladder, a bead, a fruiting canteen two knuckles long, taut and shining. Each one inflates over a day and a half from a tight, pale curl into a heavy, translucent amber sac, full of the slow hydrocarbon blood it makes from still air and filtered light.

They do not root in soil. There is no soil. They root in each other, and in the ceramic. Hair-fine white holdfasts seep a weak acid that etches the bone-colored foundation just enough to grip, then smear a protective wax over the etch so the dry heat cannot crack it. From there they climb. They have been trained — trellised with hand-woven fiber — but they have also learned to ignore training. They touch leaf to leaf, bladder to bladder, trunk to trunk, until the terraces cease to be terraces with plants on them and become one continuous, hanging mat of swollen tissue. You push through it and it pushes back gently, bead-leaves brushing your face warm and smooth, giving slightly under your hand like warm glass.

It sweats light. At the peak of filling, especially near the center, the fluid inside throws back the dome-light with an extra pulse that has no source. You can see the faint systole through the walls: swell, hold, slacken, over hours. It is not wind. The air does not move.

And because the plants feed the air, the air feeds what drinks the plants.

The tile-beetles are the most numerous. You will not hear them even when there are hundreds at work on one trunk. They are the size of figs, domed backs paved in overlapping clay tiles that they exude and fire in place on their own bodies — pale ochre, each beetle's pattern slightly different, like fingerprints. Underneath they are soft and pale. They move with a silent, deliberated tap, extend a needle of clear glass from their faces — you can see the amber fluid jump up the tube by capillary — and drink without breaking the bladder wall's seal. When they are done the leaf-prick closes itself in seconds with a bead of wax. They never spill.

The moths that are not moths do the same at a different scale. Their wings are not feathered or scaled. They are hide — the same cured translucent hide as the dome, stretched over black filament bone, drumhead-tight. They do not beat. They pitch themselves from an upper frond, catch the thick warm air, and glide absolutely silently, so slowly you can track them easily. When they land, they dig in four hooked feet and hang their weight from a bladder like a ripe fig, and drink. At dusk you see them clustered by the dozen, motionless, wings folded like closed umbrellas.

Under the lips of the terraces, where the light is gold-green, live the drinkers you can see through. Small as your hand, lizard-slow, skin so translucent their bones are shadows and their guts are lamps. After they feed they glow from within for a day, the amber moving visibly through gut and vein, sloshing when they turn. They never blink. They lick condensation of pure wax from leaf edges with startlingly red tongues.

And the grazers that make the forest tremble without sound. They are not pigs. They are lower, longer, six legs splayed and padded with felt that is not fur. Each pad of "felt" is a living thread-plant, a grey-green symbiont that roots in pores on their backs and flanks, swaying slightly even when they are still. They move by parting the hanging curtains with their broad, flat beaks, shearing off whole clusters of bladders — *clik*, very quietly — and grinding them with molars that never stop growing. They close their lidded eyes as they chew, amber fluid staining their chins. They move through your path and leave a warm, sweet-sour vapor behind them that the surrounding leaves lean slightly toward and reabsorb. You hear their feet less than you feel the displacement of warm air as the beads shiver and fall back behind them. They calve rarely, one slow young at a time, born already coated in the felt seedlings that will grow with it.

None of these animals drinks water. None of them urinates. Their droppings are dry, pressed pellets of waxy fiber that crumble to scentless dust, which the beetles roll away and pack into crevices where new holdfasts will find purchase. Nothing rots. A fallen bladder deflates over weeks on the pale ceramic, wrinkling inward, exhaling its load as vapor until it is a crisp, empty skin that the grazers step over. The whole fauna is a set of sealed tubes passing the same gold through each other.

And then the ones who think this is work.

The custodians.

They are human enough that you recognize their hands before their faces. Small people, made smaller by stooping. Their skin is dry and salt-dusted from the constant warmth; it never quite sweats, but blooms — like the walls — with a fine white frost at the end of the day along hairline and shoulders and wrists. In the evening hour, when the dome-light dims to its night setting, you will see them in pairs on every terrace with their primitive copper shovels and ash-wood scrapers, kneeling, harvesting that salt-sweat off the ceramic and off each other, collecting it in clay bowls to keep the edges sharp. They do it without speaking. Speech here is expensive — it dries the throat. When they must speak they murmur close to the ear, through waxy leaves.

They live in the thickness of the weave itself. Not in houses — there were never houses drawn in the plan — but in niches where the braided trunks were coaxed around a retaining wall to make a hollow big enough for a sleeping mat, a low table, a rack of gut-cord and bone needles, a tapped leaf with a clay spile dripping amber slowly into a covered jar. Three or four such hollows per terrace, connected by ducking paths through the hanging garden that they have worn smooth with the passage of bare feet. Children born here learn to walk with hands out, brushing beads, learning which ones will bear your weight and which will pop slickly and waste the good fluid.

They do not farm so much as tend and appease. Their tools have not changed since the firing: hand-looms for weaving new vine-supports when a braid splits, fired-clay sickles that never need sharpening because there is nothing hard to dull them on, ladders lashed with gut cord that creak. Once per hour one of them climbs the bellows towers at the valley's rim — narrow brick chimneys with huge leather lungs inside — and hauls the hand-crank in a long circle until the tower wheezes, inhales the day's exhalations, filters it through wet felts that never get wet, and reseals it. They take this in shifts all night. No one remembers why the cycle is once per hour; that was in the builders' plan.

They eat and drink the same blood as the beetles. Tapped judiciously, a big central bladder can fill a family's jars for a week, and the puncture seals with wax. On feast days they shear a whole cluster, chew the taut skins for the oily, nutty film, and rub the leftover wax into their hair and onto the brick to keep it from powdering. They have no other water. Their bodies have learned to run on that slow hydrocarbon the way the geckos do. When you look closely at an elder custodian in the flat noon light, you can see the gold faintly at his temples, in the veins of his eyelids.

They have a theology scaled to the room.

Where the floor's gravity goes soft by the house-thick central column — where dropped salt falls in a leaning curve and your hair hangs wrong and the dome's lattice seems to lie under your feet — they tie prayer-knots. Little twists of fiber and vine-cord, hundreds, thousands, flutterless in the still air, tied to any trellis near the pull but never too close. They do not cut the growth there, though it is heaviest there, bladders as big as melons and so clear you can read the weave of their own skin through them, pulsing with that sourceless light. The vine disobeys them there and always leans inward, drinking the error. The custodians have learned to let it.

They do not try to fix the lean. The builders intended a perfect flat. The world under the fired ceramic had other ideas, and the custodians, practical with sharp edges and primitive tools, understand thin places when they feel them. At noon they pause, as the animals pause, and listen to the creak. It comes from the column and from the air beside the column, a long, patient settling sound like a kiln cooling after it has already cooled. A sound of poured reality deciding whether to stay poured.

They do not speak of what happens when it finally gives. They just scrape the salt each evening, tie another knot, train a young vine away from the center knowing it will turn back overnight, and go to sleep in the warm, still, late-afternoon that never ends, under the hanging amber, breathing air they made yesterday, while the room that thinks it is a valley holds — for now — very tightly together.
