They live like moss lives - not *on* the bowl, but *as* part of its way of holding together.

### The People

You expect mountain people to be spare. Leaner, quieter, weather-scoured down to essentials. These aren't.

Living in a volume instead of an area makes you compact and loud. The cold and the thin-breath air up here should have made them broad-chested and slow-moving - and in a way it did, you see it in the calves and shoulders, in how a sixty-year old woman will carry a basket of wet tubers up three terraces without changing her breathing - but the bowl selects against spare. You can't afford to be spare when rot takes your boots if you don't oil them every evening and the walkway outside your door needs re-lashing before winter. You have to be busy, thick-handed, constantly mending.

So they've become a dense people for a dense place. Short, mostly. Not stunted so much as pruned. Men and women both wide through the hips and thighs for climbing ladders that are really just notched roots. Skin pale under the wet, then ruddy at cheeks and knuckles from being damp ten hours a day. Hair left long because cutting it in this humidity just makes it frizz and cutting tools are needed elsewhere; it’s kept braided back with bark cord, often with living things in it. A child will come tear past you with a fern gametophyte sprouting from his braid and not notice.

Hands tell you most. No one's hands are clean. Even the babies have earth under their nails because they crawl on walkways that are half soil. The adults have hands like root-burls - swollen knuckles, fingers stained black-green from myrtle tannin and walnut hull and copper verdigris, palms mapped with fine white scars where bark cord has bitten in over years. They are weavers first, everything else second. Watch a man repair a bridge: he doesn't hammer so much as *sew*, drawing a green cedar withe through a joint, pulling it taut with his teeth while his foot holds the shake. Their clothes are weave too - not woven cloth brought in, but made right there on that pit-loom under the great cedar. Thick, felted wool from the bowl's own small sheep - a shaggy, rain-proof breed the size of dogs that live most of their lives on the upper walkways, eating lichen off the rails - mixed with nettle fiber and soft inner bark, oiled with rendered marten fat until it sheds water. Everything smells faintly of smoke and lanolin. On dry days they hang it all out and the whole bowl is strung with steaming jackets like prayer flags.

They talk the way the water talks: incessantly, overlapping, without much hierarchy. Sound carries here whether you want it to or not, and they stopped trying to be private generations ago. A husband and wife arguing about where to hang mushrooms will be heard by twelve neighbors who will all offer opinions without being asked. Children are not hushed, they're just folded into the noise - a toddler wailing on a terrace becomes a cue for the old man next door to start singing louder, not to comfort but to cover, to contribute, because silence would be stranger than wailing. When the honey-swarm cry goes up it isn't an alarm so much as an invitation: everyone who isn't holding something perishable drops it and climbs.

There are no chiefs. There are Rememberers, which is different. Old women and a few old men whose job is not to rule but to remember the exact braid-pattern that keeps a coppiced myrtle wall from blowing out in spring winds, or where the seep behind House-That-Leans has to be left unblocked or it will undermine the cistern-mere. They don't command, they nag. *"You took that stone, Leri, where's your moss?"* and Leri knows better than to argue because last year someone didn't replace the moss and their sleeping platform grew a shelf fungus the size of a plate overnight.

They are relentlessly practical and therefore casually intimate with the non-practical. Every house has a small niche cut into its living trunk where they leave a thimble of honey or a polished beetle wing for the thing that, depending who you ask, is either the spirit of the house-tree or just good manners toward the tree itself. Midwives know forty uses for clubmoss spores and also know to hang a bundle of white lichen over a new crib so the night-cloud "doesn't mistake the baby for still water." No one sees a contradiction.

Children run the high bridges - the rope-and-wicker ones the adults avoid unless they must - barefoot even in the cold, toes prehensile from gripping wet cedar. They know before they can read how to test a mushroom with the tip of the tongue for the bitter tingle that means leave it for the salamanders, how to coax a swarm with smoke without burning your eyebrows, how to sing the crow-birds down by whistling through myrtle leaves. By seven they can split a tuber blind and by ten they can re-weave a wind-torn roof thatch before lunch while carrying on three conversations.

At night, when the cloud lowers and turns the bowl into a lit terrarium, they go inside and become small golden lights in the green. Families sleep not in rooms but in nests - platforms slung between living beams, curtained with oiled bark-paper, sharing heat. You hear them through the thin walls that breathe leaves: coughing, love-making, muttering in sleep, an old man telling the same story about the year the mere froze over despite the dripping. It all condenses and drips out again by morning.

### The Other Occupants Who Were Never Guests

You cannot separate the people from the things that were here first and never left.

The white-collared crows - *korri*, the children call them - are not pets, not pests. They are citizens. There are more of them than people, perhaps four hundred, nesting in the eaves and chimney-hollows, stealing anything shiny or salty. They have learned to imitate the pump-creak and a baby's cry and the specific two-note whistle people use for "look out below" when dropping compost. Old korri will sit on a lintel during a mending lesson and heckle until chased. Young ones follow children hoping for dropped bean-vines.

Underneath: the amphibian substrate. Every channel, every barrel lip, every cupped leaf holds a tree-frog the size of your thumbnail, lime or rust colored, throats pulsing. By day they are invisible. At dusk they become the dominant voice, a glassy choir that makes conversation pitch itself a little higher to be heard. The giant salamanders - forearm-long, slate-backed, with feathery gills retained into adulthood because why metamorphose when everything is already water - live in the cistern-mere and in the stone runnels. The villagers step over them, move them gently out of washing basins, thank them for eating midge larvae. One very old one lives under the communal anvil and is considered a better judge of copper-beating rhythm than most apprentices.

The midge pillars and jewel beetles are weather. The martens and the small, shaggy bears - mountain bears, not much bigger than large dogs, coats black-brown and always damp-looking - pass through on their own routes. No one feeds the bears, no one hunts them. A bear will cross a high bridge at 2 a.m., claws clicking on half-logs, pause to sniff a sleeping porch, and continue because there is nothing for it here it can't get easier in the woods outside the rim. A marten will steal an egg and be cursed loudly and then forgiven.

Even the plants assert occupancy. The cedars that form house-ribs are not lumber, they are landlords. You can feel them grow. A door that rubbed clean in summer will stick by autumn because the living post has swelled a centimeter. The myrtle walls shed aromatic leaves right into soup pots; you pick them out or you don't. Mushrooms appear overnight in the middle of floors, in boots, in folded laundry - pale fanners, blue milkcaps - and are harvested with a knife kept by the hearth for that exact purpose. Moss erases any attempt at permanence. Leave a wooden spoon outside for a week and when you come back its bowl is furred.

What lives in the bowl, then, is not a village *in* a forest.

It's a knot. A tangle of human hands and living wood and bird throat and frog song and cold mineral drip that has learned, over centuries, to be one organism with many kinds of mouths. Cut any one thread and the others tighten to take up the slack.

It does not invite you, precisely. But if you stand long enough in the drizzle at the knee-high walkway level - breath ghosting, ears full - someone will lean out from a doorway that is also a coppice and wordlessly hand you a hot, oiled cloth to wrap around your hands. Because cold hands are a problem for the whole knot, not just for you.
