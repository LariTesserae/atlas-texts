Nothing lives *in* it the way something lives in a house.

Everything lives *with* it. Like organs in a body that has decided to keep them.

There is no master here. No apex. No cave king. That's not the kind of trap this is. This is a gentle trap, a keeping-place. What lives here lives because it fell, or slipped, or followed damp down, and then found the terms agreeable enough to stay. generations ago. Long enough that leaving would be a kind of death now.

Start with the walls, because that's where most of the life is. You think the walls are stone. They're not. Not anymore. They're gecko.

You saw flicker. That was inaccurate. When you stood still and let your eyes soften, you saw it: the limestone is continuous with a breathing skin. Mourning geckos,house geckos, a third kind smaller than your smallest fingernail that has no name outside this pit. Translucent, amber-pink, the color of wet limestone when you hold light behind it. Their tails are fat with water. Their toe-pads leave no mark on the moss and no sound. Dozens per square meter is not a figure of speech. Lift your hand from any rock and three more will fill the warm shape of it within a minute, throat-pulsing in sync with each other, drinking condensation off the stone with a tongue-flick you only see from inches away. They don't call. At night, when the air finally cools by two degrees, they sing just below your hearing - a vibration in the stone more than a chirp. The whole sinkhole tenses and releases.

Under them, and over them, the moss. It is not ground cover. It is the ground. *Thuidium* and *Fissidens* and a liverwort that grows like overlapping green tongues, felted a hand deep over the lower terraces. If you press it, cold water beads up around your fingers even in the hot season. It is louder than any animal here: it sighs when you step off it. Inside it live the thumbnail frogs. *Eleutherodactylus*, or something that was *Eleutherodactylus* before the pit edited it.

They are the exact orange of the shelf fungus two feet away. They are the exact brown of the wet clay. They have dispensed with tadpoles entirely - no water to spare for that. They lay three eggs the size of peppercorns in a moss pocket and guard them until tiny, perfect frogs walk out. They do croak. You were told you wouldn't hear them, and you don't, unless you kneel and put your ear to the seep. Then you hear it: a chirp like a wet fingertip on glass, ultrasonic-thin, meant to travel six inches through heavy air and no further. The male inflates a throat sac bigger than his head and the sound is swallowed instantly by moss. That is courtship here. Private.

Above you, in the shallow undercut where the limestone has weathered into a smooth, scooped brow, the bats. This is the only colony that could live here - not insectivorous, not enough flying prey in a closed loop, but frugivorous. Jamaican fruit bats, *Artibeus*, fat and dog-faced, packed so tight their wings overlap like shingles. They left the scrub above generations ago because figs fruit here two weeks out of sync with the outside. A fig falls in the pit every hour, soft into silt, with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The bats wait for dusk. At 18:40 every day the light finally fails enough for them, and they drop without a sound - no screech, no chatter, the damp air won't carry it - and braid through the vines on wings you feel as pressure, not hear. They shit seeds onto the moss, and by morning white mold has already found the shit and is eating it. Everything is eaten quickly here. Nothing is wasted in a trap.

Between these layers move the quiet hunters.

The snakes are vine-snakes, *Oxybelis*, and a small, blunt-headed boa that has become strangely pale, the color of limestone dust. They don't strike; they pour. You watch one for ten minutes thinking it's a vine, until its tongue - black, forked, tasting the heavy air for frog-skin - gives it away. They take geckos with a patience so complete it doesn't feel like predation. They take frogs. Sometimes the bats take them, if a young one falls.

And the invertebrates who own the floor. You thought abundance would mean noise. Here abundance means silence, because noise is expensive in hot, still air. The ants - leaf-cutters the size of rice grains - make highways along the old moss-spine wall, carrying green confetti in an endless line that never breaks, even at night. The beetles that look like polished seeds - iridescent *Chrysina*-like scarabs - don't buzz; their elytra are fused too heavy for flight in this humidity, so they walk, ticking faintly on dry leaves. The dark butterflies - *Morpho peleides*, but heat-stunted and night-shifted - don't flap so much as fall up and down the light shaft, tasting salt from sweat on the stones where those clay figures were left. The mantises are the color of dead elephant ear, and take moths by reaching out a half-inch, with no lunge at all.

There are no humans living here. Not anymore.

The steps prove someone tried. The silt floor is too small, too wet in secret ways, too dim to grow anything a human wants. Corn molds before it tassels. But someone comes. The fruit rinds in the niche are not old. Three days, maybe four. The clay figure with no face has been recently turned upright after falling. Someone climbs down barefoot - the scoop in the steps - to collect the cold water from the hand-deep basin in the north wall, because that water never warms and never silts. Someone leaves river stones, smooth and white, that do not belong to this limestone, as an acknowledgement. A keeping of the keeper.

If you stay until the bats leave and the geckos finally sing loud enough to feel in your teeth, you will feel that something larger than any of these lives here too. Not a creature. A concurrence. The pit itself, respiring. Heat in, moisture in, seed in, bone in. Light bouncing until it forgets the sun. Sound eaten. Time composted into silt that is always being remade by worms.

All of them - moss and frog and bat and beetle and the absent person who leaves fruit - are just ways the wound has learned to breathe.
