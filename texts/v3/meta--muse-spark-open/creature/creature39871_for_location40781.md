They live here the way things live in a joint - pressed together, necessary, held in place by pressure.

### The People

There aren't divisions the way a surface town has divisions. No children playing in streets, no shops. Everyone you see is working, or has *just* been working, or is about to be. But they are not uniform.

**The Suited** are most of them. You see them first because they move.

Broad, short bodies made broader and shorter by layers that never come off: canvas and rubberized wool and reclaimed wetsuit and brass all laminated together by mineral crust until the suit weighs more than the person inside. The helmets are old - copper and bronze, pitted black at the seams, three small glass eyes in front that are always fogged at the corners. No two faceplates clear the same way, so you learn people by how they clear: a wipe with a thumb, a tilt toward a warm pipe, a knuckle-tap that sends condensation sheeting.

Inside, faces are pale in a way that has nothing to do with sun. Pressure-pallor. Cheeks puffed slightly, eyes always a little wet, lips cracked from breathing recycled air that has been through too many scrubbers. They don't talk. They can't, not in suits, not in water. They flash.

Every helmet has a caged amber bulb on its brow and two small shutters over it - eyelids they work with a chin lever. *Open-closed-open*: I'm passing on your left. *Long-long-short*: Pump three is coughing again. *Fast flutter*: Move. They have had a hundred years to get efficient with it. Whole conversations happen in a few blinks while two suited figures do the slow sideways shuffle past each other in an alley no wider than a coffin, neither touching the other or the hot pipe beside them. They are expert at not touching.

They live in the stack. Not apartments - nests. A hab-module meant for four now holds twelve hammocks strung elbow to elbow in water that stinks faintly of iron, sweat, and ozone. To get into your hammock you climb through what used to be a ventilation trunk. Privacy is a hanging tarp of bacterial leather that does nothing for sound but at least breaks a light. They sleep in suits half-off, arms free, helmets off for the first time, and what you notice then is how small their heads look without them. How soft. How they all have the same cauliflower deformity to the cartilage of their ears from pressure over years.

They are not unhappy. That's the wrong word down here. They are *adjusted*. They tend. Each one knows a specific circuit - not the whole town, just a ten-meter stretch of pipe and valve and trembling gauge that is *theirs*. They stroke rust off it, they listen to its ticks with a stethoscope pressed to metal, they bleed it when it gets air-logged. They lean their forehead against warm concrete when the cold gets in too deep. The Engine rewards them for this with heat, and the heat is indistinguishable from love.

Then there are **The Unsuited.** Fewer. You only find them deep inside, in the pressure hulls that still hold - a perfect sphere or a long cylinder where the air is still air and the water is kept outside by walls an inch thick.

They never left. Or they adapted further than suits allow.

They are naked in the warm, sour air of their hulls, their skin gone the color and texture of the bacterial sheets outside: bloodless white, faintly translucent, damp even in air. Hair long and floating even without water, because they keep the humidity at 100%. Fingertips pruned permanently into ridges. Eyelids thickened. They don't wear brass because they don't need to leave. They are the memory of the town. They sit in front of walls of dials that mirror the Engine's flanks, watching needles that have trembled in the same arc for three generations, marking logs on waterproof paper that is already half pulp. Their legs are thin - atrophied from never walking against current. Some of them have let their feet web slightly with scar tissue where they had blisters opened again and again.

They breathe in shallow sips. They pray, if that's the word, by placing both palms on the warm inner wall of the hull and feeling the Engine's thrum in their molars, in their sternum.

When a Suited needs to pass through a hull to get to the next alley - because doors were abandoned and the shortest route is now straight through someone's air-filled living room - the Unsuited do not look up. A hatch sighs, water sloshes, a suited figure drags itself through with a toolkit clanking, dripping on their floor. They make room. This is choreography, too.

And then there are the children, if you can call them that. Born down here, in air-hulls or, increasingly, in flooded modules where a mother in an open suit labors with a midwife operating a hand-pump air bell over her face. They are small and they never cry loudly - water teaches you early not to waste air. Their eyes are huge and dark-adapted, built for amber halos. They learn to swim before they learn to crawl, to blink helmets before they learn words.

### The Other Residents

The town would not be alive without things that are not people and do not care about people.

The **white streamer weed** - not plant, not animal - grows one stalk per warm seep, exactly where a hairline crack in concrete lets Engine-heat bleed out. Six feet long, thick as a wrist at the base, hair-thin at the tip, ghost white and utterly still except for a slow lean toward heat. If you brush it, it doesn't move away. It sticks, briefly, as if tasting the rubber of your suit. The Suited leave it alone because where it grows, the pipe won't burst that winter.

Under the cold pipes, the **isopods**. They are called woodlice by the old Unsuited as a joke. Each one the size of a dinner plate, the size of a suitcase when they uncurl. Slate grey, fourteen legs folded neat under them. They don't scavenge so much as *wait*. Hours, days, pressed flat to mineral crust. Then all at once, at a signal you never see, every isopod in an alley will shuffle two inches to the left. No one knows why. The Suited step over them.

Drifting stupidly through the halos, **the fish**. Blind, lidless, their white flesh showing the black line of gut through the side. They bump into helmets, into pipes, into each other, never learning. They were not brought here. They came because light, even this poor amber light, is the only light for a hundred miles. They circle a bulb until they exhaust and sink, where an anemone arm will eventually find them.

Outside the bowl's lip, where no Suited ever goes, the **Anemones** - the true owners of the site before the concrete was poured. Door-sized, then larger, mouths like folded leather, tendrils as thick as insulated ducting that move on a timescale of days. They have grown accustomed to the town's waste heat. On cold nights when the Engine pulses harder, they lean *in* over the rim, just a few tendrils draped over the outer railing, tasting the water for brine snow and rust flakes and skin. They are not hostile. You could swim up and touch one and it would only close, very slowly, around your hand. But everyone knows not to. The Suited avert their helmet lights when they have to work near the outer wall, so as not to invite.

Lowest in the hierarchy, everywhere and invisible until you turn off your light: the **mat**. Bacterial leather in sheets of pink-white and bruise-yellow, humming faintly with its own weak phosphor where it coats the hottest pipes. It is what keeps the town from dissolving - it eats sulfur, it makes heat visible, it holds rust together. The people don't tend it. They make space for it. A pipe completely covered in mat is a healthy pipe. A pipe where the mat has receded, leaving bare sweating iron, is a pipe that will be dead by morning, and the Suited whose circuit it is will stay awake all night tapping it, trying to coax the mat back.

They all live together because none of them can leave. The fish can't find the dark, the isopods can't find the cold, the mat can't find the heat, the Anemones can't find the brine, and the people - suited and unsuited alike - can't find any air but the air the Engine tears out of seawater one molecule at a time.

So they stay stacked, four high streets in half a kilometer, breathing each other's exhaust, stepping through each other's homes, blinking amber at each other in the dusk that never ends.

If you stand still long enough in one of the long black reaches between lights, hand on a cold railing, you will feel them all around you without seeing anyone: the thrum in your teeth, a boot-tink three levels up, a tendril settling, a fish bumping your thigh and moving on, and very close, just at the edge of your halo, a helmet light holding perfectly still - watching you learn not to look up.
