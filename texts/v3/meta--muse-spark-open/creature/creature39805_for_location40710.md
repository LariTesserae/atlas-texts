Nothing lives here the way something lives in a forest.

There is no one to count. No herd, no flock, no city, no chorus. If you came here expecting inhabitants — bodies moving against the stone — you would at first say the place is dead, and for a long minute you would be right.

It takes a different kind of looking. You have to let your timescale slip.

### The Inhabitants That Borrow Water

The first residents you notice, if you are patient, are the amber discs.

They only exist when there is pooled water, which means most of the time, for most of the basin, they do not exist at all. Or rather — they exist as something else, tucked away. They are in the potholes near the center, the ones that hold brine for weeks after a rain. Thumb-nail sized, semi-transparent, flatter on the bottom than the top, like a contact lens left in tea.

Watch one long enough and you realize it is not drifting. It is holding still while the water moves.

It hangs a millimeter above the silt in the slow turnover of a convection cell. No fins. No cilia you can see. No mouth. It rides heat. Warm water rises beneath it, cools at the surface, sinks at the edge of the bowl, and the disc tilts just enough to stay in the upwelling. Every few minutes it pulses — not a swim stroke, a contraction — and squeezes a bead of clearer fluid out from its rim, then reabsorbs it. That is eating and breathing and excreting at once. It is filtering dissolved iron and sulfur out of that little loop, precipitating a faint dark ring around itself on the silt below, farming the bacteria it will then reabsorb.

When the pool finally dries — and they all dry — the disc does not die the way you think. It settles. It flattens. It loses water until it is a varnish, an amber stain on the bottom of the bowl, no thicker than dried paint. Cracked. Indistinguishable from mineral crust. In that state it can wait months. Years, if necessary. It has no urgency because the rain has no schedule. When water returns it peels up at the edge, hydrates, inflates, and resumes hovering as if no time passed. There might be ten thousand of them in the central pitted zone, or ten million as varnish, waiting. Even when the pools are full, there is so much space between one and the next that you could stand and watch a bowl for an hour and call it empty and be almost correct.

They don't know each other. They don't need to.

### The Inhabitants That Are Color

Up close, the rust reds and sulfur yellows and copper greens on the terrace lips are not quite rock.

They are skin.

It's a biofilm, but that word is too thin for what it is. It's a felt, microns thick, made of archaea and bacteria that have learned to eat stone and breathe steam. They are the reason the colors look like moss from a distance. They are doing the work that moss would do: holding water, softening light, making food from air — but they do it without chlorophyll, without green. They do it with iron.

You can tell which terrace is alive and which is just stained by touching it on a dry afternoon. The living crust is leathery. It gives slightly under your fingertip, like drying rubber. The dead crust cracks. If you watch after one of those patternless four-minute rains, the living bands wake up first. They darken. They swell almost imperceptibly and begin to gently fizz — tiny oxygen or hydrogen bubbles, too small to pop loudly, beading and releasing and beading again. They are photosynthetic when the sun is out and chemosynthetic when it's not, switching metabolisms the way you might switch hands when one gets tired.

Their lives are as slow as the terraces themselves. Growth is measured in mineral laminae, a season's rain laid down as a fraction of a millimeter. An individual colony may be decades across. It does not move. It does not want. The heat that feels like blood-temperature to you is perfect for them; the steam that crackles with static is useful to them — the little ticking lightnings between Towers dump fixed nitrogen onto them like fertilizer.

They do not hear the roar. That is the remarkable thing. Where you hear an unbearable, even industrial thunder, they live inside it. The constant *THUMP-thump-THUMP* of gas through the Tower throats vibrates the stone at a low frequency they can use. The films pulse very slightly in sympathy. If you put a stethoscope to the terrace edge you can feel it more than hear it — the whole painted skin trembling, not in distress, in entrainment. They have tuned their cell division to the vibration. The basin is humming them along.

They are the bulk of the biomass here. Ninety-nine percent of whatever counts as alive in this place is this paint. And it is sparse even so — long pale stretches of bare salt between bands, kilometers where nothing has found purchase.

### The Inhabitants That Are The Place

And then there are the Towers.

It is tempting to say they are not alive. They have no DNA in the way you think of it. They were built drip by drip around fumaroles, mineral accreted on mineral. But after a very long time — long enough to forget it was ever doing anything else — a structure so consistently hot, so consistently wet, so consistently full of moving chemistry, stops being a place where life might happen and becomes life itself.

Inside the throat of each chimney, in the pores of that bulbous slag-like cap, in the black glassy sludge that wells and spreads at the base, lives a continuous community that is the Tower. It is not one organism. It is a chimney-reef. Tube-building bacteria lay down iron silicate around themselves, die, and become the tube for the next layer. Thermophiles line the inner steam channels like gut flora, stripping sulfur from vapor, dumping acid that etches more pore space. The steam you hear as *HSSSSSSSS* has been filtered through them. The color of the sludge — black, then rust-red as it oxidizes — is their waste product.

Each Tower has a voice because each has a slightly different microbiome, a slightly different throat diameter, a slightly different porosity. One hisses higher. One thumps slower. They are not communicating. They are simply breathing out, each at its own equilibrium, and have been doing so for longer than weather remembers. In that sense they are the most alive thing here: they manufacture ground. The valley floor is their shell, their output. They are building the basin they stand in, very slowly, without intent, the way coral builds a reef without ever having decided to make an island.

They do not know the amber discs are there. They do not need to.

---

That is who lives here: things that wait as varnish, things that grow as paint, things that stand as slow furnaces and extrude their own world.

Nothing with eyes. Nothing with legs. Nothing that will ever make a road or need light from a window. Nothing that hurries, because hurrying here means drying out before the next rain.

If you stay a night and watch the dull cherry glow at the Tower seams, it is easy to imagine the whole basin as a single, extremely shallow animal — a palm held open to the sky, warm as blood, breathing out through many small mouths, listening to its own roar so constantly it has forgotten it is making a sound.
