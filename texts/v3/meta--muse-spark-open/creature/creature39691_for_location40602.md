Nothing lives here quickly.

That’s the first thing you have to understand. At first you will think the valley is empty, because you came looking for movement, for call-and-response. You will stand on the compressed moss of a trail and listen for it and hear only the metronome of water — *plink... plink... plink* — from frond to cushion to cistern. But emptiness, here, is just speed blindness. If you slow your own clock down until your breath makes its little cloud and disappears before you expect it to, the valley resolves.

It is densely, staggeringly inhabited. Just not by anything that hurries.

### The small tenants

Lowest and oldest are the ones that were always here, scaled to the moss.

The chrome beetles — you’ll see them first if you crouch — are not decoration. Each one, the size of the first joint of your thumb, is a perfect, cool oval of living metal. They do not fly. They walk the warm lines: the copper pipes, the seams of boilers, the black threads of the condensation sails. On the sunward side of any pipe there is a faint white bloom, a fungus they farm. They graze it, furrow by furrow, with mouthparts too small to see, and in return their legs carry its spores. Lift a beetle and the fungus underneath will be gone within the day — dried to a pale scar. Put him back and within the week it’s velvet again.

Higher up, drifting in the unmoving air between fern trunks, are the pale moths. They are big enough that you think at first they are loose scraps of paper, or shed fern skin. Four to six inches across, translucent white with a faint nacre, they don’t beat their wings. They *tilt* them. They ride the valley’s one slow draught — that constant, walking-speed fall of air down-valley — and adjust, banking a degree at a time. You will see one hold position for an hour above a bromeliad, lowering itself only as the little pool inside evaporates to reveal a crown of algae to drink. It never startles. If you breathe on it directly it will simply angle away, as if you were weather.

And rarer, because it requires distance: the heron. You’ll only catch it when you look up at the far wall and realize a thread is moving. Two meters wingtip to wingtip at least, but so pale and so glassy-feathered that the hard sky shows through it. Its neck is too long even to be folded; it flies with it outstretched, a slow javelin. It crosses from serration to serration without a single wingbeat you can catch, riding thermals that you can’t feel down here. It never calls — the air is too thin and too baffling for calls to carry, and besides there is nothing for it to say quickly. It lands in the sheer gorge where the pools are, stands for a long time, and does not fish so much as *wait* until a fish forgets it is there.

These things live here the way lichen lives on stone: precisely, without announcement.

### The grown machines

But the valley’s real biomass is not animal at all. It is the third thing, that the machines have become.

The factories are alive, but not awake. Each flywheel, turning once per minute on its cushion of steam, is a heart that never had to stop. The condensation switches — simple bimetals that swell with dew — click open, and a press sighs down on a sheet of mica, and a conveyor sighs it on. Algae tanks lining the ceilings glow that cool green work-light, feeding on the faint heat of the bearings and on sun through clouded glass, and that light feeds lichen, and that lichen is read by a chemical sensor as “particulate load,” and a vent twitches. It’s not intelligence. It’s metabolism.

Moss is its skin. Roots are its wiring. Over decades the copper pipes have not corroded so much as *been lined*, a second vascular system of rootlets threading inside them, sipping the distilled water and leaving behind a faint acid that keeps scale from forming. A vine finds an expansion joint and does not crack it; it *gaskets* it. A tree-fern growing from a boiler hatch does not destroy the boiler; its canopy keeps rain from the open gears, its roots hold ash that would otherwise clog.

If you put your hand on a lattice gantry you feel two temperatures: the sun-hot steel and, just under it, the cool live give of moss. If you listen very close, with your ear against the iron, you can hear water moving *through* the metal, not just in it.

Nothing here distinguishes between grown and built. The valley tried settlement and then forgot the distinction.

### The slow people

And then there are the people, because of course there are still people. Not many. Never many. You could live here a year and meet only two or three, and think you imagined the third.

They are not a tribe. They are not survivors. There is no village, no chief, no story of collapse. They are just the ones for whom this tempo is correct. Some were born in the foreman’s cabin on stilts; some walked up from the lowlands years ago and found their ears stopped ringing.

You’ll know them by absence before presence: a fresh compression in the moss, a condensation sail that has been wiped clean with a sleeve, a cistern lid left open a finger’s width to keep it from sealing. They are thin in the way high-altitude people are thin — not starved, but pared — with large, dark eyes narrowed permanently against that excessive white light. Their skin is pale and then weathered to the exact grey-pink of young basalt. Their feet make no sound because they don’t wear hard soles; they wear felted moss-boots, or nothing, and the moss accepts them.

They don’t talk. Not because they can’t, but because the valley has taught them that sound is wasteful. Sound here is eaten instantly; to be heard you would have to shout, and shouting would cost water. When they need to say something they do it with deixis and tempo: a palm held under a dripping frond tip to show *wait*, a tapping of two fingernails on a copper pipe in the rhythm of the drips — *now... now... now* — which means *enough, it’s full*. The children, when there are children, learn to read the gloss of a leaf to know if it can be taken.

Their whole day is water-work, and it is so slow it doesn’t look like work.

In the blue, breath-cloud morning they walk the harp-frames, tilting each one a fraction once the bead threshold is reached, watching the silver threads begin. They do it by hand, even though the frames can tip themselves — because a hand can feel if a thread is fouled with lichen and needs clearing, and a switch cannot. At noon, when the light is at its most brutal and the shadows are ink, they do nothing. They sit in the absolute shade under the twenty-foot fronds, under the factories’ eaves, and let the moss take their weight. They drink thimblefuls from bromeliads, not from cisterns; the cisterns are for the machines, which are less tolerant of thirst.

In the evening, when the mist comes up like milk and the algae tanks come on, they move again. That is when they tend the glasshouse, harvesting moss deliberately — cutting it in sheets as you would sod, laying it to dry on the warm sides of the engine-houses — and when they oil, with a rag, the one bearing that all the moss in the world has not quite learned to tend.

They endure by matching the flywheel: one revolution per minute. They age slowly. A woman you meet at the trail-compression may be thirty or seventy; the cool air and the diffuse, plantfiltered light preserve. They do not build new structures, and they do not tear old ones down. When a roof collapses they garden it. When a child is born they do not clear a new home; they find a cabin where the condensation on the inside of the windows has spelled, for a season, *empty*, and they move in.

If you stay until night truly pools, and you are quiet — truly quiet, until you can hear the almost inaudible sigh of a fern frond unfurling — one of them may stand near you at the rim of the gorge, not looking at you, just breathing, making his little puff of cloud in unison with the valley breathing out.

And you will understand then that the valley is not inhabited. The valley *is* the inhabitant: moss and beetle and moth and heron and steel and water and slowly, slowly, the people who have agreed to be its softest moving part.
