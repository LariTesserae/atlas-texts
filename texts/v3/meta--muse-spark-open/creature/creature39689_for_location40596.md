Who lives there are people who have learned to live like condensation.

Not the people who fell with it, if it fell. Not the engineers or soldiers or miners who might have cut it, whoever first poured the caliche and left their boot-prints in it before it set. Those people are gone the way any founders are gone — names painted over on the pump house, then painted over again.

Who lives there now are the second and third layer of occupants. The ones born to the idea that the sky is a coin.

They don't look like desert people from outside.

Desert people outside are lean, wind-scoured, wrapped to keep water in. The people in the bowl are paler than they should be, in the way that cave fish or workshop apprentices are pale — not from lack of sun, but from filtered sun. The amber dusk that filters down is not strong enough to tan. When direct light does come at noon, they avoid it. So their skin is light, dust-powdered, with the permanent soot-dark of forearms that work near the forge, the reddish chapping on cheeks from kiln-heat rather than windburn. Their eyes are narrow by habit, not genetics — they squint against sodium glare and steam even in dimness.

Bodies have gone soft and compact and efficient in the heat. No one is fat; there's not enough spare water for that. No one is tall and lanky either; they have grown to fit the alleys. Broad shoulders, short legs, a way of turning sideways without thinking to slip between a wall and a pipe. Knees and palms are callused in patterns you don't see on surface nomads — the heel of the hand thick from dragging barrels, the outside of the forearm smooth-shiny where it brushes hot corrugated roofing a hundred times a day. The west wall cooks at 3 p.m.; everyone has at least one old burn scar, pale and papery, that they don't remember getting. Children learn before speech: *don't touch the wall*.

They dress like the place itself: layers not for cold but for burn and drip.

Even at 40C they wear long sleeves. Loose, salt-stained shirts cut from reclaimed canvas and sheeting, cuffs buttoned at the wrist, collars up. Light trousers, tucked into boots or wrapped at the ankle against grit and sudden drips. At dawn — the hour of sweating — everyone adds the same thing: a poncho of clear salvage-plastic, oiled tarp, raincoat with the seams re-taped. Not to keep rain off. To funnel it. The poncho's shoulders channel the drip from pipes and laundry lines into gutters. They stand outside their doors in that brief rain and tilt themselves so water runs down their arms into buckets. You can tell a newcomer because they try to stay dry.

By ten, the ponchos come off and are stretched out as collection awnings, propped to drain into cisterns. By noon, they are shade-cloths again.

Hair is kept short or bound tight. Not style — steam. Long loose hair frizzes, catches on rebar, drips constantly. Most shave their heads in summer, which is always. Older women keep thick vines of gray hair oiled flat against the scalp, like the vines they tend. Jewelry is impractical; it burns. What ornament there is is functional: a copper wire bent to hook a bucket, a pressure gauge face polished and worn as a pendant because its numbers still mean something.

They are not loud people.

In a cup forty meters across with tin for walls, loudness is discourteous and useless. Children are taught to speak under the hum of the cables, to pitch their voices so they don't carry up the walls and echo. Conversations happen at arm's reach, often with a hand lightly on the other's shoulder to feel the words over the compressors. They don't shout across the well, even though they could hand a cup across it. They go and say it.

For the same reason, they listen extremely well. Hearing is the primary sense here, more than sight, because sight stops at the next catwalk. They can tell which pump is cavitating by its cough, which barrel is full by the timbre of its drip — *plink* vs. *plunk* — which pipe-joint has a micro-leak by the faint hiss in the vine leaves. An off-worlder might think they are ignoring you while they stare past you; they're actually listening to your valve. Or your breathing. Because there are no insects, no birdcall, their auditory world is stark and precise: human sounds against machine sounds against water sounds against rock settling. The lack of animals has tuned them. They hear the bowl itself.

And the bowl has taught them a schedule that would look perverse on the surface.

Dawn, 04:30-06:00: Everyone is up. Doors open. Barrels out. Faces tipped up. The whole town harvesting the air. Children with small tin cups scraping droplets off the underside of catwalks, old people wringing out cloths into pails. Talk is brisk, efficient. This is prayer and payday at once.

Mid-morning, 06:00-11:00: Transfer and maintenance. The cistern keepers — not a formal job, but everyone knows who they are, usually the oldest women who don't need to climb — test covers, add lime, chase evaporation with boards and tarps. The vine-tenders prune, redirect runners toward a sweating joint. The machine shop crowd opens up the pumps, checks the seep bleed at the north wall. The seep is communal and contentious. It never dries. Whoever lives closest to it gets damp floors and lime-skin in their lungs, but also the right to divert first liters. There's a rotation, negotiated daily, that no outsider would understand.

Noon, 11:00-14:00: Retreat. The column. The sun stands straight overhead and turns the bottom into a furnace. Flatbread cooks on iron, yes, but people do not. Shade-cloths go up in the well, alleys empty. The forge does not run; the pumps either. The rule *never both at once or the lights dim* is obeyed with religious literalism. This is nap time, repair-in-place time, school time under shade where the teacher whispers numbers so the heat doesn't hear. Babies sleep in dampened slings. The whole town seems to hold its breath while the rock radiates.

Afternoon, 14:00-18:00: Convection. The updraft starts — that silent, massive exhale up the walls. It carries heat off and brings, for the first time, the smell of outside: distant sage and hot dust from the plain above, diluted, almost imaginary. People come back out, energy restored. The barter alley opens in the central well. The evening market is not loud; it's a dense, shoulder-to-shoulder shuffle of buckets, rewound motors, grown ocotillo cuttings in drums, cups of seep-water that tastes of iron. Because nothing is on a grid, trade is by route: you know who needs what two tiers up because your pipe passes their door.

Evening, after the walls begin to release heat like ovens: Social time. Music, but quiet music — plucked strings muted with felt, because drums would shake dust into cisterns. Storytelling about the collapse, which changes each time: was it a bomb, a burst aquifer, a ship? No consensus, and no anxiety about that. What matters is the lesson: *the break is still fresh*. Rock settles. At 3 a.m. a flake will pop and someone will get up and mark where it fell.

They have a relationship with the wrongnesses that is not fear, exactly. More like husbandry.

Everyone has seen the second catwalk that isn't there. Everyone's heard the drip from a dry pipe. The low tone that comes when the pumps are off together — that molar-hum from the seep-crack — they actually use it. When they need to find a hairline leak in a joint, they cut the engines for sixty seconds and stand in the hush and listen for that tone to modulate. Young people feel it in their teeth and laugh nervously. Old people close their eyes to hear it better. To them it is not haunting; it is diagnostic. The bowl being deeper than its measured depth is just another fact like the wall being too hot to touch.

Children born here think the outside is the aberrant place. They are brought to the lip rarely — the climb is dangerous, the wind above is shocking, the sky too large. They cry in the open desert, accustomed to enclosure, to filtered light that pools. They are most themselves in the dim, in the hive.

There aren't many of them. The bowl holds maybe two hundred and fifty, edge to edge. Fewer than that can collect enough water, more than that and the seep can't keep up. They know this in their bodies. Pregnancies are spaced, celebrated carefully, not forbidden — just weighed against barrels. Elders are kept, because memory of pipes is survival, but no one is kept alive beyond repair the way the pumps are. They die quick and are put over the wall at night — carried up the bolted stairs to the rim and left on the flat for the desert to take, because there is no soil deep enough to bury them inside.

No pets. No livestock. That absence has left a hole they've filled with machines and vines.

Each family tends a boiler as if it were a goat — listening for its breathing, feeding it mineral water, wiping its valves. Each balcony's stunted ocotillo in its oil drum is named. People talk to the vines, adjust them like limbs. In place of fauna, they have cultivated attention itself. They are attentive people. Hands always busy, eyes always on drip rate, ears always cocked for settling.

If you lived there a year, you'd become them a little: your voice would drop half an octave, your shirt would stay long-sleeved, you'd stop expecting birdsong and start hearing pre-dawn as a kind of weather-front moving down basalt.

You'd learn to stand still in the dust-column at noon and watch motes hang too long without remarking on it, because someone else needs the cistern cover you are standing on.

You would become, like them, industrial and domestic and quiet — a small, complete, stubbornly human ecology fit perfectly to its cup, humming low, conserving drip after drip.
