Who lives there doesn't live there the way people live in other places.

There's no conquest in it. No dominance. You don't settle a place like the Valley. You come to terms with it. And if you're born in it, you come pre-termed.

### The People

They are cold people, even when warm.

The first thing you notice is how quiet *they* are. Not shy — saturated. Children are taught early to move in felt, to speak low. Voices here are small, close-range things. A mother doesn't shout for her kid in the moss-tunnel alley; she taps twice on the steam pipe and the kid taps back. Men who work the drop forges communicate in hand signals half-occluded by moss. Laughter happens with the mouth closed, exhaled through the nose so it doesn't fog and hang. After a few generations, throats have adapted. Everyone is a little hoarse from breathing wet air that never quite leaves.

They look damp. Even the driest of them — the forge-tenders who live leaned against factory heat — have a sheen. Pale skin gone ruddy and rough at the cheeks from the scrape of cold and then steam, cold and then steam. Hair that never quite dries, kept short or braided tight against dripping pipes. Eyes light, often grey or lichen-green, because bright eyes see better in diffuse noon. Hands are what give them away: swollen-knuckled, stained black at the cuticles with basalt dust and moss dye and machine oil that never washes out. No one has clean hands. If you do, it means you don't work, and if you don't work, you don't keep your right-of-use.

They dress like the buildings. Layered, accreted. Not outfits so much as assemblages: oiled canvas smocks to shed condensation, wool felt vests underneath that stay warm when wet, reindeer-hide boots soft-soled to feel the hum of the dynamo through the floor. Everyone wears leather aprons with too many pockets. Copper bracelets - not jewelry - to ground out the strange field that makes your teeth ache on heavy-gravity Thursdays. Moss is not just around them, it is on them. A foreman will have a living collar of it growing up the back of his coat where the steam hits his shoulders. Old women cultivate tiny gardens in the hoods of their cloaks.

Physically, they are compact, low to the ground, like their city. You don't get tall here. The young basalt gives under tall weight, and low ceilings of birch-root and pipe teach you to stoop early. They walk with a particular gait: short steps, knees soft, ready for the ground to tilt or sigh six inches left overnight. When they stand still they sway a little. Visitors get motion sick.

And they are all, in a sense, maintenance workers.

There are no professions that are not also tending.

*The Scrapers:* mostly children and the old, who move through the city each dawn with flat bone knives before the work whistle — which isn't a whistle but a change in pressure — shaving moss off windows, off lenses, off the insulators on the gantries. Without them the city would go dark and blind in a week. They sing while they work, very quietly, songs that are just lists of streets.

*The Oilers:* who walk the pipe forests with long-spouted cans, anointing hinges, joints, exposed brick, the axles of gantry sledges. Their smell precedes them. They are holy in a practical way; a door that sticks on a heavy-gravity day can trap a family in a burning room.

*The Sledgewrights:* carpenters who never use foundations. They build everything — houses, workshops, chapels, latrines — on birch runners or basalt stilts with wide, snowshoe feet, lashing walls with living willow withes that flex instead of crack. A Sledgewright can, with ten neighbors, drag a house off a rising hot spring in an afternoon and will expect tea and thanks and no further payment, because next week his house will need dragging too.

*The Hydrosts:* greenhouse-tenders who live ten-high in the stacked glass tiers against the mill walls. Their skin is greener than other people's, their hair bleached from grow-lights. They breed the impossible: thumb-hummingbirds that shouldn't survive winter, strawberries the size of fists, insulating vines that fruit in -10C. They speak to plants the way the Oilers speak to hinges. Their chapels are grafting chapels.

*The Canal Listeners:* fishers and water-diviners who pole flat-bottomed barges through the braided river that never learned its name. They read the water's doming when gravity goes light, read the steam to know where new basalt is about to pillow up and block the channel. Their children can tell, by taste, which cistern will go sulfurous next. Their fish have see-through heads and lantern eyes, though the water is shallow; the Listeners wear necklaces of those dead lanterns when they navigate the night canals.

*The Rememberers / The Boiler-Priests:* boiler-suited elders who keep no written deeds because deeds would be lies. They keep position. They remember that the soapworks was three buildings to the north before the sink of '83, that the birch now load-bearing in the turbine hall was a sapling planted by a woman to hide a rust stain. They bless new ground with copper wire and boiling water, which is just good engineering — copper seeds moss, boiling water tests if the ground will hold. People bring them chalk stones that have moved.

What unites them is a temperament: Patient Turbulence.

They do not plan for permanence. They plan for renegotiation. A wedding contract here includes the clause "until the ground parts us." A child is taught to draw a map in chalk, then to watch the rain erase it and draw it again, better. They have a humor about it — black, damp humor. When a man wakes up and his door opens on a wall of new moss where an alley used to be, he swears, then cuts a new door where the moss is thinnest. When gravity goes light, the crane crews tie themselves down and play dice on the vertical wall for a joke, floating.

They are not superstitious, but they are animist in the only way an industrial people can be: everything is alive because everything acts alive. The dynamo has a mood. The moss has opinions about which brick it will eat. The cold has a weight. If you oil something, you thank it, because ingratitude produces rust faster. This is not religion, or not only religion. It's maintenance protocol verified by a hundred years of hiss and sink and bloom.

They breed quickly and grieve quietly. You have many children because a cold damp takes a few early — lung-moss, the white cough from spore-heavy winters — and because many hands keep a district from stalling when the city reconfigures. When someone dies, you don't bury them deep; the ground won't keep them. You lay them in the moss mattress on the hill, and the moss takes them by morning, and birch grows there sideways the next spring. No headstone. Just a slightly warmer patch of ground.

### The Others, Who Were There First, Or At Least Don't Need Right-of-Use

The humans are only the noisiest animal in the negotiation. And they are not noisy at all.

**The Reindeer Commute.** Not wild, not tame — municipal. Thirty, fifty at a time, moving on schedules no human has fully mapped, down avenues that were turbine halls last year. They eat wall-moss with surgical tongues, leaving perfect clean rectangles that humans use as noticeboards. They step over sleeping people. In return, humans leave the cistern lids open for them in summer and never, ever fence. An old bull with frost in his antlers will stand in the middle of a market and not move until the gantries stop overhead, and the market will flow around him. Their calves are born in the warm lee of the forges and learn the taste of iron before grass.

**The Pipe Foxes.** Small, soot-black, eyes like hot rivets. They never come outside. The entire steam-pipe network of the city — kilometers of dripping, clanking intestine — is their forest. They hunt condensation rats and loose hummingbirds in there. Workers leave them scraps of lard on pipe joints for luck; the foxes leave tiny, perfect pawprints in new rust that the Oilers read like augury. You hear them sometimes at night: a soft *thit thit* above your ceiling, exactly as loud as a dripping pipe, and then not.

**The Gulls and the After-Gulls.** The true constant. Fat, white, cold-gulls nesting in exhaust vents, riding the steam thermals, utterly unbothered by variable gravity. They hover without wingbeats when the field lightens, dropping like stones when it heavies, and they know. When the gulls all leave a roof at once, humans look to see what new island is about to hiss up from the canal. Alongside them: crows that collect copper wire, pigeons that nest in moss three feet thick, and that owl — seen once, remembered forever — with a wingspan that eclipsed an alley, that flew low and slow between tenements without touching a wall, as if the turbulent air made room.

**The Hummingbirds.** An insult to the climate. Bred for pollination in the greenhouse stacks and then gone feral, wintering on factory heat and engineered winterbloom. They are everywhere in the central wards, thumb-sized, impossible metabolism burning so hot you can feel them pass. The only real buzz in the quiet. They swarm in the birch-tunnels that used to be alleys, a living static. Children catch them gently and let them go for the heat of them in the hand.

**The Fish With Lanterns.** Translucent-headed, light-behind-the-eyes fish in water that has no business being that deep or that dark. But the canals are never still and never empty. They flicker in schools under bridges where fungal farms bloom. Some say they weren't stocked; they condensed like the dew. The Canal Listeners say they remember being river fish before the river existed.

**The Moss Itself.** And the lichen, and the dwarf birch that learned to grow sideways, and the vines bred for insulation that ate their mills. It would be wrong to call this vegetation *flora* as if it were passive. Here it is a citizen. It holds walls together, filters water, eats soot and excretes soil. It decides which wall to seal shut and which roof to make into a roof-garden. It dampens sound. It negotiates. When you scrape it off your window, you do it respectfully, leaving a border so it knows to come back. If you don't, it will take the window entirely.

And underneath/between/around it all:

**The Valley.** Not a god. Nothing so coherent. A young place still deciding what it wants to be, embryonic geology with an index of erosion near zero because nothing has had time to erode. People feel it as a low-grade attention. Shadows that linger a second too long. Reflections that show the city as it was or will be. The faint tingle in copper before new basalt pillows up, steaming and already mossed, sometimes with a half wall from last time still attached.

The Valley doesn't live *in* the city. The city lives *in* it, like moss on basalt.

And like moss, like foxes, like pipe-workers in felt, it is mostly quiet.

It is never still.
