It does not live in the City. It *is* the City. Or rather — the City is the part of it you can see.

What lives there was never one thing. You came looking for a single species, a single builder with a single name, but that is a mammal idea. Nothing that large and that patient is ever alone.

The first citizens you meet are so small you almost do not meet them at all.

They are on the outer terraces, where the wind hits. They are the weather-workers, 3 millimeters long, the color of weak tea, blind. You would take them for specks of dust if the dust did not march in perfect lines against the wind.

They are the Paper-Makers. Or rather, their great-great-grandmothers were. Their jaws are not jaws as you understand them; they are shears, shovels, trowels, pulp mills. They leave the mouths of the City in the late morning when the sun softens the outer wax, climb out onto the plateau grass a few hundred feet from the walls, clip it with a *tick-tick-tick* you cannot hear over the roar, chew it, mix it with saliva that contains a fungus that contains an enzyme that turns grass into paste, and carry it back.

One mouthful is nothing. A million mouthfuls a day, for ten thousand years, is a stratum. A lunule the size of a house. A terrace. A rampart that from a distance looks like stone.

They do not know they are building a mountain. Each one only knows: take the grass, make the paper, patch the place where the rain struck yesterday. The mountain is what appears when you add up ten billion of those decisions over a time span no single worker lives to see. The workers live forty days. The City lives in centuries.

If the Paper-Makers are the masons, then deeper in, where it goes warm and truly dark, are the Gardeners.

You smell them before you see them. The whole interior smells of yeast and warm bread and turned earth even though there is no earth. That is their breath. They are ants — but not the ants you know from your kitchen. These are bigger, chestnut-brown, with heads like polished knuckles. Their highways are the grooves worn an inch deep into the floor; you feel them under your boots as ruts. They walk in columns that never end, each carrying a pale green flake of leaf, a petal, a scrap of the vine that grows everywhere.

They do not eat the leaves.

They chew the leaves into a paste even finer than the Paper-Makers' paste, and they feed it, deep in the humid heart where the temperature never drops below 86 degrees, to something else: great sponge-beds of fungus, white and grey, that swell out of the walls like lungs.

The fungus is the real citizen. Or the ants are the fungus's way of walking. It does not matter which you call the builder. The fungus cannot walk. The ants cannot digest cellulose. Together, they can turn the whole plateau's summer growth into sugar, into ant, into more fungus, into heat.

That's where the warmth comes from. Not from the sun — at this elevation you should be shivering. It is the compost-heat of an acreage of fungal gardens the size of a downtown core, all breathing at once. The condensation that beads on your neck is fungal sweat. The white fruits and tubers farmed in alcoves are its fruiting bodies, pinched off and eaten before they can sporulate. The Gardeners tend them with a care that looks like worship: licking away mold, moving larvae away from rot, carrying their dead sisters out to the middens where pill bugs will take them.

And then deeper still, where the green thins because even the patient vines cannot stand the heat and the traffic, are the Wax-Makers.

They are heavier, slower, almost silent. Bees? Wasps? The words fail because they have been here too long to be either. They are the color of old amber and they never see the sun. Their abdomens are wax glands. They hang in chains from the ceilings of the nursery galleries, holding each other leg-to-leg for days, shivering, sweating wax in translucent scales that their sisters chew and press into place.

Their architecture is different. The outer City is paper — matte, fibrous, sound-deadening. The inner City is wax and silk — warm to the touch, faintly luminous. You put your hand on a wall in the deep galleries and it gives slightly, body-warm. That is where the light comes from: not windows, but panels of thinned wax the builders have stretched under vertical shafts, diffusing the sky into that brown dusk.

Their children are why the wax matters.

In the hexagonal cells — perfect, endless, each just larger than your thumb — lie larvae, fat as forearms, translucent, blind, pulsing faintly. They do not move. Hundreds of thousands of them. The air above them vibrates with nurses — smaller, restless, antennae never still — feeding them drop by drop from mouth to mouth, cleaning them, turning them so they do not stick to the wax. The sound here is worst of all: not buzzing but a soft, incessant gurgle and *pop*, like a bog breathing.

And at the very center, where the air is too hot and too thick with carbon dioxide and pheromone for you to stay long without feeling faint, are the Queens.

There is not one. There are many. That is why the City is the size of a mountain range and not the size of a hill.

Each one is a factory made of abdomen. They were once winged and small and could fly — twenty years ago, on a single wet afternoon you missed. Now they are each larger than a horse, glistening, striated, immobile, buried in a retinue so dense it looks like a second, living body. Attendants stroke them, feed them, carry away the eggs as fast as they slide out — one per second, *plip. plip. plip.* — down to the wax galleries. Their heads and thoraxes are absurdly small on top of that mountain of ovary; they look like afterthoughts. They do not think. They do not need to. The City thinks for them with chemistry.

That chemistry is the true government.

You cannot see it, but you are already breathing it, tasting it. Every wall, every highway, every chamber is painted — invisibly, constantly — with hydrocarbon trails that say: build here, garden here, carry here, guard here, die here. An ant detours over your boot without breaking stride because your boot is not in the sentence. If you stood still for a day, they would start to paint you into it. A vine brushes your cheek to taste your salt because the Gardeners let it; the green is not invader, it is plumbing. Those pale leaves spread flat to catch the brown light and exhale sugar that mites drink that beetles eat that Paper-Makers pulp.

That is the fauna, too. Not just the builders.

No wall that perfect stays empty. Where the builders make crevices, the lodgers come.

The polished beetles big as your fist in the lower galleries are not guests; they are armored tanks that live by being ignored. They blunder through columns, exuding the exact peace-smell of the colony, and eat wax, and fungus, and sometimes larvae, and the workers let them because the code says they are walls.

The sheets of mites that look like moving dust are the cleaners. The wasps with paper wings that crawl instead of fly are police and parasite at once. The small dark birds that scream at dusk and nest in the paper cliffs — they are not insects at all, and they were not invited, but they have learned that the paper mountain never freezes, never gets rained on inside, and is full of flies. The bats that pour out for an hour every night are the night shift, taking the moths that would otherwise take the wax.

Even the sound is a lodger. That deafening pressure you felt from a mile out, that high whine behind your teeth from millions of crickets all slightly out of phase — they were not brought in. They came because nothing else in the world makes a warm, damp, dark, vibrating box this big. The City sings because everything that loves a dark hive has moved into it.

And it keeps growing.

Not fast. A fallen loader blocks a road for three days and no one minds. A wall collapses and is rebuilt in a year, mouthful by mouthful. The outer rampart migrates thirty feet east per century as wind eats the west face and workers patch the east. If you walked for a week in one direction, you would barely see change — only the shade of brown shifting from tea to rust to bone, only the species of ant on the highway changing as you cross an invisible chemical border into the territory of a sister queen.

If you stand still long enough, you understand: the City has no inside and outside the way you do. There is no bedrock to be found. Even the air in your lungs has been through a thousand spiracles before you, exhaled humid and warm from a nursery wall. The damp on your skin was condensation on wax a minute ago.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is *occupied*, totally, to saturation. Every square inch is accounted for, worked, reworked, tasted, carried. It does not need you. It did not evolve a place for a large, sweating, solitary mammal that stands still and watches.

But leave your pack on the floor for an hour and come back. The Paper-Makers will have worried at its strap to see if it is fiber. The Gardeners will have tried to cut a flake from its canvas. A vine will have shifted a pale leaf over it to catch its shade. A mite will have walked its length and decided.

Stay still long enough — lie down in one of the abandoned tiers where the moss is thick as a mattress — and the nurses will find you, antennae flickering, not biting, just tasting the salt on your forehead, and deciding, slowly, in that patient chemical language, whether you are damage to be repaired, food to be stored, or, for a little while, just another warm piece of the City's wall.
