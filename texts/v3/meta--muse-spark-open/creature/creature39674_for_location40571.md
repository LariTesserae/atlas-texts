It is not that anything *lives* here, in the way you live where you came from.

Living, where you came from, means insisting. Pushing against gravity and hunger and cold. Leaving a mark. Here, living is listening so hard you forget to insist.

You notice them slowly, which is how they want to be noticed.

At first you think the walls are spotted. Lichen, damp, shadow — the eye wants a simple answer for the brightness. But then one of the spots detaches.

#### The Ones That Hang

There are dozens of them. You count twenty and then give up because another three have moved while you were counting.

Call them moths because you have to call them something, and because they have the courtesy to be moth-sized. The size of a cold palm. The color of paper left in the sun. Their wings are so thin the sourceless light passes straight through them, and you can see the pitted limestone *through* the wing-pattern, as if the wings are thinking about being wings and haven't fully committed.

They do not roost so much as pause. They hang a fraction away from the wall, not quite touching it. Absolutely still. You can watch one for a minute and forget to breathe, and it will not move a filament.

Then — without flex, without push — it will be somewhere else. Three feet to the left. Upside down near a pocket of water. Behind your head. The move has no travel in it. No blur of flight. One frame it is there, the next frame it is there, and the air between is undisturbed.

If you are unlucky enough to have one pass close to you, you will not hear wings. You will hear something else leaking through the place where wings should be. A sheep-cry from the moor, but too close, inside your ear. A boot squelching in peat that hasn't been stepped in for a hundred years. A tally-mark being scraped, *scrit*, one line at a time. The sound lands only while the creature is mid-air, and cuts off neat as a door.

And when you look again at the place it left — it is still there. Not a shadow. A faint after-wall. An image of its own underside, still clinging to the stone like a decal of moth, slowly fading. The creature doesn't leave the wall. It *takes* the wall with it, and leaves a copy behind until the copy remembers it's not needed.

They weigh nothing. If one settles on your shoulder — and one will, if you stay still and cool enough — you won't feel it until you turn your head and see the light coming through it, and through you.

They are not many creatures. Or they are not *only* many creatures. Sometimes you blink and there are fewer, but each one is slightly larger. Sometimes you breathe and there are more, but each one is paler. They are learning how many of themselves there should be.

#### The Ones That Pick

Down by the water pockets — those perfect, round, unconnected hands of water that ripple toward the ceiling when a drop hits them — the floor is busy at ankle level.

Shrimp-isopods. Woodlice that forgot they were supposed to have eyes. They are clear as cold broth, and you can see the pebble of their gut working, and the faint knot of a heart that beats out of time with yours.

They pick. That is all they do. They pick their way over the knuckled nodules of new limestone, nose to stone, legs moving with a terrible care. And with each step, the stone dimples *upward* around the foot. Not down, as weight would dent it. Up, as if the creature is pressed to the underside of a membrane and the rock is thin latex giving way. If you crouch — and you will crouch, because the cool makes you want to be small — you can see daylight through some of them for an instant, not this bright sourceless light, but real moor-light, with a blade of that translucent grass waving, *above* them.

They do not eat. Not visibly. They pass a leg through a water pocket and the water will flex around it without wetting it. But if one pauses too long at the edge, the water will suddenly hold, for a flash, a perfect white outline of its insides — its skeleton if it had one, which it doesn't, not in this world — and then let go, and the creature will shudder and move on, as if it was shown something impolite.

There are beetles too, beetle-things, black and high-domed, moving slower than anything should move and still be alive. Their carapaces are not shiny. They are deep. Look into one as it passes over your boot and you won't see your boot reflected. You'll see a night that has never been over this moor: a black sky with two small, close moons, one chasing the other, and a wind in grass you don't recognize. The beetle carries that sky on its back and doesn't know it. If you try to touch it, your finger will feel a faint static pull, like before a storm, and the beetle will tuck its legs and be a stone for a while.

#### The Ones That Watch

At the edge of every pocket sit the frogs. That is the wrong word but the shape is right: humped, throatless, eyes like wet beads. They are the color of the raw limestone, so at first you don't see them at all, until you realize the nodules around the largest pocket are all facing the same empty corner.

They do not croak. They do not blink. You can wave your hand over one and it will not flinch. It is not watching you. It is not watching the moths or the shrimp or the beetles.

It is watching the corner where nothing is.

If you sit with them long enough — and the room's quiet will make you sit, because standing begins to feel like an interruption — you will follow their gaze.

There is nothing in that corner. Just young rock, damp, with the tool-marks of its own making still ridged like thumb-prints. And condensation, beading, ticking. Except one bead there never falls. It swells, larger than any other, until it is the size of a knuckle, a marble, an eye, and it does not fall. It just holds, reflecting the room back at itself, too bright, too still.

When you lean in, you will see that the reflection is wrong. Your shoulder is not where your shoulder is. The entrance slit is on the wrong wall. And behind you — where your back is — there is a shape standing precisely where you are standing, but not you, watching that bead from the other side.

The frogs see it even when you don't. That is their work. They sit and keep the corner from being empty.

No one eats anyone else here. Or rather — and this is important, this is how you can stay — eating hasn't been agreed upon yet. A moth will settle on a water pocket. The water will hold the moth's impossible skeleton, and the shrimp will pause, and the beetle will carry its other-night onward, and nothing is taken. The dynamic is active, crowded, constantly exchanging, but it is an exchange of *places* and *versions*, not flesh. A predator-prey system for things that have not yet decided which is solid.

This is why the cool preserves. This is why the light has no shadow. The room is not finished being a room. The limestone is still being extruded, still being thumbed into corners. The water hasn't learned downhill. The animals have slipped in through the thought where the rule is thin, because unfinished places are bright if you are looking for somewhere to become.

They will let you be one of them for a while. If you are quiet. If you don't drink.

They will find the tally marks on your skin and read them, and find you accurate enough to sit with.
