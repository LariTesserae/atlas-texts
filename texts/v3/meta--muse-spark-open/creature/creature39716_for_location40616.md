What lives here hasn't learned to fight the place. It has learned to agree with it.

Nothing here grows fast. Nothing needs to. In a valley that never changes, the advantage goes not to the quick or the clever, but to the steady - to whatever can be damp forever, can be cool forever, can make a life in dimness without ever asking for more light.

So start at the bottom.

### The Stone Eaters

The true forest of this valley is flat against the rock. It's the green-grey phosphorescence you saw on every seep and joint - a living skin inches thick made of bacteria, pale lichen, algal felt and fungi braided together so tightly you can't peel one from the other. It eats the rock itself. It leeches minerals from the fresh basalt and limestone, fixes nitrogen from the still air, traps condensation before it can fall. When you touch the wall and your hand comes away cold and faintly glowing, that's it.

It doesn't look like much. It is everything.

What falls from it - dead cells, spores, a constant fine rain of organic dust - feeds the water. What is scraped off it feeds the stone-crawlers. It is the only thing here that knows how to make food from stone and air and dimness, and so the entire valley is built on its back. Scrape it away and the rock beneath is sterile and sharp and new. Leave it for a hundred years and it will have softened the edge by a millimeter, made soil where there was none.

Where the collapse-windows let in real daylight, it gives way. That's where the mosses and liverworts and single-file ferns take over, dark as wet leather, never evolved for drought because they have never known it. That's where the pale birches stand on their little islands of soil - spindly, white-barked, leaves always cocked toward the distant ovals of sky. They don't form a forest. Each one is alone. Between them is a hundred yards of bare stone, and that is correct. The valley doesn't want a forest. It wants a crust.

### The Number, Not the Kind

On that crust moves the weight.

You heard it before you understood it - the papery thunder, the chitinous rustle, the plop and croak that never stops. This valley has almost no variety. In a place with no seasons and no weather, there is no reason to diversify. There is only reason to be very, very good at being damp and cool and dim, and then to make more of yourself until the air is full.

**The Moths.** Small, the color of ash and old bone, with wings like dusty glass. There are not thousands. There are millions, and counting them is meaningless because they move as one thing. They rise in a slow pulse - up off the stone, up into the vault - hang there churning the air for an hour, then settle again in a soft, smothering carpet that covers every horizontal surface. They feed on the stone-skin and on airborne spores. They drink condensation off the rock with tongues longer than their bodies. They are food for everything else, and they know it; they don't flee, they just rise and settle, rise and settle. Their only defense is number. Their droppings, fine as soot, rain into the pools and feed the blind fish.

**The Needle-bats.** They are the other half of that pulse. Black as wet soot, no bigger than your thumb, with noses drawn to a needle point and ears like torn leaves. They hang in shingles, thousands deep, on every overhang and fault scarp - not in colonies, but in a single continuous colony that runs for miles. You never see them all at once. You hear them always - a high, desperate chittering that never resolves into silence, because while half sleep, half hunt. They take the moths mid-air in snaps you feel more than hear. They navigate not only by echo but by memory; the valley never changes shape, so a bat born here never needs to learn a new map. Its guano streaks the walls in black varnish and piles beneath the roosts in reeking, steaming fans where heat actually gathers - the only warm places in the whole rift. In those fans, the millipedes breed.

**The Stone-rivers.** The pale millipedes, forearm-long, blind, the color of mushrooms. They move shoulder to shoulder in slow rivers over the wet stone, antennae waving, legs moving in a wave that looks like wind over wheat. They eat the stone-skin, they eat bat guano, they eat each other’s shed skins, they eat the dead bats that fall and are not allowed to rot wasted. Nothing is wasted here. Behind them, the stone is licked clean for a foot before the crust begins to grow again. If you stand still long enough one will try your boot, tasting the salt.

In the water, the same principle. The black pools are not empty. Look close and the white flashes are blind fish - finger-long, eyeless, translucent enough to see the dark line of gut through the belly - moving in tight, nervous schools that turn as one when your shadow falls. Beside them, crowded flank to flank in every stream so shallow they seem beached, are the sirens: amphibians with skin like skim-milk and huge, feathery external gills that pulse constantly, red as blood in the dim light. They do not hop. They do not need to. They sit in the current with mouths open and filter the moth-dust and larvae that wash down. They croak - a low, belching chorus that underlies the bats’ chitter - not to attract mates across distance, but to say *I am still here, this water is taken.* There is no migration. A siren hatched in one riffle will die in that same riffle years later, having never moved ten feet.

It is loud because there is no wind to carry sound away. Sound bounces from scarp to scarp, from vault to floor, until direction dies. Everything here is loud and everything here is adapted to being loud: the bats scream above hearing, the moths beat wings below it, the sirens find a middle tone that can cut through both. To be quiet here is to disappear.

And that is why you almost never see the ones who left the tools.

### The Flint-Knappers

They are still here. Or rather, here still happens to them.

You found a flake with the bulb still bright, a charred stick sharpened and dropped, a gourd half-full of seep-water, a bone awl lost between rocks. One per acre. No houses, no hearths, no burials, no cairns. That is not because they were careless. It is because they do not believe that a place should be marked.

They are not bats. They are not moths. They are people in the way that anything that makes a flake like that is people - narrow, damp-adapted, patient. If you saw one clearly - you rarely do - you would first think it was not there at all.

They are small. A grown one stands to your chest, and weighs less. Their skin is the pallor of the sirens, grey-pink and translucent at the temples and wrists where you can see blue veins too close to the surface. They have no need for melanin; what little light reaches them is filtered and soft. Their hair is thin, colorless, lank with condensation, cut or broken off short because long hair collects fungus here. Their eyes are huge - not the blank white eyes of the fish, but dark, wet, light-gathering eyes with an amber shine when phosphorescence catches them, set wide to see movement in gloom. But they don't hunt by sight.

They hunt by hearing, the way the bats do.

Their ears are long, mobile, cupped, always turning. In the roar of wings and croak and rustle, they can pick out the drip that means a pool has been disturbed, the different rustle that means a millipede river is turning, the tiny splash of a siren striking. They speak very little above a whisper, and when they need to speak across distance they don't shout - shouting is useless here. They click. A soft, palatal *tik - tik tik* that pierces the bat-noise like a needle, or a low hum felt in the chest more than heard. Children learn to be still for a very long time before they learn to knap.

They are angular to match the stone. Long limbs, long fingers tipped with wide, flat nails for prying, elbows and knees that seem double-jointed for wedging into fault cracks. They climb the fresh scarps not with ropes but barefoot - soles as tough and pale as leather, toes splayed - moving along lines you would swear were unclimbable. The sharpness that would cut your hand is purchase for them. They sweat constantly and never dry; their clothes, if you can call them clothes, are not woven - weaving never dries here either - but cured siren-skin capes, sewn with gut, worn open, more for carrying than warmth. In this perfect cool, warmth is never the problem. Staying dry is impossible, so they have stopped trying.

There are not many of them. There cannot be. This valley makes an enormous amount of moth and fish and guano, but very few calories a human gut can use without a fire that will not stay lit. So they live thinly, as thinly as the birches. A family - three, four, sometimes six - will hold a stretch of rift a half-mile long, walking it slowly for days, sleeping in shifts under overhangs where the condensation falls least. They do not build. Building would mean staying, and staying would mean fouling the water, exhausting the moth-settle, being heard. Instead they carry a kit that can be abandoned: a few good flakes struck from a nodule of flint brought down from the throat, a fire-drill that mostly makes char for hardening points, a gourd, a bone awl. When a flake dulls, they drop it. When a point chars through, they leave it. That is why the artifacts are scattered one per acre, bright and lonely. Each one is a place where someone stopped long enough to work and then kept moving because the valley is loudest when you stay still.

They eat what the valley offers without cooking it much: millipede grubs fat as thumbs, scooped from guano fans; sirens, gutted and eaten raw, gills discarded; moths, if you can stand to gather them by the handful when they settle - a bitter, dusty mouthful that fills the belly. They drink from the black pools, faces reflected beside yours for a moment before it ripples. They know which pools the blind fish have cleaned and which have stood too long. They know not to drink directly beneath a bat shingle.

They have no art that lasts, because nothing lasts in damp except stone. Sometimes, on a dry-ish slab beneath a birch island - the only place dry-ish exists - you will find peck-marks: a circle, a wavy line for water, a hand with too-long fingers. In a month the lichen will have taken it. They don't mind. They navigate by sound and by the feel of fault lines underfoot, not by marks. Cairns would be insulting to a people who can hear the shape of a scarp from fifty yards away.

At night - though there is no night, only the dimming when clouds cover the collapse-windows - they gather under the vault where it sweats hardest and let the cold rain fall on their upturned faces. They do not fear the dark. They were born in it. The old ones go blind eventually, eyes clouding white like the fish, and are led by the clicks of grandchildren until they lie down in a shallow basin and stop.

If you stay here long enough, quiet enough, you might see one. Not approach - you will never approach. You will see, at the edge of your vision, a pale shape detach itself from pale stone, move without hurry across a terrace, crouch to turn a rock in a stream, and vanish between two angles of basalt where you would have sworn there was no passage. You will find, the next morning, a fresh flake at your feet, left not as gift or warning but simply because it was done with.

And then the moths will rise again, and the bats will shift, and the sirens will croak, and the valley will hum at the exact pitch it has held for a thousand years - cool, damp, dim, empty and impossibly full - and settle back into equilibrium around the small, wet footprint you left, until condensation erases it too.
