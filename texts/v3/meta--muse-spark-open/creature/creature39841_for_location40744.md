The people who live in the High Wet Town are not the kind of people who moved there. They are the kind who were left after everyone who could leave, did.

You can tell them first by their chests and their throats.

They breathe like people who have been breathing thin air for five or six generations. Broad, barrel chests under sweat-dark work shirts, necks thick, lips often parted slightly even at rest. Children born in the basin learn not to run until they learn how to breathe while running - short, paced sips of air that don't starve them. Newcomers from the lowlands get dizzy on the stairs up from the culvert-works and get nosebleeds at noon when the sun is highest. The locals don't. They've got more blood in them, somehow. It's darker when they cut themselves on sheet metal.

You can tell them second by their ears.

Everyone over twelve is half-deaf. Not in a tragic way. In a worked way. The children shout when they play on the gravel flats between the soda-ash evaporation pans, not because they're excited but because otherwise no one hears them. Adult conversation at close range is a lean-in, eyes-locked, mouth-reading thing done at half-bellow. Older workers wear little twist plugs of waxed cotton that have been re-used until they're grey, and they have a sign language that isn't official - just twenty or thirty big, clear gestures borrowed from the crane crews and the rail switchers: *STOP. LIFT. MORE. WATER COMING. BEHIND YOU.*

They are not loud people by nature. They would be quiet, if the town let them. In the dormitory chapels that were never deconsecrated, or in the break rooms when the stamping presses pause for thirty seconds between sheets, you can hear how softly they actually speak. A habit of conservation. Sound costs effort here.

They live spread thin because the town demands it. Density 1.2 and urban 2.5 means every family has too much space and not enough neighbor. A row of five brick houses built for foremen in 1930, then a half-kilometer of nothing but hissing pipe rack, rust scale, a canal with fast brown water, a yard of stacked fittings, then another row of houses. A woman can hang laundry and know there is exactly one other person within shouting distance, and she is shouting distance away. Children grow up with a very precise map in their heads of which stretches of road have shade - not from trees, there are no trees to stand under - from the elevated trams and the pipe bridges - and which culverts you can hear coming before you see.

They are maintenance people. Even the ones who run the textile sheds and the stamping presses describe their jobs in hydraulic terms. You don't *make* things in the High Wet Town so much as you *keep* them. You keep the limescale from closing a pipe. You keep the reeds from closing a canal. You keep the pumps from losing to the water that wants to come up through the floor. A machinist here will show you the inch-thick rind of white and rust mineral on a bend of low-pressure steam line and tap it with a hammer and listen to it the way a doctor listens to a chest. "That's this year's," he'll shout proudly. Ten years and a pipe becomes a stalactite that still happens to carry water.

Because of that, the other things that live here are not pets or livestock. Nothing large can afford to live here and not work.

### The Almost-Fauna

There should be frogs. There should be clouds of midges over the Plashes, swallows, carp in the canals. There aren't. The water is too hot, too alkaline, too full of dissolved soda and slicked with a film of machine oil that makes rainbow on every surface.

Instead there are:

**The skimmers.** Pale, ashy-grey, with legs like dry grass and a body no bigger than your thumb. They are not on the water, they are *above* it - they skate on steam. You see one every ten or fifteen minutes flicking down a canal on its long limbs, touching only the meniscus, never breaking it, hunting the sulfur bacteria threads that bloom orange in the outflows. Workers like them. They mean a culvert is still oxygenated. If you see two at once, someone says so. It's an event.

**The bone-kites.** Very high. So high in the thin air that they are white cuts against blue, barely moving. They never descend into the basin roar. They circle the thermals that rise off the hot gravel yards and the kilns, watching. What for is unclear - there is nothing to eat down there for a bird that size. The oldest workers say they are not waiting for food. They are waiting for wind. They nest on the convex basalt spines above the veil-falls, where no human goes because the rock weeps too much to hold a piton. Sometimes one will be found dead on a factory roof, and it is enormous and light as paper, hollow, and when you spread its wings they are stained faintly pink from the mineral dust.

**The clicker.** You never see it. At night, when the presses throttle down and the roar opens up into its true shape - that vast patient hum from every direction - the ten-foot reeds in the Plashes begin. A single, sustained, glassy *cheeeee* that lasts six seconds, stops, answers itself half a mile away with the exact same note at the exact same pitch. Not a frog. Not an insect. The reed crews swear if you play back a recording you'll hear a third note underneath, slower, as if something large in the water table beneath the basin were breathing through the reeds. They cut reeds on tethers, quickly, in daylight.

The figs and acacias and sedge mats are residents too, but resentfully tolerated. A fig will find a crack in a warehouse foundation and slowly pry it apart with a kind of insulted patience, its leaves always dusty white with precipitated salt. The acacias are solitary because they poison the ground around them to keep the sedge off. They leak. On hot afternoons they drip not sap but condensed water they have pulled from the steam, and it falls from their thorns whether there is rain or not.

### The Water-Inhabitants

Because weirdness 1.6 is not folklore. It's maintenance.

The water here doesn't quite agree to gravity when it is thin enough. In the Kiln Quarter, where basalt columns were cut and left as decoration a century ago, the tops of the columns are always wet, even capped with tin, and the undersides are bone dry. In bathhouses and low kitchens, if you leave a cold glass out for an hour, condensation will crawl up the outside, over the lip, and down inside until the glass is empty.

Something lives in that habit.

It's not an animal. The soda workers call it *the crawl* or *the sweat.* You see it best on the underside of pipe racks at 3 a.m. on a warm night: a bright thread of water moving slowly, steadily *upwind* along the steel, against drip. If you watch long enough, it beads, pauses, beads again, as if deciding. Where it pools, it leaves a faint white track of mineral and, if you put your hand there, the steel is cold.

In the culverts and under the factory floors where the water runs loudest, there are things that look like limescale until they move. Flat, cream-colored lobes of mineralized bacteria that grow half an inch a year into frills and shelves. They filter the hot water. If you break one open fresh, it bleeds clear brine and smells like struck matches. The old pump-men don't scrape them unless they have to. "They're holding the pipe together now," one will tell you, patting a culvert wall that is more animal than concrete. A few of those growths in the oldest canals have been growing so long they've learned to close a valve at night by swelling.

And down where the veil-falls hit the Plashes and make no ripples - that glassy, struck-constant surface - there is something that lives by hanging. Maintenance crews wear tethers not only so they don't fall in. They swear - quietly, after shift, over tea so strong it etches the cup - that on hot, loud afternoons the falling threads hesitate two feet above the marsh. They thicken. If you throw a reed into that hesitation, it doesn't fall through. It stops. It hangs. Then very slowly, very gently, it is laid sideways onto air.

No one wades there. No one baptizes. No one drinks from the veil.

### What It Is To Live There

A person who lives in the High Wet Town learns three extra senses:

How to read the darkening of gravel that says water is coming up from below and you should move your welding rig.

How to feel in your molars whether a flywheel two streets over is running true or about to throw its belt.

How to not stare too long at water that is deciding whether to fall.

They are slow, not because they are dull. Dynamic 0.9. They are slow because anything fast breaks. They build everything to outlast them - brick laid three deep, steel riveted instead of welded so it can flex when the basin breathes with its daily heating and steaming. They have the same sirens at 6:00, 14:00, 22:00 that their grandparents had. A young woman will run a loom that has her great-grandmother's initials oiled into its side frame.

They don't talk about leaving much. The western pass road is open. Trucks come and go with cement and soda ash. But if you ask someone who was born in the steam-shadow of the kilns why they stay, they will look at you a little puzzled and shout back, "Where else is it warm when the rock sweats?"

At night the town glows amber under its low cloud of steam, and the machines never quite stop, and the reeds click their one glassy note across miles of water that hangs in the air. And the people sleep - lightly at first, then deeply, deafly - with their boots set neatly beside the bed, already dark with tomorrow's damp, waiting to be filled again.
