What lives here lives close to the ground, and close to the edge, and lives fast.

Not much of it, if you count by kinds. Not much of it if you count by weight against the mountain. But what is here is completely here — fitted to this one room the way water is fitted to this one bowl. Nothing is accidental. Nothing is passing through except you.

Start small, because small is where most of the living actually is.

The mat itself is alive. It's not soil with plants on top of it, it *is* plants, centuries of them half-unmade. Sphagnum and feather moss building themselves upward while their own dead compress underneath into black peat that holds ten times its weight in water. You pull a handful apart and it smells like cold iron and tea. Inside it live things you will never see clearly: springtails flicking like fleas of glass, mites red as paprika, white blind worms no thicker than thread, all chewing the dead into peat. Without them the bowl would fill with its own past and drown.

On top of that sponge is the forest that never gets to be a forest.

Alpine sedge — *Carex*, *Kobresia* — not grass, exactly, but close enough that you call it grass until you kneel. It grows in tight tussocks that shiver even when there is no wind in here, because bumblebees, fat and loud in the thin air, barge through it. Dwarf willow, *Salix herbacea*, a tree that decided to be a carpet. Its trunk runs sideways under the moss, its leaves are the size of your fingernail, glossy, toothed. In late summer they turn red all at once and the whole floor blushes. Between them, cushions: moss campion making domes so hard you could stand on them, saxifrage with its fleshy rosettes clinging to every seep line, star gentians white and blue that only open when the sun hits them direct. You have to get on your hands and knees to see that this is not just green. It's a meadow in miniature, with canopy and understory and glade, all three centimeters tall. Every leaf is waxy, or hairy, or rolled in on itself to keep what little heat there is.

This is what eats the sun and the weeping water. Everything else eats this.

The air over it belongs to the midges first. They don't live here in the way you live somewhere — they *are* the air here for a few weeks. Columns of them rise in the sun-shafts that get past the rim, thousands in a dancing cylinder that holds its shape as if hemmed. They hatch from the peaty pools that never quite dry, they live a day or two, they breed and die into the mat again. Their hum is the bowl's baseline, stopping and starting when a cloud moves.

And the things that live on midges:

Swallows. Which should not be here, at this elevation, and yet are — alpine swifts or crag martins, dark-backed, pale-bellied, sickle-winged. They don't soar so much as cut. They live in the wall itself, in every dry crack and bubble-hole of the old granite, where they have glued cups of mud and lichen and their own saliva. Two thousand nests, maybe more, pocking the upper amphitheater like a honeycomb you have to squint to see. At dawn they pour out all at once and the bowl fills with their screaming — not song, a high, dry *sri-sri-sri* that bounces off stone until you can't tell direction. They work the midge columns until the columns vanish. Then they hang in the wind that goes *over*, riding the shear above the rim without ever really leaving.

Lower, the ground-keepers:

Pikas. You hear them before you see one — *eep!* sharp as a pinprick, then again, *eep! eep!* from a different rock. They are mouse-sized but not mice, round-eared, the color of lichen. They don't hibernate. So they live by hay. All day in the short summer they run the same paths between sedge patch and sun-slab, jaws stuffed with stems twice as long as they are, building little haystacks under overhangs to dry. You will find a dozen of these curing piles, neat as a farmer's, tucked where rain can't hit. One pika, one pile, defended with absurd courage. If you sit still long enough you see the owner dart out, turn its hay, scold you, vanish.

Marmots are their opposites — they *do* hibernate, for eight months, so now they are frantic with fattening. Family groups, five to ten, whistlers. The adults are big as housecats, grey-brown, blunt-muzzled, with black caps. They have dug under the boulders where the peat meets scree, burrows that go down into the warm dark the mountain keeps. They have latrines, lookouts, nurseries. One always stands while the others feed, forepaws tucked, and when the lammergeier's shadow comes it whistles — a single piercing note that slaps around the bowl twice. Then every marmot is stone, then gone.

Between their feet, voles run tunnels in the thatch. You won't see them, but the fox knows where they are. And the beetles — carabids, black with violet edges, flightless — hunt at the edges of every pool, flipping over drowned midges.

The predators are few because the bowl is few. But they are *present* in a way lowland predators aren't. No territory to spare.

The fox: a high red fox, not the russet of woods but washed-out, sandy, its winter white still grizzled on its flanks in August. Thin, leggy — altitude makes everything leggy or squat, nothing in between. It knows you are not prey and not danger; it looks at you once with amber eyes and goes back to mousing, pouncing in that stiff, joyful way foxes have, breaking through moss to pin a vole. It dens somewhere in the scree outside the slot; it comes in to hunt like you go to a pantry.

Weasels, smaller and meaner, slip like water through vole runs. You hear a chitter and a vole shriek cut short.

And above, waiting more than hunting: the lammergeier. Bearded vulture. Two of them, maybe a mated pair, maybe mother and big young. Wingspan three meters, wedge tails, rust stained on their bellies from bathing in iron pools. They don't need to kill in here. They circle so slowly it looks like they're hung from the sky. They are waiting for a marmot to die, a fox to leave bones. They eat bone. They will take a femur up high and drop it on rock to crack it. They have learned the bowl's one trick — everything that dies here stays here a long time before the snow takes it, and there is no tree to hide it.

Even higher, sometimes, a golden eagle passes over without dropping in, because there is no need to enter a room that is already full.

And then there is what *used* to live here part-time, and still does in residue.

People. Not dwellers. Users. Transhumant shepherds, probably — Bakhtiari or Andean or Alpine, it doesn't matter, the type repeats. Young men or whole small families who learned the cleft from an uncle and brought six, ten goats up when the lower pastures burned. The low dry-stone fold with one wall fallen proves it: shoulder-high, windbreak, not house. The hut under the house-boulder is more intimate. One course of slabs for a roof, smoke black still in the cracks after years of no fire, a single wooden peg hand-whittled and driven into stone to hold a waterskin or a cheese bag. A few strands of undyed wool caught on splintered juniper. A hearth of three stones. They ate here, slept badly in cool air, listened to marmots and worried about foxes and lammergeiers the way you worry about neighbors. They left when the sedge thinned, before the first snow that would close the slot. Cairns — three stones, never more — are their politeness to the next person. No graves. You don't winter here to die here.

Which leaves the last resident, if you can call it that.

At the bottom, where all the braided under-water meets, the circle. Fourteen stones, lichen-washed, set level with intention but without mortar or art. Inside, the peat gives way to a lens of water, clear down to black silt, never more than a hand deep, always full, always cold. It doesn't ripple like the peat pools. It holds sky without distortion.

Things live *around* it, not in it. The marmots detour. The voles don't tunnel near it. The fox drinks downstream, not from it. The swallows and pikas drink from it freely, even prefer it — you will see a line of tiny three-toed prints in the silt. But nothing grows in it. No algae film, no midge larvae, though every other pool is wriggling. Put your hand in and your bones feel the mountain's under-river going by, that low hum through stone and tooth. It's not warm, not cold the way water is cold. It's cold the way deep rock is cold.

You could say nothing lives in the circle. Or you could say the bowl itself lives there — the place where all its weeping is gathered and counted before it's sent down through darkness to come out somewhere else as a spring people will drink from and never know came from a room high up that is not on the way to anywhere.

Sit long enough on the springy ground and you understand the rule everything here abides by: keep it. Keep water in moss, heat in fur and feather and stone, food as fat and hay and bone. The whole improbable lushness is storage against the long white. It all lives here because it has learned, precisely, how to wait.

And when winter does come, it all goes under — marmot curled on marmot, pika asleep against its hay inside the rock, swallows gone south, peat frozen solid, circle iced to a milky eye — and the bowl holds its breath until the sun gets weight again.
