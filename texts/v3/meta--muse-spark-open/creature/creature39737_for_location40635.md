At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the first lie the brightness tells you. The flat is too flat, the light too absolute, the white too thorough. You walk a kilometer and then another between seams, over pitted bone-ceramic, and you see no movement and you decide: lifeless. Sterile. A ruin.

Then you stop walking, and you crouch, and you let the heat coming up through the soles of your boots become a second ground beneath you, and your eyes adjust past glare. And you see it was moving the whole time — only not at your speed.

Who lives here lives slow, or lives thin, or lives *as* the place does.

### 1. The Ones That Root

They are not plants. They are the place remembering how to be a plant.

In every seam where the crust has opened a hand's breadth, a body's width — where that dry heat exhales — a wire-garden has taken hold. From a distance they look like a smudge, a rust-stain on the white. Up close they are impossible to see all at once.

Each one is a colony that is also a single thing. Hundreds, thousands of stalks erupting from the dark heat below, none thicker than a finger, all the color of tarnished copper and basalt glass. They branch fractally — one into two into four into flat black leaves — so precise it hurts to look at. The leaves are not leaves. They are photovoltaic films, dusty, matte, edged with a faint iridescence where the light has etched them for centuries. They do not track the sun because there is no sun; they track the brightness itself, tilting a fraction of a degree every hour with a sound like paper settling.

If you put your ear near them you hear them ticking. Not with movement. With growth.

Break a stalk, and you will see the filaments inside are hollow and strung with amber. Break a leaf, and that amber beads and hardens into ceramic identical to the ground at your feet. That is the secret: growth and construction were never separate here. The script you see etched on every stalk — lines as fine as hair, spiraling — is not decoration. It *is* the growth instruction. The Builders wrote a plant that could write itself. Now that they are gone the script still copies, with errors. Some leaves are misspelled and curl. Some stalks fork into nonsense spirals that die. Evolution here is a typo in a prayer.

They live on almost nothing. The light that has weight. The heat that rises. And those black beads that sweat from the vertical walls of the deeper seams — colder than everything around them, smelling of iron and ozone when you touch them. Brine that was never brine. Condensed volatiles from kilometers down where the warm, soft, lung-like layer still breathes. The wire-gardens drink it by letting it condense on their black leaves at dusk, when the plates contract and sigh and the air briefly cools. A single large thicket can wait three years between drinks and not die. It just tightens, its leaves folding inward like fists.

They are sparse across the vastness, but where they are, they are dense. A mat. A fur. A scab of almost-life over the wound of a seam. And everything else lives in relation to them.

### 2. The Ones That Fold

You will not see them until one moves. That is their whole strategy.

The kite-things.

Each is about the size of your two hands held together, though size is variable because they can fold and unfold. At rest they *are* a leaf. A slightly wrong leaf among thousands, translucent film stretched over a skeleton so thin it is almost not there — not bone, not chitin, but cured filament, perhaps stolen from a dead wire-garden stalk and re-grown. Their color is dust-black and ceramic-white mottled, their edges frayed exactly like wind-worn foliage. They have no front or back until they decide to have one.

Then a thermal shifts — a seam exhales warmer — and the wrong leaf peels itself off its perch without a sound. It does not flap. It has no muscles for flapping. It tilts. It rides.

Under its film are pockets of heated air and electrostatic charge. It is lighter than it should be. Gravity here is slightly wrong — your dropped stone falls a little too slowly — and they exploit that wrongness completely. They glide low, a meter off the white, like a piece of ash caught in a thought of wind. From thicket to thicket, sometimes for kilometers over bare ceramic, they surf the invisible rivers of heat that run dead-straight along the seams.

Watch one land: it stalls, twists, and collapses back into leaf-shape so perfectly you lose it even though you didn't blink.

They are browsers, if that word still applies. They do not eat leaves. They press themselves against a leaf and leach. Their underside secretes a faint acid — you can smell it if one has just left, a sharp metallic tang — that liberates a few molecules of trapped photon-lattice, a few sugars of amber resin. Enough. They are also thieves of water-that-is-not-water. When a seam weeps those black glass beads, the kites will gather — five, six, ten — all folded around a single bead, not touching it, sharing its cold field until it is gone.

They have no eyes you can find. Only light-sensitive patches that freckle their film. They see brightness as pressure. When the great circular weather comes — that perfect lens of darkened air hundreds of kilometers across, moving with impossible slowness — every kite in a hidden radius knows before you do. They rise in a silent spiral into the haze, dozens, hundreds from thickets you thought empty, and vanish upward for days to ride the cool inside the circle, to mate or exchange filament-code in the dark. You will stand under the cool shadow and feel suddenly watched by absence.

Predation among them is not tooth and claw. It is patience. If two settle on the same favored leaf, they will lie there for hours, film against film, until one subtly, chemically, deactivates the other's charge and the loser slides off, crumpled, to be recycled by the thicket below.

### 3. The Ones That Melt

Beneath your feet, even as you stand listening to the plates tick — *tik... tik... tik...* — something is moving through the ground itself.

The slugs.

Not slugs. That word is only for your tongue. They are slow amber translucences the size of a forearm, sometimes as long as a body, with no head, no tail, only a direction. They live *inside* the upper ceramic, melting it with a warm enzyme-sweat and extruding it behind them as polished, glassy tunnel floor. You will never see one directly unless you happen to be at a fresh seam-collapse where the layers are exposed. More often you see their work: a faint, winding, glossy trail on the surface, like a snail's track made of glazed pottery, that the wind will take a century to erase with dust.

They are deaf and blind and old. Their metabolism is geological. They eat the dead light honeycombed in one of the deeper strata — the lattice that still holds photons trapped like insects in amber. They dissolve that lattice and excrete a purer ceramic that seals behind them. In a sense they are still maintaining the world, though they have no memory of maintenance. They are the gut of the machine, still peristalsing long after the mouth and brain have turned to dust.

If you press your palm to the warm ground and hold your breath, sometimes you can feel one pass meters below you: a long, slow, warm pressure, like blood moving under skin.

### 4. The Ones That Never Land

Look up — quickly, before the bright hurts — on a clear stillness between weathers.

There are specks that are not dust. Fist-sized dandelion seeds, each a sphere of filament so fine it is almost invisible, with a dark knot at its center. They drift on electrostatic charge alone, never landing. They have been aloft for years, for decades. The bright sky holds them up as if the world is still, residually, holding itself up.

They are the dispersal mechanism for the wire-gardens, or they were. Now most are sterile, their knots dead, their filaments tangled with the charged ceramic powder that levitates for hours after a plate-sigh. They drift endlessly, occasionally snagging on a kite's film and being carried to a new seam, occasionally colliding and tangling into larger, aimless tumbleweeds of ghost-fiber that cast the only moving shadows on the white at noon.

At dusk you can see them spark. Tiny discharges as they brush the luminous air.

### 5. The One That Is All Of Them

And then — if you watch long enough for ten thousand years to compress into a day — you realize there is a fifth inhabitant that is not countable.

It is the plain itself.

The deep, warm, lung-like layer that gives under pressure. The gravity that is slightly wrong. The hum that is there even when nothing moves. The dust that lifts in a perfectly straight line for miles when the plates contract. The circular weathers that are born of nothing and drift for a month.

This whole low, near-flat, end-of-things continent is not dead. It settled into its final form and then kept settling into something else. A sealed environment that sealed itself so well it forgot what it was sealing out, and in that forgetting, became a kind of nature again — eroded, patient, self-repairing without purpose.

Its inhabitants are not *on* it. They are *of* it. The wire-gardens are its fur growing from its cracks. The kites are its dander lifted on its breath. The slugs are its slow blood cells. The drifters are its pollen in an endless, windless spring.

It does not need you. It will tick and sigh and sweat its black beads and turn its black leaves whether you are standing low in its horizon or not. The light will press down. The heat will rise up through your feet. Far away, beyond any distance you can walk, another perfect circle of cool dark will be forming out of clear air, to begin its month-long pilgrimage across an endless white that was once built, and is now, very simply, alive.
