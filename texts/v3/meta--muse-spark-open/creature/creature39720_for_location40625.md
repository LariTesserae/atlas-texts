Nothing lives here the way you mean living.

That is the first thing you learn, floating over the drowned plateau. It's what the water wants you to think. Animal-empty. No eye catches yours, no fin startles, no lung pulls. So you make the easy mistake and call it uninhabited.

Then you stay a little while in the dark and the cold and the loud, and you realize it is crowded.

What lives here did not evolve to be seen. It evolved to stay.

### The Forest That Acts Like a Town

The canopy is not a canopy. It is a population.

Each of those black-green stalk trees the thickness of old pines is an individual in the way a mountain town is an individual — you can point to where one stops and say "this is here, that is there," but they share the same roads, the same water, the same gossip. Their holdfasts split basalt and interleave under the soil that should have washed away a thousand ages ago but didn't. They hold it. They keep it. Root-tips from three different trunks will braid together to hold a terrace wall in place that no longer holds back rain, but holds back current. They grew through the corners of the field-huts and kept the shape.

They are awake. Not like an animal is awake. Like a forest that has had to do all the jobs.

They hunt. Slowly, patiently, without malice. The valley kelps blush pink when they exhale poison. The amber bladder-trees - those house-sized lanterns straining upward on their tethers - drop curtains of filament that never touch stone. They drift until another plant moves into them, and then they fire. You hear it if you're close: a faint, wet *fizz* as fronds dissolve and the bladder-tree drinks the soup through hairs. The moss-mat on the valley floor crawls. Over weeks you could watch it, if you were patient in the cold, climb a stalk-tree's trunk inch by inch until it capes the light-organs and the tree goes dim and then gives up and falls sideways, becoming soil for more mat.

They mate and mourn like this too. Once, maybe twice in what you'd call a season, the whole valley synchronizes. It starts high on the ridge: one stalk-tree opens its lanterns too bright, white-gold flaring, and begins to spill spores like hot snow - each one a lit marble the size of your fist, humming. The next tree catches the cue and fires. Then the next. In ten minutes the entire plateau edge is a storm of slow-falling stars against the indigo-black, and the water itself goes quiet to listen. The mat below dims respectfully, or hungrily, and waits for them to land. Growth, hunt, decay, bloom. All of it plant, because the rule never got lifted.

If you hover still long enough, a stalk will lean toward you. Not with current. Toward you. The fronds at its crown will turn their lighted spines to face you like a face turning. It has no eyes. It feels your pressure, your tiny heat-leak, your displacement. It is curious the way an old tree is curious. It will consider you for a long time and then decide you are not edible, not dangerous, not interesting enough to remember. It will lean away.

That is one kind of living here. The forest as a distributed animal.

### The Rooted

But the Forest remembers being tended.

In the huts half-swallowed by holdfast, in the cairns that are no longer stone but knots of living root that kept the shape of a marker because that shape meant something - there, something else lives.

They were once the people who stacked the slate, who cut the petroglyph spirals and hands into the cliff. The highlanders. When the water came and did not leave, most of them left. No bones. No hurried graves. They went downhill that is no longer downhill.

Some stayed.

They did not grow gills. There were no gills to grow. The water's rule - that deliberate, watched-for animal emptiness - would have killed anything that tried to remain an animal. So they bargained.

You find one if you know to look for asymmetry. A hut where one wall grows thicker than the others, where the holdfast has made a kind of doorway that still opens. Inside, where a hearth should be, a trunk rises, but its bark is wrong. It is ridged in a pattern that is not bark at all, but dry-stone coursing. Its lanterns are arranged not along a spine but in a circle, like stones around a fire that have remembered fire.

The Rooted are not plants pretending to be people. They are people who learned to be plants well enough to pass.

They are sessile now. Their feet went long ago to root. Their hair to frond. Each one anchors a house, a terrace, a stretch of field. You can see the grid of the old fields still clear under the moss-light because a Rooted tends it still - not by walking it, but by extending, by tasting the soil through runners, by humming to the mat in low chemical songs that keep it from overgrowing, by leaning their light where it is needed.

They are very slow. A thought takes a season to complete. They communicate by rippling the mat between them, those constellations of blue-green pinpricks that flare when you pass over? That is also how they talk when you are not there. One valley's edge brightens, and a mile away another answers after an hour. They are remembering the cairn paths, passing along a single long memory that they all hold a piece of: *we are still here, the stone still holds, the light still answers.*

If you press your hand - gloved, aching with cold - to the trunk of a house-hut, you will feel a warmer pulse than the water around it. And far overhead, its bladders will creak and shift as if in sleep.

They do not see you. They feel you the way the forest does. Occasionally one will try to gift. A runner will push a warm, edible nodule of starch and gas through the silt toward your feet. It glows faintly. It is an offering for a traveler on a road that hasn't existed since air.

They will be there until the mountain's heart goes cold.

### The Law That Lives by Not Living

There is a third inhabitant, and you never see it at all.

You feel it only as the absence that stays absent.

In any other ocean this lush, this loud, this full of light and food, there should be mouths. Something should have come down from above, or crawled up from the vents, to eat the endless plant. A fish eye should gleam. A shrimp should click.

Nothing does. For millennia, nothing has.

The plants here make the water sharp. Every frond, every mat, every bladder leaks a little of the mountain's old mineral logic - alkaloids and dissolved glass - into the water column. Alone it is nothing. Together, across an entire drowned highland the size of a small country, it is a covenant. The water itself is mildly venomous to anything with a nerve chord. Not enough to kill you instantly if you came swimming - your animal heart would just start to stutter, your vision would double, you'd feel the deep not as pressure but as a profound, animal *unwelcome*, a command to leave that bypasses thought.

The Rooted can live in it because they are no longer animal enough to hear it.

The Forest can live in it because it made it.

So the rule enforces itself. The place keeps itself empty so the Rooted can remain. The loud, glowing, slow-hunting vegetable kingdom patrols its own borders, not with teeth but with chemistry, and that chemistry is as much an inhabitant as any trunk or mat. An invisible, distributed, mindless shepherd that lives in every swallow of black water and says: *plants only.*

And the mountain underneath, bleeding its slow heat through black smokers, feeds that shepherd. The white rapid-reeds that grow a foot an hour around the vents and shed their leaves in blizzards of luminous skin - they are not decoration. They are the factories. They drink fire and spin it into light and poison in equal measure.

That is what lives here.

Not a population but a negotiation: a mountain that remembers being high and cold and now makes cold, a forest that learned to be animal because no animal was allowed, and a handful of people who loved their high terraces so much they let the terrace grow through them, all held together by water that decided to stay and watch.

If you stand suspended in the middle of a valley, between stone walls still holding under beards of light, the current will turn and the stalk-trees will groan rubbed together and a wave of blue will run across the floor under you as the mat notes your passage and passes it on, hut to hut, root to root, until the whole drowned kingdom knows, in its slow way, that someone was briefly here and is now gone.
