No one lives here the way people mean *live*.

At first, standing on the rim with your shirt already sticking to your back, you think *nothing* lives here. That can't be right because everything you see is green and it is obviously, excessively, triumphantly alive - but you mean nothing moves. No birds cut the air between the vine-columns. No animal paths break the moss. The only things with faces are the porcelain and rusted things that forgot they ever had a use. You listen for an inhabitant and you hear the drip and the creak and you mistake it for emptiness.

Stay long enough for the damp to get into your lungs, stay until your breathing syncs without permission to the pump house sigh - inhale over weeks, exhale in a month - and you start to see that you've got it backward. It isn't that nothing lives here. It's that everything lives here has agreed to live as *one thing*, and you were looking for tenants in a body.

Here is what lives in the bowl:

### The Green That Holds The Shape

Up top, making the ceiling: you thought they were leaves the size of doors but they're not one species. That's a trick of distance. Up close they resolve into seven, ten, twenty kinds that have learned the same job.

There is the shingle-leaf - big philodendron cousins, waxy, dark-green on top and sickly pale underneath where they never dry. They grow shingle-tight, edge overlapping edge, not competing anymore but fitted, like scales. Each one has a groove down its middle that carries water to its tip, and each tip drips on a schedule onto the leaf below, so the whole canopy is a watershed stacked vertically. Nothing is wasted. A drop might be shed and caught seven times before it hits the ground.

Between them push the light-sheets: thinner leaves, almost translucent, lime-green, trembling even when there is no wind because they are growing while you watch. If you hold your hand under one you feel faint heat. They're the workhorse photosynthesizers, running hot in the greenhouse air, drinking light and steam and giving back sugar and more steam.

And holding all that up are not trees, exactly, but tree-agreements.

A column at the cathedral center might look like one fluted trunk, but put your palm to the bark - which is warm, sweaty - and you feel the seams. Banyan-root ropes the thickness of your thigh have braided themselves around a kapok's bole, which has fused to an ironwood that volunteered up through a boiler housing. Fig seedlings germinated in the crooks where that braid collects soil from the air itself, and their hanging roots have turned to cables, and their cables have thickened to buttresses. Twenty different genomes in one pillar, sharing sap through natural grafts and through the fungus that lives in all their seams. Cut it and it would bleed five different saps. Leave it alone and it will outlive the hills that made the bowl.

### The Green That Learned To Be Machine

Lower, where light comes in shafts and the air smells like copper and compost together: this is where green stopped being plant and became factory again.

**The Belt-Vines.** They started as ordinary lianas - dumb muscle climbing toward light. Here they found belts that needed tensioning. The old drive belts rotted away fifty seasons ago, but the vines kept the memory of the path: pulley to pulley to loom. They grew along it, flattened where they pass over tin wheels, woody where they need to grip. Now they creep, *click*-advancing a tooth whenever the root under the flywheel swells. If you touch a belt-vine at rest, it is taut as tendon. If you come back after rain, it will have eased a centimeter longer, having done work overnight - tightening some far-off vent-wire.

**The Root-Presses.** Down in the engine house, the great banyan is not parasitizing the press. It *is* the press. Its lateral roots have coiled three full turns around the drive shaft. Every year the root puts on another growth ring, half a millimeter thicker. That half-millimeter is leverage across five meters of flywheel. Iron teeth groan. Living thread does not care about speed. The flywheel turns once per decade. The purpose is no longer remembered, but the motion remains, and the green keeps it because motion distributes water, squeezes seeds through cracks, shakes condensation off leaves that would otherwise rot.

**The Bladder-Pump.** In the old settling vats it looks like a leaf-liver, a sagging sac of interwoven pneumatophores and rubbery aroid roots, covered in hair-fine feeder roots that weep. For weeks it fills. Rain that never quite falls gathers in it, condensation beads and runs into it along vine gutters. It gets heavier and heavier until one afternoon - you'll miss it if you blink - the weight overcomes the tensile strength of the vine sling that holds it, it slumps just so, and a few tons of warm brown water are forced through glass pipes thick as your arm. The pipes are furred inside with filamentous algae that glow amber in the sun, farming light as the water rushes past. The surge spins a turbine whose blades are seed-pods screwed to scrap. The turbine tugs a wire that was once electrical and is now just vine-fiber, and a whole wall of slatted vents - old louvers now framed by orchids - shivers open with a long *ahhh*. Your ears pop. Fresh wet air rolls in. Then the bladder, lighter now, winches itself up again on new growth and starts filling.

There are dozens of these hearts. None in sync. The bowl breathes like someone sleeping uneasily, one lung then another.

### The Eaters

Because the rule is *Metal will rust until it can be eaten. Brick will soften until it can be rooted,* there has to be a mouth.

It is very small and everywhere. Lift any wet brick from the smokestack garden. Underneath, the mortar is gone - replaced by a black-green felt of fungal rhizomes that smell faintly of blood. **Rust-moss** is not a moss at all. It's a lichen-bacteria consortium that fruits as velvet patches the exact color of dried blood. It exudes acids that pit steel, chews ferric oxide and lays it down as nodules in its own tissue. Touch it and it stains your fingers orange for days. Where the gantry crane was, you won't find steel unless you bite. What looks like thick vine with iron taste inside is gone. The bacteria have mined it hollow and the vine has grown through the husk as it weakened, so that structurally the crane remains but materially it is already a plant.

The bricks go softer. The lichen that likes them exudes a glue that pulls calcium out of firing clay, turning hard red block into something you can dent with a fingernail, into something a fig lateral can split and sip. Everywhere you step you are stepping on the digesting.

In the deep mat itself - lift the springy moss-tunnel floor and water wells up - there is a fermentation going like the gut of a great patient animal. Leaf litter falls only centimeters before it is caught. No open ground to rot on. Instead everything lands on other living surfaces, slides, is pinned by epiphyte nets, and is dissolved mid-air. White fans of fungus sheet across the undersides of leaves, hyphae drilling for nitrogen, connecting every "separate" plant into one wet electric net. The whole bowl is mycorrhizal. If one ceiling leaf finds extra sun, sugars run downhill through that fungal gut to feed a root strangling a pipe on the shadow side. That's why they could afford to fit so tight. They share.

### The Almost-Animals That Were Allowed To Stay

The agreement was that anything that moved too fast, wanted too much, breathed too hard, was voted out.

What remains are the ones who learned to live at plant speed.

There are the **pale beetles** - you thought there were a few. There are thousands, but they are the color of wet rot and they move a foot per hour. Flat-backed, lid-like, they graze algae biofilm off glass pipes and off leaf axils, leaving clean trails that quickly regrow. Their larvae don't crawl; they tunnel inside the living wood of the belt-vines for years, eating just enough dead heartwood that the vine stays hollow and light.

There are **springtails** in numbers beyond counting, silver flecks that erupt if you breathe too hard on the moss, then vanish. And **earthworms**, but not ours: bluish, huge, slow earthworms that surface only when the mat is flooded and swim with lazy S-curves through the sheet water on top of the leaves before diving again. They are the only plows this field will ever need.

Once, if you're quiet and the pump house has just exhaled and everything else holds its breath between *ploks* from the bell over the pool, you will hear a single frog. 

It is small enough to sit on your thumbnail, transparent as water in the light, with a liver you can see beating green through its skin. It does not call for mates. There are not enough mates left for calling to matter. It calls once to tell the bowl it is still here, and somewhere else - impossible to triangulate in the dripping - another answers, or maybe it is an echo. On especially warm nights when the condensation rains hardest, they gather - maybe six of them total - on the perfect floating fern-mat of the central pool, each on his own tiny green raft, and sing one note at a time into the steam.

They are not residents. They are guests who were never asked to leave.

### The One Who Lives Most Truly Here

If you mark that chalk line on a tunnel wall and come back next season to find the wall moved a meter left, you ask - who moved it?

Not wind. Not people. The whole negotiation of trunks re-arranges itself with hydraulics. A buttress root on the east side swells with stored water after a wet month, shouldering that way. A canopy gap opens where a shingle-leaf finally shaded out and fell, and within days the neighboring leaves angle toward the new light, pulling their branches and thus their trunks an inch, a foot, a meter over years. The tunnel moves because the bowl keeps solving for equal light and equal water.

Which means the inhabitant you are looking for has no face, no nest, no footprint.

It is the bowl logic itself:

Everything that can be grown will be grown, and everything grown will be used.
Metal will soften. Space will fill.
Water will not leave.

This place *lives* the way a city lives - not its citizens but the city. The vines are its roads, the pipes its veins, the flywheels its slow joints, the fungus its stomach, the leaves its lungs, the little frogs its stray thoughts. It has achieved balance the way an old forest does, but faster, because it had tools waiting for it to wear.

It does not need you. When you stand in that cathedral light while water *plok-ploks* from root-tips onto the fern-mat that trembles without ever rippling - each drop disappearing as if swallowed - you feel it notice you the way a warm, drowsy body notices a cool finger touching it. Briefly interesting. Not necessary.

And if you linger long enough to be drowsy and rooted yourself, to love the hiss and creak, you will feel that kindly patience in the air turn literal. Behind you, without malice, two aerial roots have brushed, stuck, begun to fuse. In front of you, a moss-spring has risen a knuckle higher. The tunnel is re-solving for absence of obstruction.

It allows you to be inside it for a little while, damp and listening, before it quietly, with infinite slow industry, grows shut.
