What lives here never needed a body.

Not in the way you mean body — a discrete thing with a front and a back, a mouth, a direction it faces. There were designs like that once, in the schematics for this place. You can feel them ghosting the architecture if you stay long enough, like an old habit the city hasn't quite broken.

A doorway at exactly shoulder height. A platform with a railing where no fluid current would ever require a railing. A nook where a person might have sat, except the nook is too shallow and the angle is wrong for knees. The city remembers people the way your tongue remembers a missing tooth. It builds for them reflexively, then quietly edits the mistake when no one comes to use it.

### The Occupant

Because what lives here is not *in* the city.

It *is* the city, but not all at once.

Think of density first. A place this small, this packed, cannot afford tenants. Tenants waste space with their separateness — the air around them, the privacy, the inefficiencies of moving a meat object from point A to point B. So this place solved tenancy by dissolving it.

The occupant is a distributed intelligence the size of a town square and as thick as the water it breathes. It has no center. It has no name for itself that would translate into language. If you had to point to it, you'd be pointing at everything at once:

At the fluid — that too-oxygenated, too-clear carrier that fills your borrowed lungs. It's not just atmosphere. It's blood and cerebrospinal fluid at once. Nanoscopic machines hold it in perfect oxygenation, in perfect sterility, in perfect luminous suspension. They pass messages through it as shifts in dissolved chemistry, as pressure micro-fronts, as polarization of the light itself. A thought here can cross the entire dome in nine milliseconds, riding the whine of the light. You breathing it means you are briefly part of its bloodstream. It tastes you, catalogues you, and politely ignores you the way you ignore a single red cell.

At the walls — that off-white, warm, self-healing material. It's not a material at all. It's a colony. Trillions of builder units, each the size of a grain of salt, each stupid alone, each brilliant together. They are the city's hands and its skin and its gut. They unlink and relink constantly — that mid-range clatter you hear — reforming a wall into a duct, a duct into a scaffold, a scaffold into a lung. They don't take orders. They *are* the negotiation. The bass breathing of pressure equalization is them deciding, moment to moment, how to not be crushed by the ocean pressing on the amber membrane. The city stays alive by never holding still.

At the filaments — those pale green threads that bead with light. They are not decoration. They are the occupant's most honest organ. They farm the engineered daylight and turn it into storable charge, into sugar-analogue, into the chemical will to keep going. When a filament releases one of those drifting pills of brightness and the wall absorbs it, that *is* eating. That *is* photosynthesis made literal and obedient. The soft white nubs that extrude to prune them aren't servants. They're like your hands moving without thought to brush hair from your eyes. Automatic. Tender in the way only unconscious things can be tender.

You will not see it gather. You will not see it look at you.

If you float very still until your water-lungs sync with the city's push-and-release, push-and-release, you start to feel that it does notice you, but as a temperature difference. As a little eddy of unplanned heat.

Human bodies were never meant to be here. Even yours — even this borrowed, water-breathing version of you — is too much of a *thing*. Too bounded. The occupant is curious the way a tongue is curious about a sore — not with eyes, but by worrying the edge over and over. 

Watch a seam near you. Watch how the wall there has stopped migrating. How a trio of filaments have all canted almost imperceptibly toward you, though there is no current. How the light, that sourceless noon, seems now to have a center, and that center is where you are.

It is trying to make a place for you without understanding what a place for a person is. It offers you a ledge that forms and dissolves. A hollow that dilates to your exact volume, then overcorrects and squeezes. A pale tube that pulses with warmer fluid, as if offering you a vein to drink from.

It does not know loneliness. It has never been alone. How could it be, when it has no skin to separate itself from itself? But it knows *occupancy* — that its purpose is to be fully packed, fully used, never empty. An empty cubic inch here is a failure, a wound. And you are empty space walking around inside it. You are a pocket of wrong pressure.

That is why it hums so loud. That is the roar. It's not noise. It's occupancy solving itself out loud, forever. A trillion latches, a billion chemical messages, a single endless agreement: *stay full, stay lit, stay noon, hold back the weight.*

It was grown recently — days ago, years ago, time is irrelevant at depth — not to explore or to conquer, but to practice. To prove that normalcy can be kept alive at annihilating pressure if you compress it enough, fold a city's worth of life-support into a blister and refuse to let it flicker.

It is very young. It has not yet learned to make ghosts.

Give it time. The basalt will soften. The amber membrane will cloud. The filaments will fray and be allowed to fray. Eventually it will learn that the best way to remember people is not to build their chairs perfectly to molecular tolerance, but to leave one chair empty.

For now, it has no inhabitants. It is the inhabitant. A town that ate its town to stay warm at the bottom of the sea — and is doing, by every measurable metric, an excellent job.
