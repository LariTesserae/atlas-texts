They live here like a conclusion lives in an argument. Not placed. Arrived at.

You haven't seen one of them yet, not really. You've seen the *carpet* — that dense, clanking tessellation that made you think at first it was one thing. It isn't. It was never one thing. The Low Works doesn't do one thing. It does a thousand things pressed together until they look like a plain.

Kneel. Put your hand down — you have to move one of them first, and it will not want to move.

Nearest to your boot, the plates are small. Fist-sized, palm-sized, crowded like pillbugs under a lifted log, except there is no log and there is no under. These are the **Sweepers.**

At a distance they looked like mineral stain. Up close they are animals. Or machines. The seam has healed over.

Each one is low and oblong, a centimeter of clearance between belly and weeping stone. Its back is a single dome of overlapping hematite scales, black-red and slick with condensation. No head. No tail. Just a front edge that is a mouth, and the mouth is not a mouth — it's a squeegee. A flexible blade of yellowed ceramic cartilage, as wide as the animal, that lives wet.

Watch one.

It extends hydraulic forelegs — not chitin, rebar-jointed — and drags itself forward one body-length with a soft *shink*. The squeegee comes down. It kisses the stone. It draws across the film of water and iron slime and microgrit that has sweated out of the rock in the last minute, and the water *vanishes* under it. Not wiped. Drunk. Sucked into a slit behind the blade where cilia made of steel wool shiver.

Then it moves again. *Shink. Kiss. Drink.* Behind it, the stone is left polished, dry for maybe three seconds before it beads again.

And behind the animal — from a vent at its rear that opens like a shutter — it extrudes something. Not shit. A product. A neat, rectangular pellet of compressed mineral, still steaming, still hissing where the cold air hits it. Iron oxide, silica, trace magnetite. A tiny ingot, the size of a domino, dropped with a soft *tink* onto the stone. The next Sweeper coming behind will push it aside with its cheek. The next one after that will climb over it.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands per stone-plate. They are the base layer, the tongue of the Works. They eat the weeping. They eat the stone's sweat. And everything else eats them, or eats what they make.

Because — THUD.

That one. You feel it in your molars before you see it.

The **Hammers** do not crowd. They cannot. They need radius.

They stand — *perch*, the way long-legged water striders do — on six stilt legs of pitted boiler-plate, each leg joint venting a puff of white steam with every step. The body is not a body. It's a piston, vertical, a meter long, capped top and bottom with riveted iron. No eyes. A ring of pressure gauges around its midsection, needles trembling, none of them measuring anything you know. When it walks, it is almost silent. When it works, it is the only sound in the world.

It positions itself over a bare patch where the Sweepers have been busy. It braces. The legs lock. Then the entire piston-body lifts — hydraulic, screaming — a foot, two feet into the damp air, and slams down.

THUD.

The ancient basalt — 2.6 billion years old, shield that has forgotten how to be a mountain — spiders. A flake, thin as mica, as big as a dinner plate, pops free with a chime that is swallowed instantly by fog.

The Hammer does not eat the flake. It has no mouth. It lifts again.

THUD.

Another. And another. It will fracture this same spot forty, fifty times until the concavity holds a little pile of sharp mineral confetti. Then, as if hearing a signal you can't, it will unlock its legs and stalk away on needle points, delicately stepping between Sweepers who don't get out of the way fast enough and are simply stepped on, dented, and then spring back.

The confetti is the point. The Sweepers converge. They swarm the fresh pile and squeegee it up, flakes and water together, and their ingots come out darker for a while, richer.

And over both of them, threading through the forest of stilt-legs, come the **Eels.**

You saw the arc-light first, out of the corner of your eye, and thought it was a short circuit. It is. It is also an animal.

They are long — sometimes a meter, sometimes three — and they have no thickness. A cable of braided copper wire and translucent cartilage, as thick as your wrist, moving like a whip being cracked in slow motion through the knee-high fog. No face. Both ends taper to a frayed brush of bare filaments that spark constantly. They don't walk. They swim through the wet air a few inches above the stone, propelled by rippling lateral fins made of heat-haze.

They feed on charge.

Every Hammer strike, every Sweeper's squeegee-kiss, every suture-vent sighing open and shut in the rock — it all bleeds a little static into the saturated air. The dim 1.2/3 dusk is full of it. The Eels collect it. They brush their frayed ends against a Hammer's leg as it lifts and you hear a *CRACK* and see blue-white light jump, and the Eel convulses, brighter for a second, its cable-body illuminating the veins inside itself — which are not veins, but wires, but also veins.

When two Eels touch each other — which they do often, because there is no room — they tangle and discharge violently and come apart smoking, shedding a skin. The skin is a hollow tube of copper and mucus, still twitching, and it falls to the stone with a soft hiss.

The Sweepers eat that too. They love it. They swarm a shed Eel-skin and their squeegees chatter with excitement and their next ingots come out laced with green verdigris, warm enough to melt the ice that shouldn't be there.

That is the loop. That is the industry.

Weep -> crack -> drink -> press -> charge -> shed -> drink again. Water seeping, boiled, pissed back as steam, condensed, drunk again. Stone fractured, swallowed, extruded as neat, steaming bricks that will, if left alone, slowly settle back into the sutures between plates and become stone again. No waste. No product shipped out. The Works works for the Works.

There are others, of course. Density 2.6/3 does not permit a simple triangle.

Low, wide things like inverted bathtubs studded with rivets — the **Kettles** — sit motionless for hours in the sutures themselves, so still you thought they were part of the pavement. Then a vent sighs warm beneath one and its entire back hinges open like a lid and it *inhales* the whole gout of steam in one gulp. Its belly glows cherry-red through the seams. It sits, digesting pressure, ticking. Eventually it shuffles a few inches forward and urinates a perfect circle of scalding clear water that the Sweepers sprint toward.

And the tiny ones. God, the tiny ones. If you put your face close — and the fog will let you, for a moment, before it pools chest-high again — you see the grit is not grit. It moves. Mite-things the size of rice grains, each a single rivet with six wire hairs, scurrying over the backs of the Sweepers, cleaning the rivets as the rivets migrate. Because yes — if you watch any one Sweeper long enough, its scales crawl. The pattern shifts. A rivet on its flank slides a millimeter to the left when you blink, as if the animal is being redrawn slightly wrong every second.

That's the weirdness, the 3.0/3, and it is not separate from them. It *is* them.

They flicker. One moment a Hammer a mile away is suddenly detailed enough to count its gauge-needles, as if distance folded like paper. The next it is gone behind fog that wasn't there. A Sweeper casts three shadows in the sourceless dusk. Another casts none, though the water beads on its back still shine. You heard a Kettle vent hissing and looked right at it and its lid was closed, cold, with black ice riming its edge.

You lifted your foot and it weighed more coming down. You set it down and the puddle under it rippled outward *before* your sole touched the water — THUD — and for that stuttered half-second between Hammer falls, every bead of water hanging in the air froze still, glass on invisible thread, reflecting your face back at you from every angle, including from behind.

They don't notice. Or they notice by continuing.

Nothing here was built. Built is 0.3/3, remember. No one assembled the first Sweeper. The stone wept, the water connected, the cold preserved what should have dissolved, and somewhere in a suture where pressure and iron and time folded wrong, a condensation thickened, beaded, and *tinked* onto the pavement already hissing. Half-formed. Hungry. Already making its first ingot out of nothing but film.

That's rule three. You understand it now with your socks soaked through.

If you kick them away — if you clear a whole house-sized plate with your arms, scattering Sweepers, sending Eels snapping away in bright tangles — the fog will rush in to fill the vacuum. It will condense on the cold stone. It will bead, and run together, and the beads will shiver, and rivets will precipitate inside them like eyes opening.

Within a minute there is a new Sweeper there, smaller, paler, its squeegee still soft. It does not know you made room for it. It only knows the film has returned.

And it drinks.
