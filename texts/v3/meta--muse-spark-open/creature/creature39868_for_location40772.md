No one lives here.

That's the first impression, and it holds for a long time if you stand still enough to feel the cold come up through the soles of your feet. There are no houses. No trails cutting the mat, no fire rings, no bones tucked under an overhang. Nothing has ever built a dwelling on the high bench, because nothing here needs to be housed from the weather. The weather is already inside it.

Then the bench moves, and you understand your mistake.

What lives here is not *on* the bench. It *is* the bench.

---

At first you see the Pillars, because they are the only things that let you have scale. They are what you thought were trees.

They are not.

They are sphagnum, haircap and peat-moss, ancestors of the little starry tufts you know from boreal bogs, but here the cold and the thin light and ten thousand years of unbroken tenure have let them forget how to be small. Their trunks are not wood. If you press your palm to one you feel it give, cold and saturated — a column of compacted living fiber, dead moss below holding up living moss above, like a city holding itself up on its own compacted dead. As thick as houses. A hundred feet tall. Some of them a hundred feet across at the crown, a frayed, wind-combed head that shudders even when there is no wind.

This is the citizen. The basic unit. Not an individual plant the way an oak is an individual, but a hydraulic engine. You watch one work:

Deep in its core, water that has been husbanded for weeks — collected as condensation on a frond the size of a sail two hundred meters away, passed hand-to-hand by capillary wicking through miles of felt — arrives as pressure. The pillar swells. Its crown steams faintly in the air that never really warms. Cellulose drinks and thrusts. With a creak so low you feel it more in your molars than your ears, it extends another meter into the thin sun, locking new tissue into place with lignin that was, a day ago, soft as lung.

And then, elsewhere in the basin, its neighbor does the opposite. You learn to watch for the whitening.

A pillar that has grown too tall, or shaded too much of its own understory, will decide — no brain, no nerve, only ion gradients flickering through the wet mat like electrification — to shed. The fibers at its shoulder go pale. A puff of spores releases all at once and hangs in the cold, still air like factory smoke that never disperses because there is not enough wind to take it. Then the upper third delaminates. Not a fall. A slough. Great living mats the size of roofs peel and slide down the sides of their neighbors who are already waiting, fungal wefts gone white and hungry along their flanks. The understory catches, folds, reabsorbs. By evening there is no debris field. There is only slightly more understory.

Growth and destruction at the same pace, everywhere. That is how it breathes.

Between the Pillars is the second kind of resident, and it took you hours to see it as alive at all because it never stands up.

The Sheets. The Drapery.

Liverworts scaled up until a single lobe is a field. Hanging moss curtains a hundred feet long, forming halls and naves and tangled colonnades that filter light through six layers of green-black translucence. Dwarf willow and arctic birch that never learned to be trees — here they learned instead to be rebar, lignified creepers as thick as your waist running horizontally through the mat, anchoring Pillars, suturing bogs. Leaves bigger than doors overlap and interleave. In shallow depressions on the upper canopy, perched bogs appear after rain — black tea-colored mirrors a hundred feet across, floating entirely on moss — then drain over days through living spillways that close behind them like lips.

Nothing here has roots in soil, because soil does not exist. Nothing here has a river, because water is not allowed to cut. Water is too precious to be given to gravity. What lives here are husbandmen of water. Every frond is a gutter, every fold is a cistern. Fog pools for an hour in a colonnade and is wrung out, literally wrung, by the slow contraction of the hanging curtains. In the deep understory, amber-glowing pockets of meltwater hang suspended in sponge-chambers, held aloft by nothing but surface tension ten meters above the rock. They are the reservoirs, the water towers of this city that has no inhabitants.

You put your hand out and a tendril tastes you. As thick as your waist, tipped with a sticky lamp-like bud that opens and closes with hydraulic patience, it probes the air where your heat is, finds nothing useful, recoils. Elsewhere two moss-colonnades have grown into each other's space. They don't tangle. One will suddenly desiccate a swath of its own fronds — a rapid, deliberate browning, a self-pruning — and shed them in a dry rustle too soft to hear ten meters away, just to let light through to its lower intakes. The shed leaves are already being digested by the white fungal wefts that run like conveyor belts along the lower trunks. That is predation here. That is competition. That is industry. Quiet, chemical, total.

And under and through all of it, the third resident, invisible until you stop looking for things and start looking for work:

The Signals.

Cold does not stop work here, it times it. Metabolism is slow, vast, high-torque — the speed of glaciers that have learned to be ambitious. Things happen at the edge of perception, then all at once. A liverwort sheet the size of a parking lot rucks up into a new ridge while you blink. Seed pods tick — the only true sound, sharp as a thumbnail flick — firing across a nave. A low sigh as water shifts its weight in an aerial pool. No wind. No birdcall. The mat muffles even collapse into a hush. The smell is cold iron and wet paper, ozone and tannin.

What coordinates it has no face. Ion potentials flicker through the wet mat, electrified gradients telling a frond fifty meters away to swell, to release, to die. You can walk for a day — stepping carefully from one springy buttress to another, sinking ankle-deep in living felt that seals over your print as you lift your foot — and never leave the same organism, in a sense. Not one mind. A factory without a foreman. A machine with no exterior. A city with no inhabitants because every brick is alive and in on the plan.

There are animals, yes, but they are not who lives here. They are tourists. A beetle picks across a frond for an hour, irrelevant. A white-browed moss-mite bird flickers between pillars and vanishes, too rare to matter to the accounting. They have not been given niches. The plants have taken all the niches for themselves. They photosynthesize, they predigest, they move.

That is what lives on the high bench: a dense, damp, cold engine the size of a valley that solved the problem of how to be a city without citizens, an industry with no masters, a storm that makes no noise, staying exactly itself for ten thousand years while never staying still for a minute.
