It doesn't live *on* the plateau.

It lives *as* the plateau.

#### The Host

The first thing that lives here isn't an animal. It's the country itself.

The Sponge is one organism, or it was, before distance and wind made any idea of oneness meaningless. Call it a colony, a continent of clones that have forgotten they were ever separate. Its life is vertical.

Each Tower begins as a thing you could mistake for something small: a rosette on the black rock, a crown of spoons. The leaves are black-green, waxy to the point of shine, thick as a forearm and warm. Touch one and it gives slightly under your hand, like cartilage. They are bitter to the taste and antifreeze to the core — loaded with glycerols and sugars it brews in slow fermentation deep in its own pith, so that even at -50C the sap stays liquid. That fermentation is why every tower thrums faintly if you put your ear to it. A low, slow fizz, a brewery the size of a cathedral keeping itself from freezing by staying very slightly drunk.

From that rosette it lifts. It doesn't grow like a tree — rings outward — it extrudes like bone, like coral. It braids itself upward into a hollow lattice, living struts as thick as old oaks at the base and thinning to fingers at the crown a kilometer up. The walls are tension and lace: outer skin of interwoven vine-bark, boiled-leather tight, drum-taut against the wind; inner galleries of branch and aerial root that never needed to decide if they were trunk or arm. The whole thing is mostly air. You could house a village in the hollowness of one and have darkness left over.

It drinks without rain.

At this height, on this young basalt, there are no watersheds. No rivers. No springs. The sky is too cold to give rain. So it harvests the only water there is: itself.

Every evening when the sun thins to nothing and the aurora unspools, the temperature drops like a hammer. The towers, holding their fermentation heat into the night air, become the coldest and warmest things at once. Frost flash-forms on them by the ton — beards of white crystal three hands deep on every outer vine, rime inside every hollow. At false dawn that frost melts. It doesn't run off. The lattice is wicked. Capillary thread-roots, hair-fine and pale as nerve, drink it sideways, upward, downward, channeling it into the cisterns it has grown to hold it: great bladders slung between struts, translucent, amber-brown, taut. Some are the size of huts, some the size of houses. You hear them before you see them, sighing and ticking as they swell. The water inside is tepid, tannic, utterly alive with algae and blind shrimp-things. It is the only standing water for a thousand kilometers.

And it listens.

With so little light — polar night for months, then a low, blue, horizon-scraping sun that never gives from overhead — phototropism would have starved it. So it found another compass. The wood of the Tower is piezoelectric in its own way; vibration encourages lignin deposition. Where the wind sings loudest through a gap, it thickens. Where hoppers thump, where wings beat, where something sings steadily, it reaches. A Tower is an ear turned inside out. Make a rhythm inside it for a month and the walls will purse toward you, grow a shelf, a niche, an acoustic horn to better catch what you are making. Leave it silent after a colony of moths dies off, and it will thin, and loosen, and finally go aeolian — widening its own throat until the wind plays it like an organ at such amplitude that its own struts shiver apart. A silent Tower collapses singing.

That is why the plateau from above looks empty. From above you see only black rock and a sparse scattering of dark dots, impossibly far apart. The distances between Towers are true desert: wind-scoured basalt, glassy, shivering with ice quakes, too exposed for anything to linger. No animal crosses it by foot. The life is not between. The life is *in*.

Each Tower is a country.

#### The Tenants

Enter one. Any one. Push through the respiration slit — a vertical lip the plant left open where the wind is most constant — and the weather stops. The thin, cutting high air is replaced by something else: a damp, warm, tannin-smelling darkness that hangs in your lungs. This is the second atmosphere the Sponge makes. Condensation beads every surface. Your breath doesn't crystalize here. It joins the drip.

The light is amber. Not sunlight. The fungus does that. In seams where water runs most reliably, the Sponge cultivates a symbiont — a crustose, luminous fungus that glows amber and tired gold, brightening when water passes over it, dimming when it dries. The whole interior pulses slowly with hydraulic breath. It is enough to see by, if you've lived your life in it.

And it is crowded.

The lowest galleries, where bark sloughs and rot begins, are grazed by the Tumblers. They are pale, blind as cavefish, the size of heavy dogs, with hind legs sprung like a flea's and forelegs reduced to little mantis hooks for clinging. They don't walk along the branches, they *spring*, elastic tendons storing insane energy, ricocheting from wall to wall inside the hollow with soft thumps of their padded feet. They are always listening for the crack of a new shoot, which they chew to pulp. Their young they carry not in pouches but in damp hollows between their own shoulder muscles, where the infants root like parasites until old enough to jump.

Above them, in the warmest, stillest air of the mid-shaft, the Moths hold. There is no single swarm; there are rivers of them. Their bodies are not much bigger than your hand, but their wings are a meter across, translucent, veined like frost, utterly silent in the way only very large moths can be — a soft displacement of air rather than a buzz. Thousands, millions in a healthy Tower. They never see the sky. They live their whole lifecycle on fungus-light and on the Sponge's pollen-puffs, great clouds of rust-red spore that the plant sighs out once a season. When they spiral, the draft of them is a second wind. The air vibrates with scale-dust. They are the great pollinator, the great food, the great cloth — their silk, left in sheets across old galleries, is harvested by everyone.

Who grazes the outside is different. The Shinglebacks. Low-slung, feathered but not birds, lizard-like but not lizards, with overlapping plates of leaf-shaped keratin over dense down. They cling to the outer windward skin with hooked claws and chew the waxy leaves, their tongues black from antifreeze compounds they steal and sequester in their own blood. During storms they lock themselves flat, become part of the bark, invisible. At night they squeeze inside through the smaller pores to sleep in the warmth, exhaling little clouds that freeze on the vine.

And because there are grazers, there are gliders.

The Rippers are what the people call them, though the word is a poor translation for *the ones who cut the wind*. Lean, six-limbed: four legs for clinging and killing, two vast rib-membranes they stretch between elongated ribs like a flying squirrel of nightmare proportion. They are snow-white and eyeless too — they don't need eyes in amber dark — but their ears are dishes, their snouts pitted with vibration sensors. They nest in the crown where the wind is a knife, and they hunt by riding the Tower's own drafts. They will launch from the lip of one Tower and glide five, six, seven kilometers in the thin air, absolutely silent until the last second, when they emit a single, sharp, glassy cry before they strike. That cry is their sonar, their weapon, their declaration. It carries for kilometers in the cold and makes every Tumbler freeze mid-spring. You hear them at night, one after another: *chit—chit—chit*, and then a soft impact and the rustle of moths exploding outward.

Between all these larger lives is a filigree of smallness: beetles that bore and ferment, mites that farm fungus, blind white shrimp in the cisterns, bats that aren't bats but something convergent, hanging in the upper galleries and singing at frequencies too high to hear, tending their single pups.

None of them ever touch the rock. To fall to the basalt is to die of wind and cold within minutes. They are an arboreal fauna without trees, an aerial fauna that never sees sky. Each Tower is a walled, warm, wet world adrift in a cold desert ocean, connected to its neighbors only by wind and by the desperate leaps of gliders and storms.

#### The Singers

And the people.

They are small now. They were not bred for this. No one is bred for 9 kilometers up. But the ones who stayed when the plateau shouldered itself into the stratosphere — or came afterward, walking the one impossible pass before it sheared — they learned to be smaller, slower, more efficient. Barrel-chested as all high people are, with blood thick as oil, cheeks and fingers permanently dusky red under pale skin. Their eyes are large, black-ringed from squinting into amber gloom and aurora; their lungs have a second catch in them, a little hold before they exhale, so their speech has a lilting hitch. They don't live long. Thirty-five is old. Forty is mythic.

You won't find a village. The word doesn't mean anything here. A Tower can only feed so many mouths before the cisterns run low, so they live as families: four, seven, twelve in one hollow, kin and claimed-kin who have earned the right to tie in.

Their houses are not built. They are coaxed. Over years, with piss — the salt and nitrogen are the only fertilizers that matter — and with patient, daily song, they persuade a Tower to grow them a floor, a lip, a windbreak. All their tools are the Tower's leavings: leaf-leather cured over low fires until it is hard as armor but light, needles of shingleback claw, knives flaked from the black basaltic glass at the plateau's skirts, brought up by the handful in leather bags as pilgrimage. Fire they have, but small: they burn the fat-rich lichen-cakes that grow where sap weeps, or rendered moth lard, in stone bowls that make more light than heat. Heat is a sin to waste. Heat is what they wear. Layer upon layer of moth silk and tumbler-hide, stitched so finely you can barely see the seam.

They do not farm the Sponge. The Sponge is too big and too fast. You can watch it grow if you sit long enough — a creak, a pop as loud as a gunshot as a new strut swells and shoulders apart last year's wood, splitting even basalt at the roots. They tend it. Each family knows the cisterns in their Tower the way you know a neighbor's cough. They lance them with bone taps to draw clean water, cork them with wax when the skin stretches too thin. They carve little runnels with flakes of rock to lead sap to where they want it. They gather the frozen sap beads — clear, sweet as winter apple, ferociously alcoholic as they begin to ferment — as their fruit, their sugar, their rite.

And they sing.

Every Tower has its song, not for beauty — though beauty has accreted around it — but for maintenance. A low, sustained, throat-sung drone, passed mother to daughter, family to family, the specific resonance that keeps *that* Tower's walls thick. On long nights under the aurora, when the wind's organ howl rises to a scream and the whole kilometer of hollow shivers and leans ten meters off-true, you can hear them all: each occupied Tower humming to itself, a chord across the basalt, holding their houses in the world by the act of making noise inside them.

Children are taught early: never be silent. If you are scared, hum. If you are alone, drum your hands on the branch. The Tower that forgets your sound will open its throat to the wind and sing itself to pieces, and you with it.

Between Towers they travel rarely, and only at the coldest, stillest part of deep winter when the air is heaviest and a glider can stay aloft longest. They wear wing-suits sewn from layered moth wing and silk, stretched over light vine frames, and they go not by sight but by ear, gliding from hum to hum across the dark, across the emptiness that looks like nothing from above and is everything within.

They carve faces in the living wood at every crossing — not gods, but reminders: *she who found water, he who fell, the child who first made this Tower answer*. The sap bleeds around the cuts and heals dark. In a century the face is puckered, swallowed, grown over. They carve another.

They do not think the plateau is hostile. The word hostile requires intent. They think it is young, and distracted, and very busy becoming something else, and they are very small guests who have learned which draft to stand in so as not to be noticed by the wind.

If you stay with them until you stop shivering, until your lungs learn the hitch, until you can hear your own heart in the great hollow between the organ notes, you will feel it too — that your breath, too, has become part of the damp, that your little warmth has been folded into the fermentation that keeps the whole impossible furred continent from freezing.

And outside, on the black glass that has not yet learned to be soil, the next rosette is already spreading its waxy leaves toward the sound you didn't realize you were making.
