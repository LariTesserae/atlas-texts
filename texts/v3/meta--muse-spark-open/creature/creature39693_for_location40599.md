They shouldn't be here.

That's the first thing you understand about everything that lives on the Table. At three miles up, on old polished stone, under light that has no mercy and wind that never stops — nothing *should* live here. And so everything that does has made a bargain with the condition itself.

#### The Shawl-Wings — The Air Itself

You thought the plateau was empty because you were looking for things *on* the ground. The ground is the void. The sky is crowded.

They fill it — **wind-feeders, shawl-wings, gauze-kites.** Abundant to obscenity, 2.2/3 where there should be 0/3, because they have solved the problem of there being nothing to eat.

They don't eat the stone. They eat the wind.

Each one is about as long as your hand, but weighs almost nothing. A kite-shaped body of translucent gray cartilage and stretched skin, two broad, paper-thin wings that never really flap so much as *thrum* — 80 beats a second, a blur — and two tail filaments twice as long as the body that act as rudders in the constant 20-knot pour. Their heads are all mouth: a combed, baleen-like sieve of black bristles they hold open as they ride.

They don't hunt. They strain. The never-changing wind off the northern icefields brings them a soup of ice crystals, airborne lichen spores, mites torn from the crust, condensation droplets, the desiccated dust of their own dead. They hang in it like baleen whales in a current, mouths open, wings thrumming to hold station over the plateau's lip where the updraft is strongest.

That is the sound. That deafening 2.9/3 that never varies. Not wind through rock — though that low organ chord is under it — but a million tiny wings at once. A cicada roar so constant your skull tries to edit it out. It doesn't get louder at noon or softer at night. At night they glow, too — a faint, ember-pink bioluminescence along the wing veins, so the whole sky above the Table looks dusty with coals, thrumming, always thrumming.

Individually they are fragile. You can tear one with a breath. Collectively they are the biomass of this world, the grass and the buffalo and the birds all at once. When they die, they don't fall like birds. They fold, and drop like ash, spangling the basins, adding their brief salt to the lenses of water.

This is why the Table can be damp, cool, bright and nearly without plants and still be *abundant.* The pasture is in the air.

#### The Basins — The Ephemerals

In every pothole, every giant's soap-dish smoothed into the quartzite, there is a second world that lives by the hour.

When the dew condenses at dawn — when the stone exhales — or when that sudden vertical rain hammers for ten minutes and vanishes, the bowls fill. And the water, for a few hours, is not empty.

**Pallid water-lice, basin shrimp.** The size of rice grains, translucent with a single black eye-spot. They were never anywhere else. They live as cysts in the dry rock dust, invisible for days. Then water touches them and they *hatch* all at once, frantic, paddling, filter-feeding on the bacteria that bloom in the lens, mating, laying, dying before noon. By the time the sun has bleached the pool dry again, they are already dust again. A whole generation in a morning.

Crouch close and you see the pools shiver without wind. That's them. Thousands per palm-sized bowl. Millions in the house-sized ones.

Above them, on the slick lip where the water meets stone, live the **mist-mites** — barely visible red or white specks that shiver across the lichen crust. They drink condensation directly off the rock grain. They are eaten in turn by the shawl-wings when the wind plucks them loose.

#### The Crust — The Patient Ones

Vegetation here has given up on being vegetation.

It is sparse — 0.9/3 — because it agrees with the stone: don't be ambitious. Don't stand up. The wind will take you, the light will burn you.

So it lies down.

**Lichen.** Black-green, rust-red, bone-grey. It covers the leeward side of every fin and flute in leathery sheets, as thin and hard as dried paint. It grows to exactly the damp line — where the condensation reaches — and stops, as if cut with a knife. It doesn't spread. It doesn't die back. It maintains. Centuries old, growing a millimeter a year, tasting stone and light.

In the seep-cracks where the big basins slowly leak into the rock, that lichen gives way to cushions of **alpine moss**, hard as upholstery foam, dark green and cold to the touch, and to the only thing that dares to be soft: **jade-rosettes.** Tiny succulents with waxy, polished leaves that clasp the rock like hands, storing a day's dew in their flesh.

And the trees that are not trees: **flag-conifers.** One or two per acre, density 0.5/3 made visible. They never get taller than your knee. The constant wind has combed them sideways, bark stripped on the windward side, branches only on the lee, twisted into bonsai knots. They grow for a hundred years to get that big. They produce cones the size of your thumbnail.

And then, inexplicably — because damp allows for miracles — the **five-petaled purple.** A single flower with a stem thin as thread that rises out of the center of a condensation pool, blooms for a morning, perfect and impossible against all that basalt glare, and is dead by the time the pool dries. No one knows where its seed waits. Even the people don't pick it.

#### The People — The Lees

There are people. Not many. They have to be thin, or the air won't let them stay.

They are high-chested — barrels for lungs — and dark, not from sun-tan but from constant light-injury, skin cured to leather, eyes narrowed to slits beneath heavy brows. They wear shawl-wing leather, translucent gray capes that still faintly smell of ozone, stitched with sinew. They carry nothing that can be taken by wind.

They do not build. **Built 0.3/3.** That was your mistake — looking for houses, for walls. A wall here is an insult to equilibrium. The wind, steady as a river for centuries, dynamic 0.3/3, will patiently unstack any cairn by morning. Not violently. Just inevitably. This place does not permit accumulation. Bodies don't rot; they sublime into dust. Lichen doesn't overgrow. Even the pools fill to the same line every time.

So the people **move.**

They are crouchers, not dwellers. They know every wind-flute, every organ-pipe rock that sings that low roar, and they sleep in its lee, curled around the stone that still holds a little sun-heat. They drink from the basins on hands and knees, lips to the lens. They carry hooked poles — long, light wands of dwarf-conifer wood tipped with flaked obsidian — and **nets.** Nets woven from shawl-wing filament and gut that sing when you pull them through thrumming air.

They fish the sky.

Standing on the lip where the updraft roars strongest, they cast and skim, and the shawl-wings, stupid with abundance, tangle by the dozen. The people eat them raw, or dried into sheets. They burn their oily bodies for the little heat they allow themselves at night. They read the wind's chord to know when the rain will come — a slight change in the whistle through the Aeolian teeth that you would never hear under the thrumming.

Tech 0.8/3. Flaked stone. Bone needles. Fire, but small and shielded. No pottery — it would crack in the cold that lives in the stone. No herds — nothing to herd. No gods that demand temples. If they pray, they pray to the steady wind, by listening to it.

If you stay, you learn their economy: take just enough condensation, just enough sky-meat, leave no mark, and the Table will let you continue to borrow its condition. Take too much, try to stay in one place, try to build — and the light will bleach you, the cool will steal you, the wind will erase you, as it has erased everything else that tried to change.

They are not surviving *on* the plateau. They are part of its maintenance — another crust, thin, dark, patient, thrumming faintly in time with the wings, perfectly settled into an old, bright, cool, damp void that sings one deafening note forever.
