Here is what lives there.

Not who you expect at first. Stand on the rim and you think: *nothing lives here.* Too young, too cold, too loud. A crack in the earth with a dead factory in it. Then you get down there, down the maintenance ladder where your gloves stick to the rungs, and the air hits you - wet stone and old diesel and living peat - and you realize you were wrong about the proportions. It's not a factory with some plants in it.

It's a plant with some factory in it.

### 1. The Carpet

The primary resident is not a species but a congress. It is easiest to call it the Moss, though that insults it.

On the floor of the graben, *Sphagnum fuscum* and *S. magellanicum*, cold-black and wine-red at the tips, have grown beyond ground-cover into a body. Two to four meters deep in the flats, hanging in curtains off the headframes where condensation never stops. It holds 20 times its weight in water that never warms above 2°C. Under it, permafrost and peat preserve everything perfectly - a dropped wrench from 1963 is still bright where the moss holds it, the wooden handle softened to cheese.

It breathes. Slowly. In summer-noon when the shaft of sun hits the floor for 137 minutes, it photosynthesizes in a green-black flush. At midnight under the sodium lamps, it does something else - grows toward that false orange sun, etiolated but persistent. You can see it in the way every shoot within twenty meters of a lamp leans, not up toward the rim where the real sun lives, but sideways toward the buzzing bulb.

It is the architect. It does not grow *on* things; it grows *through* the idea of things. The stacked spruce bolts in the lumber yard are no longer wood - they are a moss-bolt composite, the wood rotted to humus inside a perfect moss cast that still holds the rectangular shape. The handrails bead with water because the moss exhales against them. The corridors you walk through are not paths you cut. They are pressure tunnels the moss left open because wind moves through them and brings nitrogen. Step off them, onto undisturbed sphagnum, and your boot sinks to the ankle and then to the knee and the sound changes, and you understand you have stepped onto a back.

If you stay still long enough - and the cold forces you to stay still, to conserve - you can hear it: not a sound, but the cessation of drip as the mass swells with water, the faint creak of a million hydroids tightening as ice forms in them.

It does not welcome. It does not reject. It simply continues.

Within it and under it, its tenants:

**Dwarf Black Spruce (*Picea mariana* subsp. *lithos*)** - no taller than 3 meters, trunks no thicker than a man's forearm, packed 40 to a square meter where the wind is worst. They are old. You count rings on a fallen one and get 140 years for a trunk you could encircle with two hands. Their needles hold rime year-round as insulation. Their roots do not go down - there is no soil - they go *sideways*, grafting into each other in a horizontal net so that no single tree falls alone; they lean together, a slow crowd-surf toward light. Cones never fully open; seeds germinate inside the half-open cone, a green tuft sprouting from its mother in the air.

**The Rot** - white crust fungi *Antrodia* and *Fomitopsis* that have learned to eat creosote-treated timber and rubber belt. They glow faintly, not romantically - a sick foxfire along the underside of the conveyor where the belts scream. That is the true base of the food chain here, not sunlight but diesel.

### 2. The Deaf Things

Sound is geography here. In the flats near the turbine, 95-110 decibels constant, low-frequency. The stone walls reflect it without loss. Any animal that communicates with sound cannot live here. So what lives here has given that up.

**The Raven (the one).** There is usually one. Not a species, an individual - maybe a lineage of individuals that has held this territory since the plant was built. He is large, oil-black, his feathers grown thick and shaggy against the damp cold. His syrinx is damaged, perhaps congenitally; his call is not a croak but a hoarse *click*. He doesn't call often. He lives on the generator housing behind the sawmill, where waste heat from bearings keeps a patch of metal at 18°C. He has learned to pick the fat, torpid moths that stun themselves against the sodium lamps and to cache bits of hot grease.

He is deaf. Not totally - necropsy of one found its predecessor with cochlear bone thickened by constant vibration. He navigates by sight and by feeling the thrumming through his feet. When the turbine stutters - when an intake screen clogs - he is the first to know, lifting his head before any gauge would show it. The steppe crews say he will follow you if you work on the turbine, watching with a hard, irritated eye until you finish, as if you are his incompetent assistant.

**Lamp-moths & Ice-midges.** Where there are orange lamps in polar twilight, there will be insects, even at -5°C. *Eilema depressa borealis*, a lichen-moth, slate-grey and flightless in females. The males flutter weakly around each lamp in a halo of maybe two meters, never leaving its light cone, living their whole adult lives in that sodium-orange cylinder. They feed on the algae that grows on the lamp housings themselves, watered by condensation. In high summer their larvae carpet the spruce bark in a moving grey felt.

And under the ice plates in the low spots: *Diamesa* midges, wingless, black as basalt chips, that skate on the underside of the clear ice in the tarns. They have antifreeze proteins so efficient their hemolymph never freezes. They are the only animal that truly breeds here, in numbers - billions - and their dead pile in windrows under the moss like poppy seeds.

**The Vole That Doesn't Squeak.** *Microtus miurus chasmophilus* - if you dig, which you shouldn't, you'd find their runs not through soil but *through* the moss, perfect smooth-walled tunnels a vole-width wide, insulated and always damp. They have lost vocalization entirely; their auditory bullae are small and fused. They communicate by thumping the moss with their hind feet - *thump-thump* that travels through water-saturated peat better than any cry. Their fur is stained peat-brown and smells of diesel. Owls do not hunt them; it's too loud for owls to hear anything. So they die old, of cold.

Everything that stays has learned to be small, low, vibration-smart, and indifferent to light that isn't orange.

### 3. The Keepers (Intermittent Humans)

No one lives here. That is important. People *inhabit* it the way you inhabit a bus station between buses - on purpose, briefly, with a ticket out.

They come from above. The steppe communities - Nenets-adjacent, but no longer nomadic in the old sense; they run snowmachines and satellite internet and reindeer in unequal measure. They call the graben *Khalmer-Ya*, the dead river wound. Their grandparents worked the basalt quarry as Soviet conscripts; their parents were told to forget it. They remember anyway.

Two to four times a year, depending on how hard the winter is, someone is sent. Usually young - 19 to 30, male or female, it doesn't matter, chosen for small size and steadiness. They wear quilted telogreikas still, over modern membrane, because cotton holds heat when damp and this place is always damp. They carry oil in jerry cans, a pry bar, a spare screen, and ear defenders that never quite work.

They descend the ladders left where paint has held - orange lead paint still glossy under rime - and do a circuit that takes six hours: clear the turbine intake of sphagnum and ice needles; knock rime off the belts with a rubber mallet; drain the pump; check the lamps. They shout "ALL CLEAR!" though no one is there to hear, because protocol says to. They piss on the hot turbine housing - the steam is the warmest thing they will feel all day - and watch raven-moths scatter.

They don't stay. That's doctrine. You do the work and you climb, even if it is noon-light and beautiful. Because the cold pools. Because if you fall asleep in the deaf roar you get hypothermic without shivering; the sound masks your body's warnings. Because the moss makes you dream strangely - old hands say staying more than a day makes you want to lie down in it to see what it would grow over you. Two workers in the 80s did stay, or tried to shelter a storm. They were found in spring, perfectly preserved, sphagnum already stitching their eyebrows together, their ears bleeding.

But they leave marks: boot prints in frost that freeze overnight into glass-smooth molds; names carved into boiler house tin with a nail - ANYA 09, KOLYA + VERA, a hammer and sickle someone re-carved deeper; a cache of dry biscuits left in a sealed ammo can for the next person. The next person always eats them.

There is a superstition that if the lamps go out, the graben will close. Not geologically - spiritually. That the light is what keeps the plant from forgetting it was quarried. So they keep the turbine clear. Not for power - there's nothing to power anymore - but for promise.

### 4. The Mill Itself

And finally, the thing that lives there most stubbornly, though it never lived at all.

The hydro-turbine, the belts, the conveyor. A 1957 Francis turbine, Soviet-built, over-engineered by a factor of three. It turns at 250 rpm no matter what. It has worn a perfect groove in its own bearing and now runs quieter than it did new - the machine has fitted itself to its work. When the river sinks low in February, it cavitates and screams, a different scream from the belts, and ice shatters off the walls. When melt comes in August, it thunders.

It is not intelligent. It does not need to be. It has one need - clear water - and the humans give it that, and in return it gives light and sound, and light and sound give everything else a place to be.

If it stops, the lamps die. If the lamps die, the moths die. If the moths die, the raven leaves. If the raven leaves, no eye watches the intake, and moss chokes the screen faster. Within three winters, the graben floor would be dark and silent again, the moss knitting over the conveyor as it has knitted over everything else. You would stand there and need a headlamp to see your hand.

So in a way, the turbine is the keystone species.

---

At midnight, when the sun from above is gone and the sodium islands are all there is, and the spruce crowd leans in and the basalt disappears upward into nothing, this is the census: a vast, wet, cold mind of moss holding a handful of mute insects and one deaf bird and a machine that will not stop shaking, tended by ghosts who visit four times a year to keep it all awake.

It is not thriving. It is not dying. It is continuing, which in early country, country that still shows its teeth, is the same as living.
