Almost no one lives here. That's the first thing you have to understand. Not no one's home — *almost* no one's home. Which is emptier.

You could live your whole life on that avenue and not meet another person. The city was poured for eight million and now it holds maybe eight hundred. Maybe eight thousand on a census year when the nomads loop back through. The density never rises above *encounter*. You don't get crowds. You get sightings.

What lives here instead is three overlapping ecologies that have learned to use an empty city the way hermit crabs use a shell.

#### 1. The Chorus — The Things That Own The Sound

They own this place the way insects owned Earth before people did. Completely, indifferently.

Closest to you, always, are the **Glasswings**. They look like dragonflies only until one lands. Then you see they are as long as your arm, their thorax is armored in matte black chitin that drinks the amber light, and their four wings aren't membrane — they're lattice, etched silica, vibrating rather than flapping. That's the scream you hear. They don't buzz. They *resonate*. A single one passing at head height will make your teeth ache. There are clouds of them between the terrace walls, hawking for smaller things, mating in mid-air spirals that last for hours because the air is so still and warm they never have to land.

Below them, in every cistern-lake, canal-cut, and basalt pocket that holds still water for more than a week, are the **Balloon Throats**. Someone on the design team clearly thought "frog" and someone else thought "air-quality monitor" and neither stopped the other. They're amphibians the size of mastiffs, smooth, mottled grey-green, with hind legs that fold like scaffolding and a vocal sac that inflates to a translucent pink sphere a meter across. When it deflates it doesn't croak — it shears. A metallic *SHRRIIP* that carries for blocks down the avenue-canyons. You will never see just one. Where there's one black mirror of water, there are twenty throats floating like buoys, eyes just above the surface, drinking light through the lilies. At night — which isn't night — they all call at once, and the mist vibrates.

Above them both, in the praying forests of pale-bark trees, live the **Shoulder-birds**. They have feathers, but their wings have an extra joint halfway, like an elbow in the middle of the forearm, so when they fold they don't fold — they collapse, origami-small. When they open again they almost double their apparent size in an instant, flashing undersides that are not colored but patterned with light-producing filaments. Every building ledge is white with their guano, every dawn chorus is them shrieking territorial geometry at each other in sounds that ricochet perfectly between those clean-walled 3-meter drops. They watch you. Curious. Not afraid. Nothing here is afraid of people because nothing here has had reason to be.

Insects, frogs, birds — that's the easy taxonomy. But none of them are quite insects, frogs, birds anymore. The Canopy's rationed light, the mineral-rich condensation, the heat... they've been growing fast for generations in a place where nothing erodes and everything accretes. Leaves are bigger, carapaces thicker, calls louder. They're not mutated. They're *indulged*.

#### 2. The Curtain — The Things That Grow Where People Should

If the Chorus owns the sound, the Curtain owns the surfaces.

It's not moss. The city staff called it **Veil** in the few maintenance logs left running on loop in tram shelters: *veil density at 74%, veil clearance requested*. It grows as hanging tapestries meters long off any vertical face — deep emerald turning to rust-red at the tips where it flowers. Each frond is made of folded paper-leaves that are photosensitive in real time. Stand in front of a wall of it when the Canopy brightens a patch — that sudden noon-spotlight moving like a searchlight — and you'll hear it: a soft rustle and pop as a thousand leaves snap open toward the light in unison. When the mist falls, they all curl closed to drink. Underneath the Veil, the basalt sweats.

The trees are its anchors: **Praying Whites**, thin, fast, starved-looking trunks that shoot straight up from the basalt cracks, bark peeling like sunburn, each topped with five or six enormous translucent leaves. Each leaf is a solar sail the size of a tabletop, veined with red, and they don't track the sun. They track the dark veins of the Canopy, tilting with audible creaks when the lattice shifts. Their roots are the most alive part — white, cable-thick, exposed along the terrace drops, constantly groping for new purchase. Cut one and sweet sap bleeds out that Glasswings will swarm to in seconds. Leave a shoe on a ledge and a rootlet will find the laces by morning.

And then the flowers. The manufactured ones. No one planted them. They ride the Veil and erupt from tram seats, doorframes, balcony railings — perfect spheres of petals that glow faintly from inside, white, pale blue, arterial pink. They open with a *pop* you can feel in your sinuses, release a plume of warm, yeasty perfume that hangs in the humid air like incense, then close again an hour later. The Shoulder-birds avoid them. The Balloon Throats eat them whole. If you stay too long near a cluster, you'll notice the Veil underneath pulsing slightly faster, as if photosynthesizing through you.

The Veil isn't sentient. But it's managed. It was designed to be the city's lungs, insulation, and paint job all at once. With no hands to trim it, it has decided the entire basin is its pot.

#### 3. The Sparse Ones — What Little Is Left Of Us

People do live here. Just not enough to be a people anymore.

You'll know they've been somewhere because absence otherwise would be perfect. A doorway cleared of Veil. A housing stack where fifteen stories of windows are dark except for two, where someone has hung cured Glasswing wings like laundry to dry — they make excellent, if loud, window screening. A cistern-lake where lily pads have been cut into a path.

The ones who stayed, or came back after the Departure, are called — when they talk about themselves at all, which is rarely — **Hollowers** or **Tenders**. Most are born elsewhere and drift in. Young land does something to certain kinds of human mind: the lack of erosion, the folded space, the forever 6PM. They come for quiet and then forget how to want noise.

They don't live *together*. That would defeat the point of an empty city built for millions. One per stack, sometimes a family of three per district, often a kilometer between occupied buildings. They dress in reclaimed transit-uniform fabric because it breathes and repels water, faces dyed amber by perpetual filtered light. They farm nowhere — you don't need to farm when vines fruit every ten days and every canal has fish fat as pillows that never learned to fear a hook. Instead they *tend*. They cut Veil back from transit grooves so the humming doesn't gum up. They unclog a condensation spout so the mist keeps falling on schedule. Not for the city. The Canopy doesn't need them. For the deal: if they keep a little corridor walkable, the city lets them stay without noticing them.

If you meet one, you won't talk much. Sound carries too well and hurts the ears after years of listening to the Chorus. They communicate in gestures, in leaving things. A bowl of fig-like fruit outside an open tram door means *thank you for not sitting here*. A line of white stones across an avenue means *don't go this way, the light trap stays bright there and you'll cook*.

And some of them have started to change, like everything else indulged here does. Children raised under low, diffuse amber for fifteen years grow taller, thinner, their pupils permanently wide. Some Tenders have let Veil root into their balconies and then into their skin — not parasitically, just... close. You might see an old woman whose hair is braided with living paper-leaves that open when she steps into a bright patch. She'll tell you — if she tells you anything — that the Canopy itches less when you wear a little of it.

#### 4. What Lives In The Fold

This is the last ecology, and you only glimpse it if you linger.

Rule Three. Space is not respected. Walk into a housing stack that should be thirty meters deep and your footsteps echo like a cathedral. Look through the wrong door in the right light and you'll see not a room but a long, dim hall extending far beyond where the next building should be. The city folded volume like laundry when it was grown.

Something else realized there is shelter in that kind of emptiness.

You won't see them well. They're shy of direct attention the way deer are shy of wolves. They're called **Phase Things** by the few Tenders who bother naming them: flickers at the edge of vision, like heat haze taking a shape. In the doubled hallways, in the avenues that take longer to return than to leave, things move that are perfectly adapted to being big in a place that looks small.

A shadow detaches from a doorway and resolves into a drifter as large as a bus, translucent, boneless, filtering Veil spores out of the air with cilia you can only see when the mist catches them. It has no eyes. It lives entirely in the folded rooms, slipping between balconies through spaces that shouldn't connect. The Shoulder-birds mob it silently whenever it emerges fully into real space.

Smaller ones are more common: knot-like tangles of air that live in the long sound-channels, feeding on resonance. Stand somewhere very quiet — rare here — and you'll hear a *tick-tick* that is almost in rhythm with your heart but not quite. That's one listening to you.

They are not dangerous. Like everything in the City After the Canopy, they are overfed, under-pressured, left alone in a vastness poured for someone else. They drift, they graze, they echo.

And all of it — Glasswings screaming off basalt, Veil breathing open-closed, Balloon Throats shearing, Shoulder-birds flashing, an old Tender trimming a veil-shaggy tram, a folded-space whale sighing through a lobby that shouldn't exist — happens under the Canopy. Which isn't alive. But which lives here. Stationary, patient, cycling pressure every hour, dripping its lukewarm mist, moving its bright spotlight for no reason you can see, humming through the still-warm rock beneath your feet.

It tends the forest that replaced its city.

And in all that immensity, with kilometers in every direction to vanish into, absolutely nothing is in a hurry to find you.
