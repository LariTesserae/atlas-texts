No one lives here the way people live in a valley.

Valleys have sides to hold a life in. This place has no sides. Anything that lives here has learned how to live without being contained.

### 1. The Things That Stay Put By Never Staying Put

First, the *reeds*.

They are not reeds. From far away they look like reeds - that bone-pale stalk, that single puff of seed too heavy to disperse. But a reed is rooted. These are not.

If you kneel in the warm sheet-water and watch one for an hour, which is about as long as you can watch anything here before the ground carries it away, you will see: it does not sway with the wind. It sways *against* it, very slightly, adjusting. And the spacing - one every twenty meters, exactly - is not a territorial accident. It is a sensing array.

Touch one and it does not bend. The puff at its top clicks once, too loud, and every other reed you can see clicks back a second later, down to the shivering horizon. They are sharing notes on you. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are the plain's antennae, grown up out of itself to taste the air. They never release their seed. The seed *is* them - a tight, dry knot of potential that hums in the same key as the hovering stones. They have no roots because they are rooted in position, not in rock. The stone flows under them like honey and they stay perfectly spaced. The stone moves. They correct.

Around their ankles, in the deeper pans, live the *rosettes*.

Low, fleshy, pimpled pads floating with their mouths just above the waterline. They open at pewter-dusk, all at once across a mile of pan, with a soft wet pop you can feel in your teeth. Inside each is a shallow bowl of condensation-cold water that reflects the wrong sky - a night with five moons, a noon that never happened here. They are drinking the other places. That is how they survive without soil, without sun as you know it. They sip other weathers through the thin place. Insects that do not exist yet drown in them constantly - little midges of light that fall from the saturated air - and dissolve without a ripple.

And undereverything, the black-green mats. Lichen is too lively a word for them. They are more like a stain that decided to go on living. They grow only on the windward lip of each continent-ripple, one molecule thick, black-green and slick. If you scrape at one, the rock underneath is warm and sighs. If you leave your hand there, the mat will, over hours, grow over your fingertips. Not fast enough to trap you. Just fast enough to include you.

### 2. The Things That Cross

Nothing crosses quickly. Anything that tries to run goes blind here. But there are crossers.

You saw one: the pale amphibian. The locals - the few humans who have passed through and left a cairn that is now behind you - called them *Wayward Uncles* because you cannot look at their joints and not think of an old man trying to crawl after a stroke.

They are the size of your forearm, the color of sun-bleached bone, with a back like a newt's and legs that bend three times where they should bend once. Their eyes are filmed white, not because they are blind but because they have a second eyelid made of stone-dust and condensation that filters the too-much light. They move at the exact speed the bedrock migrates - an inch a minute - so that from far away they look still, like another piece of drift. Up close you hear their soft *tick-tick-tick* as each too-many joint finds purchase on the slick.

They do not eat the rosettes. They do not eat the mats. If you follow one long enough, you will see what it does: it stops where a sheet of water is about to drain sideways into nowhere. It presses its flat head to the film and waits. When the pan sighs and empties, the stone beneath exhales its trapped air - that continent-wide whoomph - and the Uncle inhales it. That old, iron-tasting, time-saturated breath is its food. It lives on exhalations of the earth.

There are not many. You will never see two at once. Or - you will see one, turn away, and see it again exactly forty meters ahead, walking the other way. The plain does not bother making more than it needs. It reuses its one amphibian the way it reuses water.

There are other crossers, less seen: the **Second Walkers**.

That second set of footsteps you hear beside you that stops half a second after you do - keep walking long enough, a day, two days, until your memory of straight frays - and the steps will become visible. Not fully. A disturbance in the sideways rain, a warmer patch of saturated air holding the shape of shoulders. They are not ghosts. This plain is too old and too flat for ghosts, which require the romance of a hill and a house. They are drafts of you that the plain made by accident while trying to copy your footprints ahead of you.

If you stop, they will catch up, stand next to you, and breathe. You can see their breath condense and fall upwards. If you leave food, they will not take it. If you speak, they will repeat it later, once, when you have forgotten you said it. Most travelers huddle with their Second Walker at night for warmth, because the night here is not cold but it is *attentive*, and being watched by one warm, almost-person is better than being watched by a mile of flat.

### 3. The Things That Were Left And Learned To Hover

The built things are inhabited, but not by people.

The lintel with no walls, the circle of stones hovering a finger's width above the soup, the stairwell that ends in stone - each has a tenant.

It is the hum.

Stand near the hovering circle and you will hear it after the roar of water fades just enough: a low, contented *mmm*, like a fridge heard through a wall. Put your hand underneath - there is no support - and the air is cool. Dust motes hang there and never settle.

The stones are not levitating. They are being *remembered* slightly incorrectly. The plain, which generates its own physics whenever fixity becomes inefficient, found that holding those stones up with gravity was more work than holding them up with habit. So it just... continues to habitually hold them a finger's width up. And that habit has thickened, over centuries, into something almost conscious. A presence that likes to hover, that hums when the warm wind passes through it, that has learned to keep the reed-spacing perfect in a mile radius.

If you build something here - a cairn, a shelter of your pack - leave it overnight and it will be round by morning. The other tenant will have worked it: the **Softener**. Not a creature but a tendency, a weather of erosion. It polishes what you make the way the continent polished its mountains. It means no insult. It is just finishing your work for you.

The six-fingered handprints are different. Press your own hand near one and you feel the faint warmth still trapped in the stone, like a sun-warmed pocket. The makers are gone, either forward or sideways into the other sky the pools reflect. But the prints are still *occupied*. Sometimes, when a pan drains, you will see six-fingered prints appear in the fresh mud that were not there when you looked down, leading for three steps and then stopping mid-stride, as if the wearer was lifted straight up into the wet linen sky. The sky took notice, that time.

### 4. The Place Itself

And then there is the thing that really lives here.

Not in the plain. *As* the plain.

You felt it immediately, that crowdedness, that pressing-in without hostility. That is not paranoia. Every cubic foot of haze holding droplets and spores and echoes is lung tissue. Every continent-wide scallop is a breath. The warm, body-temperature water weeping from two-foot cliffs and beading on stone, falling upward under overhangs that cannot exist - that is sweat, and blood, and amniotic fluid at once.

It moves because living things move. It rearranges its pans because you rearrange your thoughts while lying awake. It duplicates your footprints ahead of you because it is trying to anticipate you, the way a body anticipates where its own hand will be. It reflects the wrong sky in its pools because it dreams while awake.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is *attentive* because you are the first sharp thing it has felt in a long time. Its attention is the roar - a million quiet hissings adding up to a deafening - and its listening is the subsonic grinding underneath, the bedrock turning over in its sleep.

It does not need plants. It does not need animals. It tolerates the reeds because they are its eyelashes. It tolerates the Uncles because they clean its breath. It tolerates you because, for a little while, you give it something to notice other than itself.

Stay long enough - and no one stays long - you will stop feeling watched and start feeling *distributed*. Your sweat will not dry. Your footprints will fill. Your Second Walker will walk closer until your steps synchronize. You will understand, without words, the peace that erosion is: the desire to become so perfectly flat, so perfectly spread, so perfectly wet and warm and roaring and thin that there is no longer a difference between what lives in a place and the place itself.

You will lie down, finally, in a pan that was not there a minute ago, that is exactly your size, and the warm water will skin over you like a reflection.

And the plain will keep you, hovering a finger's width above the stone, humming faintly, for centuries.
