Nothing lives here the way living is supposed to work.

Not comfortably. Not completely. Nothing has moved in. Everything here is either holding on, passing through, or in the middle of becoming something that could live here, given another thousand years when the rock softens and the water decides what it is.

What does live here is defined by the pocket's three refusals: it refuses soil, it refuses quiet, and it refuses to let water leave.

### The First Tenants

The ones who got here first because they never needed to be invited.

**Black lichen and the cushion plants.**

In any crack where wind has forced a pinch of grit — granite dust from the ridge, a fleck of old mica, a grain of dead insect chitin carried up from the valley on an updraft — the lichen has taken hold. Not green. Black, or near-black, a tarry crust that looks like the rock sweating. It doesn't grow *on* the stone so much as *into* the first millimeter of it, dissolving basalt just enough to anchor itself against the constant spray.

It thrives on abuse. The mineral-heavy mist that the wall throws inland three or four times a minute feeds it. It likes cold. It likes noise, or rather, it does not care about noise at all. If you crouch and look close, you see it's pitted with tiny cups, each one holding a bead of that salt-ocean water the wall makes without an ocean.

Among them, in only the few cracks deep enough to hold a teaspoon of true dirt, are the cushions. Alpine starwort or something like it, but stunted beyond recognition. Each plant is a tight, hard dome the size of your fist, hugging the rock so low it seems painted on. Its leaves are waxy, overlapping like shingles to shed the spray. For fifty-one weeks a year it looks dead. For one week — late July, early August, depending on when the snow finally lets go of the notch — it erupts in a fury of tiny white flowers, five-petaled, each no bigger than a fingernail, all open at once, all turned not toward the sun but *away* from the water-wall. They set seed frantically in the windless roar and then close again.

They are not thriving. They are enduring immaculately.

### The One That Tried

**The pine.**

At the highest eastern lip, where the black-grey pages of rock are tallest and give a little shelter from the thrumming, there is a single krummholz pine. You could call it a tree the way you could call a hunched old man in a storm a standing man. Technically true.

Its trunk is as thick as your thigh, but it only rises two feet before it gives up on verticality and twists hard to the east, away from the pocket, branches flagged like hair in wind. Bark stripped to polished grey on the water-facing side by perpetual salt-spray and grit. Roots like pale fingers forced into fissures where there is no earth, gripping raw quartz, drinking only from condensation and what the air throws at them.

It is the only thing here that casts a real shadow. And it has learned to live leaning. Its needles are short, yellow-green, dusted white with dried minerals. Cones small and tight, many of them closed forever, waiting for a fire that will never come in this damp. It makes almost no new growth. In a lower forest it would be a sapling in twenty years; here it is perhaps eighty, a hundred, a bonsai made by noise and cold.

It doesn't shelter anything else. No birds nest in it — the sound keeps them away. It stands alone, a witness tree for a place that hasn't decided to be a forest yet, enduring the roar by turning its back to it.

### The Commuters

**Things that visit water without belonging to it.**

On calm days — if you can call any day here calm — you'll find one, maybe two water striders on the interior surface of the pool. Not on the vertical wall; even they have limits. They skate on the relatively horizontal center, where the churn briefly flattens between convulsions.

They shouldn't be here. There's nothing for them to eat, nowhere for them to lay that won't be ripped apart. Their long legs dimple water that is too cold and too turbulent to hold dimples for long. They move in frantic, short jags, constantly correcting. When the wall booms from a gust of outside wind, they skitter toward the rock edge and freeze, sensing the vibration through their feet before your ears hear it.

Above, rarely, a lammergeier — a bearded vulture — will cut across the sky. From inside the fold you see it twice: once directly overhead, a flicker of cream and rust, and once in the water-wall, huge and bent, its wingspan stretched and rippled into a sea-monster shape. They never land. The thermals are wrong inside the pocket, torn by the cold exhalation of the wall. They glide over, tip their head as if confused by a flash of sea where no sea should be, and are gone. Even carrion birds know this place offers no corpses worth eating.

These are passersby. They make the mistake of thinking the water is a lake.

### The True Residents

**The pale flickers. And the water itself.**

If you lie flat on your stomach on the sharp eastern ledge — ignoring the way the rock bites your elbows, ignoring the deafening bass that now vibrates directly in your ribs — and stare down into the fifteen-foot lens where light goes strange and oily, you will eventually see them.

Not immediately. At first there is only churn: clear, cold water moving without wind, siltless despite its violence.

Then: a shape. Hand-sized, translucent to the point that you aren't sure you saw an edge at all, only a difference in refraction. No eyes. No obvious front or back. A thin membrane, like a jellyfish that forgot to become a jellyfish, like a smear of heat-haze *under* water.

There is only ever one visible at a time, which is how you know there are many. You fix on the one near the northern rock face and blink, and now it — or another — is pulsing near the bucket that spins forever without sinking.

They never surface. They never approach the wall. They live entirely within the turbulence, and more precisely, they *are* fed by the turbulence. Watch one hold station: it doesn't swim so much as unfold. A faint ribcage of something firmer inside the membrane catches the chaotic current and flexes. They bloom open when an eddy forms and snap gently shut when it collapses, harvesting momentum the way a filter-feeder harvests plankton.

They are not fish. Fish have direction. These have patience. They flicker in and out of phase with the water's own light, sometimes catching the sun and throwing a brief, rain-slick rainbow across their backs — oil-on-water color, precisely what you saw from above. Sometimes utterly invisible. If you lower a hand into the pool — the water will ache instantly to your wrist bones, a clean, sterile cold with that impossible far-ocean salt on it — they keep their distance, not out of fear but out of indifference. You are a warm, solid interruption in a place that is learning to be currents and tension.

And then there is the other occupant, the one that makes all the others possible:

**The wall.**

It is not alive the way an animal is alive. But it behaves with intention. It hangs. It shivers. It decides, moment to moment, to remain. Water molecules arrive from seep and snowmelt and condensation at the back of the bowl and instead of running downhill as water must, they join the lens, join the churn, join the sheer face that meets empty air and stops, taut as skin.

Currents race *up* it. Eddies breathe. When outside wind hits its outer face, it drums and bows inward but does not break, spraying the lichen and the pine and you with cold birthings. A bead detaches, hangs impossibly in mid-air like a planet, trembles, and snaps back as if on elastic. It produces sound without a source — that low thrumming that makes your sternum hum, those sudden glassy shrieks when the ripples go too fast.

It is too orderly to be chaos and too chaotic to be order.

If the translucent things are its fish, then it is their ocean. A single room of ocean that climbed a mountain to see if it could exist somewhere smaller than itself. The answer, so far, is yes.

### The Ghosts of Attempted Residence

And finally, what *almost* lived here.

Humans. Not residents. Pilgrims.

The dry-stack windbreak on the sheltered south side, waist-high, stones placed with care to blunt the spray so a sleeping bag might stay dry for a night. The fire ring with its damp charcoal that never quite dries because the mist never quite stops. The carvings: hand-deep wavy lines on the softer basalt, tally marks for swirls — someone sitting for hours, days, trying to map which way the wall turned, as if understanding could make it belong. The iron piton with the sinew-tied wooden toggle, rusted to the color of the lichen. The white plastic bucket caught in the pool's permanent gyre, spinning slowly, endlessly, never pulled under, never ejected, turning with the water's thoughts.

All scattered. All temporary. The kind of leavings made by people who came up the three-flat-stone cairns, felt the roar in their teeth, watched themselves reflected small and vertical in an impossible sea, understood that nothing erodes here because nothing is allowed to finish, left an offering of attention, and went back down before the cool soaked through their sleeves.

No one stays. To stay would be to learn to live like the lichen: to stop needing quiet, to stop needing soil, to learn to drink spray.

Nothing here has learned that yet, except the water that learned to stand up.
