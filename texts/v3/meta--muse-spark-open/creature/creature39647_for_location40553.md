They live here. Not on it, not beside it — *in* it.

### The People Who Learned to Hang

At first you think they are human and you are not wrong. Then you watch one move past you and you are not sure.

They are human the way a mangrove is still a tree. Same origin, different covenant with water.

You see her first — the figure who offered you the rope. She is not swimming toward you. No one swims here. To swim is to thrash is to waste is to die. She pulls, hand over hand, along a kelp line as thick as your forearm that thrums under her touch. She is ballasted with a net bag of smooth white tufa stones at her waist, just enough to make her neutral. Her hair is not floating free; it is cropped close or braided tight to the skull, crusted with fine green filaments, because loose hair catches and snags in the forest. Her skin is dark — not sun-dark, there is no real sun — but deep olive-brown, iron-stained from the water, almost green at the knuckles and knees. Her chest is out of proportion. Barrel-wide. When she pauses to look at you, you see her ribcage expand once, slowly, massively, and then not again for a long time.

They can do that. They can hold.

A child can hold for six, seven minutes without panic. A working woman for twelve. A stone-diver, after years of practice and probably some quiet selection that no one calls evolution, for twenty, twenty-five. Their blood is different now. More to bind the oxygen. Their hearts beat at forty when they are still. They move the way you move in a dream where hurrying is forbidden.

Watch her face. There is a faint, pale crease around her mouth and nose, like a tide line — the mask of those who duck often into the air-bells. The skin there stays a little drier, a little more human. The rest of them is always wet, always slightly swollen, pruning never quite setting in because the water is too warm. Their eyes are large and have a little extra lid, not quite a nictitating membrane, more a sheen. They squint often, not from light but from silt. Everyone here has a low, permanent case of green in the whites of their eyes.

They do not speak to you. Not yet. Speech is expensive.

#### How They Speak

When she wants you to follow, she does not call. She *pings* the rope.

Everything in the hanging city is connected by rope — living rope, kelp rope that grows if you feed it silt and trim it. A tug in the right cadence travels hundreds of meters through the lattice, house to house to house. Two quick pulls: *look*. Three slow: *come/need help*. A long sustained tremor, hand vibrating on the line: *danger, vent*. Children learn rope before they learn words. Elders can hold an entire conversation without opening their mouths, sending tremors down different lines with different hands, while they keep their faces submerged in work.

When they are close, within arm's reach under a breathing bell, they whisper. Their voices are hoarse, infrequent-use hoarse, like people who have been underwater too long. Which they have. The language itself has thinned to what can be said on one breath. Short sentences. Nouns that are also verbs. No shouting. You never raise your voice in a place where sound is eaten and air is made.

If you stay under long enough, you will hear them hum. Not to each other. To the ropes. To the houses. A low, chest hum that travels through wood and water better than words, that calms a child who has been down too long, that tells a kelp trunk which way to bend as it grows. The forest listens a little.

#### What They Do

No one does one thing here. You cannot afford specialists when everything requires slow attention.

But the city sorts itself by depth, and depth becomes role.

**Near the Light — The Tendrils and the Children:** At 8 to 12 meters down, where the olive-gold disc is still a disc and the leaf-canopies make their stained-glass of living green, the platforms are broadest and brightest. This is where the air is made. Children — eight, nine, ten years old, too small to dive deep and too buoyant to be trusted low — tend the lungs.

Each lung is a house-lung, a single monstrous leaf bladder, grown inverted, the size of a small boat. You saw it shivering. Inside it, the water weeps gas. All day the forest pearls oxygen, bubble by bubble, and the bubbles snag on hairs under the leaf and merge and rise into the bell. It can take eight, ten days to fill. The children brush the underside with soft brushes to keep tufa from sealing it, they sing-hum to keep shrimp from colonizing it, they measure its fullness by the tone it makes when tapped — a dull thud when empty, a bright glassy *pong* when ready.

This is a holy job, though they would not call it that. The child who tends a lung decides when a family breathes. A child can sentence their grandmother to an extra day of sipping water through their skin if they tap wrong.

The children themselves look different. Their eyes are huge, still adjusting to water. They move in bursts and have to be reminded to slow. They play a game where they exhale a perfect silver ring and try to swim through it before it breaks. The rings always break too fast in this thick water — which is the point. It teaches you not to waste.

**Midwater — The Weavers:** At 15 to 25 meters, where light is a general green and you cannot tell where the surface is, the women and the old men weave. There is no loom. The house *is* the loom.

Living kelp-wood pillars, still growing, are coaxed, bent, tied to each other with living cord. A wall is a root trained sideways. A floor is a mat of stitched lily pads that are still photosynthesizing under your feet; you can feel them warm. The weavers stand in toe-loops, hanging vertical, their lungs held, pulling filament — the green hair algae that grows in veils — into cord, into cloth, into net bags for stones. Their hands are webbed, not literally, but functionally: callus bridges have grown between thumb and forefinger from years of working wet fiber. They never cut with metal because there is no metal. They knap tufa: white, soft enough to shape, hard enough to slice filament if knapped fresh. A good knife lasts a month before the water dissolves its edge again.

Weavers know time by growth. "When the north pillar flowers" is next season. "Three trimmings ago" is last year. They hum constantly while they work — not songs, but single notes held until breath runs out — and the vibration keeps the filament from tangling.

**Low and Heavy — The Divers:** At 25 to 40 meters, where the green goes teal and then amber and the water is noticeably warmer, where the basalt is still ticking as it cools, the divers work.

They are almost all men, not by law but by lung. You need mass down here. You need to fight buoyancy. They wear net vests of stone, they oil their skin with fermented kelp grease so the iron bacteria doesn't eat them raw, and they sink. Slowly. It takes a diver ten minutes to fall thirty meters, arms out, not swimming, just descending, watching the silt curtains part around him.

His job is to pry. New rock is born daily, fragile white tufa terraces precipitating around a vent, black basalt pillows cracking open like bread. The diver finds a good piece — not too hot, the water around it shivers but doesn't scald — and wedges a tufa pick under it and levers, patiently, for minutes, until it snaps with that soft thunk that the water swallows. He bags it. He ascends slower than he fell, pausing every five meters to hang on a rope and let his blood fizz out. If he rises too fast, he gets the trembles. Everyone knows someone who rose too fast. They lie still for days after, joints fizzing, until the water takes them or doesn't.

Divers are scarred. Not cut-scars — burn-scars, pale branching lichens where a vent belched too close and the warm water became hot water for three heartbeats. They are respected the way roofers are respected: they keep the city from losing its anchors. Because everything is anchored. Every platform is moored to basalt with four, six, eight growing ropes. The mountain is still growing, you remember. A rope moored to what you thought was stable ground last year might this year be a meter higher, taut as a tendon, singing with strain. Divers are the ones who notice, who re-tie, who cut if a chimney starts to crack.

And the tenders who are not children, women, or divers: the **Bell-Keepers**. One per cluster of five or six houses. Old women mostly, lungs ruined from a lifetime of shallow breathing, who now never submerge without a bell. They remember when a particular lung burst, who drowned, who lived. They decide the breathing order when air is short. They will offer you, the stranger, the first breath, because hospitality here is air, and to withhold air is murder. They will watch you duck your head up into that trembling silver dome, they will watch your face break the membrane into gas, they will watch you gasp the green-iron-tasting air and weep without knowing why, and they will count your breaths.

#### What They Are Becoming

If you stay a month, you will see a birth. It happens in the lung.

The mother hangs beneath the bell, her head in air, her body in water. She does not scream — you cannot scream and hold air. She breathes, then bears down in water, then breathes again. The baby comes out not crying — there is no air to cry in — but writhing, eyes open, holding its breath by instinct for a terrifying long minute before the midwife scoops it up and lifts its face into the bell. Its first breath is not taken, it is *given*, pushed up into it. Some babies don't take it. Those are wrapped in white tufa cloth and tied with a stone and allowed to sink, very slowly, into one of the warm mud basins. The forest takes them. What is still, grows.

The old die differently. When they get too slow to make it to a bell in time, they simply stop going. They find a favorite lily mat, they lie back in it, they let the water do what it has always done — wear. Their families tie a cord around their wrist for a day, in case they change their mind. Most don't. The water is womb-warm. Dying warm is not the worst thing.

And around them, always, the other inhabitants that are not people but are still the city:

The forest itself — patient, stupidly lush, creaking, oxygen-pearling, spore-smoking, holding their houses up while it tries to smother them.

The rare animals — that single canoe-sized blind manta that passes over the city once a week like a moon, filtering nothing and everything, never acknowledged; the hand-sized glass shrimp that lives in the bell-hairs and cleans them and is therefore never eaten; the jelly bells that the children count to time their breath-holds: *one jelly pulse, two jelly pulse...*

And the mountain. Ticking. Pushing. Making new ground under their feet while they try to live on water held in the sky.

She is still waiting, the woman on the rope. Her chest rises once more, huge and slow. She pats the air-bell beside her house — *pong*, it is full — and with her other hand she makes the simplest tug on your rope: *come, breathe.*

You have been holding your breath since you arrived, without realizing.
