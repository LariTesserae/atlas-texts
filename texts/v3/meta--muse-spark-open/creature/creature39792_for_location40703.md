Nothing lives here.

That is the first true thing to understand about it, and you have to understand it slowly or you will lie to yourself. Not that everything died. Not that it was abandoned. That it never learned how.

There is no soil for a root to test, no rot for a worm to taste. No lichen has ever crusted the brick. No insect has ever found the warm puddles. The air is too hot and too wet and too new, and the floor is too occupied — every inch already claimed by the work. Life has no vacancy here.

And yet if you stand long enough on the low, laid plain — if you stop trying to walk toward the kiln-dome that will never get closer and just let the heat settle on your shoulders — you will realize you are not alone. Not because something has arrived. Because the place itself is inhabited by inhabitation without inhabitants.

What lives here are three things, and none of them are alive.

First, the Hands.

You will never see them. That is their nature. But they live here the way a habit lives in a house after the owner has left. Look at any stretch of floor and you will see how the dark, still-warm blocks were not extruded or poured but *placed*. Each one about as long as a body. Each one pitted. On the eastern edge of this one, a palm-print where it was shoved. On the western edge of that one, four finger-drag lines where mortar was smoothed by a hand in a hurry. On another, the shallow, confident score of an adze that had no business being that sharp.

Kneel — only for a second, the brick is still remembering the kiln — and you see the mortar between them has never set. Black, tacky. It strings if you touch it.

No tools are left behind. No bodies. No footprints except the ones you make and then find filled in behind you. But the work continues. That is what lives here: a labor without laborers.

You hear it before you understand it: that distant, elongated *thunk* of wood on stone that takes too long to cross the damp air. The creak not of weight but of the *idea* of weight settling. And when you turn your head back, there is a fresh handprint in the tar, steaming, the whorls of the thumb still perfect before the condensation beads and erases it.

They do not build toward anything. There is no wall rising, no city planned. From a thousand directions at once, the floor feathers out into wet, undone dark — raw courses thinning like knitting — and then fills in while you blink. A continent of tile shivers like cloth and resettles a foot to the left. Your trench is gone. Your path is now a channel of warm, clear water that has no source.

The makers live here as momentum lives in a flywheel. An endless, primitive, horizontal industry — hammerstone-logic, rope-drag, kiln-dome logic — building at a planetary rate with no architect and no rest. If you wait, you will feel them pass through you. Not as ghosts. As pressure. As the moment when the air shudders and another block thumps softly into place somewhere that is both inches and miles away, like a book closing in another room.

Second, the Almosts.

Because the world is so hot and so wet — water sweating out of the bricks themselves, beading on the underside of the low sky, running in half-inch-deep channels half a mile wide — life keeps *trying*.

It does not succeed. That is important. Nothing here has ever succeeded.

In a chisel-mark puddle that reflects a light with no sun, an orange-black film will bloom. Mineral, bacterial, fungal, none of those words quite fit. For an hour it will hold, a faint fur on the water, and you will think: colonization has begun. Then the puddle will be on the ceiling, or the channel will move, and it is gone without a corpse.

On the mortar, a slick will briefly hold the shape of a handprint that no one made — five fingers a little too long — and then dissolve back into damp sheen. In the air three feet off the floor, where personal clouds the size of rooms are born and drift at knee height and rain onto themselves, something like spores will try to clot, try to be weather, try to be alive, and then precipitate out as warm, tasteless water on your boots.

They are not life. They are rehearsals for life. Pre-life, or post-life's echo, testing the floor and finding it already full. If you watch them you will feel a strange pity, because they want so badly to stay, and the floor has been laid too fast for them to ever learn how to erode, how to soil, how to root.

They live here the way a dream lives for a minute after waking. Briefly. Perfectly. Doomed.

Third, the Floor itself.

You thought it was ground. It is not. Dig with a sharp stone, as others have before you — there are scrape-marks everywhere if you look — and you find no earth. Only another course of blocks beneath, damper and warmer. And beneath that, another. The world is floor all the way down. A planet-sized mosaic of dark, hand-laid brick with no bedrock and no core, only more making.

And the floor is not still. Quiet, yes — the silence here is budgeted, vast between sounds, so that your own breathing is too loud — but never still. That is the final inhabitant: the turbulence of a place that is hyperactive and quiet at once.

The floor lives. Not as an animal lives. As a construction site lives when no foreman is watching and the materials have learned to lay themselves.

Watch a low scaffold of lashed black timber that encloses nothing. Watch a wall three blocks high that has stood for a thousand years or a thousand seconds — erosion has not learned to happen yet, so you cannot tell. While you watch, distance will thin. The scaffold that was far off is suddenly behind you without you having turned. The wall is inside your shadow even though the light is sourceless and dim as perpetual dusk. You set two identical bricks side-by-side, you look away to check the hissing puddle at your feet, you look back and one brick is larger.

The floor breathes. Blocks lay themselves when unobserved. Districts ripple. New feathered edges bleed into being out of wet dark that was not there a moment ago. And always the heat presses down — low sky, low scaffolds, low world that has chosen horizontality as if verticality were forbidden — and always the water condenses and rises and falls without ever having rained.

You live here now, too, for as long as you stand on it. Not as a resident. As an interruption. Your boots make a temporary high place. Your puddles reflect a light you did not bring. Your shadow points the wrong way. The floor will grow by one more row toward an elsewhere that never arrives, and in a little while, after you have left or dissolved or been moved elsewhere by a fold in space that felt like blinking, there will be a fresh thumbprint in warm brick where your foot was, already beading with water, already beginning to fade.

Nothing lives here. And everything here is being lived *through* — by hands that never arrive, by almosts that never hold, by a floor that never stops laying itself down in the hot, wet dusk where physics has come loose at the seams.
