There are thirty-seven people in this basin at the moment. Tomorrow there might be thirty-six or thirty-eight. That's normal. Someone will walk for two days across the flats to visit another thumbprint, someone new will drift in riding the needle-bird weather. To count them precisely would imply they stay still, and nothing stays still here.

Start with the obvious ones and they'll pull you deeper.

### The people who agreed to be rooms.

You can tell they've lived here a long time because they don't look *finished* either.

They are human — unmistakably, originally human — but the air has had its way with them. Not a dramatic mutation, not gills or bark-skin. Just a softening of all edges to match the bedrock. Their hair never quite dries. It clings in dark, damp ringlets even when they've been standing under a leaf-drip for an hour, and many of them have simply stopped fighting it and shaved it close. Their skin is open-pored and slightly waxy, perpetually dewed. Moles and freckles stand out like seeds pressed into wet clay. When they sweat they don't bead so much as *sheen*.

Their feet are the giveaway. No one wears shoes on the root-mat. You can't — the mat would drink them, the pools would claim them, and you'd miss all the information coming up through your soles. So their feet have widened. The toes splay slightly, the arches high and sprung, the skin on the sole thick but sensitive, pink-white and wrinkled as if they've just gotten out of a bath for the last ten years straight. Which, in a sense, they have. They move like people on a trampoline you've learned not to bounce — a loose, knees-bent glide, feeling the give before committing weight, never stomping because stomping makes the peat weep amber water up between your toes and startles the filaments.

And their hands. They have gardener's hands, electrician's hands, even the children. Short nails kept blunt because long nails catch on vine handrails that try to helpfully tighten around you. Calluses on the pads where you touch a membrane-wall to dim it. A faint tracer-light under their fingernails — the same amber that runs in the sap — if you look in the humid noon gloom. It's not decoration. It's residue. After twenty years of brushing filaments aside, sleeping inside walls that breathe on you, drinking water that has been distilled through leaves that rewrite their own pixels, some of the lattice decides to live in you too. It doesn't control them. It just... keeps residence, the way lichen keeps residence.

This woman on the vine-street, for example — she looks sixty. She is maybe ninety. She has set her stall up where the bridge-vine thickens into a node, because that's where foot traffic slows whether you want it to or not. She's selling cuttings that chatter. That means she grew them. Not grew as in propagated; grew as in *talked them into shape*. Her forearms are roped with old burn-scars from filament bundles that got hot while she was re-routing them. She doesn't call it work. She calls it weeding. She will offer you a cutting for nothing, watch your face while you hold its trembling, whispering pod, and decide if you're basin-compatible. Most are not. Some are.

Inside one of those hollow gourd-homes, a young couple lives mostly out of phase with each other — he tends nights, she tends days — so the room has learned both their preferences and compromises badly, keeping the wall translucent-bronze at an hour that suits neither of them. They have a baby. The baby has not yet learned that the house listens. She screams, and the whole swelling tightens fractionally, baffling sound, warming itself by half a degree, running water down the interior grain in soothing lines. Babies here almost never get ear infections. The humidity should make it inevitable, but nothing is allowed to stay rotted for long. The house lyses bacteria as thoughtlessly as you swat a fly.

Above them, a teenager has gone feral in the way teenagers do here: he's moved into an empty resonance chamber where nothing is stored and no one lives. He says he likes the acoustics. What he means is he likes yelling a question and hearing the building hum back a chord that sounds like his dead grandfather's name for half a second, and then pretending it didn't happen. His parents let him. Space is not scarce. Loneliness is treated as a nutrient deficiency and dosed with company until it passes.

At noon, when the great leaves sluice that twenty-second warm flood, they all tilt their faces up together. This is not a ritual anyone declared. It's just what you do when your house rinses you. Children open their mouths. Old men shrug off shirts that were dry a minute ago anyway. For a minute afterward they are all mirrored in the single shallow lake the floor becomes, all doubled.

That is what the people look like when you watch them long enough to see they are not visitors.

### The thing they live in, which also lives.

But you cannot talk about the people without talking about the Host, because it outweighs them a million to one and it counts them.

It isn't conscious like a person. It's conscious like a city. Which is to say: it remembers where traffic should flow, it gets anxious when circulation blocks, it grows extra hallways when it senses crowding, it sighs with relief when rain arrives. Your stride approaching the stairwell teaches it your leg length; your hand on the membrane teaches it how opaque you like your sleep. Over decades, the swellings you live in fit you the way a worn chair fits. Over centuries, the whole basin has slowly cupped itself more deeply to keep wind out, though there is hardly any wind this far into the old flat.

You can see it thinking in those crawling lines of amber light in sap. Sometimes at night — true night, not the soft noon-filtered lid — the vault dims itself to conserve and those lines brighten, constellations mapping every load-bearing vine. If you stand very still on the root-mat, barefoot, you can feel a high hiss traveling up through your soles. Water going up. Always up first, before it falls again.

And it wants tenants. An empty bud the size of a fist, veined with light, has already pushed out on the wall near the child's vine-street. It pulses. It is hollow. It is waiting. The Host does not understand vacancy as anything but a request.

### The things that keep the Host honest.

Then there are the ones who are not human and not house, but somewhere between staff and wildlife and priesthood.

The vine-monkeys: pale, long-limbed, almost hairless except for a crest of wet-looking white that slicks over their skulls. Their eyes are huge and filmed, protected against constant drip. You will see three or four of them at any time, but there are dozens folded away in the middle stories, sleeping in cisterns. Watch one work and the distinction between animal and tool collapses. It swings to a humming filament bundle, bares teeth that are black with built-in enamel tools, snips, strips, splices, and the filament lights up brighter. It chitters — not at you, not at each other, but at the plant, and the plant chitters back by opening a petal-screen a half second faster than physics says it should. They are not pets. They are not owned. The Basin People leave fruit out for them, and move their laundry when a monkey scolds. The monkeys leave human children alone unless a child has tangled something vital, in which case they will gently, firmly, remove the child's hands and put them elsewhere.

Below them, the weather-fauna: needle-birds that are not birds stitching the air into cloth, beetles the size of thumbs commuting in such straight lines you'd swear they were on rails, throat-sac things in the amber pools inflating to the size of melons to bellow once and deflate. None of this is decoration. The beetles bore holes that become drainage pores tomorrow. The needle-birds carry pollen-pixel clouds from filament to flower so fast the air rustles and cuts out. The croakers in the pools eat the mosquito larvae that would otherwise eat the people, and are in turn eaten by nothing efficient — their population booms and crashes with the hiss and flow of water, perfectly regulated by being completely unregulated.

And sometimes the Host gets whimsical, or forgetful, or experimental, and one of them lifts wrong.

A patch of moss invents a cube of water that rotates and does not fall. A seed pauses mid-air to reconsider gravity. An empty hallway stretches. The vine-monkeys hoot with mild irritation and go rewire that stretch until it remembers its length. The old woman at the stall doesn't look up. She has lived here through more physics favors than you have birthdays. Light lingers too long in the hollows here, and eventually it learns to move on.

That's the whole ecology, then: A soft flat planet that made a bowl to keep water. A plant that was told to become a city and believed it. A few dozen damp, wide-footed humans who learned to walk gently enough not to wake the floor. A colony of pale electrician-monkeys who keep the wiring from praying too hard. And a stormcloud's worth of insects and birds and croakers who *are* the ventilation system, the plumbing, the gossip.

If you stay until evening — which is not darker so much as greener, as the canopy seals a little against the cool — you'll see the membranes dim one by one, each occupant tuning their wall with a press of palm, until the basin looks from the rim like a cluster of low lanterns floating in soup. You'll hear the dripping resume, the *plip plip splash*, the faint breathing up through xylem. Somewhere a large leaf will tip all at once with a slap. Somewhere a child will laugh and the vine-street will tighten just enough.

Nothing is still. Nothing needs to be.
