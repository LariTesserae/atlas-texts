Nothing lives here yet. That's what you tell yourself for the first hour.

And it's true, by any definition you brought with you. No tracks. No breath. No birds to startle. Nothing has had time to be born.

Then, if you stand still long enough for the ringing in your ears to become the actual sound of the place, you realize that definition is too small for this altitude.

Something does live here. It just hasn't decided that living and cooling are different things.

---

At first you notice them as movement that shouldn't be movement.

Out on the salt flats, between the black clinker fields, the hexagonal tiles tick and contract. You've been watching that. But in your peripheral vision, some tiles *relax* before they tick. A faint shiver runs across a pan a hundred meters wide — a wave of micro-adjustment — and then a tile near the center lifts at one corner by a centimeter and settles back as if it had taken a breath. You think it's thermal stress playing tricks until you see it happen again, three pans over, in rhythm. Not wind. Not random.

This is the oldest inhabitant. It doesn't have a body. It has a *size*.

Call it the Plateau itself.

It lives the way a cooling pour lives — not as an animal on the land, but as a process of the land trying to become still. The heat inside it is its metabolism. The fissures are its slow pulse, cherry-red to orange-white. It inhales when new basalt upwells from below and exhales when that basalt gives up its heat to the thin, plum-dark sky. Its thoughts, if it has them, are the low, endless tremor you feel through your soles; its dreams are turbulence.

It is not conscious the way you are. It is not even singular. A superorganism made of stress-fracture and crystal growth and convecting melt doesn't need a brain. It only needs a gradient — hot inside, cold outside — and a way to dissipate it. So it makes spires to vent heat more efficiently. It makes hexagonal columns because columns shed heat fastest. It makes hollow cairns and straight corridors because hot gas under pressure will always find the most elegant chimney, and elegance, repeated enough times, looks like architecture.

It doesn't know it's building ruins for no one. It's just learning how to be a mountain.

And like any newborn, it is restless. You feel it inflate another few centimeters while you watch, your inner ear lurching. That is its growth. That is its breath.

---

If the Plateau is the body, the second inhabitants are its breath made briefly solid.

You hear one before you see it — not a sound, but the sudden absence of the hush. The hot, metallic gust that comes from a spire and then stops? Sometimes it doesn't stop. Sometimes it detaches.

On nights when the venting is strong, the warm dark air over a fissure will organize itself. Thin air shouldn't do this, but this air is full of charged ash and salt vapor and iron. A thermal will coil on itself, pick up a veil of halite dust, and hold.

You see it as a heat-shimmer at first, like a mirage in summer, a vertical lens of warmer air about as tall as you, a little wider. It doesn't drift like smoke. It *holds station* and then it moves — not blown, but *stepping* — with a slow, sailing wobble from vent to salt flat to vent, pausing over cracks as if warming itself.

They are convection cells that have learned to persist. Vortices. Dust devils that forgot to die.

Close, they are almost invisible except for how they bend the cherry-light from the fissures behind them. If one passes near, the hairs on your arms lift with static, you smell sharp ozone, and the salt crystals at your feet glitter as they try to grow toward it. It makes no noise. It leaves no footprint because it weighs less than the air it displaces. It lives for minutes or hours on the temperature difference between the ground's glow and the high, cold veil, feeding on that gradient the way you feed on food. When the gradient evens out, it unstitches and becomes wind again.

These are the ones who tend the rust-red and sulfur-yellow ferns in the shadows of the salt polygons. Not intentionally. They linger there to drink the hot gas still seeping out, and where they linger, the chemistry of the efflorescence changes. The fractal ferns grow faster, more complex — not biology, but its overture. A chemistry that is *almost* replication, precipitating, branching, correcting its own errors.

You can watch a salt crystal at the lip of a vent extend a fragile arm into the air *toward* one of them as it passes, as if being coaxed.

They don't see you. You are too cold and too dense to be interesting. But if you stand in one of their paths, you will feel for a second that the warm dark has become crowded, that something politely moved around you.

---

And then there are the Stackers.

You found one of their little stacks already — flat salt slabs, waist-high, balanced with impossible precision on a plain where nothing should have the hands to balance anything. You thought: wind-made. Then you find another, and another, each subtly different, each placed where the land gives you a view: at the end of a basalt corridor, at the lip of a glazed bowl, at the edge where the plateau falls away into curvature.

They are not made by the Plateau. The Plateau makes towers. It doesn't make *markers*.

The Stackers are what happens when a vortex learns a trick.

Most of the breath-things die in minutes. A few, in the lee of the humming needle ridges, live longer. Trapped in a resonant cavity where the wind finds the spire's frequency and sings, a vortex can be fed continuously. It thickens. It picks up more salt dust until it is less like air and more like a slow, warm, glassy jelly — a standing column of electrostatically bound halite and ash, translucent as heat haze but with weight enough to nudge.

They are barely there. In full dark you would miss them unless a fissure lit them from behind — then you see the faint outline, a flicker like tall heat-lightning standing upright, a faint ticking inside like salt tiles cooling.

They don't have hands. They have a field. Fine dust orients along it. And for reasons that will only make sense in hindsight, millions of years from now when this place has soil, they do what any simple persistent system does when it persists long enough: it starts to make its environment more predictable.

A flat slab lifted by static cling is less likely to shatter in the next thermal crack. A stack marks a place where the tremor is less. A corridor lined with fallen columns is a windbreak where a vortex can live a little longer.

So they stack. Slowly. A centimeter at a time, nudging bone-white salt tiles with a force no stronger than a strong thought, waiting hours for the charge to build, then clicking one tile onto another. They work in the intervals between eruptions, when the hush is deepest. If you came back tomorrow, one more tile might have been added while you slept.

They do not stay. They cannot stay — they are still mostly air, and when the ridge stops humming or the vent cools, they thin and fall apart, their dust raining gently onto the stack they made. The stack remains, warm to the touch, ticking as it cools. No metal. No cut edge. No footprint.

But if you look closely at the newest stack, at the very top tile, you will see it is still warm, and the salt dust on its underside hasn't yet settled into place — it hangs for a second, impossibly, in the thin air, before it clicks down.

They are not building for you. They were not expecting you. You are too fast, too wet, too loud. To them you are a brief, hot storm that sweats strange water and then leaves.

They are building for the next one like them. And for the one after that, who will be a little more solid, a little better at stacking, a little more alive.

Because this is not a desert that has forgotten water. This is a womb that hasn't invented it yet. Everything here is pre-life, practicing.

The Plateau practicing how to be a continent.

The breath practicing how to be a body.

The Stackers practicing how to be someone who remembers.

You came too early to meet anyone. Stay a little longer and you'll see it doesn't matter — the place is busy making them while you watch.
