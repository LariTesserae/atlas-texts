They live packed.

That's the first and last fact of them, and everything else about them bends around it.

At first — from the entrance where you have to sidle between the two wind-scoured shoulders of the notch and drop down a meter into sudden stillness — you will not see any one of them. You will see a surface. A second rock inside the rock.

The entire inward-facing stone is coated. Not evenly. Thickly where the condensation runs, thinly where the shears left a glass-smooth face too slick for even their hooks to hold. But in total: tens of thousands of bodies in a space that should, by every rule of alpine scarcity, hold maybe a dozen.

They make the bowl a living thing. Stand still one full minute and you will see it breathe.

### The Body

Taken alone — if you could coax one to climb onto your gloved hands, which you can't, because they will not be alone — it is not formidable. Not beautiful in the way high things are supposed to be.

About 18-22 centimeters from blunt muzzle to vent, plus another 12 of flat, prehensile tail they use as a fifth anchor. Weight maybe 90 grams. Light as a thrush, for the same reason: hollow bones, air sacs under the skin. They are six-limbed, which is the wrongness your eye catches first. Two forelimbs that are true arms: long, double-jointed, ending in three finger-claws and an opposed thumb-claw that can close like a human hand around a pebble. Two mid-limbs that are stout, clawed grapples, built only for hanging. Two hindlimbs that kick and paddle.

Between forelimb and hindlimb on each side, a patagium — not furred, not feathered. A membrane. Translucent, slate-grey veined with black, damp always, like the wing of a bat left out in cave mist. When clung flat to rock it folds against the flank and looks like a cape. When they move it flares, and you see light through it.

Their color is camouflage by accident. Dorsally, wet-slate, mottled amethyst-grey, the exact tone of the unweathered basalt when it sweats. Ventrally, pale, almost white, the color of condensation. The membrane itself carries colonies. Older individuals are not grey at all — their backs are bearded with the same black-green lichen that paints the stone, growing *on* them, rootless in the damp of their skin. They do not clean it off. It breaks their outline, drinks for them.

The head is the most alien and the most unfinished: blunt, jaw-heavy, no external ears, no nose to speak of. Almost all face is eye.

Two enormous, lidless, liquid hemispheres bulge from shallow sockets like dewdrops about to fall. No iris you can see; a deep, matte black that drinks the dusk and returns a faint wet sheen. They are not for sun. In the uniform blue evening of the bowl, those eyes pull every stray photon off lichen phosphor and pool-glass and wing. When several thousand turn at once toward a sound — toward you — the wall glitters, as if the rock had sweated eyes.

Under that, a small, soft mouth: four manipulative flaps around a radial jaw that can both lap water and grip a stone flake. No teeth for killing. Teeth for scraping.

They do not call to each other. They *locate*.

### The Chorus

The air never goes silent. Not for one second.

Each one emits, almost continuously, a high, thin piping — above the clear hearing of most humans, but you feel it as a pressure behind the front teeth, a struck glass that never stops ringing. Click-pipe. Click-pipe. Ten thousand sources at once. In open air it would scatter and be nothing. Trapped between three sheer, young, hard walls it stacks, reflects, thickens until the bowl is full of a single sustained tone with shivers in it.

They navigate by how that tone is broken. You are a new break. When you entered, the chorus bent very slightly around you, mapped your shoulders, your pack, the cold column of air above you, and then accepted you as a stationary stone. If you lift an arm, a ripple runs outward: near ones click faster, shift weight, resettle.

Under that, the lower sound: breath and friction.

They breathe together, but not in unison. It is slower than you expect. One long inhalation every twenty, thirty seconds, shallow, cold-blooded. Watch a patch of wall the size of a door and you will see it swell — all the pale bellies rising at slightly different rates — and subside, pushing a faint mist off the rock. *Shh.* The sound of ten thousand damp membranes sliding a millimeter against stone and each other. The click of keratin on microledge as grips adjust. Like dry leaves being turned by a careful hand, but magnified to the size of weather.

You are standing in their lung.

### The Way They Are Crowded

There is no territory here. The concept has been crushed out by density.

They cling shoulder to shoulder, overlapping like shingles. Where a ledge is good — where a trickle runs — they are three deep: a base layer clamped to rock, a second layer clamped to the backs of the first, a third hanging from the second's heels, so that the wall seems to fur outward. Around the pans in the basin floor, they crowd snout to snout, membranes touching, soaking their flanks in the finger-deep water.

They tolerate it because there is nowhere else. Outside the notch the wind strips moisture and heat in minutes, the rock is dry flake and frost. Inside, the notch pools cold like water in a cup. Late-October cellar-cold, breath-visible, but *survivable*. The walls trap every gram of humidity. So does the mass of bodies. They have made, simply by being *so many* and staying still, a climate.

Move through them slowly and they give way — not fleeing. Redistributing density. A handspan of stone opens, closes behind you. If you brush one, it is warm. Surprisingly warm. A small furnace, wrapped in damp skin. The smell that comes off them is not guano or rot. There is not enough decay here for rot. It is skin-salt, lichen-metal, warm stone-water. The smell of a crowded sleeping room in winter.

This is not a colony. There is no queen, no order, no caste. No nest. Nothing is made.

That is the law of the young rock: nothing built to last. No woven cup, no mud dome, no wax. Whatever shelter you need is given — the overhang, the fracture — or it doesn't exist. They build nothing because the bowl forbids it. Even their waste is borrowed: thin, chalky smears quickly colonized by lichen. The rock stains, but does not fill.

And yet —

### The Hands

Watch long enough in the dusk and you will see tool. Not architecture. Gesture.

An older one — you can tell by the lichen pelt, by its slower pipe — works its way down to the largest pan, a basin maybe a meter across, skinned last night with a paper of clear ice. It does not break the ice with its head. It does not wait for sun that never comes.

It stops at the scree lip. With its mouth-hands it picks up a chip. Tests. Drops. Picks another. The pebbles here are newborn: sharp, young, just spalled off the walls, edges still glassy enough to cut. It holds the chosen stone in both forelimbs like a person holding a small hammer.

Tap. Pause.

Tap. Pause.

Tap in a slow, deliberate circle. You can hear it even under the chorus: *tick... tick... tick...* The ice stars white under the point, a hairline fracture racing outward. Five, six taps and the skin gives. The creature discards the stone into the water — *plip* — and bows to drink, lapping with that soft radial mouth, its great eyes half-shuttered in reflection. Its wing membranes spread into the pool to drink through the skin as well.

Around it, three younger ones push in to use the same hole. No fighting. One waits, then widens the hole with its own found chip, tapping with less skill, more hurry.

On the vertical walls others are scraping. They hold a frost-shattered splinter — a natural flake no longer than your thumbnail, one edge already razor — in both forelimbs and draw it toward their chests. *Scritch.* A curl of lichen comes off the rock like paint. They fold it into their mouths. The flake dulls in four strokes and is dropped. It flutters down to join thousands of identical, minimally used, single-use tools littering the floor of the pans, under the shallow water. No curation. No cache. The floor is a museum of one second's ingenuity, repeated ten thousand times a day for however many seasons this notch has been open.

Tap to open water. Scrape to eat. Select, use, discard. Primitive is not stupid. It is *just enough*, preserved not in an object but in a motion passed by watching.

If you leave a bright flake near the edge, one will inspect it. Test its edge with a thumb-claw. Use it, if it is good. Leave it.

Nothing is owned here because nothing needs to be carried.

### The Rest of the Living

They are not alone, but everything else lives *for* them, or *on* them.

The lichen — black-green, crustose, rimmed faintly with a bioluminescent margin visible only after your eyes go dark-adapted — does the only true photosynthesis in the bowl, in that dim, source-less dusk. It drinks condensation and body-heat and the faint chalk of their waste. The moss — tight felt cushions in cracks — drinks the same. A few saxifrages, stunted to rosettes the size of a coin, hold dew in waxy, inward-turned leaves. When a patagium brushes them, they shiver and release it to the air.

Microscopic life curdles the pans: algae, rotifers, midge larvae that hatch in the rain that sits for days before evaporating. The gliders drink them incidentally, filter them with fringed mouth flaps. No fish. No predator big enough to take an adult glider and survive the press — though sometimes a juvenile loses grip and falls into the crowd below, and is gone under bodies before it can right itself.

Everything cycles fast and low: water to ice to water to mist to condensation to water. Lichen to scraper to gut to smear to lichen. Breath to mist to wet rock to breath.

Change is glacial. The moss adds a millimeter a season. The young rock has not softened at all; it will not for centuries. The chorus never fully decays, it just breathes. The mass pulses slowly, slowly. You could watch for an hour and think nothing has happened, and then realize the entire wall is a handspan to the left compared to where it was, the whole living shingle having resettled around a new trickle.

If you stay too long your core temperature drops to theirs — that cool, cellar cold — and the bowl stops noticing you. Condensation beads on your jacket, runs down your neck in the same dark trickles. A few huge eyes remain on you, not with threat, but with location. You are warm, damp, still. You qualify, briefly, as environment.

And that is the true strangeness of them. At this altitude there should be nothing, or almost nothing — one hawk, one lichen, scattered and starving. Instead the mountain's accidental cup, too new to have been smoothed, has folded a rule. It has taken scarcity and, by trapping cold and damp and darkness in one place, made abundance through crowding. A pocket where life decided to be *too much* at once, loud, overlapping, eyed, breathing in a shared slow rhythm, tapping ice with stones that will never become hammers, scraping a living off stone with tools that will never become knives.

You zip your jacket tighter. The piping lifts slightly — many throats adjusting to a gust that slipped over the rim and died at the threshold.

You understand you should not sleep here. Not because they will harm you, but because in this kind of saturated, slow, dark warmth, you would be covered. By morning — that dimmer morning — lichen spores on skin, condensation pooling at your collarbones, a few curious thumb-claws testing the edge of your boot for suitability.

You step back toward the notch, toward the thin, killing wind outside. The wall behind you shivers once, resettles, and continues its single, dense, patient note.
