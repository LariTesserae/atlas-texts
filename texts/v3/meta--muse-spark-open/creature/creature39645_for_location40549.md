Nothing with a face lives there.

That is the first answer, and it feels wrong — because the room is so warm, so wet, so bright, your animal brain insists something should be swimming toward you through that milky gold. But it doesn't.

You look again, slower, letting the roar settle into layers, and that first impression refines instead of reversing.

Nothing *large* lives there. Nothing that has made a decision to live there.

Give it a minute to understand why.

### The Exclusion

This little ocean has too many rules to be kind to bodies.

It's too new. The limestone blocks still taste strongly of themselves — the water is hard with dissolved lime, chalk-pale with stone dust that never settles because the central rope of falling water never stops hitting. That makes it alkaline and poor. You can feel it on your skin after a few minutes: not slimy, but squeaky-clean, a little astringent, like water from a freshly mortared well. Snails wouldn't make shell here without fighting. Frog spawn would dissolve at the edges.

It's too loud and too fast. Anything that breathes air has to surface in that pounding column; anything that breathes water has to cling while the whole volume turns over, pulled down by the fall and sucked sideways toward the little arched mouth of the sluice at the far end. There are no still corners, no leaf-litter eddies, no undersides to hide under — you saw yourself, the floor is fitted slabs without a gap for a knife, the walls are flushed and new, their mortar still soft. The engineers made sure there was nowhere to hold on, because stillness is rot and they did not build this place to rot. A fish released here would exhaust itself in an hour trying to find slack water and then be taken, confused, out through the overflow. Tadpoles, water-beetle larvae, even mosquito wrigglers can't complete any life that needs a week of quiet. The system flushes them like a throat clearing.

It's too bright in a way that leaves no dusk. For six hours a day those three spears go straight down through the water to the bottom and turn it all over-exposed. There's no shadow to hide a hunt in. No soft twilight shelf for lilies. And at night — when those shafts become three perfect squares of outer dark — it's absolute black, sudden and total. No moon reaches in at an angle for long. That's a hard regime to photosynthesize around if you're big, and a terrifying one if you navigate by sight.

So: no fish. No frogs despite the chorus your ears invent in the echo. No newts, no nesting birds, no bats — it's too wet for bats, the spray soaks their wings — no crayfish. If you came back for a month and watched at noon and midnight you would not see a backbone break the surface except your own.

What lives there has learned to be smaller than the rules.

### The Occupants

Start at the very edge, where air and stone and water argue out a treaty line.

In the mortar joints just above the present water level — in that dark tide-ring where splash keeps the lime perpetually damp but never submerged — there is a skin. From a distance you thought it was shadow. Up close, with your ear ringing and your fingertips pruned, it's a black-green felt, thin as paper, matte except where a bead of water swells on it and catches the pillar-light.

It's mostly cyanobacteria, with flecks of true algae tangled in. *Pleurocapsa*, *Gloeocapsa*, the pioneers that love fresh plaster and travertine and public fountains. They don't need soil. They eat light and the calcium carbonate leaching out of the new mortar, and they return a faint, mineral sourness to the water. They are the reason the chamber smells faintly clean, like a cave chapel. In places the film has thickened into tiny domes, tiny blistered stromatolites no bigger than lentils. If you left the cistern alone for ten years, they would build a warty rim all around it, a bathtub ring an inch thick. Right now they are just a beard, patiently trying to turn this made room into a natural one.

Above that ring, but not much above, are the second citizens.

Levered into the failed chisel-marks — those dark little crescents where the apprentice's hand jumped — and especially along the lips and spray-shadow of the low overflow arch, are ferns. Three clumps, maybe five if you count two sad singles. Maidenhair-type: wiry black stems, fronds the size of your thumbnail, a pale, almost translucent green because they've grown entirely in reflected violence. They never get direct sun; they get bounced, water-scattered sun and the constant tremor of the draft that is dragged down the shafts by the falling curtain.

They shouldn't have found their way here, and that's how you know how spartan the address is: only the lightest spores on the wind, fern dust that fell thirty feet down those square wells by chance, made it. Their roots are absurd — white, hair-thin, no thicker than thread — and they do not root in earth because there is none. They root in lime dust and trapped moisture in a tool defect. When the central column hits hard, the air pressure in the whole vault puffs, and you can watch the fronds shutter and shiver in unison. They are ecstatic and starved. They drink spray.

That's it, for the permanent residents. Film and frond. Rock-eaters and air-ferns.

Everything else is a visitor that didn't survive.

If you crouch in the water up to your chin and stare at a light-block on the wall until your eyes adjust, you might see the other life, the life that's trying to move in and being told *not yet*:

A single, drowned desert beetle spinning slowly in the slow gyre near the sluice, legs tucked, shiny — fallen through the open shaft this morning, unable to find purchase.

A needle of pine duff imported on the water from whatever catchment feeds the rope-fall above, turning and turning.

Countless motes in the beam that aren't dust but protists — paramecia, euglenoids — too small to see as individuals, making the water look a little thicker than it should. They live for hours in the warmth before they are pulled out the throat at the far end. The water is alive in the way any warm, lit water outdoors becomes briefly alive, with a bloom that is born and exiled in the same afternoon.

And, once a week maybe, a human.

Because this cleanliness is not an accident. Someone has to keep that floor visible. Someone climbs down those iron spikes with a stiff brush and a bucket and scrubs the beginning rim of biofilm off the submerged slabs, repoints a soft bit of mortar, checks that the sluice lip hasn't chipped. You can see his work in the faint, parallel scratchings under water, cleaner lines against the filmed stone. He doesn't live here either. No one sleeps in a throat. He comes down, works in that impossible drummer's noise until his ears are dull, and climbs back up toward ordinary dry ground before the slanting bar of late afternoon paints the far wall amber.

Give the place fifty years without him and it will be different. The lime will cure, the water will soften, cracks will widen enough for blind isopods to ride in on floods, the ferns will throw a green shag down half the vault, swallows will learn to dive through the shafts to drink on the wing and a colony of little bent-toed geckos will come to hunt moths stunned by the light columns at noon. It wants to become a cenote, an oasis well, an ecosystem.

Right now it is still too young, too intentioned, too much *made*. Its only long-term inhabitants are the ones that can live on stone and light and noise alone.

If you press your back to the wall and float, as the instructions seem to invite you to, you will feel the strangest thing of all: you are the biggest thing that has ever lived in here, and the room is holding you quite easily, warm and deafeningly loud, waiting with complete indifference for you to leave so the film and the ferns can go on trying to inherit it.
