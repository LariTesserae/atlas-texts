They live quietly. That's the first thing to understand about them.

You won't see them at first. You'll stand in that central square at noon, empty as you were told it would be, with your footsteps and the tick of the trough and you will think the town is abandoned. It is so compact, so stone-colored, so perfectly still, that a person standing in a doorway is just another shadow between stones.

Then the shadow will move. Or a door you thought was shut will not have been shut. Or you will hear the thin cough of a chicken from a yard that looked swept clean and empty.

They don't gather. They don't come out to see you. There is no bustle to join. You have to learn to notice them one by one, the way you learn to notice pikas — not by looking for the animal but by waiting until the rock reveals it was never just a rock.

There are about eighty of them. There have always been about eighty of them.

Lowlanders would call them villagers. Peasants. Mountaineers. None of those words fit quite right, because all of those words imply a kind of struggle against the mountain, and these people are not struggling. They have come to an agreement with it.

They are short, for the most part. Not stunted — compact. Wide through the shoulders and hips, low center of gravity, legs like the stilt-stones under their granaries. The kind of bodies you get when for twenty generations no one has had to reach for anything, only carry. Their skin is the color of dry riverbeds, reddened at the cheeks and knuckles by the wind and then faded again. Their hair goes grey early and then stays grey for a long time. Their eyes are pale from squinting against honest light.

Old and young look disconcertingly alike until you are close enough to see hands.

The old have the hands of the town itself — knuckled, spotted, nails packed with stone dust that will not wash out. They move slowly, not from frailty but from economy. They will spend an entire morning fitting a single replacement slate, tapping it, listening, tapping again, shaving a corner with an iron that has been reworked a hundred times. They never appear to be working hard.

The young — there are very few — have the same quiet. Children here do not shout or run in packs. A lowlander child would think them sad. They are not sad. They are listening. A boy of seven will sit for an hour beside a cistern with his ear to the stone, learning the difference between the sound of full and the sound of enough. A girl of nine will be given a pile of fallen wall-stone and told to make it stand without mortar, and when it falls she will be told to make it stand again, not as punishment but as grammar. Their games are maintenance: who can sweep a yard so the wind leaves no dust, who can balance a slate on another slate, who can be quiet the longest in the tower while the goats move below.

Because that is what they do. They maintain.

There is no mayor here. No lord. No priest in the lowland sense. There are only keepers, and the keepings have worn themselves into four or five old bodies who remember how.

The Water Keeper is almost always a woman past childbearing. She is the one who lifts the wooden lid off the black spring every dawn before light and looks at it — not to see if it is there, it is always there — but to see its mood. She listens to the tick of the cliff-troughs. She knows which barrel under which roof will be sour by autumn and must be scrubbed with juniper ash. She decides, when the sideways rain comes, which cistern gets opened first and which stays shut, so that no single house takes too much and no single house takes too little. The children think she talks to the water. What she actually does is quieter: she assures it that it is still caught, still clean, still needed.

The Stone Keeper is an old man or sometimes two old men who are brothers without ever saying so. Their job is not to build. Building is finished. Their job is to notice the almost-invisible lean. The hairline crack in the third tower that has been a hairline crack for thirty years and must remain a hairline crack for thirty more. When a step's polished bowl grows too deep and threatens to hold ice, they will chisel it flatter by a millimeter and then polish it again with river sand carried up from the valley once, decades ago, and kept in a jar. They keep the Pile — the stack of replacement tiles cut a hundred years ago — counted. When one is used, one is cut, not because they need it now but because the count must remain.

The Goat Keeper is younger, because he must climb. He watches from the low towers, not for enemies — there have never been enemies who wanted this place badly enough to climb for it — but for weather and for straying. He knows all eleven goats by the pattern of their bells, which are tiny and iron and never left to rust. He knows which juniper scrub they may strip and which must be left for berries. He speaks least of all.

And then there are the others, whose titles have been forgotten but whose functions remain: the woman who tends the communal kiln though it is fired only four times a year and always yields the same number of pots, one of which will crack and be ground for grit; the man who mends everything iron until the iron is too tired and must be folded into something smaller; the grandmothers who keep the stunted willow pollarded into its fist and gather its whips for baskets; the mothers who keep barley.

None of them are busy in the way you understand busy. Their days are full of small, exact repetitions. Sweep. Listen. Mend. Replace the tile. Sweep again. It would look like poverty to a lowlander, or stagnation. It is neither. It is practice. It is prayer without a god.

Because here is what living in equilibrium does to a person:

You stop wanting more.

Not because you have been taught to be humble, but because more would break the fit. Another goat would overcrop the thin terrace. Another child would require another cistern. Another house would block the gutter that feeds two others. They have seen, in the dry-fit of their walls, what happens when you force a stone where it does not want to go: the whole wall remembers and leans, years later, all at once.

So they self-regulate the way the mountain self-regulates. Old people joke — as you were told — that when someone dies, someone is born, neatly, on time. It is not quite a joke. The women know the herbs that prevent and the herbs that permit, and the couples wait. Not from cruelty. From courtesy. A death is mourned for exactly as long as it takes for the water in that house's cistern to be redistributed, and then a birth is welcomed because now there is room for breath again. No one calls this law. If you asked them why they do it, they would look confused, the way you would if someone asked why you don't inhale and exhale at the same time.

That is why the town feels so empty. Those bare yards and vacant pens and threshing floors with nothing on them are not ruins. They are lungs. They are the spaces left deliberate between presences, so the wind can find its corners and make its low note, so a person can stand in an alley and hear only their own footsteps and know they are not crowded. The town was not built for more people than live here now. It was built for exactly these people, with room for their absence as precisely as for their presence.

Even the animals live by this sparse logic. The few goats are not pets and not quite livestock; they are co-tenants who give milk and take scrub and understand quiet. The chickens are hardy and small and lay sparingly, as if they too have agreed not to overproduce. The pikas and hawks and foxes are not theirs. The wild does not press in and does not need to be driven out. It simply continues, sparse and widely spaced like the pines on the talus — one hawk, one fox-cry a month, enough.

In winter, when the dry snow comes and sits for months, they live even quieter. They close internal shutters, burn juniper that snaps and smells sharp, tell no new stories because the old ones still fit perfectly: how the western cliff was pink this morning, as it is every morning; how the spring held; how the Pile is still counted.

If you stay long enough, you will start to see what lives here beyond the people. Not ghosts — the place is too complete for ghosts. What lives here is a kind of attention.

The town itself is attentive. The alleys listen. The cisterns echo when you drop a pebble, not to be pretty but to tell you how much is left. The slate roofs hold down the wind. The bare yards wait to be swept. It is a human knot, yes, but the knot is tied so well it has become a thing in itself — a low, broad organism that breathes once per generation, drinks condensation and sideways rain, and keeps its eighty cells moving slowly inside it, replacing a tile here, a hinge there, never growing, never shrinking.

Approach one of them in the square at noon and speak in your normal lowland voice and they will smile, not unkindly. They will answer quietly, because there is no need to shout. They will ask if you have had water. They will not ask where you came from or where you are going, because those gradients — the mess of building and falling, rerouting after storms — do not mean anything up here.

They live waiting for nothing at all. That is not resignation. That is how something endures when everything else erodes.

If you leave before dusk, you will think you visited a quaint, dying hamlet.

If you stay until the sun paints the cliff white and then plain daylight and then pink again on the other side, and you hear the eight o'clock axe-thunk three alleys away and the lid creaking shut over the black spring, you will understand: nothing here is dying.

It has simply finished becoming itself, and has invited its people to do the same.
