No one lives there the way a person lives in a house.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for the tenants. A doorway must have someone to walk through it. A sleeping platform must have a sleeper. A well must have a thirsty mouth. The village-that-is-a-forest plays on that instinct cruelly, because it is very good at making places for bodies.

No bodies ever came.

Or rather - a body did come. One body, divided into a thousand incompatible shapes, all of them plant, all of them so pressed together you mistake them for a city.

### The City Itself

The primary inhabitant is the mass. Not *in* the bowl. The bowl's inhabitant.

It is not a forest. A forest has individuals you can count. This is a superorganism that forgot how to be separate. Up here, above the tree line where wind would flay anything that tried to stand alone, the only viable strategy is to become each other.

What lives here is a **climax knot:**

* **The Willow Mothers** live on the leeward side where they can afford to be tall. They are not trees. They are dwarf willow and dwarf birch that should be ankle-high mats on tundra, but here they have found a trick. They braid. A hundred stems braid into a column as thick as your torso, then hollow themselves by programmed death in the center, leaving a warm chamber lined with pith. They do not think. They *remember* slowly. Sap pressure is their nervous system. When sun hits the south face of a tower, sugars move, auxins shift, and over days the whole column leans a millimeter toward the light, dragging its neighbors with it. The doorway you squeezed through yesterday is a place where two Mothers decided last summer that they needed more shade between them. Their thresholds are low because willow wood bows under snow-load, and bowing makes an arch.

* **The Peat Mind** lives underneath everything. Ten feet deep in places, a black-brown mattress of dead moss holding living moss on its back like a raft. It is the city's memory and its basement. Everything that dies here falls a centimeter and is held. Pollen from a century ago, a ptarmigan feather, a flake of basalt - all laminated in wet, acidic peat that never fully rots in the cold. It holds water the way a sponge holds breath. Step on a "street" and you are stepping on its skin; it sighs and rebounds. In the central plaza, the circular carpet of cropped moss is where the Peat Mind is thinnest and tightest, pulled taut over standing water. It crops itself. The shoots there grow short on purpose, grazed chemically by the colony to keep the well open, because the well is how the city drinks the sky.

* **The Lichen Tilers** live on the windward edge where nothing else can hold. Sheet lichen and crust lichen, blankets and scales, grey-orange and black-green. They are the city's weatherproofing, the slaters and thatchers. Each tile is a composite creature - fungus farming alga - and they overlap with incredible, patient precision, each one growing just to the edge of its neighbor's drip-line. They weep constantly. Touch a wall on that side and your hand comes away orange and damp and faintly bitter. They are the only part of the city that can photosynthesize in full storm, and they do, dully, even under a centimeter of ice.

These three are not neighbors. They are organs. The Tilers are skin, the Mothers are bones and lungs, the Peat is gut and archive. Between them run the **Cushion Weavers** - moss and liverwort and saxifrage grown into such dense, domed hummocks that a single hummock the size of a cottage might be three hundred years old and genetically one individual. They are the city's masons. They grow into the wind, they seal seams with fresh leaf, they make those translucent roofs that glow green at noon. You can watch them work, if you are willing to watch at the speed of cold. Leave a boot-print in a hummock wall and come back in two days - the print will have softened, furred over with new protonema, as if the wall healed a wound.

This is what lives here: a village-shaped plant that learned architecture because architecture is just frozen botany - shelter, drainage, heat retention.

It is urban because density forced intelligence without a brain.

### The Tenants

A city that perfect cannot stay empty. It grows tenants the way a body grows beneficial bacteria - small, blind, perfectly fitted to its halls.

They are not citizens. They are furniture that moves.

**The Herds.** White oribatid mites, each no larger than a grain of salt, moving in herds of thousands through the moss-hair forests. From your height you will not see them at first. Crouch nose-to-pith in an alley and you will see the moss stems tremble though there is no wind. That is them. They farm. They chew the dead leaf tips and excrete fungus, then eat the fungus. Their trails are highways no wider than a hair, kept clear of spores. When the city shifts a wall, the herds reroute overnight. They are the only livestock, and the only farmers, and they are both.

**The Carpenters.** The ice-crawlers - grylloblattids - wingless, eyeless, the color of old ivory. They are the only things in the bowl that appreciate the architecture as architecture. They tunnel through the pith of the Willow Mothers, not to eat - the pith is too tannic - but to live inside the warmth. Four degrees warmer than outside is a paradise at this altitude. They bore perfect circles, the diameter of your little finger, and leave them polished with their own silk. Rain collects there. Later, a mite herd will drink there. Later still, the willow will grow around the hole and incorporate it as a window for its ice-pillars. The crawlers do not know they are craftsmen. If you put your ear to a tower wall, you can sometimes hear the faintest *tik tik tik* as one gnaws.

**The Ghosts.** The pale lichens that glow. Not brightly enough to read by. Just enough that after your eyes adjust to the green submarine dusk, you realize the edges of the alleys are faintly marked, as if by low-wattage emergency lights. They are not lights. They are lichen that make their own luciferin as a byproduct of dealing with too much UV filtered through cloud. They cluster thickest near the sleeping platforms - those eerily body-sized hollows of woven root - and near the central well. It is not invitation. It is coincidence: those places are dampest, and they like damp. But coincidence feels like design when you are cold and the light is dim.

And then there is the tenant that isn't there yet.

You.

The city has never seen a human village, but it has evolved every solution a human village needs - insulation, cisterns, windbreaks, shared walls for heat - because cold and wet pose the same problems to moss as they do to men. When you enter an alley, your shoulders brush both sides, and both sides exude a faint resin to seal the abrasion. When you breathe in a chamber, condensation blooms on the ceiling and drips into the little cupboard-holes the crawlers left. When you sleep on a platform, your weight compresses the root-weave and it springs back slower in the morning, holding your shape for a day before forgetting.

If you stay too long, and few do, the Peat Mind will keep the shape a little longer. A new shoot will lean into the draft you made. A doorway will pinch a half-inch narrower to better hold the heat your body gave it. The city does not want you. It has no want. But it is terribly, slowly good at accommodating pressure - wind pressure, snow pressure, now body pressure.

That is its hospitality. It makes more city around you until there is no room for you at all.

That is why there are no skeletons here. Only that feeling, in the perfect circle under the oculus where rain falls without touching wood, that the place is inhabited by the *idea* of inhabitation. A village waiting for villagers so long it grew them out of willow and moss and mites, in miniature, in blind white herds and eyeless carpenters and sleeping hollows that fit a curled child exactly.

You walk its circumference before your hands get cold. You leave by the seam you came in through, if you can find it - it will have shifted, a little more sealed than before, as if after your visit the city decided it was, after all, complete without doors.
