They don't live *in* it.

Not the way you do.

At first, when you were looking for the builders — the forearm-long hollows, the bone needle still quivering — you were looking for the wrong kind of inhabitants. You were looking for a smaller version of yourself. Something that sleeps in a bed and wakes up and farms fly larvae in a dish.

Something like that is here. But it is not *what* lives here. It is only the part that looks most like you.

Hold still a little longer in your mind, with your hands still cold from where you kneeled, and let the room resolve again at its true scale.

### The First Folk

The moss is not architecture. The architecture is a behavior of the moss.

That red-stemmed sphagnum — *Sphagnum capillifolium* and its cousins, centuries thick — did not happen to make a room. It was convinced to. Century after century, shoot after shoot, it was nipped, thinned, shaded, dripped on, until its natural urge to dome and hold water became this: pillars, vaults, a low lung. It still grows. If everyone left, the room would seal itself shut in twenty years, the ceiling rootlets finding the floor and knitting. The city only exists as long as something keeps asking it not to.

And the first thing that does the asking is not a person at all.

It's the mites.

Not incidentally. Not infestation. Look closer at that slow orange procession along a moss stem. Each one — oribatid, fat as a poppy seed, armored — is carrying something. A spore. A grain of peat. An egg smaller than dust. On that terrace you thought was a retaining wall of stolons, they move in braided lines, thousands wide, polishing the living surface with their mouths, pruning fungal hyphae you can't see, grazing algae off the leaf-cups so the weep-water stays clear.

They are not kept. They are the keepers. Without them the moss felts over, chokes, rots from its own damp.

Above them, the springtails — the glitter you took for dust — do the air work. They vault and land and vault again, and each landing tests the tension of the water film that covers everything. Where the film is too thick, they gather and drink it down. Where it is too thin, they avoid it, and their avoidance tells the moss where to sweat more. Their glittering is a census. The room reads itself in their jumps.

In the pool, the fairy shrimp hang motionless except for the endless beating of their feathered legs, which don't swim so much as *stir*. They keep the central mirror from stratifying, from going anoxic and black. The diving beetle that patrols its edge is not a predator straying in. It is a shepherd. It culls the shrimp that slow. Its larva below, blind and huge-jawed, cleans what dies.

This is not a place where things live. This is a place where living is a *job* that many small bodies share. The woodlice that lumber along the terraces like cattle are not cattle. They are bulldozers, chewing the dead sphagnum heads that would otherwise mat and drown the living tip below. The spider in the alley is not a pest. It is the night watch. The winter gnats whose hum you can barely hear are not a cloud. They are the bellows, moving air when the peat above clamps down too tight.

None of them know the city. All of them are the city.

If you stayed with a lens for a week you would see it: the canals you thought were combed into the floor-mat are not combed. They are *worn* — by worm and water and mite-foot, deepened by the same path taken ten million times. The house-form hollowed into the eastern pillar was not hollowed. It was eaten clean by larvae and then held open by moss that learned to grow around the empty space because the empty space was reliably dry.

The room thinks with small legs.

### The Second Folk

And then — because a system this tight, this cold, this wet, this endlessly weeding and pruning and stirring itself needs a memory longer than a mite's life — there is something else.

You felt them by absence. The sleeping hollows indented to a length slightly longer than your forearm. The bone needle, still warm. The leaf-cup water still rippling.

They are not fairies. They are not spirits. If you found one curled in that white sphagnum pantry you would not think *magical*. You would think *unfinished*.

Imagine a person made for this exact light, which is almost no light.

They are long and low, the length of a hare, the weight of a wet glove. When they stand — which they rarely do — they go on two legs, but more often they move on all fours, belly just clear of the sighing mat, because the ceiling is a crouch and because their hands are also feet. Their skin is not skin the way yours is. It is damp, permeable, lightly haired with fine, water-shedding filaments, liverwort-pale underneath and moss-spattered over the back and shoulders where the colony has been allowed to grow *on* them. Old ones go quite green. You would mistake them for a tussock if they stopped moving, which they do for hours.

No eyes, or nothing you would call an eye. In that grey-green dusk, eyes are a costly vanity. They have shallow, lidded pits on either side of a broad, flat head that sense change in luminance — enough to know ceiling from pool, gap from curtain — and nothing more. Their world is smell and taste and vibration. Their snouts are soft, blunt, fringed with whiskers stiff as sedge bristles that read the water-film the springtails test. Their ears are just membrane, flush to the skull, tuned to drip and rustle. When one pauses over a moss stem and seems to listen, it is listening with its whole underside to the mat's sigh.

Their hands — four of them, because the forelimbs and hindlimbs end in the same — are narrow, long-fingered, webbed at the base for swimming the brief pools but split to a fine point for picking. Each finger ends not in a nail but in a translucent spade of keratin, slightly spooned, perfect for scooping a mite egg without crushing it, for lifting a shoot by its tip, for tying that dried sedge thread with a bone needle.

They speak seldom, and not in words. When two meet on that moss bridge no wider than your hand, they touch snouts and exchange a brief puff of air — breath rich with what they've eaten, where they've slept, whether the basalt below is seeping colder this week. The little tree frog you heard, throat puffing pale gold in the thatch? That is not a frog living here by accident. That *is* one of them, or rather, the sound they make when they are alone and content and moving water — a low, cold *ping* that carries through the living ceiling and tells the others: *this tier is still draining*.

They do not build. They *tend*. Their technology of patience you saw — the scraped liverwort, the nipped shoot, the bone needle left in the training vine — is not construction. It is conversation. A shoot nipped here says *branch there*. A thread tied there says *remember this arch*. Over generations, the moss remembers better than they do, holding the shape in its compacted felt.

They have no fire, because fire would kill the air. Their cold stone for cracking seed husks is also their clock: they press their bellies to it to slow their hearts in the deepest winter, when the ice skin on the pool finally thickens and the chamber must go still or starve. Their pantry of white sphagnum tips is not food, though they can eat it. It is bandage, diaper, shroud. Everything that dies here — mite, shrimp, tending one — is tucked into white tips so it doesn't rot wrong. The tips drink the death and turn faintly pink.

They are few. Perhaps a dozen in a chamber the length of a small house. That is why you thought it sparse. They leave long respectful emptiness between dwellings so the moss can breathe, and long respectful silence between themselves so the springtails can be heard. They are not lonely. They are spaced the way alveoli are spaced.

And they are shy of you the way a lung is shy of a knife — not from fear, but from category error. You are wind and birds and bright sky and weight. You cannot crouch small enough or stay wet enough to be allowed to see them move. When you push aside the curtain, they go still and become tussock, and the spider keeps shivering, and the mites keep processing, and you think the city is empty.

When you back out on hands and knees, careful not to break the lip of moss that is a mouth, they unflatten.

One picks up the bone needle. One touches snout to leaf-cup and tastes that something large, salty, and grieving has been here and left. One goes to the central pool and stirs it with a webbed hand where your shadow cooled it too long.

The weeping continues. The ceiling drips its cold pearls. The thousand legs resume.

Underneath, the lights stay on — not lamps. The faint green glow of life making more life in the dark, reflected in a forearm-long palm, in an orange mite back, in the wet whiskers of a face that has no eyes and does not need them to know exactly where its world ends and it begins, which is nowhere.
