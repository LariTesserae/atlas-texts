No one lives here the way you live somewhere.

Nothing *resides* in this basin. Everything is held, for a little while, between the press of roots and the dissolution of rot, and then it is passed on. Habitation here isn't settlement — it's saturation.

If you stand long enough to let your eyes and ears stop looking for absence, the black resolves into tenants. Not one dominant species, but a mesh that is so tight you can't pull on one thread without moving a hundred others.

### The Light-Bearers

First, everything that is not an animal and therefore rules everything.

The canopy itself is the primary citizen. The so-called trees are not single trunks but colonies — a hardwood armature strangled and fused with epiphytes, with fungi, with vines that have become wood themselves. Their leaves are broad, black-green, and translucent at the edges where their veins light. They make that sickly chartreuse, that bruised violet, not to be seen — but to eat. Photosynthesis turned inward. Each leaf is a dim lantern pressed against the humid air, converting its own glow back into sugar in a closed, incestuous loop. When a leaf dies and falls, its light goes out before it hits the ground, and in that half-second of darkness something is always waiting.

Below them, the true architects of the glow: the fungal boulders. They swell along the young basalt ribs where the stone is still hot to the touch, digesting the mineral itself. They are amber, teal, corpse-green, and they pulse. Slowly. A breath every twenty seconds. Their light is not for feeding but for farming — they lure motes of glowing pollen, clouds of spores, the innumerable hand-sized insects that rattle like maracas in the fern breaks. The insects come, they dust themselves, they lay eggs in the fungus's porous skin, they die and are absorbed. The basalt beneath them is pitted and etched where they have eaten it, which is how you know the rock is temporary here. The fungus will crack it, root and hyphae will wedge it, and the valley floor will sink another inch under the weight of humus.

And on the floor itself, the moss. A felt meters deep, springy, luminous under duress. It stores the light of the day it never had. Step — a bloom of cool fire spreads from your boot in a slow ripple and fades. Drag a hand — a comet trail. That light is a warning, and a scream. Every luminous footprint says *something heavy is here* to every predator and scavenger within ten meters.

### The Loud

Because everything here hears everything.

**The Stridulators:** You heard them first. Insect analogs but wrong — chitin stretched over air sacs, legs like jointed stilts. Some are as small as a knuckle, some as big as your open hand, all of them in the ferns making noise by rubbing, tapping, scraping file-against-scraper at frequencies that stack until the dark hums. They don't call for mates so much as they *jam* each other, a deafening competitive static through which a female must pick out the one clear tone. When she does, they couple mid-air in a brief flash of opposed bioluminescence and fall already being eaten by the next thing.

**The Drum-Wings:** Those leathery thrums passing over your head. They aren't birds. They aren't bats. Think manta made of membrane and gristle, the size of kites, the size of doors, utterly silent except for the air they move. Their wings are broad, translucent, veined with their own pale lure-light running in racing stripes along the leading edge. That light draws swarms of gnats, which ignite when startled — green fireworks — which the drum-wing then sweeps through with a scooped, chinless mouth. They navigate by thunder. Every wingbeat is a low-pressure drum you feel in your molars. When a chorus of the howling things in the canopy starts — that two-tone wail that never resolves, five voices answering from all directions — the drum-wings stall and hover, listening, because that chord means a troop of weavers has found fruit.

**The Glass-Bellies:** Lift that fern you disturbed and watch them again. Hundreds of amphibians, each no longer than your thumb, skin like wet cellophane through which you can see a glowing gut, a beating heart, eggs like pearls. Their bellies are lanterns to find each other in the litter. They do not croak. They *tick*, like cooling glass. They live in the suspended lakes — the bromeliad cups fifty feet up, each one a complete, black, tannin-stained world. A whole generation of glass-bellies can be born, breed, and die in a single cup without ever touching the forest floor. When your branch-shower falls, it overflows their lakes and they rain down, ticking in panic, and everything eats them.

### The Tool-Makers

They are not civilized. They are not dominant. Intelligence here is not a crown but a cheap trick that several lineages have stumbled on because everything valuable is locked inside something else, and the forest rots any tooth or claw that tries to specialize.

**The Knappers — *Lithoscuria***

On the sharp, new basalt you heard the tap-tap-tap. Go look.

Low, wide, black as the rock itself, six limbs and a back like poured iron. Not true crabs — they have no sea to return to — but stone-crabs, mineralivores that have learned to be butchers. Their forelimbs are asymmetrical: one ends in a dense, hammer-like knob of calcified chitin, pitted from use; the other is a delicate, three-fingered hand. They prospect. They run that hand over fresh basalt, feeling for the milky seam of chert that veins the young stone. When they find it, they brace, lift the hammer-hand, and strike. *Tak.* A flake peels off with a conchoidal fracture, steaming faintly where the impact heated it. Razor edge.

They don't keep it. They immediately drag the lantern-fruits — heavy, woody spheres the size of melons that hang from the strangler vines, their rind glowing faintly from within — to the anvil of the rock and slice. Inside is pulp that ferments instantly in the damp air, smelling of wine and rot. They gorge, get drunk, fight each other with their fresh tools, snap the blade in half, discard it. By morning the discarded chert is already filmed over with moss, its edge dulled by hyphae. By the next day it is soil.

Watch a juvenile learn. It hammers too hard, shatters the core, squeals — a high, metallic chitter — and an adult will cuff it aside and finish the strike correctly. Then the adult steals the fruit. There is no apprenticeship. There is only theft.

If you leave metal here, they would try to knap it, fail, and ignore it.

**The Weavers — *Arach lemur***

Higher. Always higher. Where the vines braid themselves into cables thick as your body.

They are lemur-like only at a glance: forward eyes huge and wet, adapted to amplify the faintest amber fungal glow, bodies small, 40-50 cm long without the tail. But their tail is another limb, and their hands are wrong. Four limbs, each ending in a hand with two thumbs opposed on either side — four opposable digits per hand. They need them.

They do not build nests. Nests would be eaten. Instead they build *intentions*. A band of six to ten weavers will select a gap between two bioluminescent liana trunks where the drum-wings commute. For hours they will strip living, still-glowing vines — the vines twitch and leak sap-sugar as they are pulled — and braid them into a net. The silk isn't theirs: they harvest it, stealing from the vast sheet-webs of spiders the size of dinner plates that tolerate the weavers because the weavers clean parasites from their legs. They weave vine and stolen silk together into a sticky, faintly luminous lattice a meter across, anchor it with living knots that tighten as the vine tries to keep growing, and wait.

When a drum-wing hits it, the whole canopy rings like a harp string. The weavers swarm — chattering in clicks and soft hoots, that two-tone wail — and they do not kill with tools. They kill by holding on until the membrane tears, then biting the thin skull behind the eyes. They eat in the net, dripping blood that makes the moss below flare and fade, flare and fade. When they are done, they roll the net up, tuck it into a crotch of the tree, and sleep piled together, breathing in unison. The net, left in the damp, will germinate if they don't reuse it in two days.

They talk. Not language as you know it, but referential hoots: *net-tension*, *wing-coming*, *stone-crab-below*. Their eyes meet yours in the dark and you see a calculation: *are you fruit, are you predator, are you rock to be ignored?*

And there are others you haven't seen yet because the forest is too dense to see ten meters. Things that make permanent nothing.

Something like a pig, but blind, its snout a broad, luminous disk it drags through the moss to scorch bacteria.

Something like an ape, but twice your height, that moves by breaking saplings in front of it and walking the brief tunnel before it closes.

Something colonial, ant-like, that does not make hoppers or hills but hollows out living leaves from the inside and lives in the still-green, still-glowing blister.

All of them loud. All of them temporary-tool users. A stick to probe, a leaf to carry water, a shard to scrape. Then discard. The forest's rule — *rock is temporary, life is permanent* — has trained intelligence to be disposable. To hoard is to rot. To cache is to feed fungus. The smartest thing here owns nothing for longer than an hour.

That is why you hear no walls, no roads. The most successful mind in this valley is the one that can make a perfect knife in eight seconds and forget it completely eight seconds later, while the moss erases its footprints and the condensation gathers on the leaf above you, waiting to fall again without a cloud.

Step carefully. The light under your foot is already fading.
