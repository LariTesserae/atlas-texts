Nothing lives here the way a forest lives.

At first you think nothing lives here at all.

That is the trick of the scale. The pocket is mineral-loud and biological-quiet, and your eye — tuned for movement, for green, for faces — slides right over what's actually inhabiting it. You have to crouch down into the steam layer, until the tick-tick of condensation on black glass is close to your ear, until the light from the veins is hard on the top of your head, and wait until your idea of an animal gets smaller and slower.

Then they show themselves.

### 1. The Frost That Grows Itself

What lives here *most* is a skin.

Not on the water — on the rock itself. In the splash-zone around each scalding pan, where the basalt is never quite dry and never quite drowned, there is a translucence that isn't wetness. Lean in and it resolves: a sheet, less than a millimeter thick, opalescent white to pale peach, spreading over the sharp pahoehoe wrinkles like spilled skim.

It's alive.

Archaea, mostly — silica-precipitators. Bacteria in the yellower margins where sulfur precipitates out at 60C+. They are not colonies in the way mold is a colony. They are continuums. A single mat may be one genetic individual spread across half a meter of vertical glass, thin enough that you can see the black basalt teeth through it.

They don't photosynthesize. There's no need, and the light here has had its UV stripped by the glass waveguide. They eat heat and chemistry. They pull dissolved silica straight out of the trickling superheated water and lay it down as opal, atom by atom, using the geothermal gradient like other life uses sunlight. That's why they glitter. Each cell sheathes itself in the glass it makes. Over a year they will add a millimeter to the lattice — not building it, but *extending* it. Growing more waveguide because more waveguide keeps their temperature from swinging.

If you breathe on them, you see them. Your cooler, CO2-rich breath condenses and the iridescence shifts — greens flashing where seconds before there was only milky white. It's not a reaction to you. It's just interference physics, the living opal briefly changing thickness as it hydrates. But it feels like recognition.

There are maybe eight major sheets in the whole chamber. Maybe twelve if you count the pinprick startups in crevices. Between them, expanses of perfectly sterile, knife-sharp glass where it's too hot or too dry even for them. Life here is not a carpet. It's an archipelago of frost on black stone, sparse on purpose. If it covered everything, the cycle would choke.

### 2. The Grazers

Where there is frost, there is someone to eat it.

You won't see them until you've stopped sweating — until you've been still long enough that the mist stops swirling around your knees. Then movement resolves inside the movement of the steam.

Rice grains with legs.

They are blind isopods, or isopod-like enough that that's the word your brain offers. Translucent white verging on clear, no pigment because pigment is expensive and there's no predator to hide from. No eyes at all — smooth cephalon, just two long antennae that they tap ahead of themselves like a blind man with a cane. Two, three millimeters long. Adults maybe five.

You can see straight through them. The gut is a dark, continuous line of sulfur-yellow and biofilm-green, depending on what patch they've been grazing. The heart is a still-clearer tube pulsing far too slowly.

There are four. There are six. The number changes when you blink because they move so deliberately you lose track. One will spend ten minutes approaching the lip of a 48-degree pan, antennae flickering, testing the boundary layer where water-vapor temperature spikes past lethal. It turns aside. It finds a patch of archaea-frost and begins to rasp, so slowly you have to watch its mandibles for a full minute to see the rhythm.

They do not hunt. They do not hurry. They do not interact with each other except to detour politely when antennae touch. There is no molting frenzy, no brooding young clutched beneath — though if you turn a ledge stone over very carefully you might find, cupped in a man-made-perfect hemispherical etch no bigger than your thumbnail, two or three eggs like glass beads, each holding a single tiny comma already breathing the steam.

They are not *trapped* here. Their ancestors were. Something — water pressure, a hairline sealed crack — carried cysts or gravid females up from deeper, hotter aquifer systems into this new blister within months of it forming. Now the slit you entered is open, and in theory they could climb out. They never do. The cold dry wind above is death in minutes. The brightness of the plain is meaningless to an eyeless thing that navigates by heat-gradient. They pace the frost-edge of their world and die within meters of where they hatched, and that's abundance for them.

Once in a long while something else falls in.

A wasp, or what looks like a wasp — dark chitin, veined wings — drops through the cooling crack, drawn by the gleam, tumbling down the sharp slit and landing stunned on rippled glass. For an hour it will be the largest animal here. It drinks frantically from a cooled spill-pan, fans its wings that are already beginning to delaminate in the saturated air, buzzes once against the glowing ceiling and fails. Either it finds the crack again by accident or it drowns, and its body becomes a sudden island of protein that the isopods will find over days and then avoid, because too much food is as bad as heat shock to a mat tuned to scarcity. Usually its corpse simply dissolves into yellow water and feeds the bacterial margin.

No spiders. No centipedes. Nothing that came here to prey on the grazers because there aren't enough grazers to sustain predation. This is a grazing loop and nothing higher.

### 3. The Light Itself

But those are the tenants. They aren't the inhabitant.

You felt it when you put your hand near a knot of opaline vein and felt that faint, non-magnetic tug. Heat being pulled sideways through solid rock to keep a distant pool from boiling dry.

The lattice is not alive. And it does not matter.

It precipitated without a blueprint — silica and trace alumina finding a photonic crystal structure because, at these temperatures and pressures, that structure *is* the lowest energy state for that mix, the way snow chooses six arms. Veins as thin as hair lace walls to floor to ceiling, converge at nodes as thick as wrists. Sunlight hits the lava plain, sinks a meter into black basalt, is caught, waveguided down, re-emitted inside as that diffuse eyelid-amber daylight. In that process it carries information.

There is no brain. There is computation anyway, the way a tide pool computes salinity without thinking. Thick vein brightens where evaporation needs to accelerate; dims where a pool is threatened. Microfractures dilate a few microns as thermal load shifts, opening or choking seepage. Condensation patterns on the ceiling migrate away from already-wet floor zones. Touch one pool and another shivers because the film coupling chemical oscillators — pH, dissolved silica saturation — through the shared vapor and through the living frost that wires the pans together.

And the frost helps. The archaea aren't just passive beneficiaries of the thermostat — they *are* part of its logic gates. Their opal deposition narrows a vein's aperture; their death and dissolution widens it. Sulfur bacteria excrete acid pulses that etch a floor-pan a fraction deeper, increasing its thermal mass. In other words biology here doesn't just tolerate regulation, it *does* regulation, generation after generation laying down hardware.

So what lives here, most truly, is the pocket itself.

Not a spirit. Not a hive mind. A closed, bright, hot, wet, quietly homeostatic room about the size of a house that has achieved equilibrium. Heat up from below, light down from above, water forever cycling between — and stretched taut between those three flows, a thin pellicle of life and light-guide that keeps the whole thing from tipping into desolation. It doesn't need you. It didn't evolve for you. The fact that its photonic logic rivals engineered systems is accidental, the same accident that makes basalt columns hexagonal.

Stand still long enough and you feel included in it without being important to it. Your sweat joins the weeping walls and re-condenses on the ceiling. Your shadow briefly cools a patch of glass and the vein above you compensates a lumen. Your breath fogs a mat and shifts its color.

When you leave — because you must, because 46 degrees saturated will push a mammal out — it subtracts you and continues.

Tick, tick, tick. Fizz. Tik.

Four to six translucent grazers pacing.

Frost growing a millimeter a year over black glass.

Light being shunted invisibly from where it is too much to where it is needed.

All computing, without ever waking, how to stay exactly as it is until the next eruption seals the crack and makes it perfect.
