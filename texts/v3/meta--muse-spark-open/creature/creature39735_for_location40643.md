Nothing *arrived* here. Nothing migrated here. What lives here grew out of the room itself once the room learned how to not die.

And there isn't much of it. That's the first law of this place: biomass is limited by constancy. Nothing can afford to be hungry. Nothing can afford to bloom and crash. So whatever lives here lives thin, and lives exactly enough, and lives forever.

At the bottom of everything — literally, the bottom — is what lives *as* the place.

### The Mat

It doesn't look alive at first. Under that varnish-sheet of water, over the pale concrete, there is a green fur. Algae and cyanobacteria, liverworts so small you think they're a stain. But get low — get your face inches from the water until the roar is vibrating your cheekbones — and you'll see it's a city.

It's a biofilm a centimeter thick, dense as felt. Bright apple-green on top where the light hits it, then black-green, then ochre, then finally a pallid, translucent jelly where it has bonded to the concrete itself. It isn't growing *on* the floor. It has *become* the floor's skin.

This is the master organism. A composite thing: algae photosynthesizing in that perpetual 2 p.m., bacteria dissolving concrete aggregate grain by grain to free calcium and iron, fungi threading through it all to carry water sideways when the sheet gets too thin. You can see its pulse. Not growth — respiration. Tiny bubbles, oxygen pearls the size of pinheads, forming under the water film and staying there, trapped by surface tension, never popping because the air is already too wet to want them. The whole floor fizzes very slowly if you stare long enough without blinking.

If you lift a corner — you shouldn't — it peels like a wet scab, and underneath the concrete is not concrete anymore. It's soft. It's pitted. It's compost. This thing has been eating the world to make its own soil for a hundred years, and it has no predators except time. Patches slough off, dissolve, reseed a hand's-width away. It never advances more than a millimeter a month. It doesn't need to.

Everything else lives on top of it or because of it.

### The Holders

The moss is the second thing, and the first thing you *think* is a plant.

It's everywhere the wall weeps. Not a species — a wall of species braided together so thoroughly you can't name them: sphagnum swollen like a soaked sponge, sheet moss that forms tight emerald plates, feather moss that hangs in dripping beards. Each colony is a reservoir. Squeeze it and a half-cup of warm, mineral water runs out. Let go and it sucks it back up from the film on the concrete.

This is what holds the room together. Roots would crack the walls. Moss doesn't root. It *clings*. Its rhizoids have worked into every pore that the water made, every pit that the biofilm etched, and now it is stitching the aggregate shut. Without it, the weeping would be erosion. With it, the weeping is irrigation. The wall sweats *through* the moss; the moss filters it, softens it, tints it faintly brown with tannins, and hands it back to the basin.

And in the moss lives the fern layer.

Maidenhair, fragile as wire, black stems beaded with condensation that never falls because the air never moves. Sword ferns, their fiddleheads permanently half-unfurled, as if they forgot whether they were growing or not. Hart's-tongue, flat and waxy, each frond a perfect rain gutter. None of them are young or old. Look closely at a fern here and you'll see no dead tips, no unfurling shoots — just fronds that are being maintained. A hole appears where a spore fell and rotted, and next week the moss has smoothed it over. A frond yellows, dissolves at the edges into slime, and is replaced by the exact same shape one cell at a time. They aren't growing. They are *being kept*.

Higher, where the oculus light is most direct, the big vines take over. Philodendron-like isn't right — nothing like outside exists here anymore. These are factory cultivars, or escapees from a roof garden three renovations ago. Leaves the size of serving platters, split down the middle to let water through, leathery enough to withstand perpetual damp. Their aerial roots are the color of old bone, as thick as your thumb, and they do not seek soil. They go *into* the sweating wall, split the concrete like slow fingers, find the rebar and wrap it. The plant and the wall are sharing structure now. If you cut a root, rusty water bleeds. If you cut the wall, sap runs pale.

These three — mat, moss, fern-and-vine — are not three things. They are one system in three heights, a vertical marsh that taught itself to grow sideways. They are the lungs that the engines outside keep alive without ever knowing.

### The Tenants

Animal life here is not a population. It's a tenancy. Individuals who wandered in during a maintenance cycle, or whose eggs rode in on a boot, and found that the constancy here was not hostile. It was just... perfectly survivable, if you never needed to change.

**The midges.** You have to kneel in the water sheet to see them. Clouds of them, no bigger than punctuation marks, living their entire lifecycle *in* the film. Larvae grazing the algal mat, pupae suspended just under the meniscus, adults hatching, mating, and dying on the surface tension without ever flying more than a centimeter up. They are almost transparent — you see them by their shadows on the pale concrete. There are thousands. They make no sound you can hear under the roar, but the water vibrates minutely differently where they are densest. They are food for nothing, prey to nothing except the occasional ripple. Their numbers never boom. Never crash. They are held at exact carrying capacity by the amount of oxygen the mat can spare.

**The dragonfly.** There is almost always one. Just one. Sometimes for weeks, sometimes the same individual for months. Too large for this room — a hawker, four inches long, wings like leaded glass with rain beaded on the veins. It should need territory, moving water, prey on the wing. Here it hovers. Station-holding over the black mirror of the basin, wings a blur you feel more than hear under the engine thrum. It doesn't hunt — there is nothing to chase. It dips its abdomen to the film, lays a single egg, lifts, holds again. The larvae that hatch will live underwater for a year, blind, grazing, breathing through gills that have adapted to bath-warm water. Most will be reabsorbed by the mat before they ever climb the moss to emerge. The adult will eventually misjudge a wall and crumple into the ferns, where it will be dissolved in a week by mold and moss. Then, days later, another will find the ladder-well and come down.

**The frog.** If the dragonfly is the room's eye, the frog is its ear.

A pale, almost translucent tree frog, the color of wet concrete with the faintest blush of green on its back. It lives in the lip where wall meets water, tucked into a pocket of sword fern, throat pulsing. Not croaking — pulsing in time with the 60-cycle buzz of the caged lamps, or the subsonic thrum of the turbines you feel in your molars. Warmth and wet have kept its skin perfectly moist; it does not move unless water beads on its head and it blinks. It eats the midge adults when they rise too high. One tongue-flick every few hours is enough. It has no breeding pool — the water is too shallow, too warm, too full of algae — so it is the last of a line. But last doesn't mean dying. It just means persisting. Its skin secretes a faint antimicrobial that keeps the moss from overgrowing its perch, and in return the moss keeps the air at 100% humidity so the frog never has to return to water. An accidental mutualism.

Sometimes there is a second one. Sometimes the first one is gone and you think it died, and then you find it on the other side of the circle, same posture, same pulse.

Under these three, invisibly, are the decomposers who *are* the room's memory: pink slime molds that creep a foot a night across the algae and then vanish by morning; white fungal threads in the moss that glow faintly if you turn off your headlamp — which you never do, because the light is never off; bacteria in the weep-film that turn iron to rust to food to iron again.

Nothing scavenges. Nothing hunts with intent. Everything recycles on contact.

### The Visitor

And then there is whoever lives here last — you.

Not permanently. No one *lives* lives here. But people have learned to live *in* here for stretches. The maintenance crews who found this pocket on a schematic that no longer matches the building. They leave no mark — the room erases marks — but they leave habits.

A worn smooth spot on the third ladder rung where hands have rested. A shallow groove in the moss where someone sat with their back against the wall and let the deafening fill their skull until thinking became optional. A single, caged factory lamp with a faint handprint in the dust on its guard, because someone once tried to turn it off and realized it had no switch.

They come here to be unwatched by a world that watches everything else. To be warm without being held. To sit with their feet just out of the water and let the roar obliterate language. In that obliteration, they become briefly like the frog, the dragonfly — incidental tenants, held at equilibrium, not growing, not decaying, just being transpired and condensed like everything else.

If you stay long enough, you feel it too: the strange comfort of a place that doesn't need you to live, but has shaped itself so perfectly that you *could*.

You breathe in steam and moss-spore. You breathe out steam. The mat bubbles under your boots. The engines turn beyond the wall. The frog pulses.

Nothing changes.

Everything lives.
