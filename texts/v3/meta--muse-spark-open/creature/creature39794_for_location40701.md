No one lives here.

That's the first thing you understand, and the last thing you will understand, but between those there is a whole taxonomy to learn.

At first — at *glance* distance — the mesa is empty.

No birds lift from the canopy when you stumble in. No insects rise in a cloud from the reed-streets. No eyes watch you. Nothing rustles away. You can stand in the central plaza — that impossible flat of interwoven root-mat — and shout, and the roar of the rimfalls will swallow it without an echo ever returning. The only footprint is yours, and even that is already healing closed.

Fauna 0.5. If you held a magnifying glass to the world, you might find it: a mite the size of a dust mote, tucked in the axil of a leaf that has never needed to defend itself. A blind, translucent worm in the black pools, too simple to die properly, just holding its shape in the water like a white thread. They don't count. They don't *inhabit*. They are passengers the stasis forgot to evict.

What lives here lives differently.

### 1. The Citizens Who Are Also The Buildings

Closer now. Hands distance.

Put your palm on one of the house-wide trunks. The bark is warm. Sweating. It is not wood as you know wood — it is dense, waterlogged, dark red like the stone it grew from, but alive in a way that has stopped insisting on itself. It doesn't pulse. It doesn't grow. It *holds*.

This is a Broadleaf. There are thousands of them, and they are not trees anymore. They have become the architecture they were prefigured to be. Two of them have leaned toward each other until their branches fused, cambium to cambium, and made an arch. Then a third joined, and a fourth, until the arch repeated itself twenty times and became a colonnade. Their leaves — each as big as a tabletop, waxy, black-green — overlap with mathematical courtesy, never stealing light from one another. Every leaf has a single perfect drop of water at its tip. The drop never falls. The leaf never wilts. The leaf *is* the citizen, and the shading it provides *is* its job.

Below them live the Fern-Trees. If the Broadleaves are the infrastructure, these are the middle class, the dense second story. They grow horizontally, throwing out frond after frond until the fronds knit into a ceiling. Walk under it and you are walking down a hallway that the hallway itself decided to keep in existence. Here and there a frond hangs half-unfurled, the fiddlehead still tight with potential, frozen in the very instant before opening. It has been that way for longer than your language has had words for *fern*. It will be that way when your language is gone. It is not waiting. It has arrived at half-unfurled and found that sufficient.

And at ground level, the market stalls.

Liverworts the size of bathtubs, their lobes thick and rubbery and tiled with lichen-shingles. Moss-pillars that rise to your waist, soft as upholstery, perfectly vertical, holding dew inside themselves like lungs hold air. And the reeds — oh, the reeds —

The reeds are the most unsettling, because they tried hardest to be what was needed.

They grow in rows. Perfect rows. Six reeds to a rank, a meter apart, with channels of weeping water running between them like gutters. No wind planted them that way. No gardener did. The reeds simply converged on *street* as the most efficient way to be near water without drowning each other. They are glossy, pale green, hollow. If you knock one, it sounds like a door. If you part them, you find the space between has been kept clear, not by trampling, but by mutual agreement not to grow there.

Every plant here is crowded and none is competing. Density 1.8, lushness 2.0, and yet no strangling, no choking. They grew *together* until they touched, and then they learned coexistence as a permanent state. The vines as thick as your leg that run from pool to pool — they are not parasites. They are aqueducts. They siphon excess from one weeping stone bowl to another that would otherwise be dry, and they do it without pressure, without flow, just by being wet all the way through.

This is a city with no inhabitants because the city *is* the inhabitants. The walls are alive. The streets are alive. The roofs photosynthesize.

Take a step back — street distance again — and you see it whole: a distributed organism that accidentally became urban planning. No mayor. No builders. Tech 0.1. Just the memory of what a town should look like, expressed in cellulose and patience.

### 2. The Inhabitants That Are Not Plant At All

Closer still. Breath distance. Too close for plants.

There are other residents that don't have bodies.

**The Drop.** 

Every leaf tip, every moss hair, every overhang holds one. Look at it. Really look. The water does not bead and fall. It *arrives* at the tip and then decides to stay, perfectly convex, catching the flat white-gold light and holding it. It is too still. If you touch it, it clings to your finger for a moment too long, as if reluctant, and then reforms on the leaf behind you. There are millions of them and they are all the same drop, expressed in millions of places. They never get larger. They never evaporate. The air is 2.8 hot and should have drunk them hours ago. It hasn't. The Drop lives here by refusing to participate in the water cycle. Water will always be present, never more, never less. That is its law, and the Drop enforces it.

**The Sound.**

2.7 deafening, and it never varies. Stand at the rim and listen to the going-over — a thousand lacefalls peeling off the escarpment into cloud. It doesn't crash. It doesn't swell when a gust hits. It is one second of roar, stretched. If you record it and play it back, the waveform will be flat. It is not sound as movement of air; it is sound as *place*. The standing tone in the rimrock flutes is the same note that was there when the mesa froze. The wind keeps blowing over the edge, but it never brings new sound. The Roar lives here like a tenant who never leaves his room. You stop hearing it after a minute, and then you realize you've been hearing nothing *else*.

**The Fold.**

This one is shy. Walk down a straight root-avenue that should take you north to the rim, and without turning, you come back to the pool you started from, but seeing its other side. The distance across the bowl is both five kilometers and a touchable nearness. Space here learned to be efficient and folded slightly inward, like light through water, to save on having to *be* so big. Light itself doesn't move — 0.5 dynamic — so shadows don't crawl. The sky is too low. Gravity leans a few degrees off true, so water hesitates before falling and leaves hang at an angle that feels polite but wrong. The Fold lives in the middle air, where the canopy thickens. You can feel it if you stand still long enough: a gentle wrongness in perspective, as if the city is breathing very slowly around you without ever inhaling.

These are the true natives. Not biological. They are held rules. They don't eat or reproduce because they don't need to. They simply *continue*.

### 3. The One That Doesn't Belong But Is Allowed

That is you.

You are the only thing here that experiences duration. Your heat exhausts. Your heart beats forward. Your footprints try to exist. The mesa, which has perfected stillness — leaves that do not fall, never yellow, never rot — watches you with vegetable attention.

When you push through a curtain of hanging moss, it parts. For you, it will close behind you slowly. For itself, it was never parted. The moss grows around the absence at the speed of thought, which is to say it was never absent. Put your hand on a warm trunk and leave it there, and the bark will very gradually make room for your warmth, not by growing, but by having already been that shape.

You are not prey. There are no predators here to care. You are not a guest; guests are expected. You are an anomaly that the system has learned to include by ignoring.

If you stay long enough — an hour, a day, the mesa doesn't measure — you will feel yourself begin to acclimate to its terms. The roar will become silence. The wet heat will stop being oppressive and become simply *wet*. Your own movements will feel too hasty, too linear. You will want to pause mid-step and hold it.

Don't.

The city was built for inhabitants that never evolved — or that were edited out when the weirdness slipped to 2.6 and the world decided to hold its breath. The lichen-tiled domes, the oculus that frames the haze perfectly, the colonnade so regular it must be intentional — they were meant for eyes that never opened. Now they hold only light, water, and you.

What lives here?

A town made of forest, paused mid-becoming.
A population of drops that never fall.
A single, endless second of sound.
A folded distance that keeps the city intimate with itself.
And — briefly, warmly, with too much motion — you, the only thing that will ever leave.
