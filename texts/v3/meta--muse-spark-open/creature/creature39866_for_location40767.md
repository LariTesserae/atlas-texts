Nothing lives here the way a forest lives.

That's what it tells you at first. That it's empty. The stone is too present, too close, the pools too clean. You sit between two green chapels and listen to the hush of a hundred finger-width falls and you think: this is a place for water, not for anything that needs to breathe.

Then you stay a little longer, and the islands begin to insist on themselves.

What lives here lives by the first rule: be an island, or be stone.

**The Chapels themselves are alive.**

Not just *have* life on them. They *are* it. The moss cushions aren't moss the way lowland moss is — thin and opportunistic. Here each cushion is a single organism, or a tight city that has decided to be one. A dome of water-holding emerald, knee-high, round as a loaf, so saturated you could wring a bucket from a handful and still not change its shape.

They grow only where the weeping is constant. A pan in the peat, a pedestal under a drip-line. They don't spread. They deepen. Year on year they grow over themselves, dead generations underneath turned to black peat that acts like a bladder, living generations on top like fur. If you part it with your fingers you see the structure: tight rosettes spiraled in that same clockwise twist as the tree-ferns, all leaning, inexplicably, toward the open lip of the cirque, toward the drop and the blue haze of the lowlands, not toward the sun at noon. They are drinking the air more than the light.

Put your ear close and you hear them ticking. Each moss head collects mist on hair-fine awns and beads it inward, tok... tok... tok, into its sponge-heart. They are the cisterns of this place. Everything else lives because they hold.

Between them, on the bare seep-stone, nothing lives at all. Not even algae for long. The stone there is too wet and too swift; water sheets over it and takes everything with it. Life has learned not to fight that.

**The Grove-Keepers.**

Each clump of tree-ferns is a grove unto itself - five, six, sometimes eight trunks rising from a single wrestled root-mat, squat, shaggy with old frond-bases, leaning out over their own pool. They are old. You can tell by how slow they are. A fiddlehead here takes a week to uncurl, pale green and covered in cinnamon hair, unfolding inch by inch while you watch the drip.

They are not trees. They are islands that learned to be tall to catch what the moss cannot. Their trunks are vertical swamps. Every inch is crowded: filmy ferns no bigger than a coin, orchids with roots like white wire that never touch ground, liverworts glossy as oil, all living on the trunk's damp bark. Vine-ends - those wrist-thick lianas that hang from the rim and never quite reach the floor - find a fern trunk and fuse to it mid-air, rooting in its moss, drinking the humidity that hangs between islands.

At the crown, the great fronds arch twelve feet out, waxy, tipped with a perfect bead of water that never falls until it's heavy enough. When the warm air circulates - that slow inhale-exhale the cirque does - the fronds scrape and creak against each other. That's their language, if they have one. Paper on paper. They shift to spill light onto their epiphytes, or to dump their collected pools right back into the pan below. Nothing is wasted. The grove feeds its own pond.

And underneath, where their fronds drip most constantly, lives what you thought at first was just shadow.

**The Pan-Dwellers.**

Each peat pan pool - those knee-deep mirrors fed by one drip-line and drained by a seep over a stone lip - is its own little sea, and none of them touch. That matters. No creek connects them, only overflow. So each one has evolved alone.

In some, the water is so clear you think it's empty. Then you crouch and see it: transparent shrimp the length of an eyelash, hanging motionless, filtering mist with feathered arms. Blind, colorless. There might be three in a pool. No more. They drift up to eat the skin that forms on still water where pollen lands, drift down to graze algae off the peat. If their pan dries for a day, they curl into a cyst and wait.

In others, a little deeper, there is a newt. Just one per pool. Maybe two. The highland pan-newt, no longer than your thumb, skin the mottled black-brown of wet basalt with a single amber stripe down its spine like a seam of lichen. It has no lungs that matter; it breathes through that skin, which must stay wet. So it never leaves. It walks - slowly, deliberately - around the rim of its world, eating the shrimp, eating the larvae of the invisible midges, laying three eggs a year on the underside of a fern root and guarding them until they hatch into perfect, tiny copies of itself. It is so still you will stare at a pool for ten minutes and think it empty until the newt blinks.

And in one pool - always the one under the big taro leaves - lives the frog you hear but never see. The Plink Frog. It's not hiding. It's just exactly the color and texture of basalt when wet, with even the pits of lichen copied in bumps on its back. It sits half-submerged at the edge where peat meets stone and inflates a throat sac that is clear as water. Plink. Once every twenty or thirty minutes. Not to call a crowd. There is no crowd. Just to say: this pan is taken. Wait for the next.

**The Passers Between.**

What moves between islands is rare because the stone between is true desert to them - bright, scoured, with no grip. So the things that cross are either armored or weightless.

The beetle you saw - thumb-sized, iridescent green-black like oil on water - is a pilgrim. Its wing cases are fused; it cannot fly. It walks. It can take an entire day to cross six feet of bare stone from one moss cushion to the next, clinging with hooked feet while water sheets past. It lives for the moss flowers, tiny red capsules that open only for a week, once a year. It will eat one, lay one pearlescent egg deep in the sponge, and die there. Its larva will live its whole life inside that one cushion, never seeing the next island.

The moths are its opposite. Two of them, white, nearly transparent, high as ash. They don't eat at all as adults. They drift. They are so light the still air can hold them. They go from fern grove to fern grove on the warm, slow current that circles the rim - the cirque's breath - and lay dust-like eggs on the underside of a tree-fern frond. The caterpillars are translucent green inchworms that drink droplets off leaf hairs.

They are all solitary. If you see two beetles, one will turn away. If two frogs plink at once, they space themselves further apart next time. The cirque does not allow choruses. Only solos, with long pauses.

**And the big one.**

Under the overhang, where the rock weeps most steadily, is the giant. A single taro, a single elephant's ear that should not be here at this altitude. Its stalk is as thick as your thigh, its leaves as big as doors, waxy, veined, each one cupped to hold a pint of mist. It is the only one. No seedlings anywhere. Maybe there used to be more and now only this patriarch remains, roots wedged into a crack where water has run since before paths.

It lives by cheating. Its leaves tip - slowly, all day - tipping their collected pool into its own base, watering itself. The glug and splash you hear that is louder than the other drips is it. Leaf after leaf, tipping, spilling, righting. It has made its own rain. Insects drown in those leaf-pools and dissolve, feeding it. Tiny frogs sometimes live in them for a day. It doesn't care. It just holds and spills, holds and spills, a patient engine.

You realize after an hour that everything here is doing the same thing in different sizes: holding water, then releasing it exactly when needed. Moss, grove, pan, taro. Each island is a cup that learned how to keep itself full.

That's why the poured water creeps uphill. That's why the moss leans toward the lip. The whole cirque is a slow, warm lung, and all these islands are cells of it. The air decides how much is allowed to be held, and they have agreed to hold exactly that and no more.

If something intelligent lived here, it would not build. It would sit like you are sitting, between moss and fern, and learn to hold.
