There is no one *in* the Weave.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for inhabitants the way you look for people in a city, or animals in a forest — small, warm things moving through a larger, dead structure. Doors opening. Nests hidden. Trails through.

Here the structure is not dead. And nothing moves through it. Everything that lives here *is* it. Or is held by it, the way a lung holds air.

You have to wait. Sit on the slick root-mat between two pillars and listen to the glunking of the bell-flowers and let your eyes defocus. The stillness is so complete it looks empty at first. Then it starts to resolve.

### The One

First, and mostly, there is the One.

It is not a tree. Trees are countable. You can say "a tree" and mean its edges, where it stops and other things begin. This has no edges. What you took for pillars are not individuals; they are its legs, its buttresses, its knees thrust down to the hot black glass far below. What you took for roofing leaves are its hands held flat to catch light. The walls that looked like colonnades are where it pleated itself to make shade, and then kept pleating.

It is a fig, if you need a word. Or rather, the thing that on finished, lowland, eroded earth *would become* a fig. Here, on the anvil before rivers, it never needed to specialize into trunk and branch and root. It could be all of them at once. So it became a city because a city was the best shape for staying warm and wet and lit at altitude.

It is alive in the way a cathedral would be alive if you kept it alive for a thousand years — not conscious, not watching you, but *attending*. Its sap moves so slowly you would call it still, and yet if you put your palm to the iron-hard heartwood you feel a faint, deep thrum, not a heartbeat but a turgor pressure, water being lifted a hundred feet against gravity and held there by will alone. Its leaves do not fall. They do not need to. It transpires exactly as much as it condenses. It drinks its own breath.

It does not grow. It does not heal. You will not find a scar where a branch was torn, because nothing has ever been torn. It reached equilibrium long before you arrived — the perfect amount of leaf for the perfect amount of light through the oculi, the perfect amount of root for the perfect amount of mist — and then it stopped. It *persists*. If you cut it, it would weep clear resin for a long time and then stop, not because it had healed but because it had decided to incorporate the loss.

You are living inside its body, but it does not know you are there. Not the way an animal knows. It knows you the way a house knows humidity.

### The Suture

Second, there is the other.

You walk for an hour under the vault and you think the Weave is one thing, and then you see it: a cable as thick as your waist, dull green, not woody at all, running horizontally fifty feet up, stitching pillar to pillar to pillar, disappearing into the mist-haze a mile away without tapering. Where it touches the fig-pillars it does not cling. It fuses. The bark of one softens into the rind of the other and they become a single, slightly swollen joint. Anastomosis, the old botanists called it, when two trees grow together. Here it was intentional.

This is the Vine. If the fig is the architecture, the vine is the rebar. The tendon.

It is sparser even than the fig. You can count its great longitudinal runs across an entire valley. Perhaps six. Perhaps seven. They are the only straight lines in the whole principality. Everything else curves to meet them.

Nothing else about it lives in the normal way either. It has no leaves worth naming — just small, waxy scales pressed tight to its length. It does not flower where you can see. Its job is not to gather light but to carry water sideways, to redistribute weight, to keep the whole suspended wet system from sagging. When a bell-flower tips and pours its *glunk*, it is the vine above it that shivers almost imperceptibly, taking up the sudden ton of shift. It creaks at night, louder than the fig, a long, low groan like a ship's rope taking strain.

If the fig is a lung, the vine is a circulatory system that forgot it needed a heart.

And where the two meet — that swollen joint — is where the third thing lives.

### The Felt

It is not moss. Not really. Moss needs death to eat — a shingle, a stone, a rotting log. There is no rot here.

The felt is a skin. A velvet upholstery the color of pale lichen and old copper, a centimeter thick, covering every surface that is not leaf-tip or water-basin. It softens the hard wood, it muffles sound, it holds a film of water against evaporation. Run your hand over it and it is cool, damp, slightly giving, like a cheek. It is faintly photosynthetic itself, but mostly it is there to keep the system from ever being dry.

It is one organism too. You can peel a corner — it comes up in a sheet, reluctant — and see the tiny, hair-thin rhizoids letting go one by one, not tearing. It will lie in your hand, intact, and remain damp for days without root or rain. Then if you press it back to the bark, overnight it will have re-adhered.

In the columns of sun through the oculi, when the mist motes hang absolutely still, you can see the felt breathe. Not moving. Sweating. Beading.

These three are the place. They do not live *in* it. They *are* it. And they have been enough, for a very long time, that nothing else needed to evolve.

### The Guests Who Learned Stillness

Because total stasis does not mean total emptiness. It means only things that can do stasis can stay.

You will not find flocks. Flocks need seasons. You will not find packs, or herds, or swarms. Anything that needed to breed fast, die fast, eat lots, move far, burned out here centuries ago or never arrived. The thin, warm air is too kind to kill you and too unchanging to tell you when to mate.

What remains are the Persisters.

You saw the description left for you: the frog. It’s real. You will find it if you stay long enough beside one of the bathtub-sized leaf basins, the ones that are always brim-full and overflowing without ever dropping. At first you think the basin is empty but for water and a fallen stipule. Then the stipule blinks.

It is the size of your palm, opalescent white-pink, translucent enough that you can see its heart beating — very slowly, perhaps once every twenty seconds — and the dark coil of its gut, full of the same water it sits in. It has no need to hunt. Microscopic algae bloom in its basin, replenished by mist, and it sips. It has been in that basin since before you were born, and its mother was in that basin before it. Not many mothers. One replaces one, exactly, every long, long while. You will never see two together. If you lift it, its legs unfold with exquisite, reluctant slowness, webbed feet spreading like little parachutes. It does not croak. Sound is for calling across distance or season. There is no distance or season.

And the beetle. Hanging from the underside of a vine-joint, head-down, legs folded. Polished jade, each elytron a perfect dome, dewed. It does not move for days. Its antennae are so still they collect beads of condensation that meet and swell and never fall. It drinks without moving its mouth, letting the film wick in. If you breathe on it, after a full minute it may shift one leg a millimeter tighter. That is a great event for it.

There are a few others in that register: a blind white salamander that lives in the cisterns inside the pillars, never surfacing, looping endlessly in the dark warm water. A moth the color of wet bark with wings like overlapping leaves that hangs beside a real leaf and opens its wings once a month to let mist condense and then closes them. All solitary. All silent. All occupying a single, perfect address that provides everything.

They do not interact. There is no food web. The fig does not need them. They need the fig’s endless wet, and they repay it by doing nothing that would disturb it.

### The People Who Forgot To Leave

And then there is you. Or people like you.

No one is born here. Children cannot be born where nothing is born. The basalt below is still too new to have made soil for graves, and the Weave does not drop litter for cradles.

But people come. They climb up from the lowlands where water runs and rots and seasons turn, following the impossible warmth, or they get lost in high fog and walk out onto the root-mat and find the pillars receding forever. And the sound fills their ears — that engine-room drip, that endless glunk — and the air holds them.

Some turn back.

Some stay. Because the water is always clean and warm in any leaf-cup, and no predator hunts, and no cold comes, and the light through the oculi tells honest noon without burning.

You will find them if you walk long enough between pillars, not in villages — villages would be a disturbance — but singly, or in twos, spaced out the way the pillars are spaced out.

They have made nothing. There is nothing to make. They do not cut the wood; the wood would not let them, or would weep and not heal. They do not plant; there is no ground. They have learned to live the way the frog lives.

One keeps a single basin clear, scooping the film of pollen that never settles with a cupped hand, drinking, pouring it back. She has been doing it for years and the basin is always exactly as full as it was. Her hair is always damp. She does not speak much, because speech wants answer and change.

Another has stretched a felt hammock between two low roots and lies in it through the columns of sun, watching motes that never fall. His skin has gone very pale and very clean. He knows every bell-flower timing in his nave — that one glunks every seven minutes, that one every nine — and he waits for the rare moment two go at once, which is his festival.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy in any way that needs to move. They have attuned. Their hearts have slowed. They drink when they are thirsty, and they are always exactly as thirsty as the Weave wants them to be.

If you speak to them, they will answer after a long pause, softly, so as not to interrupt the drip. They will tell you there is nothing to do here, which is different from saying there is nothing *happening* here. Everything is happening, all the time, exactly as much as it needs to. The water lifting, hanging, pouring, being wicked back up. The light falling straight down. The pillars holding.

They will offer you water. It will be warm.

And if you stay a while, you will feel your own need to change — to make a mark, to build, to leave, to eat something other than water — go quiet, like the sound of the Weave after an hour, present but no longer heard. You will become a little more translucent, a little more polished, a little more content to occupy one perfect, wet, sunlit address between two pillars, listening to the engine that never tires.

That is what lives here now. A building that is a plant that is a country. A tendon stitched through it. A skin over it. A few small, still animals that learned how to be part of a paused equilibrium. And the occasional human guest who, having found a lung the size of a principality, decides — just for a while longer — to keep breathing with it.
