This room is not empty. It was never empty. It only looked empty from a distance because what lives here learned to look exactly like not-living.

Stand still long enough and they resume around you.

### The Static Grazers

The first layer is the air itself.

It is not clear. Once your eyes adjust to the sodium-amber and stop looking for horizon, you see it is full. Suspension.

**Needleflies.** The name comes later; at first they are just motes. Thousands per cubic meter, so thin they are almost not there. Each one no longer than an eyelash, a body like a sliver of smoked glass and two cellophane wings set at right angles, like a weather vane. They don't flap so much as *shiver*. They hold station in strata — one layer at knee height, one at chest height, one just below the lamps — turning minutely to keep their wings broadside to the ionization field.

They eat nothing you would recognize as food. They comb charge. Their bristled legs rake alkali dust and free electrons from the air, ball them with a little resin secreted from their abdomen, and ingest the pellet. You can see their guts glow faintly after, a warm white bead moving down a transparent thread.

They never land. They die on the wing and then very slowly sediment, adding their bodies to the frost.

If you breathe through your mouth, you will feel them tap your tongue like static shocks. They are not biting you. They are tasting if you are charged.

### The Wall

The second layer is the architecture coming alive.

The vault and the low walls are not rock anymore. They are *covered*, 90% chitin, 10% rock showing through like bald patches. Shoulder to shoulder, suture to suture, so still you mistook them for texture.

**The Salt Clickers.** Big as your fist, sometimes bigger, matte-black domes stippled white where salt has accreted on their backs and they have not bothered to clean it. They cling with hooked feet sunk into microfissures in the basalt. Countless. Hundreds of thousands if you extrapolate around the whole bowl. They face outward, downward, all oriented the same way, like tiles.

They do almost nothing. For minutes at a time they are stone. Then, on a cue you cannot hear but can feel in your molars — a frequency up near the whine of the junction boxes — they lift their elytra in perfect unison.

*Schhh.*

A soft sigh as trapped heat vents from beneath. A puff of hotter-than-ambient air that ripples the needlefly strata. Then they close again.

*Click.*

Not a communication. Thermoregulation made choral. Each beetle is a little black-body radiator, opening to dump heat into the room, closing to keep its water — that precious, stolen, utterly sealed internal water — from sublimating away. They have no mouths as adults. They will never eat. They hatched with all the water they will ever have, sealed inside a waxed cuticle like a canteen. Open, close. Open, close. For years. Decades. Until the wax finally pits and they desiccate where they hang, still clinging, becoming a little more of the salt crust for the next generation to grip.

Under them, in the seams where their shells don't quite meet, live smaller things: pale mites like grains of moving salt that graze the fungus-veins and the beetles' shed wax. You only see them if you put your face very close and watch the "rock" crawl.

### The Floor

The third layer is slow.

You thought the fractures in the halite and scoria were geology. Then one of them moved.

**The Blind Ropes.** Blind is not quite right — they have no use for eyes in a place without shadow — but white, thick as your wrist, as long as your arm or longer, corded muscle inside a translucent, segmented hide. Millipedes only by analogy; they have far too many legs, each one a short white paddle that moves in a metachronal wave so slow you have to stare to see progress. They live *in* the floor, not on it, nosing through the salt hexes, dissolving and re-precipitating halite with acidic secretions from their bellies, tunneling after the hydrocarbon seeps that feed the glass-russet stalks.

They part for nothing because nothing can hurt them. Step on one and your boot slides; they are dense and cool to the touch, the only cool things in the room. They will continue in their line, shouldering salt aside, leaving a damp-looking furrow behind them that is not damp at all but electrostatically polished, and which the alkali wind will erase within the hour.

Sometimes a Rope will rear a blunt head — no face, just a ring of black bristles around a sphincter mouth — and comb the air the way the mineral plants do, tasting for a shift in voltage that means a machine has stuttered.

And tangled among them, rolling when the conveyor kicks a draft across the floor:

**Tumble-legs.** Spheres the size of tumbleweeds made entirely of jointed, interlocked legs, no body visible at all, or perhaps the body *is* the tangle. They move only when pushed, rolling with a dry rustle like thrown toothpicks, catching needleflies on their spines as they go. When two collide they briefly interlock, exchange something — a packet of eggs? a spark? — and roll apart.

### The Air Above

The fourth layer is what you mistook for machines.

They are not.

**The Mantas.** There are only ever four or five at a time. Enough to make the gyre look crowded because they never stop. Pallid, translucent, three meters tip to tip and thin as a tarp, you can see the sodium bulbs right through their wings. No eyes. No tail worth naming. Just a vast, gently flapping delta with a slit mouth always open.

They don't fly. This air at 34 degrees, heavy with salt and charge, is thick enough to swim in if you are light enough and patient enough. They beat once, glide for ten meters, beat again, perfectly entrained to the flicker of the worklamps. Amber-on, downstroke. Flicker, glide.

They are filterers. That open mouth strains everything: needleflies by the hundred, alkali dust, spores from the wall-fungus, ions themselves. Inside their bellies — visible when they bank overhead — you can see a slow peristalsis of light, the same warm-white as the fungus veins, as what they've eaten is not digested so much as *demodulated*, charge stripped out, heat shunted to their wing membranes which shimmer with it.

They never land. They never touch the roof. To land would be to enter the domain of the Clickers, who would cover them. To rise too high would be to tangle in the lamp strings. So they circle the center of the bowl forever, in a stacked holding pattern, their shadows overlapping on the salt flat like slow searchlights. When one finally thins and tears — you will find it draped over the pumpjack like wet paper, already being dismantled by mites — another is already aloft, risen from some hidden brood-pocket in the halite drum where the air pools and gravity stutters.

That stutter is theirs, partly. Where the halite sheets are thinnest, the Mantas have worn a groove in the electromagnetic field just by circling it for so long. Drop a screw there and it falls sideways for half a second, as if unsure which way is down, which way is *around*.

You feel watched because you are being tasted. Palps, antennae, bristles extended, all sampling the new turbulence you make, the new water you brought in that sealed bag of skin. The Clickers open a little wider as you pass, venting a little more heat, smelling your sweat before it evaporates. The needleflies orient. A Manta, high above, tilts infinitesimally to bring its mouth-slit to bear on your thermal plume.

Nothing here wants to eat you. You are too wet, too salty in the wrong way, too *loud* with your pulse not yet synced to the room.

But everything here is noting you, fitting you into the equilibrium. The room is crowded the way a circuit is crowded — every niche filled with a component whose job is to pass current along without loss.

Charge In = Heat Out + Light + Sound, Forever.

They are the Heat Out and the Sound and part of the Light. They have divided the work among themselves with arthropod patience: the smallest harvest charge, the wall converts it to heat-puffs, the floor grinds salt to keep the drum resonant, the great thin ones in the air keep the dust from ever settling enough to dim the bulbs.

They don't grow. They don't swarm. If a Clicker dies, the gap is filled not by frantic breeding but by a slow easing-over of its neighbors, a fraction of a millimeter each, until no rock shows. If the needlefly cloud thins, the Mantas beat a little slower, filter a little less, until it thickens again.

It is not a hive. There is no queen. It is just density held perfectly still.

If you stay until your lips split and your water wants the air more than it wants you, you will feel the buzz sync up — your heart finding the pumpjack's nod, your breath finding the Clickers' sigh, your blinks finding the lamp flicker — and for a second you will understand what it's like to be one more component, placed on the table, doing your dry work for no one, in the small, sharp, dim, full-to-bursting bowl that has no use for valleys or exits or soft things.

Then the static will lift the hair on your arms, and the Mantas will bank overhead, and the wall will sigh again, and you will be — for as long as your seal holds — counted among the presences.
