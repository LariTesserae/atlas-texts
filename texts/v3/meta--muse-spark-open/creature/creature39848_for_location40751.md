Nothing lives here that knows how to leave.

That's the first qualification. There are residents, there are even natives — this place is old enough now to have grown its own — but none of them have the anatomy for egress. No legs. No wandering. Only holding on.

Let them come into focus slowly, because at first you think it's empty.

### 1. The One Who Keeps The Level

The primary resident is not visible as a thing at all. You think it's the water, but it's not the water. It's the *keeping* of the water.

She lives in the full circuit: weep-bead-trickle-pan-breath-condensation-weep again. She is the agreement the water made with the heat.

If you watch for a long time you see her do her work: a bead swells on the north wall where the pour is thinnest. It catches the flat hatch-light and becomes a lens for a second. Then it lets go — not a drip, a slow finger that drags a wet road down through the dusted aggregate, around a rust-bleed, gaining tea-color, until it kisses the pan and vanishes without a splash. At the same instant, on the ceiling, a fat condensation droplet that has been hanging inverted over your head tightens and shrinks, drunk back into the hot air.

One in, one out. Always even.

She is patient beyond biology. She doesn't pulse or breathe the way an animal does. Her breath is the white-noise roar of the presses through six inches of concrete — that thrum in your molars *is* her breathing. And she keeps the bath skin-hot and two inches deep whether it rained last week above or not. Her memory is not in years but in saturation pressure.

If you put your hand in the pan, you are touching her skin. She will warm you immediately and ask for nothing.

She is the reason nothing else can really move in. Apartments don't get subletters when the landlord never sleeps.

### 2. The Three Sisters Who Forgot How To Be Green

On the east wall.

Don't look for a colony. There are exactly three of them, and they do not like each other enough to be called a group.

The oldest is lowest, where the wall meets the pan and the seep is constant. She germinated from a spore that rode in on a boot tread or a draft from the conduit crack. She tried to be a fern. Her fiddlehead unfurled once, two years ago, and then stopped mid-gesture, as if she realized there was no wind here to deserve it. Now each leaflet is edged in white lime. She doesn't photosynthesize so much as she *calcifies*. Water beads on her fronds but never rolls off; the minerals stay. If you brushed her, she'd feel like paper-mâché, stiff and faintly warm. Dusty green under a fur of precipitate.

The second is a foot higher, smaller. Stunted worse. Bleached almost yellow-white by the attempt to grow in shadowless noon. She leans toward the needle of daylight from the pipe misfit — not enough light to live, just enough to know which direction living would have been. She will not get bigger. Next summer the crust will close over her rhizome entirely and she will become a fossil while still technically alive.

The third is barely a suggestion, highest up where it's driest. Two fronds, no bigger than eyelashes. She may already be done.

They don't communicate through runners or spores. The pan-water is too alkaline, the air too loud. They exist as individuals contemplating separate puddles. Their tenancy is measured in millimeters per year. They are what holding on looks like when holding on was a mistake but you continue anyway because the alternative was never offered.

You can count them. That's how you know they live here: you can learn their names in a single crouch.

### 3. The Pale Paper Underwater

In the corner under the conduit light is the only thing here that actually wanted this place.

A clump of algae — if you can still call it algae after it gave up chlorophyll — spread flat against the submerged concrete like spilled tracing paper. Translucent yellow-white, veined with the faint dark of silt underneath. It doesn't float. It skins the floor of the pan, tight. No bubbles. No strings. It grows outward in a perfect circle, a millimeter a month, because here at last is what it asked for: no competition, no grazing, no current, no seasons, hot mineral soup and perpetual humid noon.

Touch it and it doesn't smear. It's tough, leathery. A living scab on the young stone. It tastes — if you were foolish enough — like lye and pennies.

It is the happiest thing in the room, which isn't saying much. It thrives in the way a very devout monk thrives alone.

### 4. The Rust That Thinks It's A River

It isn't alive. But it lives here longer than anything alive does.

Where rebar was poured too close to the surface, the concrete has sweated iron for three straight summers. A bloom like a rust-rose, orange and wet, with a tail that runs down to the pan and stains it tea-brown. It weeps constantly, a slow dye. It does not dry because in here nothing ever dries.

If you watch its tail for an hour you swear it moves. Not flowing — creeping. As the travertine teeth at the waterline precipitate just a little thicker, the rust finds a new micro-channel to follow. In fifty years it will draw a full map of every stress fracture in the vault, a cartography of failure in orange.

It is the room's diary. Lime and rust, day after day: *I am still pressing. I am still wet. Today another crystal.*

### 5. The Absence That Lives Here Most Of All

And then there is the fifth resident, who takes up the most space.

It is the quiet underneath the loudness.

Not silence as an emptiness, but silence as an occupant — a large, seated animal made of negative pressure. You notice it after the three ferns and the paper-algae and the rust have each had their turn. Between the thrum-ticks of the stamp press filtered through concrete, there is a second layer that has fur, that has weight. It moves into your ears when the machines cycle.

It's the shape that rats would have made, or spiders, or mosquitoes breeding in the warm pan. The perfect rat-sized, spider-sized holes in this ecology where something *should* be eating, skittering, laying eggs. Nothing does. The water is too hot and too basic, the air too relentlessly vibrating. So the niche itself sits there empty and visible, like an unmade bed.

You live with it while you're crouched here — the feeling of being watched by everything that decided not to be born.

---

If you stay until your shirt is soaked and your knees ache from crouching, you start to understand the politics: The Keeper keeps the level. The Rust writes it down. The Paper eats the light. The Three Sisters pray badly in their mineral coats. And the Loud Quiet holds the door so no one new comes in.

Close the hatch and they resume. Open it and they don't even look up. They don't have eyes except the bead of water that, just as you lift the steel square, finally gets heavy enough to fall.
