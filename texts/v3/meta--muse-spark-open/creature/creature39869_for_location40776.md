They live there because the stone lets them.

Not despite it.

You would expect something like humans, holding on. What lives there is something that *belongs* to this kind of place — a place with no soil, no softness, no season. If you came from the surface you would call them pale. If you lived with them you would understand they are the color the basin requires.

### The Gatherers

They are small. Not dwarfish, not childlike - attenuated, like they have been stretched by carrying. An adult stands to your sternum, maybe to your shoulder if it straightens fully, which it rarely does. They are always bent a little forward, head forward, hands ready.

Hairless everywhere except for a fine, colorless down on the scalp and forearms that beads condensation. Skin the color of wet limestone - grey-white, faintly translucent on the throat and inner arms where you can see the blue of blood underneath. In the bright, shadowless light they look almost luminous; in the indigo dim they vanish except for their eyes.

The eyes are the first thing you understand about them, and the last thing you get used to.

Huge, lidless or near-lidless, amber-grey to milk-white, faceted not in structure but in light - they gather the cool selenite brightness the way the salt veins do. No whites. No iris you can pick out. When one looks at you it doesn't *look* so much as orient its whole face toward you, nostrils flaring wide in the cold air to taste your warmth. Their hearing is not in ears - they have only pinholes, sealed against salt dust - but in the soles of their feet, in the bones of their jaws pressed to stone when they sleep.

Their hands are wrong. Or, rather, they are right for the work.

Long fingers, an extra joint in the thumb and forefinger, pads blackened and thickened with embedded stone dust and salt that never washes out. The nails are not nails but dull keratin spades, useful for prying biofilm off rock, for scraping gypsum, for testing a wall for its damp. A child's hands are pink-grey and soft; by age six they are already dark at the tips, like they've been dipped in ink. They talk with them as much as with their mouths. In the sound-swallowed air, language is a flicker-language - palms flashing, fingers snapping against stone to make a vibration others feel through their feet.

They do not sing. They have no songs. The air won't carry one.

### How They Live

There aren't many of them by city standards - a few thousand at most - scattered across those kilometers of bare, polished floor. That's the Density that fools you. The city is sparse, but the people are not. They are *turbulent*. They never stop.

You never see anyone own a house. You see a house being *used*.

In the morning, while condensation still runs in the street-runnels, a family - though family is the wrong word, it's more like a water-claim - will be sleeping in a low, thick-walled bowl-house near the eastern terraces, curled together in a shallow pit lined with cured fungal-fan leather, sharing heat. By evening that same house will be empty, its roof-bowl already half-dismantled by other hands to repair a cistern two streets over, its stones marked with chalky handprints to show they have been counted.

They are nomads inside a city. The city is not a place to them. It is a pile of materials in different arrangements.

Everything moves because water moves. A condensation hollow that gave well last year is dry this year; a salt slab that rang hollow now weeps every dawn. So they follow the weeping. They take apart what is dry and carry it - one limestone slab per adult, balanced on the head on a ring of woven fungal fiber - to where it is wet. Children, who can squeeze into the pitted hollows to scoop with cupped clay hemispheres, are the most valuable members of a water-claim. Elders, whose eyes have clouded with salt cataracts to a blind opal white, sit on boulders and feel vibrations and remember where water *used* to be, fifty, a hundred weeps ago, and are listened to absolutely.

They eat what the basin eats.

The black-ochre biofilm - they call it by a sign that means *the taste of stone* - is scraped with those spade-nails into clay jars, left to thicken in the cool light, then baked on hot salt slabs into brittle, salty skins that crack between the teeth and leave the tongue numb. It is not good. It is enough. The blind white fish from the pools, the size of your hand, are eaten whole and raw while they still twitch, for the water in them. The featherless runners - long-legged, nervous, tasting the walls - are chased not for meat but for the leathery eggs they hide in cairns; to find one is a feast and a festival, consumed in a silent, frantic sharing where everyone gets a fingertip's worth.

The great pale isopods, dog-sized and patient, are not eaten. They are shepherded. Each isopod rolls a perfect sphere of salt and biofilm ahead of it, polishing it larger and larger. When the sphere is thigh-high, the Gatherers gently take it, break it, and render it for salt-cakes that keep the fish from rotting and keep their own blood from thinning in the mineral water. They stroke the isopods' segmented backs in thanks. The isopods do not care.

Their copper is precious because it does not come from here. Tiny native nuggets, hammered thin, are kept for staples to hold cracked roof-bowls together, for chisels that are re-sharpened until they are slivers. A copper chisel is an heirloom. A clay water-jar, perfectly hemispherical, thin as eggshell, fired in shallow pit-kilns fed with dried fungal fan, is a life's work. To carry one on your head without touching it with your hands from cistern to house is how a youth proves they are adult. To drop one is to be silent for a long time while others help you gather the shards for grog.

They do not have leaders. They have convergences.

Several times a day - without bell or call - dozens, sometimes hundreds will flow together on a salt flat around a new-filled pocket-pool or a salt-ball ready to break. For ten minutes there is intense, silent trade: hands flashing, fish exchanged for salt, biofilm cakes for a turn with a copper chisel, a child loaned to another water-claim for a season. Then, as if a current shifted, they dissolve. No goodbye. They just walk away in different directions, pattering, until the flat is empty again except for footprints that will be gone by the next condensation.

They bury their dead under cairns of flat salt slabs, not to mark them but to make a new condensation surface; a good cairn weeps well and will feed a house that hasn't been built yet. The funeral is a run - they carry the body at a lope, silent, to get it away from living water quickly. Only the distant patting of feet tells you one has passed.

### Why They Seem Happy

In the bright, cold, silent hive, with no green in any direction and no future in which the rock changes, you would expect despair. You don't find it.

Their eyes are too wide for despair. Their hands are too busy.

Watch a child - grey limbs too long, belly distended with mineral water - chase the gnat-clouds that hang in the light columns. It leaps, mouth open, tasting them, laughing without sound, its whole body shivering with delight. Watch two old ones take apart a wall together, each knowing the other's next move without a sign, the stones ringing dully under their palms.

They do not think the basin is a prison, or a ruin, or a wound. It is the world that finished becoming itself. It is stable. It is bright. It weeps every day without fail.

Nothing grows. Everything moves. They move with it.

If you stay long enough to learn the hand-sign for *stay*, they will offer you a shallow bowl of cool, stone-tasting water in a perfect clay hemisphere, and wait while you drink, their great light-gathering eyes patient, reflecting the selenite veins above you like you were already a part of the quiet, frantic, shining floor.
