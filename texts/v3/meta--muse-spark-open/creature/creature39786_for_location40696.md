It is not inhabited the way a cave is inhabited.

A cave has residents. Bats, blind fish, things that have committed to darkness and pay for it with their eyes. This place has never committed to anything. It is too bright for cave-things and too empty for surface-things. What lives here are things that live in the intervals.

First, there are the obvious ones. You'll see them first because you expect to see something.

The lizards. *Podarcis* or something very like it, but bleached by generations of living against white stone. The limestone has eaten their pattern. From above they are invisible; from the side, in the low morning condensation-light, you can pick them out only because their throats, when they breathe, pulse a faint, bruised blue — the one color the rock cannot make. There are dozens. More than the place should be able to feed. They lie on the hot slabs with their toes splayed and do nothing for hours. They have learned the economy of the hollow: move only when food moves. When a midge blunders low enough they snap, once, and then go back to being stone.

Below them, smaller:

The frogs. You won't hear them. That is how you know they are not really frogs in the pond-sense, but something that has stayed after the pond left. thumbnail-sized, olive-black with gold dust on their backs like mica. On dry days they vanish entirely — pressed into the green-black velvet seams where algae never dries, breathing through their skin against the sweating rock. After rain they convene. Not a chorus. They gather in that shallow palm-hollow at the lowest dip, shoulder to shoulder in water barely deep enough to cover their feet, and they wait. For what? For the midges to fall. For each other. They do not croak because there is no need to call across distance here. Distance has been taken away.

And the insects, who are the true citizens. This is their room.

Pale, long-legged crickets on the north wall that never jump, only feel. Glassy wasps, thin as needles, working the fig's tiny, hard fruit with a patience you can hear if you put your ear close — a dry, papery scraping. Beetles the color of old tea-stains moving along the algae tracks as if following maps. Moths that have become lichen and lichen that has become moth; you will mistake one for the other until one breathes.

At noon, the midges. On dry days especially they rise in a column where that hard shaft of sun plants itself on the central slab, and they turn. They do not swarm. A swarm has purpose. They hang. They describe slow spirals and hold station in air that should not be able to hold them. If you watch long enough you notice they are not quite falling or flying. They are suspended in that other thing the hollow does.

And then you start to notice the absences, which are also a kind of resident.

No ants. No real predators. A swift will cut across the mouth — a black sickle, there and gone — but it never lands, never nests. It's too exposed and too bright to hide a nest. No snakes, though the lizards seem to expect them. No echo. You can clap on the central slab and the sound goes out a short way and is tired. Too much still air doing nothing. Too much space assigned to each creature. The hollow enforces solitude.

Which makes the stones wrong.

Everything else in the hollow belongs to the limestone. Sandy, pale, pitted, tea-stained. The five or six dark stones on the central slab do not belong. Basalt. River cobbles. There is no river within ten kilometers of this scrubland, no basalt outcrop. Someone carried them down, one by one, in hands.

And someone struck them.

That is the second kind of life here. Not a permanent resident, but a returner.

You can tell by the pattern of work. One stone is a core, struck, with a conchoidal scar and a steep edge retouched just enough to cut meat or scrape hide. One is a hammerstone, pitted in the center where it has hit other stone thousands of times — a dimple worn smooth by percussion. One is stained a faint brown that does not wash off, even when condensation beads and runs over it.

Around them, if you kneel — and the hollow makes you want to kneel, makes standing feel presumptuous — you see the debitage: tiny, sharp chert flakes like fingernail clippings, white frog bones split for marrow, the char-less remains of very small meals eaten very slowly, over seasons. Nothing fresh. No cut mark with a burr. No flies. The last meal here was weeks ago, maybe months. The next will be weeks from now.

Whoever it is, they are not living here. No shelter, no fire, no midden heap. The hollow is too visible from the rim to sleep in safely, too hot at noon, too cold with pooled damp at dawn. This is a work station. A place you come to when the light is right. You bring your stones, you sit where the light pools, and you work for an hour.

And you leave your tools for next time because you know no one else will take them. The lizards don't care. The frogs sit beside them.

You never see the hands.

People who have waited have said the same thing: you come at dawn, you watch all day, and no one comes. You come back after a rain and a new flake has been struck, a bone added. The fig has not dropped its fruit yet, but a fruit has been cut open. It's not shy in the animal sense — animals leave footprints, scat, the drag-mark of a tail. There are no footprints on that slab except your own, which fade quickly in the condensation.

This is where the third resident is, and it is harder to describe because it is not an animal at all, though the animals live inside it.

It is the pool.

Not the water in the palm-hollow. The other one. The light-pool.

On bright days after rain, when the air is still and damp and the white walls have been throwing sun at each other all morning, the bottom meter of the hollow thickens. It's not fog. Fog is grey and moves. This is white and still, a pearlescence like the inside of a shell, like heat haze but without heat. Dust motes and midge wings hang in it and do not settle. Sound goes flat inside it — your breathing becomes quieter to your own ears. Your hand, if you put it in, looks a little further away than your eye says it should be.

Drop a limestone flake into it and it falls... correctly, but reluctantly. A fraction too slow. As if the air were viscous, as if time there were diluted.

The animals know it. The frogs will sit at its edge. The lizards will bask just outside it. Only the midges live *in* it, turning slowly, preserved.

The tools are always just inside it. Just at the edge of where the air gets thick.

And that tells you what kind of hands use them.

Not fast hands. Not human-hurried. The hollow is a place where processes happen across long durations — the fig adding a millimeter, the condensation cutting a grain, the algae advancing a hair's breadth a year. The light-pool is that tendency made visible. A place where emptiness has learned to have weight, where the bowl has held so much light for so long that light has started to behave like a held thing, thick and slow.

Whatever knaps there has to be an animal that can work inside that slowness without being oppressed by it. Perhaps small. Perhaps pale, not like a cave albino but like the lizards — limestone-colored, sun-colored, with eyes that don't squint because this light is its medium. Perhaps with a metabolism tuned to wait all day for a single midge and call that abundance. Perhaps not a relic hominid, perhaps not a hermit, but a thing that *belongs* to this specific mis-tuning — as the blind fish belongs to darkness.

It comes when no one watches, because watching is a fast thing, and it does not move in fast time. It picks up the basalt, strikes once, well. It scrapes, cracks, eats, and sets the stone down again warm from its grip. Then it goes back to whatever seam or under-ledge it presses itself into — maybe into the permanent shade of the north wall where the crickets test the air — and becomes stone again.

You will not see it by looking harder. You see its work because it allows that to remain in your time. The rest of it stays in the thickened air, in the hollow's long, slow agreement with itself.

The hollow, after all, has never learned how to be dark. It has learned instead how to be bright for so long that brightness has become a place to live.

And that place is occupied.
