Nothing lives here the way an animal lives somewhere.

That's the first wrong thought you have to let go of when you understand this bowl. You keep looking for the inhabitant the way you look for a bird in a nest — a body, a head, eyes looking back. A thing tucked *inside* the place.

There is no thing inside the place.

The place is where the thing holds itself.

---

At first you think it's empty. That's what it wants you to think, and for a long time it succeeds, because it does not move at any speed you were taught to recognize as living.

You crouch by one of the channels no wider than your forearm and watch. The water is clear enough to be invisible, carrying only a shiver over amber peat. A petal falls into it. Or rather - let's be precise - a small white flower, one of those stemless stars growing out of the rock, detaches. It does not wilt. It simply lets go when the light tells it to, and drops straight down.

No wind takes it. Nothing bumps it in the air.

On its underside, if you lift it before it wets, you will see the faintest root-hairs of frost. Not frost. Mycelial hair, translucent, already letting go. The flower was never alone. None of them are.

That is the first thread to pull.

Every tree here — the pale, smooth-barked ones with the flat crowns of heart-leaves — keeps exactly thirty feet from the next one. Not approximately. *Exactly*, as far as a seedling can manage. Their roots run shallow under the moss like long fingers deliberately splayed, and they stop short of touching the next tree's fingers. Not because they are competing. Because they are told to stop.

Kneel and press your hand to the moss between them. The ground is warm. Warmer than the air. A faint, low warmth that doesn't come from sun on peat, but from underneath, from the slow friction of something working.

If you hold still long enough your palm starts to prickle. The moss-stools, those knee-high pillars of velvet green, are not moss. Or not only moss. Each is a dense knot of it, yes, but woven through with something paler, something that pulses when you aren't looking directly at it. Pull a piece apart — though after a few minutes here you won't want to — and it comes away in strings, reluctant, beaded with clear jelly. It smells like rain on hot iron. It tries to reattach to itself in your hand.

The whole bowl is one plant. One fungus. One animal. Those words all fail, so use the oldest word: an occupant.

It lives shallow and wide. It does not live deep like a root or high like a tree. It lives *between*. In that half-inch of black soil over stone where your boot sinks and water wells up. It is a sheet, three hundred paces across, as thin as a shirt and as continuous as skin, cupped inside the mountain's cupped hands. The trees are not separate from it; they are its way of lifting leaves into the light. The ferns are not separate; they are its spores. The moss-pillars are its breathing.

You thought nothing touches here because the place is shy. It's the opposite. Nothing touches because everything is already touching underneath. The spacing is not loneliness. It's husbandry. It farms itself. Each crown gets its sun. Each moss-stool gets its column of mist. A tangle would waste light and breed competitors. So it weeded out competition a very long time ago. That is why there are no birds, no insects, no frogs. Not because they cannot climb this high. Because the air here has been made inhospitable to any mouth that would chew a leaf. Lick one of those big heart-leaves — you won't, but if you did — they'd taste bitter as aspirin and numb the tongue for an hour. The white flowers make no nectar. No scent to call anything. The occupant pollinates itself, puff by deliberate puff, smoke that holds together and sinks exactly ten feet away where another part of itself is waiting to receive it.

It is utterly healthy because it has no parasites. It learned, centuries before the basalt softened to flesh, how to be unappetizing to the world.

And the water.

You watched the threads of water braid into the throat-shaft with the collar of ferns and you thought: the mountain drinking its own rain. In a way you're right. The shaft, that perfect meter-round throat in the lowest center, is not just geology.

It's a chimney. A gut. An inhalation.

When you stay till afternoon and the light angles and hits the eastern rim and turns the porous basalt translucent — that sudden honeycomb lace of glowing chambers you see lit up like amber — you are seeing its lungs. The stone sweats because it exhales. The beads gather and fall, one by one, with those long pauses between, timed to a rhythm too slow for a chest but exactly right for a bowl three hundred paces wide. Air condenses on every leaf because it breathes out wet. The slow channels moving inches per minute without gradient are not flowing downhill. They are being pulled, capillary-slow, across its back toward its mouth, to be swallowed and filtered and breathed out again. It circulates the same rain for months, maybe years, polishing it clearer each time. That's why the water moves too slowly to be water. It isn't runoff. It's lymph.

People — the ones with the simple knife, the chipped stone or soft copper, who come to cut the big leaves clean along the midrib and dye them red — have known this much longer than you. They don't build here, they don't light fires here, they don't sleep here, because you don't sleep in a throat. You come, you ask, you pay.

The three leaves overlapped under river stones, half-cured. The pit of rust-red water stained with crushed root and the chewed dipping stick: an offering and a transaction, both. A root that grows outside the bowl is crushed to make a color that will not wash out of the leaves that grew inside it. You take a part of it, you leave a different iron for it. It likes iron. That's why the water stains red. It drinks metal.

Does it mind the cutting? No more than you mind a haircut. The trees are its hair grown long to catch sun. It will grow more. It may even like the attention. Someone noticed its true colors. Someone saw that its leaves cure to something like leather if kept weighted. Someone learned not to tear, but to cut.

Cutting is respectful.

What lives here is patient in a way that makes patience seem hasty. It was a slick of green between rocks when the rim was still sharp. It learned to hold water to keep itself from drying and ended up teaching the stone how to hold water too. Now stone and skin have the same habit. It has settled into itself so thoroughly that weather has become physiology. Late morning light, white-yellow, falling straight down — that's not just light to it. It's food, clock, and touch all at once.

If you put your ear to the amber slab table on the western side, where the leaves have been left to cure, you will hear nothing. For a long time you will hear nothing and think that proves it's dead.

Then very faintly, under the silence that is not quiet but absence, you will hear — not with your ears — the tiny hydraulic sigh as another bead forms on the rim rock, weights itself, and falls. And another thirty feet away answers it.

The bowl is holding its breath.
