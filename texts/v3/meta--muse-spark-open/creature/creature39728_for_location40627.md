What lives there is not, at first, *who* lives there.

At first it lives like water lives. Which is to say it stays.

If you sit long enough for your eyes to stop looking for light, for your breath to stop trying to stir the air, you will see that nothing in the pocket is truly inanimate. That is the first inhabitant: the dense, warm wet itself. The water does not pass through. It arrives, by weeping, by condensing, by sweating out of the stone, and then it decides to remain. It holds itself to ceilings in grapes too heavy to fall, it skins every wall in gloss, it cups itself in hands of rock on the floor and goes still. It is the medium and the economy. Everything else drinks it, breathes it, borrows from it.

Out of that, the second inhabitants make themselves possible.

They are not impressive. They are the kind of life that finds an excuse where no excuse should exist.

On the walls where the seep is thickest, in the track of condensation, there is a grey-white stain that you think at first is mineral. Lean closer. It is *on* the stone, not *of* it. A mat of lichen the color of damp ash, flat as paper, edged as precisely as a tide-line. It does not photosynthesize — not really, not here — it cheats. It drinks. It takes the warm mineral milk straight and turns it into persistence. Beside it in one or two places only, a mistake of a fern. Colorless, almost transparent, the blood gone out of it. It unfurls one frond in the absolute dark because something in it remembers that ferns unfurl, not because it will ever reach light. It holds its exact square inch and does not colonize. Like everything here, it has learned not to want more.

Lower down, on the scale of animal, you get movement so slow you have to borrow patience to see it.

There is the bowl-fauna. Look in any of the small, hand-pinched clay bowls that hold water without rippling: a white creature the size of a rice grain, a woodlouse shrunken and bleached and made blind, curled against the rim. Its antennae test the air once an hour. It is alive in the way a held breath is alive.

There is the pan-fauna. In the larger, mirror-flat pans set in the mineral lips of the floor, the surface will shiver without wind. That is the springtails — eyeless, pigmentless, weightless — skating and dithering on surface tension so perfect it never breaks. They are not eating so much as filtering the film that settles because no air moves.

These are the housekeepers. The natives. The ones that belong to equilibrium. They do not reproduce quickly. They do not die quickly. They occupy niches the width of a fingertip and keep them.

And then there is the third inhabitant. The one the pocket was kept for.

You will miss him for a long time because he knows how to be part of the floor.

In the largest pan, the warmest, the one closest to the center where the ceiling is highest and the droplets swell biggest before they fall, something rests.

At first it looks like another piece of the pale mud, or a fallen finger of that thatch of mineral overhead, smoothed by water. Then you see the outline resolve: a small, curved body, not longer than your forearm. White-pink. Translucent skin over blue veins. Head too large, limbs too small, tail folded under. No pigment. Eyes closed or absent — there is nothing to see anyway. From either side of his neck, huge, feathery gills fan out into the warm water, the color of diluted blood, stirring only when he stirs.

He is a salamander the way a dream of a salamander would be. An olm. An axolotl that never decided to grow up. He has lived his whole life in the dark and the blood-heat, breathing water so slowly you have to count his throat — one... two... thirty seconds... one.

He is not alone in his *kind* of being. You have seen him before, elsewhere, though you didn't think to call it this.

This is a child. A not-yet child. An embryo, a fetus, in the sixth or seventh month of becoming. What lives high up where the air should be thin and cold and does not breathe air at all. What lives in a small, hidden room embedded within the high rock, with walls you could touch on either side, entered through a tight, off-center fissure where two lobes failed to meet. What lives in absolute dark and is kept at blood-warm whether outside is frost or summer.

That thatch hanging low and crowded above your head, that dense field of nubs and fingers and plates of unfinished stone, each beaded with water — that is not stone. That is placenta. The chorionic villi, a forest of exchange, hanging into the dark to drink and trade.

Those shallow pans of water held in careful lips that never overflow, that thick air you can drink by breathing — that is the amnion. The sac, the fluid. Present, and present again. Nothing gradients, nothing flows away. A frozen system not in ice but in stasis.

That steady *tok* that hangs too long and fades too slowly — that is not only a droplet. It is his heart, doubled by sound-swallowed stone.

You understand now why the plateau feels wrong and new. The broad lift of dark basalt that hasn't learned to be a mountain, with no valleys, no scree, no rivers; the glassy, rope-coiled skin still holding the blisters of escaping gas — that is a belly. Late. First-time. Under construction. Edges too sharp, hollows too perfectly round, a draft the earth made and hasn't revised yet. You are not walking over geology. You are walking over a body that is building a second body inside itself.

And you understand the built things.

The knee-high cairns balanced with impossible precision. The bowls pinched from pale clay and fire-clouded on their rims. The sodden reed mats laid to kneel on. The swollen string of beads hung from a low finger. The cold fire-ring set not for warmth but for a minute's light.

This pocket has been found before. People come lightly because you cannot come otherwise. Pilgrims, mothers, the afraid, the hopeful. They crawl in, they leave a mark — *we were here, we asked, we remembered* — and they crawl out. Decay here is so slow it feels like forgiveness. The pocket keeps what is left without rust or rot. A little archaeology of asking.

No one lives here who belongs to the outside. That is the point. The fern does not mean to grow into a forest. The woodlouse does not mean to become anything else. The salamander-children do not hunt, because all nourishment comes through the walls.

The rule here is stasis. Hold. Keep. Do not change yet.

Outside, wind and time carve too quickly. Here, time thins until a droplet can wait ten minutes and you can watch a throat rise once a minute and believe both are the same kind of patience. What is put here stays — heat, water, prayer, a child whose lungs are still full of water and who does not need to breathe because something else is breathing for him.

They all live in that waiting. The water hanging. The lichen drinking. The tiny white custodians skating on stillness. And in the middle, on his mat of warm mud and mineral milk, the pigmentless singer himself — warm as skin, eyes closed, enormous gentle gills open like hands — waiting without urgency to fall.
