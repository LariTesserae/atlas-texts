What lives here doesn't *inhabit* it. Not the way you inhabit a house. It *keeps* it.

### At first, you think nothing does.

That's the trick. You come over the lip expecting a jungle in miniature - bugs in clouds, birds, monkeys calling. A hothouse should be crowded. This one is not. It's too spare for that. Too curated.

For a long time after you enter, you think you are alone with the plants and the dripping.

You are not.

Listen again.

That *pit... pit... pit...* from the walls - after an hour it stops being a drip. It becomes a count. The dragonflies come first to teach you how to see it.

There are only four or five of them, and they are too big. The span of a hawk, the weight of nothing. Their wings are not chitin; they are sheets of mica, hammered thin, veined with black. They don't beat their wings, they *row* the thick air, and in normal air they'd fall like stones. Here, they glide too slowly, as if the air catches them. You hear every stroke when they are in front of the pylon's lens - a dry *thwick thwick thwick* - and then the sound cuts off mid-beat when they pass behind one of the invisible rooms in the air. They go silent and reappear two meters on, a little lower than they should be.

They are not hunting. There is almost nothing for them to eat. They circle the bowl, endlessly, on patrol. Not animals. Or not *only* animals. They are thermometers, barometers, shepherds. They read the drip. When one terrace gets too wet, a dragonfly will hover over it for an hour, fanning, drying. When a fern clump gets too hot, it will stir the air above it. When you stand too long on the dry plate in the center, one will come and hover in front of your face, looking - not at you, but at the column of disturbed heat coming off you, judging whether you belong in the cycle.

They work for something.

### The second thing you notice is the white thing.

You only see it as a flicker at first. In the barrel pitcher plants. The pitchers are dark wells, full of warm, tannic water, full of the slow, digesting darkness, and you think they're still. Then a white shape darts under the surface of one, fast as a minnow, too fast. It hits the wall of the pitcher and doesn't stop - it goes *through* the plant, through the stone, and erupts in the next terrace's pan, fifteen meters away, without a ripple in between.

You crouch. You wait. It happens again.

It's small, the size of your thumb from tip to knuckle, blind, featureless, white as new root. Not a fish, not an amphibian. It has no business in this much space. It doesn't swim; it translates. Where the gravity is polite but negotiable - where a rivulet runs uphill for a handspan - there is a seam, and it lives *in* the seam. The water flows toward the lowest floating step and the breathing cube because it is being called, and this thing rides the call. It is a single animal in many places. Or many animals that are a single thing. You count splashes and realize you never see two at once. You throw a pebble into a pan just as it dives, and the pebble falls slower. The white thing is not water-fauna. It's gravity-fauna. It eats the difference between where water should go and where it does go. It eats the error.

There are dozens of them, or one with dozens of entrances. If you put your hand in the warm water where one just vanished, your fingertips go numb and slightly *late* for a second after you pull them out.

### The third thing you hear before you see.

*Coo.*

Low, soft, from the terrace with the umbrella trees. Ten minutes later, *coo* - from the moss terrace, impossibly far for anything to have moved without you seeing it.

You never see the caller. You start to think it's the bowl itself, a stone settling. But then you find the molt.

Under the overhang where the orange lichen is thickest, tucked into a basalt fold that weeps more than the others, there is a husk the size of a child, translucent, empty, still warm. It is shaped like nothing - a stretched sac with too many thin feet that ended in little suction cups that match the stair-step columns perfectly. The lichen grows *into* it, through it, circuits lighting faintly inside the shed skin. You understand then that the caller was here, and left, and is still here.

It moved when you blinked. It lives in the saccade. It lives in the acoustic rooms in the thickened air, where sound carries too well and then stops. It steps from one silence to another. That's why the dragonflies go silent mid-beat - they are passing through its hallways.

It is huge and shy and it tends the garden.

### And then the garden itself tells you.

You thought the vegetation was scattered - island groves, empty stone. You had it backwards. The empty stone is not empty. The orange lichen *is* the inhabitant. The trees, the ferns, the moss mats, the barrel pitchers - those are its flowerpots.

Look properly. The lichen does not grow *on* the walls. It grows *into* the fresh basalt, etching branching circuits that pulse after sunset. Not bioluminescence. Residual heat working its way out through a nervous system. From the walls it runs under the terraces, a thin, bright web under the skin of water, connecting every pan. Each lush clump is a node where it decided to push up something green, to do work - to transpire, to evaporate, to sweat, to keep the loop going.

It planted the umbrella trees to catch the drip. It planted the ferns to hiss and unfurl and move air. It planted the pitchers to make still, warm, tannic pools for the white things to breed in. It left bare stone between them because bare stone is hottest and makes the water rise fastest.

It keeps its weather. The bowl is not a place where weather happens. The bowl *is* weather, enclosed. A lung. And the four built things are not separate from it.

The humming pylon with the delayed lens - that is its sphincter, keeping the high-altitude air pooled in, keeping the outside out, keeping the pressure thick. The three humming rods - those are its pins, holding the lichen down. The seven floating steps - those are not a walkway. They are condensers, where water goes to be still and perfect and then be released. The small black cube that inhales dust and gnats and exhales cool iron air - that is its nose. Its mouth.

You realize the pylon's distortion never collects condensation because the lichen *avoids* it, the one thing it doesn't touch.

The people who put the white pylon and the black cube here - they were here a very short time ago, in the life of a mountain this young. They drilled, they dropped, they sealed, they left. Or they tried to make a house for something that would keep itself. They didn't stay to live in it because nothing that sweats and needs cold at night can live in air that's already full.

This thing can. It likes it hot, wet, quiet, enclosed. It likes that gravity is negotiable here, because it is heavy and slow and needs to save energy. It likes that water finds your feet, because it drinks through the stone.

You standing on the dry plate in the center - the one place water avoids - you are standing on its dry tongue. The place it keeps for the sky to look in. That's why the distortion of all four pylons meets there, a faint soap-film across the mouth. It's looking up through you.

If you stay too long, your sweat stops evaporating because it has taken all the air it can hold. If you stay longer, the dragonfly will come and hover, and the *coo* will come from right behind you, and the lichen will have grown a millimeter toward your boots while you weren't looking.

It doesn't mind you. You are a brief disturbance in humidity, a little extra water it will condense out later.

But it does live here. All of it. The bowl is not containing life.

The bowl *is* alive.
