They are not many. That's the first thing to understand about who lives there, because in any other city number would be the first fact. Here number is the wrong fact. Sparseness is not a failure of population. It is the population.

The people who live on the Table are the people who agreed, without ever having to say it, to keep the intervals.

You see them first not as people but as punctuation. A woman on a causeway a quarter kilometer away with a basket. A man on a terrace wall mending a lichen-rope with his hands. Two older people sitting outside a low house with stone-warmth still in it, not talking, shelling tubers into a common bowl. They don't cluster. There are markets, but you have to be told they are markets — three or four stalls of slate and wood set fifty meters apart around a cistern-plaza, each with exactly what that week required and no more, each tended by someone who will be there until the amber six minutes and then not after, because after is not a time.

They are all ages, but not in the way lowland towns are all ages. There are children, but you will mistake them at first for small adults because they move at adult tempo. No running, no shouting across avenues. A child here learns early that sound carries too well. If you speak at normal volume on the Table you are shouting. If you shout you are a storm. So they learn to walk beside you and tug your sleeve and wait for you to bend down. Their toys are what you would expect from a place that quarries its own world: basalt beads polished until they click, pine hares carved with the adze-marks left in on purpose, slate tablets they can scratch and wash clean in a cistern. They do not break them.

And the adults —

They are builders, but not the way you think of builders. No one here is paid to build. No one is ordered to build. A house appears where a house is needed, which is rarely, because no house falls down. When it does appear, three or four people build it together over days, fitting dark stone to dark stone so the courses meet without mortar, pinning slate shingles with wooden pegs one by one. You can see different hands in the same wall. No one smooths this out. That unevenness is signature, is biography. The man who laid the north course likes his stones a little proud. The woman who laid the south course hammers hers flush. The wall remembers them both, and then, once finished, it stops remembering in the way you expect walls to age. It doesn't weather. It *is*. It joins the equilibrium.

That is their work, mostly: not to create but to tend the *already-finished*.

There is a librarian, but there are no borrowers, only readers. The library is those open-air shelves of slate tablets under a long larch-wood roof without walls. Rain can't get in at its angle; wind doesn't get in because wind here has been asked not to. The librarian — sometimes an old woman with stone-colored hair, sometimes a younger man with pine sap under his nails; the role moves, not the person — dusts the tablets with a hare-skin brush even though no dust settles. She re-slots tablets that have been read and left slightly out of true, not because the valley needs her to but because she likes the click they make when they settle. The tablets are not history, though outsiders think they must be. They are record-keeping of a place that does not change: water levels in cisterns taken at the same hour every third day; counts of fish holding station in bend three, bend seven, bend twelve; the yield of tuber rows that yield the same every time. They record it anyway, with care, as if fidelity itself were a form of prayer.

There is a water-tender, but there are many water-tenders, because water is the real citizenship here. Every house is responsible for its cisterns, those perfect sky-mirrors in basalt hollows. Every morning after the condensation-run, someone — daughter, father, grandmother, whoever woke first — goes out with a basalt ladle and skims the overnight film, lifts a leaf, checks the level with a notched pine stick, not because the level ever changes unexpectedly but because checking is how you say *I am here, you are here*. Along the river, the aqueduct-watchers walk the trestles slowly, looking for the crack that will never come, tightening lashings that do not loosen. They do it in silence. The fish below hold their places as they pass.

That silence is not an absence in them. It is a skill.

They speak quietly because the Table taught them to. Voices here are low and unhurried, vowels a little longer, as if to give the cool air time to carry them without bruising. They do not interrupt. They do not need to be heard over anything, and so they have learned to hear under everything. A rock hare's feet on slate at thirty meters — they hear that. The moths opening on the roof at dusk — they hear that too, a sound like paper breathing. They greet each other by standing near for a moment first, before words, to let the other person's equilibrium notice them and adjust. Only then: "Water held." "Water holds." "Need a hand on the west terrace?" "Come at second shadow."

No one is in a hurry because hurry is a downstream idea. Hurry assumes erosion, assumes flood, assumes rot. On the Table nothing hurries because nothing is running out. That makes them patient in a way that lowlanders mistake for slow. They are not slow. Watch a mason split stone: one look at the grain, one tap to set the feather, one clean crack along a line you didn't see until it existed. That's not slowness. That's a person who has done a motion exactly enough times that time has thinned around it.

And they are — this is important — not dead, not ghosts, not preserved. They get hungry, they get tired, they get old in their faces, though old arrives differently here, as a kind of clarification rather than a collapse. Skin thins to show the stone underneath. Hair goes the grey of lichen. Joints stiffen a little in the dawn frost. But they don't decay the way you expect. A cut heals clean in a day and leaves no scar, because scar is a story about change and the valley prefers stories that end where they began. They sleep — deeply, under heavy woven covers — and wake at the same coolness every morning to breath made visible, and that suffices.

If you stay long enough they will stop treating you as a visitor, which is to say they will give you space. Someone will leave a spare basalt ladle by the cistern nearest where you sleep. Someone will leave an empty slot on a terrace wall where your stones would fit if you wanted to lay a few. They will not ask you to stay. They will not ask you to leave. To ask would be to assume the valley has an opinion about coming and going, and it doesn't. Or rather, it has exactly one opinion: *Whatever you do will be accommodated as if it had always been there.*

That is the other thing that lives there, and it lives in them, and under them, and around them, and they know it the way you know your own blood doesn't need to be told to circulate.

The city itself lives.

Not metaphorically. Urban without dense is not an aesthetic choice. It is a metabolism. The terraces are lungs. The aqueducts are veins that chose to go overland. The hundred-meter avenues are not empty; they are the city's pauses for breath, the deliberately kept emptiness that lets moss and lichen and hare and human each have a station to hold, like those river fish each in its exact place. The moths on the roofs are not pests; they are the city's eyelids, opening and closing with the light. The tablets in the library are not memory; they are the city's habit of checking its own pulse every third day and finding it the same.

And the plateau under the city lives with a different age. It is so young it doesn't know how to be old, and so it has been made — or has made itself — unable to learn. It lives the way a perfectly cut V-ravine lives: by remaining perfect. It outsources change to the people, who provide just enough motion — a wall built, a tuber harvested and replanted, a fish taken and replaced — to keep stasis from feeling like death.

You will go down at some point to the river at noon, when the light is most honest, and you will see one of them standing knee-deep in the long lake-with-a-current, utterly still, hand hovering over water where a fish holds station. She is not hunting. She is participating. She could take it. The valley would replace it by evening, same size, same place. She knows this. The fish knows this. She is deciding whether, today, the story needs to include taking. Often, it doesn't. Often she just lowers her hand, touches the cold for a second, and wades out, leaving the fish exactly where it was, because continuity is also a kind of harvest.

At dusk when the amber comes for its six exact minutes — and you will check your watch, if you brought one, and find the valley is more exact — they all stop. Whoever is on a wall, whoever is on a trestle, whoever is reading a slate tablet or shelling tubers, they stop and turn their faces slightly up. The moths open fully. The gliders settle. The water in every hollow goes still enough to hold the sky without a flaw.

They are not worshipping. They are aligning, the way you align a stone before you tap it.

Then the amber holds, without fading, without night falling, and in that held light you finally understand them not as inhabitants but as contents. As what this place — this young, sharp, sparse, even-lit, water-pocketed, hand-built, deliberately spacious, shockingly abundant and shockingly quiet, never-changing city that is everywhere and nowhere — chose to keep inside itself to prove it was inhabited at all.

They live there by keeping the intervals. And the intervals, in return, keep them.
