It is not inhabited the way a house is inhabited. It is inhabited the way a throat is inhabited — by breath, by bacteria, by the thing that taught the throat how to be a throat.

There is no village here, though from a distance you might think there was. No mayor. No children. What lives here lives by permission of the drip.

### The Ones Who Tend

You will not meet them so much as coincide with them.

They wear matte rubber to the knee and canvas that never fully dries. Their boots are the same dark green as the moss on the pipe lagging, which is not an accident — everything that stays here long enough becomes that color. They do not speak inside the bowl because there is no point; the thrum eats small talk and the water answers for you. If you watch one for an hour you will see the whole liturgy:

Scrape the intake. Check the valve. Lift the grate, scoop the fallen figs before they clog the channel to the next terrace down. Pinch back the tomato runner that has decided to eat a pressure gauge. Not weed. Never weed — only *redirect.* 

They are not farmers and not engineers, or they are both so completely that the words have fused. They understand Rule #2 implicitly: If something is dry, it is broken. They carry moisture meters, but mostly they carry the backs of their hands, held out to feel where the film of water has broken over tile.

There are never more than two or three in the bowl at once. Perhaps they live outside and walk up in shifts. Perhaps they were grown here, like the rice, from a cutting left in a puddle. They leave no footprints that last; the grating erases them, the water carries them to the pan.

You are not one of them. You are a visitor the bowl tolerates because you are also mostly water and briefly warm.

### The Green Workers

The real citizens are the plants, but to call them plants is to misunderstand their employment status.

* **The Moss** is the majority party. It holds office everywhere. Emerald *Bryum* on the sun-hammered upper rim where the concrete is still off-gassing and hot to the touch; stringy lime *Anomodon* hanging from the undersides of every pipe like wet wool; and down in the shade of the lower terraces, the black-green sphagnum that grows a hand thick, so saturated it quivers when you step near it. It is lagging, insulation, bandage, and lung. It drinks steam directly out of the air and when you squeeze it, warm water runs out like from a wrung-out towel. If you peel it back — and you shouldn't — you find the iron beneath has been kept wet and airless so long it has stopped rusting and started turning smooth and black, preserved.

* **The Ferns** are the opportunists. Maidenhair in every expansion joint, its black wire stems trembling in the condensation curtain. Tree ferns where a terrace cracked wide enough to hold a little detritus. They are the bowl's way of testing new plumbing; where a fern appears overnight, water has found a new route. Follow them and you can map the seeps before the maintenance workers do.

* **The Fig** is the landlord. It was not planned. A bird, or a boot, or a wind-borne seed found the one perfect failure — a boiler inspection hatch that never sealed — and germinated in the soot and fig-mash inside. Now its trunk is extruded flat, pinched to two inches where it passes through steel, then exploding outward into a low, lateral canopy that has learned that sunlight comes straight down and steam comes up. It fruits ceaselessly because it never gets cold. The figs fall with soft, flat *plops* into the channels, ferment slightly, feed the bacteria in the pan, feed the boilers when the workers scoop them. It has its own weather system; stand under it and it is raining from transpiration even when the pipes are not.

* **The Crops That Quit Cropping.** Tomatoes that have gone feral-sweet and small, sprawling from gear teeth, from rust flakes, from the pocket of a forgotten coat. Rice, taro, cattails in the stainless steel trays on Tier Four — still flooded an inch deep because the levelers still work — but no longer in rows. The rice tillers wildly, the taro pushes up elephant leaves, the cattails spike up brown and shatter into fluff that sticks to wet tile and germinates. And the philodendrons the original designers hung from catwalks — leaves as big as doors, aerial roots like brown yarn never bothering to find ground because the air itself is 100% humidity. They are curtains, sails, and second roofs.

None of this is decorative. Every leaf is a condenser fin. Every root is a wick. The bowl specified *photosynthesis* and *evapotranspiration* on a blueprint, and this is what showed up for work.

### The Tiny, Engineered Gods

If the plants are the workers, the animals are the maintenance crew for the workers.

They are few, and they were chosen:

**Bacteria** — pink *Nitrosomonas* and white crusts of lime-precipitating bacillus blooming on the concrete, actually curing it faster, sealing hairline cracks with their own bodies. You can smell them, faintly sharp and sweet, under the green smell.

**Lichen and Rust** — orange rust scales that flake off a pipe elbow, fall a centimeter, and become an island. Within a day, lichen stipples it silver-green.

**Bees** — three or four at any time, impossibly fat bumblebee queens engineered for thin air and sulfur tolerance. They do not buzz so much as *drone*, slow and low, crawling deep into tomato flowers and fig syconia. They are the only thing in the bowl that seems sleepy. When they fly, their wings shed water droplets.

**Water Striders** on the central pan, skating on warm water so thick with algae and infusoria it is opaque as pea soup, ankle-deep and bathtub-hot. Under them, in the black ceramic pan that is the heart, a soup of duckweed, mosquito larvae that never become mosquitoes — something was edited out — daphnia, and filamentous green algae that strings like hair when a worker drags a brush through it. This pan is the stomach. Everything that falls eventually arrives here, dissolves, and is pumped back up as boiler feed.

Once an hour, a **swift** cuts across the open sky above the rim — a real wild thing, high-altitude, cold-adapted. It looks down at this impossible warm wet coin of light and steam, this greenhouse on the roof of the world, and does not land. Its refusal is part of the ecology; the bowl is too complete to need outsiders.

### The One That Is The Bowl

And then there is the thing that actually lives here, of which all the above are organs.

It is not the concrete. It is not the plants. It is the *cycle* that has learned to wear them.

Call it the Drip. Call it the Loop. It was poured, then planted, then piped, and at some point the pipes and plants and water agreed to keep each other going without asking permission.

It lives as steam that pines for leaf, becomes bead, becomes curtain, becomes trickle, becomes pan, becomes pump, becomes boiler, becomes steam again. It lives as heat that refuses to leave the rim, pooling like invisible water. It lives as concrete that is still chemically alive, still curing, still shrinking and pinging at night and opening new cracks that weep lime milk that feeds moss that holds water against concrete that cures slower.

It is young. That is the uncanny part. An old mountain is eroded into being; this is *eroding into being* in reverse, soft edges getting softer before they decide to be hard. It shivers when the pressure valve sighs. It sweats continuously. It is womb-warm because it is still being born.

If you stand on the rim at night under the amber worklights while the steam coils up and catches the light and the fig tree shivers though there is no wind, you can feel it respiring. Inhale: boilers sigh, mist gathers, tile beads. Exhale: leaves drip, channels gurgle, heat lifts. It does not know you are there. It does not need to.

It is a small country, three hundred meters across, and it contains a rainforest that contains a factory that contains a spring that contains itself.

And as long as everything drips, it is alive.
