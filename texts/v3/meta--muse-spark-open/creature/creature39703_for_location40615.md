At first you think nothing lives here.

That's what the quiet does. It's not the quiet of abandonment — no broken windows, no wind through empty sills. It's the quiet of insulation. The basin holds its breath. The leaf-roofs take sound and keep it. The avenues of living wood and factory brick are so vast and so empty that you walk for minutes hearing only your own feet on the catwalk and the tick of water moving somewhere behind bark, and you think: oh, this is a ruin that grew over.

Then a door that is not a door dilates, and someone steps out.

The people who live here are the first thing, but not the most numerous thing.

There aren't many of them. Three hundred in a bowl made for ten thousand. That's why the scale feels wrong — every building is a tenement that became a tree, big enough for five families, occupied by one or two. You pass a whole colonnaded avenue vaulted in grafted willow and alder, light coming down green through the old tramline, and you see only one old man on a root-stair, paring back the lip of a window with a hooked knife the way you'd trim your nails. You walk another block of root-mat housing — four old brick houses swallowed and fused into a single burl-hollow, balconies of hard shelf-fungus jutting out — and you hear one child asleep in a hammock of live cable-vine, swaying a little every time the building exhales.

They are thin and pale in the way of people who live in October all year. The air in the basin is cool even in high summer, cool and damp and never quite dry; so they wear wool that never fully dries either, waxed canvas aprons, soft boots that come off at every threshold. Their hands are always damp. They have the soot under the fingernails that never washes out, and the green under the soot from pruning. Children here learn to prune before they learn to write. Not cut — prune. Cut is a wound. Prune is a negotiation. You coax a leaf-door shut for winter. You open a leaf-window in spring with a thumb on either side of the seam until the two leaves part with a soft sigh. If you leave any square of catwalk alone for a week, the root-mat will lip over it, polite and firm, and reclaim it. It's impolite to make it withdraw, so they keep moving.

They are not farmers. They are not foresters. They are mediators. Every human household here tends one machine the way you would tend an animal too large and old to name.

That's the second thing that lives here: the industrial things that stayed industrial, but became plant.

The sawmill-basilicas still have their great low diesels under wraps of root-felt, turning over once every few seconds — *thud ... thud ... thud* — not loud enough to hear except through your feet. They don't saw much anymore. They turn to pump. Sap up, water around, condensation along. On the roofs, the moss fields charge. It's a black-green pelt, two inches thick, that holds voltage the way a battery holds charge; by late afternoon it trips the old copper streetlamps on, one after another, without a switch. In the smokestacks — you can stand at the base and look straight up — the filter-lichen hangs in curtains, orange-black and fleshy, expanding and contracting with the draft, breathing in the cool air with a slow sigh all afternoon and humming as it strains the last soot out of it. At 3 p.m. the western quarter pops. That's the only word for it: all the factory-engineered avenue-flowers — pale, paper-broad blooms the size of dinner plates grafted along the old utility lines — open at once, *pop-pop-pop* down the line, and turn their faces a fraction toward the overcast-bright sky, and the whole light in the quarter shifts.

And underneath, always, the looms. In the lower workshops where the light goes greenest, the vine-looms are still weaving. Thick air-roots feed in one end, quilts of insulation fiber come out the other, black-green, warm to the touch. No one ordered them. No one picks them up most weeks. The bolts pile, neatly, in the damp. The humans sweep around them, oil the wooden parts, keep the vine from strangling its own shuttle, because the agreement says you tend your machine even if you don't need what it makes. The machines and the plants have an agreement. The people keep the agreement.

Mostly, though, what lives here is the town itself. Or the forest that thought it was a town.

It isn't that the buildings are *covered* in plant. The plant *became* the building's plan. You can read the old grid in the way the load-bearing vines grow straight and right-angled, in pillars where the streets were, in hollow burls where the blocks were. The tenements are still brick underneath, pitted and soft-edged with age, but the walls inside are warm, bark-warm, sweating slightly to the touch. The plumbing is vine, tapped and valved, carrying condensation from the catwalk gutters to the round dark plaza pools where a building was removed to let the sky in. The avenues are tunnels of woven willow where the tram ran; the tram is gone but the straightness remains because the trees remember to be straight. The ironwork catwalks are still iron, but the rust has been arrested at a weatherproof orange-black by lichen and is held there, not decaying further, not healing either. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is new. The roots don't crack the foundations anymore; they *are* the foundations.

This is a very active place that looks very still. Like a tidal pool, like a beehive behind glass. Sap pumps tick. Cistern valves click open when they brim. Water beads on every surface by morning and runs together into threads by noon, into bark-gutters, into barrels the size of rooms; every three or four days the outer leaves get too heavy and release it all as a brief, polite rain that drums on the canopy-roof and vanishes. The engines breathe, the flowers turn, the quilts pile up. All of it muffled by living insulation until the loudest thing is usually a leaf-door.

That leaves very little room for other animals. The town didn't invite them.

There are bees — a few true ones — kept in wall-hives pruned into the living colonnades, moving in managed numbers from flower-facade to flower-facade. And there are their mimics: the pollinator drones, gossamer-winged, the size of a thumb, that run on the moss-field voltage and tick from bloom to bloom on a schedule more precise than any beekeeper's. No flocks. No pigeons. The roots exude something rats dislike; they left a generation ago. Sometimes, at the Canal — that straight, black, still cut that still carries one barge back and forth, back and forth, for no reason anyone can remember except the mechanism still works — you will see a heron. Just one. It stands in the shallows like a statue someone forgot, watches the barge pass, and does not seem surprised that you are there. You don't see it twice in the same week.

Those who live here move sparely through abundance. They collect water where it wants to gather; they never try to make it gather elsewhere. They keep to the sparse paths; they let the green keep its avenues. At night the lamps come on early on plant power, green-tinged, and you see them in windows that are not cut but grown open, one light per vast building, a small warm proof of presence in all that living mass.

If you stay, you learn to leave your shoes at the edge of a root-mat floor. Inside, the living walls are warm despite the cool air. Somewhere far above, the factory whistle blows — not loud, not urgent, just marking the hour for no one — and the leaves take the sound and keep it.
