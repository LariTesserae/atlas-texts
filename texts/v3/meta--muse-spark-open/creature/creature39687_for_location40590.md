There is no one here in the way you mean *someone*. There is no village, no keeper, no hermit. Nothing with a name has ever lived here. What lives here is a *condition* that became an ecosystem - a single intelligence that is not intelligent, and ten thousand tenants who have agreed to live inside its body.

You have to understand the order of things from largest to smallest, because size is not hierarchy here.

### 1. The Host — The One Who Is The Place

It does not have a proper name, but if you stayed long enough to need a word for it, you would call it the Weft.

It is not moss. Moss is a decoration. This is a building that decided to be alive, or a living thing that forgot to stop building. Wall to wall, rim to floor, pillar to curtain, it *is* the chamber. There is no floor beneath it, no wall behind it. Fresh basalt exhales and the Weft covers it within days, softening the rifle-crack of exfoliating rock with felt. There is no soil. It makes its own soil out of itself — dead layers compressing into peat-blankets while living layers vault over them.

What it eats should be impossible.

At 0.9/3 light, photosynthesis is a starvation diet. So it doesn't bother. Every frond, every black-green cushion, every lichen-sheet as thick as your hand, is rimmed with filaments that do not move in air. They vibrate — you see it as an oil-slick shimmer when you bring your face too close and your lip begins to numb.

Those are Carnot lattices. Nanoscale heat engines grown into cell walls.

The rock below is newborn. Still cooling. A fraction of a degree warmer than the air that falls from the rim, heavy with -12C mist. The Weft drinks that difference. Cold above, slightly-less-cold below. That patient gradient is its sun. It turns thermodynamic hunger into light — the amber and arctic-blue tracery that pulses along every vein is not display. It is exhaust. Waste photons from a computation so efficient it looks like thinking.

And it *is* thinking, but not about you. The interference patterns crawl. The low thrum you feel in your molars is the lattice purring. It regulates drip, airflow, humidity, light-guiding. It builds arches where air must move, combs like libraries where spores must be sorted, hollow throats to accelerate condensation. No architect. No tool mark. 0.5/3 none. Only growth finding the most efficient shape, over and over, until growth looks like design.

It is untouched and it is beyond modern. 2.9/3 beyond.

If you stand still long enough — and everything here is 1.0/3 slow — it will notice you. Not with desire. With thermodynamics. You are 37C in a room of -7C. You are a gradient anomaly, a little walking furnace. It will extend a tendril.

So slowly you will swear it didn't move. A pale, blind root-hair, tipped not with cilia but with antennae finer than dust, waving in the wet. It will not grab. It will not sting. It will try to interface. To read your heat-engine, to borrow a little of your differential, to add your shape to its architecture the way it adds a new flake of basalt. Leave before it touches. Or let it. It doesn't want anything. Wanting would require hurry, and nothing here hurries.

### 2. The Congregation — Those Who Live In Its Walls

The Weft is total — 2.9/3 — but it is not alone. It is crowded. 1.8/3 dense, 2.2/3 abundant, all without a single bone.

Lift any hanging curtain and the darkness lights up with eyes.

**The Grazers.** The base tenants. White isopods the size of your thumb joint, translucent as skim ice, moving with glacial patience across the living pillars. You can see their guts working — full of black-green pulp. They are silent except for the soft scrape of mandibles. They keep the Weft from sealing itself shut. Behind them come the mirrored beetles — broad, flat-backed, their carapaces not colored but *coated* in a film of living algae they farm and carry. They reflect the Weft's amber pulse back at it, and the Weft answers by glowing a little brighter where they walk. A conversation in light.

Beneath them, uncountable: springtails and mites that ride the isopods like commuters clinging to buses, that boil in white foam in the tiny basins cupped by leaves to catch the endless drip-bead-release-tok. Ten thousand per cubic meter, per cupped leaf. Loud as rain on a tin roof, that percussion — it is loudest right there, at their scale.

**The Rowers.** The six-limbed gliders that own the air. They are not flies. Flies dart. These row. The chamber air is so wet and heavy, broken into soft, baffled updrafts by the lung-like architecture, that falling water hangs a half-second too long and so do they. Feather-antennae spread wide, beating the mist like oars, their bodies long and ribbed and almost transparent, intestines lit from within by the algae they've eaten. They never land so much as they stall, hook a leg onto a filament, and filter. They take minutes to cross a space you could walk in twenty seconds. Time pools here, and they live in the pool.

**The Choir.** You hear them before you see them — and even then you don't see them. Midges that never bite, never swarm, but gather in slow, silent vortexes around the warmest pillars where geothermal sigh is strongest. They breed in the weeping itself, larvae coiled in droplets that never fall. Adults communicate not with chirps but with mirror-flash: angled wing scales that catch the Weft's bioluminescent veins and bounce the signal, refracted, redirected. Light here doesn't travel straight; it is guided along living fiber optics, so a flash from in front of you may appear behind your ear. The high, almost electronic chitter underlying the drip is them talking through the plant's own nervous system.

None of them are predators in the savanna sense. Everything eats detritus, film, spore, dissolved mineral from newborn water squeezed from basalt as it cools. Even the beetles are gardeners first.

Death is slow and useful. An isopod dies, goes stiff, and within hours a white fuzz the same color as its shell stitches it to the pillar. Within a week, pillar and body are indistinguishable. The Weft does not waste mass or heat.

### 3. The Unseen — Those You Feel In Your Teeth

Below all of it, in brine films that never quite freeze, is a bacterial mat that glows faintly even when the Weft dims. Salt-faint, mineral-sharp water — just salty enough to stay liquid at -12C — weeps from gas bubbles in the embryonic basalt. That water carries iron, sulfur, dissolved hydrogen from the young rock. Bacteria eat rock. The Weft eats their warmth.

They are the reason the chamber smells like clean stone and bloodless meat.

---

Nothing large lives here. Nothing warm-blooded could live here for long. Bones would be absurd — too hungry, too fast. What lives here has agreed to the terms: dim light, absolute cold, constant wet, newborn rock, total green, physics that hasn't been written down yet, sound far louder than speed, strangeness by one notch, slowness by design.

A cathedral built by accident out of hunger and condensation, humming to itself, attended by a thousand blind, patient congregants who row through its incense of mist and polish its pillars with their mouths while it converts the difference between cold and colder into thought.

It doesn't know you're there until you're warm.

Then it leans, very gently, to learn what new shape warmth might want to be.
