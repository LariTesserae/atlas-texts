There is nothing here that can run.

Nothing that can hide, or hunt, or look back at you. If you came here hoping for an inhabitant with eyes, you will leave disappointed.

What lives in the High Aerary is not animal. Never was. It is mineral, it is electrical, it is patient, and it covers the plateau to the horizon — alone, monarchic, uninterrupted.

### The Harp Trees — The Standing Ones

They are what you will call alive.

Each one is a city-sized loom rooted directly into hot basalt, with no soil between it and the mantle. At the base, twenty, sometimes thirty meters wide, the trunk is not wood — it is a braid of thousands of black cables, each as thick as your wrist, matte and warm to the touch and faintly oily, humming with held charge.

They rise, and then they open.

Halfway up the braid loosens. The cables separate and fan into the air in a great, lax harp-head 40, 60, 80 meters across — thousands of vertical filaments hanging almost still, each fringed with soot-black electrostatic hairs. They tremble without wind. Put your arm up under one and the hairs on your skin will lift. The air tastes of iron and ozone, the moment before a strike that never comes.

They do not photosynthesize. They do not drink.

They *precipitate.*

The warm, thin air up here at 2-3 kilometers is not empty. It is a soup of fines — salt aerosol, iron carbonyl, sulfur and silica dust planed off the young fracture-deserts, halite vapor that never found water to dissolve in. The Harp Trees hold a field. Dust rides the wind, the field catches it, and mineral accretes directly on the filament like frost.

Where it accretes, they grow. The filament thickens, oxidizes — black to verdigris to rust-red — flakes, crusts, and then splits at the tip, forking into two finer filaments. Endlessly. Millimeter by millimeter per year. No season, no leaf-drop, no flowering.

They are without hurry because nothing eats them. Touch one and it will not recoil. Break one and no sap runs; the fallen cable lies clean on the salt pan, warm and dead as wire, slowly sanded by wind into dust that another Harp Tree, a kilometer away, will pull from the air next year. A perfect closed loop.

When they reproduce, they do not seed. They shatter. A filament overloads, snaps high in the crown with a soft, dry *tick*, and sails — a glowing wire, stiff with charge — riding the wind for hours or days until it spears the basalt like a thrown javelin, sticks, and begins to braid itself a new base. You could stand here a month and not see it happen. You could stand here ten thousand years and see it fifty times.

They are not individuals the way oak trees are. They are installations. Each one is a refinery, an air-filter, a power pylon, and a body, all at once.

And they are aware only in the way a charge is aware of ground. They lean, infinitesimally, toward denser dust. They thicken on their windward side. They hum louder before a salt-storm. That is their whole mind.

### The Bellows Moss — The Breathing Ground

Between the Harp Trees, in the shallow basins where the basalt holds heat longest, lives the carpet.

From a distance you think *moss* — rust-red, amber, spreading. Up close it is nothing soft. It is a tight, interlocked field of ceramic bladders, each the size of a fist or a lung, their skins dull and porous as unglazed kilnware.

They breathe.

Not for oxygen. There is no one to oxygenate for. They breathe for dust.

*Inhale,* over twenty long minutes — a near-silent intake as internal baffles and electret fibers draw in hot air and strip it of fines, of silica, of salt. The bladder swells, tightens, warms. Then — *exhale.* A shimmer of depleted, hotter air sighs out and ripples away over the halite pan.

One bladder is nothing. A square kilometer of them, inhaling slightly out of sync, makes the whole plateau respire. You don't hear it. You feel it as a slow pressure change in your ears, a faint sigh against your ankles, as if the rock itself were sleeping.

They do not die. They split. A bladder grows too full of trapped iron, seals its throat, and buds a twin from its side. No rot, no smell. Rot needs water and bacteria. Here, the dead bladders simply go still, bleached pale by sun, and remain as shell.

If the Harp Trees are the factory towers, the Moss is the floor — the endless filter-bed that pre-screens the air and makes the Harp Trees' work possible. They have been inhaling and exhaling in this rhythm since the plateau cracked upward, and will continue until wind finally learns how to carve a valley through them.

### The Spindle Grass — The Clock

They stand alone, never in groups. A single straight stalk, six meters tall, black iron-stem, perfectly vertical, anchored in basalt with guy-wires of root-cable.

At the top: a crown of three or four vanes, not leaf, not petal — airfoils of thin, oxidized metal grown like crystal.

The wind turns them.

*Click... click... click...* one revolution every two or three minutes, ratcheted, never swaying, never bending. They are wind turbines that were never built. The rotation is turned to charge down an insulated taproot that feeds laterally, underground, to the nearest Harp Trees. They are not parasites. They are the grid.

They have no leaves to flutter, no flowers to open. Their only movement is that patient, metronomic click. If you stand very still in the terrible silence — the silence of 0.2/3, where no insect has ever scraped leg on wing — you will finally hear them: a sparse, continent-wide ticking, like a clock the size of a state, counting seconds no animal will ever use.

When a great gust comes, they do not spin faster. They lock. A brake of intergrown cable seizes the crown until the gust passes, then *click... click...* resumes. They will do this for a million years without wearing out, because they replace their own bearings with fresh precipitated iron as they turn.

---

That is who lives here.

No eyes, no mouths, no footprints. No birds to nest in the harp-strings, no insects to sip from bladders that have no nectar. A lushness of 60% cover that is empty as a clean-room, an industry with no workers, no product, no waste.

They do not know you are here. They do not need to. You can walk between them for days under their striped, flickering shade-that-isn't-shade, over salt pans that ring like ice, while the Moss breathes under your boots and the Spindle Grass clicks its slow count behind you.

They were here before sound was needed, before hunger was invented.

And they will still be here — a little thicker, a little taller, filtering the same dry wind through the same black hairs — when the plateau finally cools enough for the first rain to fall and ruin them completely.
