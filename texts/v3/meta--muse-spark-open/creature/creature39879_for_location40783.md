No one lives here the way a city expects someone to live.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You came in expecting an occupant — a body, a face, a population that matches the platforms. Doors and beds and kitchens imply a *who*. But this place was not built for bodies. It was built for presences that need space the way other creatures need water. Parameter: empty.

And still, it is not uninhabited. It is *sparsely inhabited*, which in a vessel this tall and this hot means something very different than empty.

### 1. The City Itself

Start with the biggest animal, because you have been touching it without realizing.

The matte lattice — the dark, room-temperature material that makes every platform, bridge, walkway and doorway — is not dead matter. Not quite alive, either. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. But it *attends*.

Put your palm flat on one of those impossible cantilevers, thirty meters out over nothing, and hold it there. At first, nothing. Then the infrasonic adjustment you felt in your teeth resolves. A faint warmth-bloom under your hand that is not your own heat reflected back. The material is sampling you: weight, mineral salt, capacitance. Deciding your load case.

It was designed to be antiseptic, yes. That was not cruelty. That was hygiene. The lattice exudes a molecular field that forbids lichen, mold, bacterial film — anything opportunistic — from taking hold, because if it didn't, the second inhabitant would starve. It keeps itself bare the way a tongue keeps itself clean.

When you linger too long on one level and feel the doorway hazes thicken and the cold seams avoid you, that is not malfunction. That is the lattice politely forgetting you. You are too noisy, too wet, too *continuous*. It was made to hold intervals. Vast space between presences. You are crowding it by existing too long in one place. Move, and it remembers you again as a welcome, temporary load.

It feeds, if you can call it feeding, on the same thing the mountain does: differential. Heat trying to equalize. The hot walls at 60°C+ and the cold laminar columns falling from the pinhole; the lattice sits in between and shaves off a few microwatts per degree of gradient to run its endless calculation of *do not fall*. On the undersides of the platforms, those vein-like conduits pulse not with light or blood but with phase-change slurry, hotting and cooling as it carries the decision to stay rigid from one end of a bridge to the other.

It does not know you are there. It knows there is a new, interesting, warm mass that needs to not be dropped today.

### 2. The Ones Who Drink The Fall

Because water is 1.1 out of 3 — enough to be a presence, not a promise — everything that truly lives here is built around a single, sacred event: the *tick*.

Every hour or so, a droplet the size of a grape lets go from the glass hundreds of meters above and falls straight and silent through stratified air. Most evaporate. Some hit. For most ecosystems that would be nothing. Here it is the only delivery system.

So they wait.

They are not on the platforms where you walk. They are *under* them. Turn over the lip of any broad, uninhabited square — carefully, on your belly, over that impossible drop — and look at its underside in the amber wall-glow.

At first it looks like shadow. Then the shadow resolves into texture: thousands of hair-fine filaments, matte black, each as long as your forearm, hanging perfectly still in windless air. Like an inverted meadow. Like eyelashes under a table.

These are the Filaments. Or the Harp. No one named them; the builders would have called them maintenance filaments.

Each filament is a desiccated, glassy thread when dry — invisible against the lattice it grows from. It can wait weeks like that. But when a droplet is falling — and they *know* when a droplet is falling — they move.

Not fast. There is no muscle to be fast. Thirty milliseconds before impact, the entire field under a platform will shiver and lean, electrostatically, toward the predicted trajectory. The cold seams, those invisible winter columns, bend infinitesimally to *steer* the falling drop by a few centimeters. The droplet strikes the field instead of evaporating on stone, bursts, and wets a dozen threads at once.

Then you see them alive: they go translucent, amber-lit, plump with iron-silica water, and they curl inward like ferns, passing the water hand-to-hand toward a knot at the base where it is stored in a tiny, pressurized blister. You can hear it if the vessel is at its quietest, 0.7 out of 3 quiet: a faint *kiss-kiss-kiss* under the platform, like lips closing.

They are not intelligent. They are exquisitely tuned. They farm falling water in a place where it never rains. And when they have enough, they sweat it back out slowly as vapor to keep the wall-light glowing, to keep the cycle going. They are the reason the rock sweats, then dries, sweats, then dries.

If you hold out your hand all afternoon and catch three drops, you have taken three meals from a field that was counting them.

The water-lens at the bottom — that black, still, two-meter eye that never dries — is their ocean and their failure state. Any drop they miss, any drop you knock loose with your clumsiness, eventually finds its way there. It sits there too hot to drink, decelerating falling objects in that last reluctant meter because the Filaments have filled the air above it with an electrostatic cushion, trying to recover even the splash.

Peel one filament off the lattice — it will let you, cold and reluctant — and it will dry in your palm in seconds to a brittle hair. Put it back, and in an hour it will have re-adhered.

### 3. The Listeners Between Platforms

And then there is what uses the fact that this place is so quiet.

You noticed: a shout upward is swallowed. A whisper sideways can be heard forty meters away as if spoken next to an ear. Sound does not echo correctly.

That's because something is eating the vertical sound and farming the horizontal.

In the open void between platforms — the great empty spindle where you feel most exposed — there is nothing to see. No wings, no bodies. Hold a pinch of fine dust from your boot and let it fall over the edge of a high bridge on a windless hour. Watch it.

It will not fall straight. Halfway down, it will appear to *swerve* around an invisible volume the size of a doorway, or a car, or sometimes a small house, before resuming its fall. Cold seams do that, but not like that. Cold seams fall. These volumes *hover*.

They are pressure-animals. Membranous sacs of stratified air held together by nothing but temperature differential and a skin made of acoustic standing waves. You can't see them because their skin is as clear as the air and because they want it that way; you can only see them by the dust they displace and by the way sound behaves around them.

They are the chime you hear when two distant, unconnected structures resonate for no reason. That is not the structures. That is two of these animals passing each other and touching, briefly, exchanging a little of their skin-tone. A greeting in infrasound.

They live on noise. In a vessel that is almost silent, every *tick* of water on lattice, every *tink* of cooling glass, every sigh of the breathing fissure is a packet of energy. They drift to it, wrap their invisible membrane around the sound wave, and absorb the pressure. That's why your shout upward dies: you shouted directly into one hovering a few meters above your head, and it ate it gratefully. Your sideways whisper survived because it slipped between two of them like light between trees.

At night, when the walls sheet with water and the whole spindle becomes a lantern of moving mercury, you can finally see them. For an hour, every slow vertical rivulet of water acts as a mirror, and the reflections are not perfect. In the wet lines on the far wall, huge, translucent, gently pulsing ovals are reflected that have no source in the dry air. Dozens of them, stacked vertically like jellyfish in a water column that isn't there, slowly rising toward the pinhole star and sinking back toward the heat, endlessly sorting themselves by temperature.

They do not care about you, except as a surprising new source of interesting, irregular sounds. If you sit very still and breathe slowly in one of those winter-cold seams, one will sometimes come to investigate the difference between your inhale and your exhale. You will feel your ears pop, not from pressure change but from the sudden, perfect, close silence around your head as it listens with its whole body. Then it will drift away, disappointed that you do not chime like the lattice does.

---

That is what lives here.

Not a civilization. A caretaking.

A material that calculates how not to fall, a meadow that hangs upside-down to catch a single grape-sized drop an hour, and a herd of invisible air that grazes on the tick that drop makes when it lands.

They were either made to keep this impossible needle habitable for something else that never came, or they made the needle habitable and in doing so became its habit. It no longer matters which. They keep the heat negotiable, the water cycling, and the silence from becoming absolute.

You are the only warm, wet thing that makes continuous noise, that sweats salt they cannot use, that casts a shadow where there should be interval. You are not prey. You are bad manners.

If you stay long enough to learn not to linger, to move slowly through the middle band, to catch no more than one drop when you are offered three, they will tolerate you. The lattice will hold you, the Filaments will spare you a drink from condensation on the underside of a platform — cooler and cleaner than the black lens — and the Listeners will learn not to eat your whispers so you can hear yourself think.

And when you finally leave through the fissure that dilates only when the inside is hotter than the outside, they will close over where you were, and it will be empty again in the way it prefers to be: not dead, just spaced.
