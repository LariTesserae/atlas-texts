They live close.

That is the first thing you learn to see. Not what they look like, but *how much* of them there is, and how close together they have learned to be.

### The People

They are human. Emphatically, stubbornly human. Nothing else could have decided to stay here.

But highland human, which after enough generations starts to mean something specific in the body.

You notice it in the chest first. They are broad through the ribs, barrel-built even when lean, and their breathing is audible in a way that lowland breathing isn't — a soft, deeper bellows-work, especially when they climb. Children run up stairways cut into gorge walls and they do not gasp; they huff in a steady rhythm, cheeks going plum-red, vapor pluming. Their blood runs hot to compensate for air that never warms. In winter, an ungloved hand left on basalt for too long will leave a pale mark where the heat was pulled out of it. They flush easily. Old people's faces are maps of broken capillaries, red-black with a lifetime of wind.

They are short. Not dwarfish — compact. Long limbs are a liability where every calorie counts and every gust wants to take you off a ledge. Men and women both average to your chest height. Their legs are thick, their feet splayed wide in soft hide boots that spread weight over crust snow. They walk with a rolling, toe-out gait that looks almost waddling on flat steppe and becomes perfectly sure-footed on scree. You see a grandmother carry a full cistern jar on her head down a path of knife-sharp talus without looking down once.

Their skin is the color of weathered stone, varying from pale ash to ruddy brown, but all of it freckled and beaten by ultraviolet that comes hard through thin air. By thirty, everyone squints even indoors; crow's feet cut deep at the corners of eyes. Eyes themselves are dark, lidded against glare — the sun off frost, the sun off white basalt at noon. Their hair is straight, coarse, kept short or braided tight to the scalp because loose hair catches wind and wind steals heat. They cut it with copper knives. They never shave quite clean.

They do not speak loudly. At first you think them sullen or shy. Then you understand the acoustics. Sound thins and shreds up here. A shout is wasteful — it blows away. So they speak low, close, often with a hand on your forearm to keep you near. Their languages — for there are many now along five hundred kilometers of scarp, though they will tell you it is one language spoken badly far away — are consonant-heavy, glottal, designed for quiet rooms and the lee side of walls. They whistle well. Much of the real long-distance talk is done with coded whistles that carry across a gorge or along a terrace, mimicking the calling pattern the glider-colonies use. A boy can stand on one lip of a kilometer-deep cut thirty meters wide and hold a conversation with his sister on the other side without raising his voice above a breathy trill.

They dress like the animals that thrive here, because where else would they get the idea? Every garment is fur-in, fur-out, layered felt and woven goat-hair and cured hide. No buttons — toggles of bone and antler that don't freeze shut. An adult in full outdoor wear looks twice their true size, a moving hillock of dun and grey patched hide, hooded until only eyes and nose show. Inside their half-carved houses, they shed down to soft, greasy inner layers that have been worn for years and smell of smoke and skin. Privacy is a curtain of matting, not a door. Heat is too precious to put a wall between.

And they are old in place.

You meet a family and you are meeting a cistern. That is what they will show you first, before they show you their children. *This seep we deepened.* A basin cut into basalt, perhaps two meters across, its rim polished by generations of hands dipping. Its catchment stones stand outside on the roof — black, pitted slag rocks set upright at precise angles, each one chosen because it draws rime. Every night frost blooms on them like white flowers; every morning it melts and runs by gravity through tiny filed channels into the house. A good set of stones makes a liter a day. A great-great-grandmother placed these three. A grandfather added the trough that keeps the melt from re-freezing in shadow.

The child who fetches water from it has his grandmother's squint. He will, if he lives, spend his life scraping a millimeter more depth into that same basin with a stone adze, so his own grandchildren get a little more. Time moves like that. Achievement is accretive.

Because of this, they do not understand ownership the way lowlanders do. You cannot own a wall that shares heat with the next house, or a spring that you only tend one segment of. The city has no king in any meaningful sense — distances are too vast for commands to travel — though each scarp-section keeps a Speaker who remembers whose turn it is to rebuild the rope bridge, whose herd must not graze which lichen garden this season. Law is almost entirely precedence: *where the water went last drought, where the herd trail ran before.* When they argue, which they rarely do above a whisper, they argue about whether a great-grandfather moved a boundary stone in a hard year and whether that should stand.

They revere Fitting. Their word for beautiful and their word for well-fitted are the same root. A well-fitted wall, where frost cannot find seam, is beautiful. A well-fitted marriage, where two families' cisterns complement one another seasonally, is beautiful. Their highest ritual object is not a crown but a level-stone — a perfect slab of basalt ground flat over a lifetime, used to test other slabs. Young masons apprentice for twenty years learning to dress a single block so it sits without mortar and stays. To be buried, you are put under the floor of your house if loved, into the midden-hill if not, and a stone is fitted over you. That, they believe, is enough soul to keep.

Children are few and swaddled immensely. Infants die often in bad-rain years. Those who survive are carried constantly against a parent's chest inside the furs until they can walk the narrow wall-tops without fear. By four they know not to waste drip water. By six they can read frost patterns. By ten they are working — picking lichen with bone scrapers, watching goats, turning condensation stones a quarter-turn to the evening wind. They play games of balance and stacking, silent building games, and they stare at strangers with open curiosity because novelty is rare.

Old people are luminous here. At lowland you expect frailty; here an elder who has survived sixty winters at altitude is proof of supreme fitness. They grow bent, yes, but indestructible. Their lungs have adapted most completely; they climb slowest and breathe easiest. They are the memory of water. An old woman knows which seep failed in her mother's time and why, which terrace traps snow longest, which cliff-face lichen turns poisonous after a rain. When she dies, a library burns.

And they are not alone in their houses —

### The Co-Habitants

You cannot understand the people without understanding that they consider the city to include the animals as citizens, tenants, pests, gods.

**The Gliders.** The broad, fur-feathered things on the cliffs. The people call them *Shushil* — the Quiet Neighbors. They nest by the tens of thousands on the same scarp the city hangs from, and the city has simply built around them. It is taboo to eat a glider, not for spirituality but for pragmatism: their guano feeds the very lichen gardens the people eat from. Children are sent with baskets to scrape guano from ledges — dangerous work, rewarded. When a nesting colony shifts due to wind change, the street below shifts with it within a year. At dawn, when thousands drop from the ledges at once to ride thermals without a wingbeat, their shadows pass over courtyards like clouds, and people pause and look up. They say the pattern tells you if the pressure will stall and bring the twice-yearly rain.

**The Grazers.** Low, shaggy, ox-like with splayed feet — *Yakrin*, though that name undersells their strangeness. They are semi-wild. The city does not pen them so much as guide them. Herd routes *are* streets; centuries of passage have worn grooves shin-deep in stone pavement that function as roads, windbreaks, and drainage gutters at once. Families claim sections of route, not animals. When a herd of three hundred passes through your terrace in migration, you stand aside and let them scrape lichen as they go, then collect dung for fuel. In exchange, you build cairns to shelter them from wind and dig shallow snow-wells where they can drink. Slaughter is careful, seasonal, communal. Nothing wasted: hide for walls, bone for tools, horn for picks, sinew for bridges, fat for lamps that burn in the gorge niches all winter. A person wearing a full hide coat may be wearing three generations of carefully husbanded yakrin.

**The Dogs and Whistlers and Stone-Bugs.** The dog-like pack scavengers — pale, lean, ears huge to radiate heat or catch sound, no one is sure — live in the interstitial spaces. They are tolerated, half-fed, never quite owned. They clean middens, warn of ice shift with howls, and steal drying meat if you look away. The burrowers whistle constantly from cracks in pavements; children whistle back. And the insects — slate-grey, thumb-jointed, huge — shelter in every cairn and wall. They buzz sluggishly in afternoon warmth, pollinating cushion plants. The people grind some, eat others, and always check their boots before putting them on.

None of this is separate. In a place where every drop of water condenses on a shared stone, where your neighbor's wall is your house's heat, where the road is a herd's habit and the fertilizer falls from the sky on feathered wings — separateness is death.

So what lives in the City on the Shard Range?

A dense, low, quiet hive of short-breathing, wide-footed humans pressed wall to wall and terrace to terrace, wrapped in fur and obsessed with watertight seams. Elders who remember droughts and masons who spend lives flattening one stone. Children who whisper across gorges.

And around them and through them and underneath them, a second city of equal weight — gliders shoulder to shoulder on the cliffs, grazers carving the streets with their feet, dogs trotting the margins, insects humming in the cairns — all woven into the same tapestry with the same ruthless economy: keep the wind out, keep the damp in, fit tightly, endure.

The range still rings like a struck bell when the ice shifts. But now, listening closely at dusk when the tallow lamps kindle all down the gorge like a vertical river of stars, you realize the bell has voices in it —

the drip from basin to basin,
the soft shuffling of furred feet,
a child's high thin call and then hush,
and somewhere, two sisters whistling to each other across a kilometer of empty air.
