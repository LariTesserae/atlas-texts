Nothing lives *here* the way you mean it.

Not at a density anyone would count. If you stand still long enough for your census to catch up with you, you'll understand that 0.0 doesn't mean dead. It means whatever lives here was never taught how to be counted.

This is what lives here now:

### The City Itself

First, because it’s the most populous thing on the plateau.

It is not abandoned. Abandoned implies a moment when hands let go. This city never had that moment — it just thinned until the hands weren’t needed. What remains is not ruins, it’s metabolism.

The city lives the way a reef lives: by moving water and heat through a body.

Its lungs are the low factories with the sawtooth roofs, inhaling the hot wet air and wringing it out on cold iron baffles so it can drip, run, collect. Its gut is the waterworks — screws and Archimedean lifts and spillways that haul scalding brine uphill without ever asking why. Its blood is the grid of boulevards you thought were for transit. They’re riverbeds. When the haze gets thick enough to rain — not real rain, but the air condensing all at once — those eight-lane avenues run an inch deep with hot mineral water, reflecting the amber dusk-light, carrying dissolved rust and salt to the next cistern to be boiled again.

You hear its pulse at 1.1: Mostly quiet, until it isn’t.

*Clank.* A trip-hammer three blocks over, working a billet that no one ordered, because the billet is there and the heat is there.

*Groan.* A conveyor starting because a sensor finally got wet enough.

*Tssssssssh.* A spillway opening like a valve in a throat, dumping a sheet of water down the concrete sluice you were standing next to a minute ago. Then quiet. Then dripping.

If you stay long enough you stop hearing machines and start hearing habit. The city is not automated in the human sense — a program running. It is *habituated*. The basalt weeps, so the pumps pump. The air gets heavy, so the condensers condense. The salt crusts, so something scrapes it off and presses it into mineral wool and moves it to another building where it will wait until the next cycle dissolves it. Nobody told it to become weather. It just became more efficient than weather.

On the high basalt table, this is the apex animal: a low, dark, steam-breathing city-reef spread across old stone, doing what reefs do best - being patient while everything erodes around it.

It doesn’t need you. It barely registers you except as a briefly cooler patch in the air it exhales.

### The Black Bloom

Second, because they were here before the grid was laid and will be here after the trestles rust through.

There is nothing green here, you were right about that. Green lost early — too hot, too sulfurous, the water too sharp with iron and electricity. What won was black.

It looks like spilled tar at first, then like dried oil, then like leather left in the sun. In the cooler seams — under the north face of every wall, under every trestle foot, around the lips of every weeping spring — the basalt is patched with sheets of it. A fungus that is not a fungus. A lichen that gave up on alga. A bacterial mat that learned to live like varnish.

When the steam is thick, it softens and tightens, glossy black and featureless, sealing itself over the rock to drink condensation straight through its skin. When the hot wind shears in from the plateau edge and flash-dries the city, it cracks. Not randomly. Into perfect geometric plates, hexagons and pentagons pulling apart with a faint ticking sound like cooling ceramic, exposing wine-purple basalt underneath for an hour until the seep comes back and it knits itself whole again.

It does not move. It does not need to move. It grows at the speed rust grows — which here is to say, actively. Around any perpetually dripping pipe you’ll find it has crept a millimeter up the iron, eating nothing, making nothing, just being a second skin that knows how to be wet here.

Don’t step on it when it’s soft. Not because it will bite — it won’t — but because your bootprint will stay there for a season, a pale, dry scar in its back that the steam will slowly have to heal around.

### The Filterers

Third, because they are so easy to miss until the air moves wrong.

They cling to the vertical weeping walls wherever the seep is steadiest — those places where bright threads of water string down the basalt all day and never dry. From a distance they look like damp paper that got stuck there and died. Up close they aren’t anything like paper.

Each one is the size of your spread hand, translucent, veined with something darker than vein. A bell, a mantle, a little parachute of living cellophane pinned to the rock by a blind sucker-mouth. It pulses. Slowly. About once every time you remember to breathe deliberately in this thin, heavy air. With each pulse it pulls a film of water over itself and filters it — sulfur, suspended iron, spores, salt, stray flecks of the black bloom, whatever the city has sweated out that hour.

They don’t eat. They *strain*.

By themselves they are nothing. A stain on a wet wall. But heat gathers under the trestles and the city exhales unevenly, and then — without warning or coordination you can see — a whole section of wall lets go.

That is what you saw from the administrative terrace. Not a flock. A drift. A hundred, two hundred of them releasing at once and hanging in the hot air like ash that forgot gravity. They don’t fly. They *hang*, riding steam. Turning inside-out slowly, trailing their veined curtains, filtering the air itself now. They will ride the haze until it thickens and carry them, blind and pulsing, to the next weeping wall to cling again.

If one lands on you it feels wetter than water. Cold. It will stay a second, taste the salt on your sleeve, decide you are not a wall worth living on, and peel away.

### The Swimmers and The Hound

Last, because they are rare. Very rare, the way large animals are rare in a desert.

In the runoff channels — the concrete sluices, the half-pipe gutters that cut behind the habitation blocks, the actual eroded rills in the basalt where water has finally found a preference — something flicks.

White against black stone. No eyes. Shrimp the length of your thumb, then twice that, blind as milk glass, flicking upstream through water that would leave your skin pink and blistered. They live their whole lives at a simmer. They graze on sulfur bacteria and on the slough of the black bloom and on each other. If you kneel by a cooling pool at dusk — that hourless dusk that passes for noon — you can see their shadows passing under the steam.

Where there are shrimp, eventually something drinks.

It comes in the long gaps between mechanical groans. Low. Long-bodied. You called it a heat-hound because some worker painted that word on a bulkhead near the furnaces fifty years ago and the name stuck to the steam.

It is not a dog. It has too much skin for its bones, bare and pink-grey except where mineral crust has grown into it like armor — white salt on its shoulders, sulfur yellow along its jaws. Its head is blunt, its eyes set wide and dark and wet, and its feet are too wide, splayed for walking on hot slick.

It does not hunt shrimp. It waits above a sump where the water has had a chance to cool from scalding to merely hot, and it laps. And then it looks at you.

That intelligence you felt is not hostile, not inviting, because those would both require it to have extra energy to spend on you. It has exactly as much as it needs to be still in the steam. It watches you with the flat assessment of a thing that also knows the rule here: don’t waste movement. Don’t make noise unless the city makes it first. Drink when the water runs, lie flat when the wind comes.

There are maybe six of them in this whole valley-sized city. Maybe fewer now. They meet rarely, they call almost never — a low whine lost under the clank of valves — and they do not get in each other’s way.

You are rarer here than they are.

---

If you go back to the highest layered boulevard tonight and watch, you’ll see the three kinds of life do what they have agreed to do.

The black sheets tightening as haze settles. The paper things lifting off a far wall in a silent, slow snow. Far below, by the powerplant with no workers, a hound-shaped shadow detaching itself from steam to drink.

And underneath it all, around it all, the city breathing for them. Plume rising. Water beading. Salt blooming white and vanishing. Trip-hammer falling alone in a hall.

No one lives here.

And that’s why everything does.
