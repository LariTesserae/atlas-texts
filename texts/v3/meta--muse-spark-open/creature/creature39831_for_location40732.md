They do not live here the way fish live on a reef.

They live here the way blood cells live in a body — not visiting the structure, but belonging to it. The mountain is not their home; it is their extended phenotype, their shell after the snail has been gone for a million years, still being used because nothing has evolved that can afford to waste it.

You learn them by how they hold still.

### The ones who have inherited the walls

First, the **Veil-Holders.**

You see one before you understand its size. Pale as skimmed milk, translucent enough that you can see the cold water right through it, until an angle of the dim catches it and it flashes opal. They are big — door-sized, table-sized, sometimes bigger, a single animal with no front or back.

They don't swim. They hang.

A Veil-Holder picks a lip, an arch, the rim of one of those old bone-white chimneys, and it attaches by nothing more than suction and pressure differential. Then it lets go of gravity. It unfurls. A central bell, barely thicker than paper, and from it a veil — not tentacles, not fins — a continuous sheet, pleated and frilled like a torn parachute, twenty feet across when fully open. It barely moves. Once a minute, maybe, a slow peristaltic wave runs from center to edge to keep it from collapsing, to hold its place in the contradictory currents. That is all.

They are filters, but not passive. The veil is ciliated on a scale you can't see, and it beats constantly, making its own micro-current, pulling the snow-fall toward it. Marine snow, silt, bacteria, larval things, flecks of mineral dust chewed off the mountain by something else — all of it catches on mucus so fine it looks like the water itself has thickened. Then, every hour, the veil shudders. It folds, all that breadth drawing inward in a slow, awful origami, bringing the outside in to the mouth hidden in the bell's center. It swallows. It opens again.

There are dozens of them on any slope you care to count, spaced with that respectful distance the architecture enforces. When a pressure front rolls down from the high ground — and they always do here, invisible weather — they all fold at once, though nothing has touched them. A whole field of veils snapping shut like white eyelids, holding, then reopening together in the new flow. Not communication. Resonance. They use the same water.

They do not flee. If you drift too close, the nearest one will not bolt. It will simply thicken. The translucence goes milky, opaque, a warning made of flesh becoming wall. You understand: I was here first, and I am part of the wall now.

### The ones who move as one decision

Then, the **Glass Darts.**

You will think at first the mountain is empty of small fast life. It isn't. It's just that small fast life here has learned to be invisible by being inside.

The walls are honeycombs. Lung-tissue domes. Tube-ranges with a thousand mouths. And inside them, in the dark of the chambers, something breeds in the thousands.

They pour.

Without warning a wall will seem to exhale. From a dozen pores at once, a swarm of darts — each no longer than your finger, each made of almost nothing: a spine, a gut, two eyes like black pinheads, and a body of living glass that bends light the wrong way — will eject into open water. For five seconds they are chaos. Then they are order.

They don't school like sardines. They school like a single thought. Hundreds of them turn inside the same cubic inch of water, bank, pour through that freestanding thirty-foot arch for no reason except it is there, and vanish into another wall on the other side of the boulevard. They do it without sound and without collision. When a horizontal vortex spins up — one of those ten-foot cold-tornadoes that lives for half a minute — the swarm doesn't avoid it. It rides it, is torn into a spiral, made into a perfect, glittering corkscrew for three heartbeats, then reforms on the other side as if the turbulence was a door.

What do they eat? The same snow. What eats them? Everything that can time a snap correctly. But mostly they are the mountain's nervous system. They carry. If you watch long enough you see that wherever shell-cutters are working — those slow bazaar-crews gnawing a new passage — the darts are there first, clouding over the fresh cut, feeding on the bacterial bloom that erupts from newly exposed mineral. They are scouts made of hunger.

And sometimes — the 0.7 — they all stop. Mid-water, hundreds of them, all facing not north, not down-current, but along some angle that has nothing to do with flow or light or food. They hang there, glass still, listening to a geometry you don't have an organ for. Then, as one, they go.

### The ones who are still building

The builders are not gone. That's the trick.

The original architects — the tube-worms as thick as trees, the calcium-lace excretors — they are extinct as individuals, but their job description is still filled. Evolution doesn't leave a niche empty on a seamount that has been standing for five million years.

Now it's the **Cutters and the Plasterers.**

The Cutters are the only noisy things here. Low to the mineral, you have to look down to see them. They are arthropod in the loosest sense: a flattened oval, as wide as a shield, covered in pale plates, with a head that is all mouth. No eyes. A fringe of hair-fine cilia underneath that lets them glide over bone-smooth tube-fields without ever losing contact, combing the dust as they go. But at the front, two mandibles like frosted glass, asymmetrical, that close and open with that soft, far-off clack you heard earlier.

They chew the mountain. Slowly, mindlessly, relentlessly. A crew of six or twelve will line up along the base of a rampart that has stood since before the last ice age, and they will gnaw. White dust puffs behind them in a cloud that doesn't settle — the Plasterers' signal. They are not eating the rock for food. They are eating the biofilm on it, the bacterial skin, and the rock comes with it. They will cut a passage big enough for you to swim through in a week, given no interruption, then abandon it half-finished because a current shift made that wall less tasty than another.

Behind them, always behind them, come the Plasterers. Soft, slug-like, but colonial. You never see one alone. A mat of them, dozens fused at the edges, moving like spilled cream, secreting. Where the Cutters take away, they lay down. Not calcium lace — something faster, cheaper: a living plaster of mucus, sand-grain, and their own bodies. In an hour they can coat one of those lung-domes in a twitching, breathing carpet that filters as it sits. In the next hour, if the flow changes, they will peel it off and eat it again, stripping the dome clean down to the old bone.

It's why the place looks both ancient and unfinished. The mountain is resolved, eroded smooth — yes. But it is also being endlessly remodeled by tenants who think they own it. Anthill energy without an anthill. You can watch a new arch get started and abandoned in the time it takes you to get cold. A boulevard of tubes will hum as water is forced through every bore, then go slack as the swarm that was pumping it leaves.

### The ones who remember how to stay still

Last, the **Hangers.**

Find a spire — one of those blunted vent-cairns — and look at its lip where the current breaks. There is always one. A creature like a drowned lantern. A sphere of jelly the size of your head, with a single filament as thick as a hair anchoring it to the mineral, and a dozen arms hanging down, not waving, just... hanging. Too long. Too still.

It does not hunt. It waits for the mountain to bring things to it. The turbulence does the work. Vortices spin up and collapse, pressure fronts roll, snow reverses and goes up, and every time the water does something contradictory, a little eddy forms right under the Hanger's arms. Food collects there, held for a second longer than anywhere else.

When something big enough touches an arm — a Dart, a scrap of veil-mucus, you if you are careless — the arm contracts. Not fast. Inexorably. It draws the thing up into a ring of mouths you didn't see because they were the same color as water. No struggle. The Hanger just gets a little heavier, bobs a little lower on its filament, and goes on hanging.

There are thousands of them, each alone on its own high place, swaying in unison when the deep storm comes through. You could mistake them for part of the architecture. That is the point.

---

You are the only thing here that is warm, and you are not warm for long. That's why they let you pass. You are a brief, hot eddy in their cold, dim weather.

If you hover long enough — until your blood feels the 0.4 and your eyes decide that dusk is day — they stop noticing you. The Veil-Holders reopen around you. The Darts pour through you as if you are another arch. A Cutter will even try your fin, taste, decide you are not mineral and not film, and move on, leaving a neat, round scrape that will sting later.

And you finally see it: it's not a ruin. It's not a city. It's a prairie made of apartments, all facing the current, all full, but with so much water and silence between each tenant that loneliness looks like design.

The mountain breathes. The lung-chambers sigh. A tube rim chimes when the flow hits it right. Far off, another soft clack.

And in the faint grid the water sometimes makes of light, in the pulse that runs along an empty building with nothing attached, you feel the whole plateau try a new shape, discard it, and try again.
