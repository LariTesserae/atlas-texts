They live damp. All of them.

Not in the way that people near the ocean live damp, where the salt comes and goes. Here damp is a condition of being. It is inside the walls and inside the lungs. You can tell someone who lives in the Pit within a minute of meeting them on the surface, even before they start sweating wrong.

Their skin has given up on being dry. It shines, always, even in the cool hour when the milk-cloud sits in the bowl. It’s not quite clammy — it’s softened. Pruney at the knuckles and at the knees and at the soles of the feet, even on teenagers, even on children. The old women on the Bottom have feet like river stones, white and pleated from standing in plank-water all day. The men who work the Middle Ring have forearms pink and smooth where the heat-rash never quite leaves. Everyone’s hair hangs a little limp. No one bothers with powder or paper that isn’t kept in a tin. Mold finds your armpits, your groin, the crease under your breasts if you don’t scrub with the carbolic soap they make in the repair shop. You learn young to check each other for the pale round patches of fungus on the back and shoulders and to not be shy about it. It’s just maintenance, like wiping rust off a handrail.

They are loud people who think they are quiet.

The Pit teaches you to half-shout. Not a yell — a carrying voice from the diaphragm that can cut through *thud... thud... thud...* and the power station drone and the rain of condensation on tin. Mothers call their children that way: *“—Sai! Sai! Move your bucket!—”* and the sound bounces off the bowl and comes back a half-second later, softened. Two old men gossiping on a pipe-bridge sound like they’re arguing from a distance, then you get close and realize they’re exchanging recipes for how to keep weevils out of rice. They’ve all lost a little of the high end of their hearing. The babies are born into it and don’t cry any louder than babies elsewhere; they just never learn to whisper. To an outsider, the whole town seems to be urgently, constantly informing each other of something. To each other, it’s intimate. They read lips without knowing they read lips. They put a hand on a forearm when they want you to listen.

They are slow people in a way that is not laziness.

No one moves fast when it is 2.9/3 hot and the air has weight and every piece of metal can burn you at noon. Even the tram on the Middle Ring seems to *screech—...pause...—screech—* rather than run. The repair crews work in a rhythm set by the frogs. You will see a man with a wrench sit down mid-bolt, halfway up a ladder, to drink water from a shared jug and watch a dragonfly for a full minute before he tightens the next turn. This is not slacking. This is the contract you make with gravity and seep. You cannot hammer the Pit into submission. You coax it. You move a walkway two feet to the left after a slump and bolt it down and say “there, that’s good now” and leave it for a year. To be impatient here is to get hurt. The rock falls fast when you rush it.

So the three Rings make three kinds of person, but they all understand each other is just living at a different depth of wet.

**The Upper Ring people** are rim-sick. They still see the sky properly for part of the day. They work with dry things: hoisting, logging the elevator, tending the cisterns where the wall-weep is caught in concrete pans and piped down. Their skin is the most sun-stained, their eyes narrowed even in shade. They are the watchers. Their job is to listen for change in the pumps before anyone else and to hose the swallow-shit off the stairs every dawn before it bakes. Children up here play the only game that still has height in it: dropping a weighted string down the stairwell and counting how long before the Middle Ring kids tug back. They get nosebleeds from going up and down too fast. Most of them want to be Middle Ring when they grow up because it’s where things *happen*, and a few of them quietly want to leave entirely, but almost no one does. What is down stays down.

**The Middle Ring people** are the heart. They smell of low-grade sulfurous fuel and hot oil. Their hands are never clean; the rust from every railing lives under their nails permanently, a brown-black crescent moon. They have the worst coughs and the best jokes. They work in pairs because the noise eats a single voice: one holds the sheet metal while the other rivets, one watches the gauge while the other bleeds the line. They have invented a whole sign language over the power station drone — flat palm for *stop*, tap on the hard hat for *look up, rock*, two fingers wiggling like a midge cloud to mean *end of shift, let’s go drink*. At noon they eat together under the amber sodium lamps even though it’s hotter there, because the light makes their food look cooked. They keep birds. Not formally — but every shop has a swallow nest they have decided not to knock down, and they talk to it. “You going to shit on my welder again?” It is affection.

**The Bottom people** are water people who have never seen a real lake. Their floors are wood over black gravel and rust-water. They pole plank-boats to check fish traps. They walk barefoot or in tall rubber boots that never dry, and their calves are mapped with leech-bites and mosquito welts they scratch in their sleep. They speak the softest, or rather, the most *pitched* — they have learned to pitch their voices just above the bullfrog chorus, that patient * jug-o-rum, jug-o-rum* that is the true clock of the place. They are net-menders, pump-wipers, cat-keepers, child-wranglers, market-sellers who spread greens grown in old elevator buckets on tarps inches above the algae bloom. They know the marsh by smell: iron day, oil day, sweet-rot day when the algae died. On sweet-rot days they wear cloth over their mouths and keep the children on the walkways. Their homes are the most decorated, because when you live in dim you make your own light — strings of stolen bulbs, bright plastic flowers that don’t mold, painted cats on stilts. They are the most amphibious. You will see an old man nap with his feet literally in the warm water, a baby asleep in a hammock over it, both breathing with the pumps.

And none of them live alone. That is the point the Pit makes most insistently.

Because for all the deliberate emptiness — those wide firebreaks and flood-surges left between buildings — the place is *crowded* with co-habitants who do not pay rent and cannot be evicted.

The bats are a people. At dusk they come out of the meter-deep scallops in the rock, ten thousand, then twenty, a smear that turns into a vortex in the open column, squeaking just at the edge of human hearing so that your fillings tingle. The humans stop and look up every night whether they need to or not. Children wave at them. The Middle Ring workers time their last cigarette by them. The guano men — two brothers, always — go out at false dawn with scrapers and barrows to collect it before the heat cooks the ammonia too strong. They sell it Topside for fertilizer, but half of it stays: smeared thin on the moss wall to keep it green, or left where it is because the swifts need the flies that need the guano. If the bats ever left, the Pit would be quieter and deader in a way that would feel like death.

The swallows and swifts are the other shift. They take the air when the bats give it back, nesting by the thousands on I-beams and ledges, mud-daub walls under every walkway. They are noisier than the bats, a constant chittering chatter that layers over the industrial drone. The humans have ceded them the high places. No one throws a stone. Instead they put up little tin awnings over doorways, not for rain but for shit. In the morning, before the sun-circle crawls onto your tier, you hose. Everyone hoses. It is a communal sound more reliable than a bell — a hundred hoses hitting concrete and tin, flushing white streaks down the weeping wall to join the condensate fingers. It is how the Pit says good morning.

Under that, the weather of insects. The humans do not fight the mosquitoes and midges; they *manage* them like weather. Every Bottom doorway has netting; every bed has it doubly. At dusk the sodium cones fill with clouds so thick they look like smoke, and the humans go inside or they burn the coils that make green sweet smoke and they play cards and shout over the whine. They bleed a little every day and do not comment. The children chase dragonflies big as sparrows, iridescent green-orange, and the adults let them because a dragonfly can eat a hundred midges before lunch. The moths hand-sized thump at every bulb until cats notice. The roaches and crickets live in the walls as a second heartbeat: if you put your hand on the warm basalt at night you can feel them ticking behind the heater-drone. No one fumigates. To poison them would be to poison the frogs and the carp and then the water would truly turn.

And the cats. The Pit is a cat town in the way old harbors are. Half-feral, sleek, heat-lazy in the day, murder-efficient at 3 a.m. They belong to no one and everyone feeds them scrap carp. They sleep on warm pipe and in motor-houses and on the backs of sleeping dogs who are too hot to care. Kittens are born in pump housings and are considered good luck for that pump. A pump-cat will be carried gently out by its mother one by one to a cooler ledge and the pump crew will leave milk-water for her. To kill a cat here would be like turning off a fan.

In the water, the introduced carp and catfish have become Pit-native. They roll in the rust shallows, stirring black gravel, mouthing at algae mats. The humans don’t much like eating them — they taste of iron and oil — but the old Bottom women do catch them, salt them, hang them over the fluorescents until they are hard as wood, and then they are chewed like tobacco during long pump watches. The carp are mostly for the cats and for keeping the mosquito larvae slightly down and for giving the children something huge and surprising to see move under the planks. “Big Grandpa,” they call the largest catfish, who has lived under the market bridge for five years and is fed heads.

And the frogs. If the pumps are the heart thudding, the frogs are the breath. They never stop. Not through work noise, not through rain. *KREK. KREK. jug-o-rum.* You think you have learned to ignore them until they stop — because a pump has snagged or a cat has fallen in — and the entire Pit wakes up in an instant with that wrong silence. Then every human head lifts. Someone shouts. Someone runs in boots splashing. The frogs resume, and the breath resumes.

What lives here, then, is not just people. It is a bargain.

A bowl of warm, weeping rock that wanted to be a marsh but got cut open, and the people who decided to keep it, and the ten thousand bats and swallows who said *thank you, we’ll take the air column,* and the millions of midges and moths and dragonflies who said *thank you, we’ll take the light,* and the carp and frogs who said *thank you, we’ll take the water you can’t pump,* and the moss and liverwort and algae who came without asking and stayed anyway, turning every north-facing seep black-green and velvet.

They live by one adaptation above all others, and it is not gills or webbed feet. It is patience. The rock widens a crack an inch a year. Rust takes a season to bloom. A stalactite grows a thumb-length in a decade. The humans, whether they arrived last year on the freight elevator or were born on a stilt-floor over iron water, learn to match that pace. They sit with heat. They live wet. They listen for when the thudding stops.

If you stand on the Bottom planks at night when only the night-shift lights are on and the water doubles them — the whole amber and buzzing fluorescent reflection wobbling under your feet — you can feel how it all lives *together*, not on top of each other. Warm water lapping your boots, warm rock at your back, warm air on your face, bat wings fanning the humid column above you, frog song rising through the soles of your feet, a cat pressing damp against your ankle, a child somewhere above you hosing yesterday’s guano into tomorrow’s cistern, all of it inside one small, loud, dim, empty, crowded bowl that breathes in and out, in and out, and does not ask permission to keep doing so.
