They live not on the Plateau, but in it. Inside it. And it lives in them.

There is no way to untangle them cleanly, so you have to start from the top and work your way down, and then back up again, the way water does here.

### The Holdings

First, the ones who make the place possible.

A Holding is not a tree. To call it a tree is to make the same mistake as calling a whale a fish because it swims. It is a colony that decided to be one thing. If you cut it — which you can't, usefully — you would not find rings. You would find hydraulics.

The core is not wood but a dense, spongy column of water-storing parenchyma wrapped in living armor, a trunk 200 feet broad at the base and 400 feet tall before it even thinks about branching. The bark is tectonic. Plates of cork the size of doors overlap like shingles on a dragon, each plate fissured itself into valleys and ridges, each ridge bearded with silver. Under the plates, sap runs thick as honey and cold to the touch, even at noon. The Holding sweats through its skin, keeping itself cool while the stone bakes.

Then the crown. It does not branch. It erupts. From the top of the column, petioles thick as a man's torso thrust outward and up, each terminating in a single leaf. But that word fails. Each leaf is a vaulted sail 40 feet across, deeply cupped, waxy on top to throw off sun, felted white underneath to trap what it exhales. A mature Holding holds eighty to a hundred of these, interlocked at the edges to form an inverted cone, a green crater that catches light and air and holds it. This is its lung and its well and its farm.

At dawn, the undersides weep. At midday, the whole cone transpires at once — you can see it from miles away, a clear column of trembling air rising straight up, as if the Holding is sighing. By late afternoon that sigh has become a cloud, small, mean, and intensely local, bruised purple-black and anchored directly above its maker by heat. Then it breaks. Not rain as you know rain. A deluge of fat, warm drops the size of marbles that hammer the leaf-drums for seven minutes, sluice down the scooped veins into the central tank — a hollow where the petioles meet, a lake the size of a house held fifty stories in the air, dark, tannic, full of life — and overflow in waterfalls down the trunk plates, filling every fissure, every carved bowl, every throat that was waiting.

The Holding drinks most of it back in an hour through aerial roots that hang like curtain cords from every leaf axil. What it does not drink, it keeps for everyone else.

An old Holding is not an individual. It is a continent in miniature. An orchid with roots like white nerves hangs 30 feet off one leaf and never touches soil in its whole life, drinking only fog. Figs strangle figs on its crown. Mosses run silver curtains down its north face that store ten times their weight in water. Ants farm scale insects in the bark valleys in cities of wax and fungus. When a leaf finally detaches — every thirty or forty years, with a crack like a gunshot that silences the chorus for a full minute — it does not fall so much as land. It sags to the stone, still waxy, still holding a gallon of water in its cup, and within a day it is a field. Grass sprouts from its dust, beetles bore into its midrib and lay eggs, opportunistic vines race across it and fruit frantically before the leaf crumbles to sweet, peppery soil.

And the roots. No one sees the roots. They go down through the old lava, through the honeycombed stone, splitting it with slow, hydraulic patience. At midday, when they all swell together, the old people swear you can feel the Plateau rise a finger's width under your feet. They are probably right.

### The Loud Ones

The second kingdom is sound made flesh.

Because the Holdings are so far apart, and the air between them so hot and shimmering, smell and sight fail over distance. So everything here learned to scream.

**The Lacquer Beetles.** In the crown, they are the cattle. Each as long as your forearm, elytra polished to a black-purple iridescence that reflects the brutal sun, they sit on the leaf veins and saw. Not eat — saw. A hind leg ridged like a file scraped against an abdomen ridged like a washboard, endlessly. One beetle is annoying. Ten thousand beetles on one Holding is the foundation drone of the Plateau, a metallic hum you feel in your sternum from a mile away. Their larvae live in the tank-lake at the crown's heart, blind and shrimp-white, filter-feeding.

**The Glass Wasps.** They are not wasps. They have no sting. They are ribbons, six inches of transparent membrane trailing a thread-thin body, traveling in rivers thousands strong. They don't buzz; they whisper. The rivers pour between Holdings on fixed highways of scent and damp, pollinating the air-grass, pollinating the orchids, catching midges in mid-air with legs like grapnels. At sunset they roost under the outer leaves in a single trembling sheet, turning the green to frost.

**The Throat-Frogs.** They solved water. No ponds, no rivers — so they became ponds. An adult is the size of a bread loaf, warty and stone-colored, wedged into a bark fissure where condensation collects. On its back are two translucent bladders that swell over a week to the size of fists, tight with stored water — its private monsoon. Inside that water, it raises its tadpoles. To call a mate across the deafening beetle roar, it inflates its entire throat sac, a bass drum of skin that drops the air pressure around it. You don't so much hear it as get hit by it: *whoom. whoom.* From fissure to fissure, across the whole trunk, a slow conversation in subsonic thuds felt in the teeth.

**The Crown-Birds.** They are not birds. They are the only vertebrates that dare the open hot air between Holdings for long. Think of a bat stretched thin, then stretched thinner, until the wing membrane is translucent crimson and veined like a leaf. Their fingers are elongated into spars, their bodies small and fierce and featherless. They live in colonies of three or four thousand in the highest leaves, folding their wings around themselves at noon to not cook. When a Glass Wasp river passes, or when a Holding's private storm breaks and flushes insects into the air, they all launch at once. They turn as one cloud, and the air rips. Their shriek is above hearing, a needle that makes every other animal pause half a second. They speak in mirrors — flashes of sun off their wings.

And beneath all that, the **air-grass.** The savanna between Holdings is not empty. Waist-high, golden, each blade rolled into a tube to conserve water, each blade tipped with a fluff of seed that drinks dew. It hides crickets the size of thumbs that never stop clicking, lacewings, hunting spiders that run on top of the grass like water striders, and snakes made of grass itself — green and thin and absolutely still until warm blood passes.

Everything is listening past everything else. To be heard, you have to be extreme: louder, lower, higher, or flashing.

### The Carvers

And then the people. They are the quietest things on the Plateau, which is how they survive it.

They are small — not by birth, by life. Childhood under white sun and thin high air keeps you compact, corded, dark as polished wood. Hair cropped short or shaved to deny parasites. Skin oiled daily with a paste of crushed nut and beetle wax that smells of pepper and keeps the sun from cracking you. Eyes narrowed to slits even in shade, pale from squinting.

They do not live *on* a Holding. They live *with* one Holding, and that Holding owns them. A family — or an extended hearth of 8 to 30 — holds rights to a fissure system on one side of one trunk. The fissure is their house. They do not build it; they negotiate it. With chitin adzes — made from the shed wing-cases of the Lacquer Beetles, harder than horn — and black glass knives from the old volcano seams, they scrape, they coax, they widen a bark valley a foot a generation. They line it with woven fiber and air-grass wax so it holds water. They train a petiole root to droop down like a tap. They tie a leaf-tip with cord to make a cistern. A hearth takes 100 years to become comfortable.

They speak with their hands. Spoken language exists — low, guttural, conserved for night when the chorus dips — but by day it is useless. Instead: mirror flashes from polished beetle elytra for distance, rope tugs for inside the fissure, and the leaf-gongs. Every fissure family stretches a patch of dead bark or thinned leaf over a hollow and beats it with a padded mallet. Each Holding has its own rhythm, its own code. You can hear a gong call for two miles even through the beetle roar, a deep *thok thok — thok* that says *storm coming, pull in the drying meat* or *child lost in the grass*.

They walk between Holdings only when they must, and never alone. The vine paths are their road and their terror. Over decades, they coax two living vines — thick as a thigh, quick-growing, strangler-fast — from adjacent Holdings to reach toward each other across the hundred-foot drop. When they meet, they graft. Over years they thicken, braid, become a swaying catenary that bounces and sighs. Crossing is a ceremony. Barefoot, because you need to feel the vine's tension. A rope around the waist, a second rope held by the person behind. You go one at a time, and the Plateau goes silent around you — not truly, but the insects pause to watch the shadow pass over the grass, a ripple of stillness that follows you. Children are taught not to look down at the honeycombed stone and its temporary silver rivers far below.

They are not many. You can walk three days across air-grass that hisses with life and see no fire but the far, faint glow of a fissure at night. They keep no herds; the Holding feeds them. Grubs from the tank-lake, roasted; nut-meats from the orchid roots; honey-like sap tapped with a reed straw that seals itself in a day; wasp larvae smoked out of roosts. They tend, they do not take. To scar a Holding deeply is exile — to be sent to walk until you find an unclaimed crown, which is to say, to die, because there are no unclaimed crowns.

At noon they stop. Everyone stops. When the Holdings breathe out together and the vaulted arches of refracting damp appear between crowns, connecting the sparse giants with light, the Carvers lay out shallow clay bowls on their bark ledges. Not to collect water — though water will collect — but to offer it back. A little of their own stored drink, poured out to evaporate. An acknowledgment. They say the Plateau inhales. They say if you have bare feet on stone at that moment, you will feel it lift.

At night, when the leaves close a fraction and the air finally, truly, drips, they huddle around their tiny clay-cup fires — too small to be seen far, for fear of attracting Crown-Birds — and listen to the chorus change key, lower and slower, and tell stories about the edge cliffs, where the world drops away and the wind falls forever, and about the smudge on the haze on the far side, which no one living has ever reached.

They do not want to reach it. There is already too much life right here, roaring, to ever know completely.
