Nobody *lives* there the way people live in a house.

No one is at home. That’s the first thing you have to understand about the throat.

What lives there lives in the way water lives in stone — by finding where it can stay.

### The Green That Holds It All

The true residents are not count-their-eyes kinds of residents. They are the carpet itself.

In that permanent dusk, in that cellar-air that never moves above 48 degrees even in August, the moss is not decoration. It is the place.

It grows in such thick, saturated cushions that the rock underneath has ceased to matter. You put your hand down and you do not feel limestone. You feel a cold, wrung-out sponge five inches deep that gives under your palm and sighs water back around your fingers. This is mostly big shaggy northern mosses — *Hylocomium* and *Thuidium*, feathered out like tiny spruce boughs — built over years into hummocks and terraces that follow the seep lines. Between them, a thalloid liverwort spreads like dark, wet leather, and in the driest foot near the lip, crust lichens cling like old paint.

These are the things that can live on almost nothing: scattered light, lime dissolved in dripwater, condensation.

And tucked into them, where the rock weeps most reliably, are the ferns. There are only two, but they own their corners completely. 

Hart's-tongue — *Asplenium scolopendrium* — great glossy straps of green, each frond a perfect tongue veined like a moth's wing, arching out from black crevices. They hold so much water their tips constantly distend and drip *plok* into the basins below. In the most sheltered pocket where the leaning slab makes a little humidity trap, a few clumps of maidenhair tremble on black wire stems so thin you think they'd snap in the roar. They don't. They have been there for decades, slowly cloning themselves along the crack by rhizomes, inch by inch.

That birch on the ramp is not really living *there*. It is dying there very slowly, very gracefully. Its roots are in rubble, in air. Every leaf it puts out is taxed away by shade. Its white bark is freckled entirely on its north side with the same moss that carpets its feet. It will never get bigger than a sapling. It is a visitor that got stuck on its way through.

Underneath all this green, invisible, is the other green: the algal slick. A single-celled film of dark emerald that coats the limestone wherever the spring line never dries. Put your lamp close and it glints. That and fungi — thread-fine mycelium running through the peat-smell at the base of the wall — are what actually eat the rock and make it into soil.

If the throat has a soul, it is this living felt: cold, absorbent, patient to a degree that feels like indifference. It grows slower than a fingernail. A millimeter a year. And it has covered everything that would hold still long enough to let it.

### The Keepers of the Pool

If the moss is the resident, the pool is the heart.

That two-foot-wide, six-inch-deep, black-mirror basin at the foot of the wall is never empty. It has not been empty since the ice retreated and left the till. The seep feeds it drop by drop, even in a dry August when every puddle in the meadow above has cracked to mud. Because of that, there is something in it that exists nowhere else for a mile in any direction.

In late summer, if you lie on your belly on the damp gravel — the roar of the hidden river filling your chest — and hold your breath until the tiny ripples settle, you will see them.

Not much at first. Just flecks. Then movement.

On the bottom, grazing the bacterial film and the limestone silt, are pea-sized white dots that crawl with one foot: limestone springsnails. *Physa* and *Vertigo*-type snails, half-translucent, shell so thin you can see the gut coil through it. They are relict populations, glacial relicts. The meadow around dried and warmed thousands of years ago and their kind died out everywhere but here, in this one cold cup. They live their whole lives in that stew-pot of water, laying gelatinous eggs on the underside of fallen birch leaves.

And if you are very lucky, or come after a rain when the seep runs strong and washes leaf-mulch into the pool — you’ll see the master.

A cave salamander. Not big. Four to six inches, the color of wet limestone itself, mottled amber-grey and pinkish, with a laterally flattened tail for swimming and external gills like little red ferns around its neck if it is young. In older adults the gills resorb but the eyes never fully develop — they remain lidless, pinpricks, nearly blind. *Eurycea spelaea* or its northern cousin, the kind folk above would call a spring lizard and never think of again.

It does not *live* in the pool, exactly. It lives in the bedding plane the pool seeps from — in the hairline sheets of water between rock, in the flooded gravel interstices you can’t put your hand into. The pool is just where it comes to hunt. It moves like it doesn’t want to disturb anyone, nosing under moss edges, sucking up copepods and blind amphipods and mosquito larvae. When your light hits it, it freezes. Then with one slow lateral undulation, it backs into the dark under the rimstone lip and is gone. It may be five years old. Ten. Some in systems like this live twenty. One individual may be the only vertebrate that truly belongs to this throat.

You could visit thirty times and never see it. The pool would look empty. It isn’t.

At night, in the full dark when even the grey scattered light is gone, the grey, stone-colored slugs come out. Big as your thumb — *Arion subfuscus* — and they work the moss tops silently, rasping algae, leaving bright silver mucus trails that catch your lamp like snail roads. With them come isopods, woodlice pale as paper, and thumbnail-sized harvestmen that walk with terrible slowness across fern fronds, feeling their way.

And once or twice in early October, the midges hatch — the tiny flies mentioned in the trace. They are not random. Their larvae have lived for a year in the thin, anoxic mud under the pool’s lip, eating decayed moss. For one evening they rise together in a standing column perhaps three feet high and a foot wide, directly over the water, dancing in the deafening roar. They live for hours. Mate, fall spent onto the water, and feed the snails and the salamander. Then for another year, nothing.

### The Passengers

Everything else is just passing through.

The cave wren — probably a Winter Wren, *Troglodytes hiemalis*, the one that loves exactly this — does not nest in the sink proper, but in the solution pockets just below the lip where wind can't reach. You'll never see her while you're in it. She comes at dusk with a mouthful of caterpillars, slips down the wall like a mouse, disappears into a moss-framed crevice that looks like solid rock until she goes in. Her song, when she does sing from the rim in May, cuts straight through the underground roar for thirty seconds, impossibly loud, complex, fluting, and then is swallowed again.

The white-footed mouse scrabbles down the cairns some nights to drink. The fox that stands at the lip looking down smells the cold and the iron and decides it is not worth the scramble.

Even the bacteria down in the unseen river — blind white amphipods, isopods, possibly a pallid crayfish the length of your finger with antennae longer than its body — they don’t live *here* either. They live *below* here, in the roaring dark that makes the walls thrum. You hear them as sound conducted up through fifty feet of limestone. They will never see this dim green room, though their water made it.

### The Other Thread

And then there is the third kind of life: the human thread.

Whoever stacked those fist-sized cairns, whoever knelt and set unmortared slabs to keep the drinking-pool from silting, whoever brushed moss from a niche and chipped a seat flat with a bad iron adze — they understood perfectly what was allowed here.

You can’t live in a throat that floods in April, that never sees sun, where sound never stops. Your fire won’t stay lit. Your voice won’t carry. So they didn’t try.

They came down. Drank. Perhaps soaked fevered feet in that brutally cold water in July. Left a birch-bark makuk gone to mulch. Cut a handful of hart’s-tongue for wound-dressing — it was once called christ’s-hair for that reason. Sat in that cut seat out of the wind to be alone, to be out of sight, to listen to the earth grind water somewhere underneath them and feel how small and held that made a person.

Then climbed back up the root-lashed ladder and left.

And the moss did what it always does. It grew over the top course of the little dam wall. It softened the tool marks. It swallowed the fallen ladder’s lower end and turned it slowly back into peat. At a millimeter a year, it forgives.

If you stay until your eyes adjust and your ears stop fighting the roar — until the roar becomes less a noise and more a pressure on the skin — the place resolves into a single balance:

A huge amount of empty, humming air.
A very small, absurdly lush amount of green containing almost all the actual life.
One cold eye of water keeping a few tiny, ancient animals alive since the glaciers.
And the sense, powerful because you have to raise your voice to break it, that you are the most temporary thing in it.

It does not invite you to stay. It does not push you out. It simply continues to drip and grow and roar exactly as it did before you slipped down the rubble ramp, and as it will after frost heaves your boot prints away by next spring.
