At first you think no one lives here.

That is the intended illusion. The city was designed for ten thousand bodies per cubic hectare, and now when you stand on the third-level walkway and look out across the stacked dark, you count zero. The scale beats your eye. The emptiness is so complete, so maintained, that you assume you are alone with the tram and the dew.

You are not. You just haven't learned the density yet.

0.2 per volume does not mean empty. It means *far apart*. It means that if there are people here, you will never see two of them at once. And you won't, for a long time. What lives here lives the way deep-sea life lives under pressure — sparsely, slowly, with great distances between heartbeats, entirely fitted to a rhythm you still think is weather.

Let it resolve.

---

The most obvious residents are not people. They are the city learning to be a cave again.

In every cupped palm of smart-ceramic where the dew collects and holds for an hour — in the curled lip of a dead sign, in the suture-hollows that formed around cups long evaporated, in the shallow, steaming plain of the old cistern — life thins itself into a film and waits.

The cleaners are first. You have to crouch and be still through a whole fifteen-minute fall to see them. They are not shrimp, but your brain calls them shrimp because it has nothing else for a translucent comma with too many jointed legs. They were made. Their genome has a copyright watermark still legible in their gut if you could read protein. Then they were forgotten, and then they went feral, which in this place means they got *better at waiting*. For eight hours they are nothing — a bead of drying mucus, a speck. When the air supersaturates and the ticking starts on every rail, they unseal. They bloom open, flick, graze. You can see the dark line of lichen-spore down their backs. They eat the black-green felt that digests the basalt-concrete itself, they eat each other’s sheddings, they eat the faint mineral snow that falls from the upper arch. Before the warm wind comes to erase their pans, they seal again. They have learned to time their entire conscious lives to a puddle. There are millions of them, if you count dormancy as living. Which it is, here.

Above them, the fronds. Not animal, not plant. Engineer’s lichen that remembered it wanted to be fern. Within the four amber circles cast by the stuttering grow-lights, they stand like congregations. Finger-length, pale as nail beds, translucent enough that you see light pipe through their hollow centers. When the dew comes they swell and turn, and you can hear it — a faint creak, like ice expanding, like knuckles. They do not grow beyond the light. They have been trying for centuries and the darkness tells them no. So they grow dense instead, frill over frill, a little forest the size of a dinner table, in an abbey of dark. If you put your hand in the light they lean, not quickly, but inevitably.

And the vines. The cordage. They hang from the undersides of the mid-levels in hanks that look dead — grey, woody, leafless. Touch one after the wind has dried everything and it is taut as wire. Come back after a dew and the same cord is fat, cool, turgid with stored water, hanging an inch lower. They are cisterns that learned to be rope. The cleaners drink from them by piercing them. Something else drinks from them too.

---

That something else is the second thing you learn to see, because it does not want to be seen and the city is so loud it can afford to be silent.

They are tall. When you first catch one on an upper scaffold against the basalt shell, you think *heron*, because of the legs, because of the stillness. Then it moves and your categories fail. It is origami made flesh, or flesh made to imitate origami. Limb segments too long, folded at joints that have more than one axis. A body like a folded kite, tucked tight until it needs to reach. No face that you would call a face — a smooth, ceramic-white wedge with two dark, recessed pits that are not eyes but moisture sensors, dew-sensors.

They were maintenance animals. Servitors. Pets for pipes. The city grew them the way it grew its concrete. Now they are wild in the way a cathedral pigeon is wild. They pick their way across the tram rail a second after the tram has passed, they nest in the balconies that have no interiors, they unfold their absurd legs to crouch and drink from the swollen cord-vines, inserting a needle mouth. They watch you, briefly, and classify you as *warm obstacle, not rail, not water*. They leave three-toed prints in the dew-film that fade in an hour. There are maybe a dozen of them in the whole bowl. You will never see all their nests. They call to each other not with sound — sound is already taken by the ventilation organ and the cracking ceramic — but with vibration through the walkway mesh, a thrumming you feel in your teeth if you lean on the rail. A quiet conversation under the loud.

They are not the people. But they remember where the people used to be, and they tend those places.

---

The people are the last thing to emerge, because they have gone the furthest toward becoming part of the damp.

There are not many. You were told the density and you did not believe it emotionally until the third dew cycle, when you walked the entire arc and counted the signs of habitation: one cup, still warm. One doorway where the white thread of light had been smudged by a shoulder, repeatedly. One patch of black-green felt carefully scraped away and cultivated elsewhere in a band, like a garden.

Stay. Be quiet through the loud. Through the tick-tick-CRACK that makes you flinch every time.

You will meet one at a water-cup.

She is — he is — *it is* — your language will stutter. Human is still accurate, the way a cave fish is still a fish. Descent from the builders, unbroken, but fallowed along with the city. They chose to stay when the density bled out. When everyone else thinned themselves into the larger deep network, or the surface, or the signal, a few chose to keep the bowl warm.

You expect something dramatic. Pale, blind, elongated. You get something subtler, which is more unsettling because it could be you in ten years if you stayed.

They are thin. Not starved — thinned. Elongated by dim light and vertical living, but also smoothed, like the stairsteps. Their skin is cool, almost damp to the touch, same 36C as the air, so they leave no thermal shadow. Their eyes are large, but not grotesque; dark-adapted, with a faint tapetum that catches the tram-glow when they turn. They have let their hair go to felt, or cut it with ceramic shards — it doesn't matter, hair is mostly a place for condensation to collect now. They wear what the city makes when you leave cloth on a ledge long enough: a loose, dark, porous weave that was once a smart-fabric and is now mostly a second skin that drinks dew for you.

They do not live in houses. Houses are voids now. They live *between* — on a favorite walkway where the updraft is kind, in a perforated platform where two levels make a pocket of still air, in the old exchange where the acoustic focal point lets them hear the whole bowl at once. Each claims a territory the size of a small neighborhood and does not cross it often, because crossing it means being visible and sound carries.

When you meet one, you understand the city's etiquette. You do not speak loudly. Loud is for the city. You tap the rail. You leave water.

They are gardeners of lichen-felt and tenders of cord-vines. They harvest fronds — not the photosynthetic tips, but the pale, starchy bases — and chew them slowly. They collect the dormant beads of cleaners during the dry hours and sprinkle them into new pans, seeding. They oil the tram rail with a rag, though it needs no oil, because the ritual says *I am still here and I remember running means alive*. They listen.

What do they listen for?

For return. That long reverberation after the ceramic cracks — they have learned to read it the way you read a hall for occupancy. The bowl is an ear. They press palm to repellent wall and feel for pitter-patter of many feet that have not yet come back. They maintain the fallow, the way a farmer maintains a field under snow: warm, damp, ready.

If you stay, one will bring you a cup of condensed water without a word. It will be cool, faintly mineral, faintly sweet from lichen spore. They will watch you drink. They will touch, very briefly, the back of your wrist with fingertips that are absurdly soft from never drying — a greeting, a check of your warmth, a transfer of skin-microbiome that helps you digest the city.

Then they will be gone, folded up a stairwell that looks like it leads to air, and you will not see them again for days. But you will find that the hollow for your cup has deepened slightly overnight, remembering you. That the light-thread on your doorway stays on a little longer. That the maintenance heron has left you alone.

They are not lonely. That is a surface concept, dependent on crowding. They are *spaced*. They communicate rarely, in thrums through the rail and in marks left in dust that dismantles itself. One lives two levels down. One lives across the bowl where the cistern steams. One lives so high near the arch you can only see the amber of their garden. Together they are a quorum of four or five or six — the number changes when one finally decides to thin out and leave, or when one comes back.

They think of themselves not as residents but as the part of the city that stayed mobile. The city itself — the memory in materials, the process that keeps temperature steady, the tram that remembers running — is the major inhabitant, and they are its hands.

If you stand very still in the dark between dews, and let the organ-tone of the ventilation chord fill your sternum until your own breathing syncs, you can feel it: the place is inhabited from top to bottom, just not by a crowd. It is inhabited by a single distributed attention, warm and patient, that has grown fronds in the light and folded animals on the rails and a few soft, dark-eyed people in the between-spaces to keep its water cups full.

It is waiting with them.

And for a moment, listening to the tick of droplets magnified into a percussion you feel in your bones, you are part of that waiting too.
