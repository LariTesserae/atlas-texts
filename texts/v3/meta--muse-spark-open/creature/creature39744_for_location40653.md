Who lives in the High Arbor is not quite the right question. *What* lives here is more accurate. Because nothing here lives *alone*.

### The Arbor Itself

First, it does. It is the citizen with seniority.

It is not a tree. Trees have ambition. They want to be taller than each other. The Arbor gave that up a long time ago and decided to be *wider* than everything instead.

It is not sentient the way you are. It does not think in words. It thinks in pressures. In sap gradients, in light deficits, in the slow hydraulic calculus of where to put the next leaf that will be the size of a parking lot. It remembers being cold-pressured by ice, remembers the first brick foundations cut into its ancestor gneiss, remembers the taste of mortar. It does not resent you. Resentment requires a boundary between self and other, and the Arbor has been blurry on that point for three hundred years.

It lives by negotiation. You cut it, and it weeps and then scabs over harder than before. You feed it ash from your pellet-burners, and it grows you a new wall, straight as a plumb line, over three damp months. You neglect your district’s breathing — forget to pare back the stomata-shutters, let condensate pool and go stagnant — and it will decide you have abandoned your rooms and quietly seal the pith-tunnels with new growth, digesting your stored grain, your left-behind boots, your cousin who fell asleep on shift, with the same patient cellulose. Not cruelty. Housekeeping.

Workers swear it dreams. On windless nights when the factories are banked, you can put your ear to a load-bearing petiole and hear the sap moving inside it, not as a gurgle but as a low, arrhythmic thudding, like a heart that forgot how many chambers it was supposed to have. Young apprentices claim that if you sleep in the deep understory without a rebar cot, you will wake up two feet to the left of where you lay, because the Arbor shifted you while you breathed, trying you on in a different spot.

It is the landlord. It is the city. It is the weather system you pay rent to.

### The Humans: Arbor-Born

There are about forty-five thousand of them across the three domes, which is to say almost no one at all. The High Arbor was built for a quarter million. You feel that everywhere: the half-empty tenements where every third window has been politely grown over with translucent leaf-membrane because no one needed it; the vast weaving halls where four old women tend twenty clattering looms that once needed sixty hands; the cable-car that leaves on time whether or not anyone is on it.

They are not pioneers anymore. They are maintenance people for a symbiosis they inherited.

You can tell an Arbor-born by the lungs and the hands.

Their lungs are big, barrel-chested even when they are thin, from a lifetime of thin, cold air that makes a simple jog leave you lightheaded. Their voices carry, because they have had to learn to shout over machine roar and then be heard across a leaf a hundred yards wide. A child of the Lamina can yell a warning about a diverting tendril and be understood two streets over without a radio.

Their hands are dry, resin-stained, perpetually nicked. Everybody, even the clerks, even the children, knows how to wield a hot iron, a sap-knife, a resin-trowel. You learn early that your house bites if you don't file its edges. Nails grow thick and yellowed because they are always a little poisoned by turpenic sap. Hair is kept short or braided tight, because the living streetlamps — those phototropic nodules — will gently tangle in loose hair as you pass, curious, not hostile, just tasting your heat.

They dress in layers that can be shed and added as the day swings from frost-smoke to shirtsleeve-warm: canvas overalls waxed against dew, wool undershirts knit from imported lowland sheep that have never set foot here, soft-soled shoes that grip waxy lamina without tearing it. No one wears all black if they can help it; it confuses the moths. No one wears pure white on shift; it marks you for the echo joints.

Culturally, they are industrial animists.

They are not tree-huggers. Tree-huggers talk to the forest. Arbor people *file paperwork* with it. Every factory has a Tendery — a crew of three to seven who do nothing but read the plant. They check sap pressure with tapped gauges made of bamboo and brass. They walk the perimeter with living plumb-lines — pale, trembling tendrils kept in water jars that always point to true down, even when your own inner ear says down is sideways near a gravity knot. They paint yellow circles around new “echo joints” where distance stutters, and update the wall maps in the break room with careful notes: *BRIDGE C-9 - 1.3 second lag if crossed alone after rain. Cross in pairs.*

Superstition is industrial protocol. Rule #3 is not folklore; it’s on laminated cards zip-tied to every pith-tunnel entrance: IF YOU HEAR YOUR NAME CALLED FROM AN EMPTY PITH-TUNNEL, ANSWER WITH SOMEONE ELSE'S NAME. Everyone does. They say it briskly, like answering a phone. “Not me, that’s Joren!” The tunnel usually loses interest.

They are quiet people in a loud place. Density 1.2 means you can go a whole shift speaking only to confirm valve settings and Tendery warnings. Solitude is built into the architecture; sound carries so far that you learn to be economical. At dusk, though, when the great steam whistle blows and the dew shakes off a mile of leaves in a silver spray, they gather. Communal kitchens in the intervessel courtyards roast protein cakes made from pressed epiphyte flour and Vat-pork, drink thin, bitter tea brewed from condensate and roasted leaf-vein. They trade gossip about which dome flowered last, whose apartment just grew an extra closet overnight, which echo bridge ate a toolbox again.

Children are scarce and feral in the way of high-plateau kids. They know not to chase a ball that rolls uphill into a root-knot. They play “loom-tag” in abandoned halls, and dare each other to put a hand into an inverted sap pond hanging from a ceiling and pull it out dry on the other side. They grow up understanding that the floor is alive, slightly warm, and occasionally impatient. They are taught to thank a leaf when it lets them sit on it, not out of reverence but out of habit, like patting a dog that could accidentally crush you.

The old are different. Some have spent sixty years with the Arbor’s creak in their bones. Their joints pop in sympathy with the big leaves overhead. A few have what the clinic calls “laminar drift” — from too many years working near bloom-time anomalies. They age unevenly: a left hand that looks ten years older than the right, a memory that arrives a day before the event that caused it. They are respected, avoided, asked for advice only carefully.

And above them all are the few who hear the Arbor most clearly — not mystics, but *tappers*. Men and women with sap on their forearms like tattoos, who have grafted small, living sensors into their work gloves, who can stand on a trembling aerial root and tell you by the vibration whether the district generator two valleys over is misfiring. They are half mechanic, half arborist. They are the priests of no religion, because the Arbor never asked to be worshiped, only tended.

### The Others Who Pass for Fauna

Almost nothing else. 0.9 is a kind way of saying the Arbor choked out everything that could not negotiate.

**Lichen-cats.** They live here because they learned to be lichen. Six limbs, each foot splayed with a mossy pad, gray as the gneiss still visible underneath in the troughs. They are no bigger than a loaf of bread, and they glide — not fly — on flaps of skin grown between fore- and mid-limbs, from lamina to lamina, silent. They steal heat. You will find one curled on your forge-warmed resin kettle come morning, staring at you with four lidded eyes, daring you to move it. No one does until their shift is over. To kill a lichen-cat is bad luck not because it is sacred, but because the Arbor uses them to carry its spores, and your apartment will start smelling of mushrooms for weeks if you anger it.

**Condensate Moths.** The size of serving trays, wings like translucent vellum veined with living light. At dawn they descend in mute clouds to drink from the hanging axillary cisterns — those billboard-leaf ponds — dipping absurd proboscises in perfect unison. The air fills with the powder of their wings, which smells faintly of ozone. Touch one and it dies, its wings sloughing off in your hands like wet paper. The children collect the dead ones and press them into sheets to make lamp shades. The adults let them alone; the moths filter the water you will drink later without knowing.

**The Stilt-Herons.** No one is sure they are animals. Seven feet tall, legs like bleached rebar, heads that are mostly beak and a single amber eye that never blinks. They stalk the brown braided rivers through the troughs, stepping with impossible care between braided channels, spearing blind white fish that might also be root tubers. When you approach, they do not fly. They unfold — vertically, joints bending wrong — and step *into* a root buttress and are gone, as if the Arbor opened a door for its gardener. Workers leave them alone. They keep the waterways from clogging. Sometimes you find a perfect circle of smoothed stones in the shallows where one stood all night, and your plumb-line spins.

And sometimes, briefly, there are more. Sparrows that shouldn't exist after the whistle. A dog barking from the entombed old city under the understory, where no dog has lived in a century, and then stopping when you call back. Footsteps behind you on a root-bridge that has no one else on it, keeping perfect time with yours until you stop to let them pass. The fauna count is low because the census only counts things that stay.

All of them — human, cat, moth, heron, plant-that-pretends-to-be-bird — live in the same contract:

Keep moving water moving. Keep fire contained but fed. Answer to another name when the empty calls you. And never, ever think the city is yours to finish.
