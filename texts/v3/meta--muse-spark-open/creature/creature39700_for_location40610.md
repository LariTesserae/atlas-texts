What lives here is not what you expect to live in an ocean. Nothing in this ocean learned to be oceanic first. Everything here learned to be high, and then learned to be wet.

### The Rooted

They are the foundation, and they are everywhere, because nothing else could have taken hold so fast on rock this new. Call them the Drowned Trees, though neither word fits right.

On land, a tree fights gravity. Here, a tree negotiates buoyancy. Their seedlings find the only purchase there is — a crack in fresh basalt, a lip of scoria still sharp enough to cut — and drive down pale, woody talons that swell and lock as they grow. You can see the grip: roots gone thick and flattened like hands clenched around a knife edge, bark grown over stone, stone grown into bark. They have to hold, because everything about the trunk wants to rise.

The trunks themselves are not wood as you know it. Cut one — nothing here has — and you'd find rings, but loose, airy, full of vertical gas channels that run its entire length like the pith of a reed scaled monstrous. Young trunks are translucent amber, older ones bleach to bone-white and peel. They shed in slow sheets that never sink; they rise to join the ghost forest of suspended decay above. They creak constantly, a low forest chorus, each trunk in tension between its anchor and its own lift.

Most never reach the membrane. About twenty meters beneath true air, they stop fighting up and turn sideways. That is where they make the False Surface.

Broad leaves — door-wide, waxy, overlapping like shingles — interlock until light comes through only as freckles and moving coins. From below they look solid enough to walk on. From above, if you could stand on a peak and look down through the clear skin of the sky-sea, you'd see a second country, green-gold and trembling, with only the highest ridges punching through it like islands. A few fronds are more ambitious. On calm days they pierce straight through the membrane and unfurl in the actual atmosphere, steaming and curling within hours, tasting true sun and true wind before retreating, bleached and curled, back under. Nothing lives permanently above that membrane. The ceiling is the top of the world for everything rooted.

Below the ceiling is a forest, not a water column. You could get lost between two trunks ten meters apart and never find open swimming.

### The Hanging

Between trunk and trunk, the middle-water should be empty. It isn't. It is choked, gently, completely, with things that live off the trees and off each other and off light that filters sideways.

Rope-weeds hang from leaf-axil to leaf-axil, sometimes fifty meters long, beaded every foot or so with warm, amber gas bladders the size of fists. They tick and ping softly all day as bladders swell and pop and re-form, lifting the curtains into slow S-curves. The red comb-weeds — feathery, meter-wide fans — do nothing but strain. They face whatever faint convection current there is and comb pollen and spores from the broth. The water itself has texture because of them: everywhere a snow of living flecks, green spores and translucent seeds that rise instead of fall, drifting upward toward light even after they've been shed. Sediment doesn't fall here so much as loiters, grain by grain, landing on a leaf, being lifted again by a bubble, never quite reaching the bottom.

The cliffs are not bare. Where a tree hasn't found footing, the carpet has. Liver-mat three feet thick, spongy and liver-dark, covers vertical walls in undulating sheets that pulse faintly when touched. What looks like moss is mostly colonial sponge, olive and rust-red, breathing water so slowly you can't see it move, exhaling it a degree warmer. Giant cliff-lilies grow straight out from sheer faces, their pads stacked like plates held parallel to the sun's columns, each pad holding perfectly still a disc of clearer, filtered water on its upper surface, like a lens. Tiny shrimp-things — the first real animals you notice, because there are thousands of them — live their whole lives inside those lens-discs, skating on the lily's skin.

This middle layer does most of the living and most of the dying for the whole sky-sea. Growth is unhurried to the point of meditation: a frond takes a month to unfurl, a seed-pod swells for a season before it detaches with an audible soft *tap* and begins its slow ascent. Fallen fronds don't rot. They bleach and hang, caught on ropes and branches, forming a pale under-canopy of the dead that can persist for a year before warm bacteria finally soften them into silt. Light down here is perpetual late afternoon, green-gold, dusted.

If you pull yourself along a trunk — because swimming through this is useless, you have to climb — you displace clouds of spores that hang in the disturbed water for minutes before resettling.

### The Drifters and Grazers

Fauna is scarce the way fauna is scarce on a newly formed volcanic island. The rock hasn't had time to make caves, shells, bones. The water is too warm to hold much oxygen. Everything that moves here pays for movement dearly, and so almost nothing hurries.

The commonest large animal — and even it is uncommon — is the Pale. You will hear grazers described as deer, but they are not deer. They are long, white, barrel-bodied drifters the size of a young elk, with no eyes worth naming, just smooth pearl patches where eyes might have been. Four soft, fringed fins run almost the length of their bodies, and they don't beat them; they undulate them once, and then glide for a minute while momentum carries them. Their mouths are ventral suckers ringed with soft teeth like baleen. They nose along rope-curtains and comb-weeds and simply *vacuum*, stripping the epiphyte fuzz, the liver-mat edges, the cloud of spores, with a faint sighing sound. When two meet between trunks, they rotate slowly around each other without touching, exchanging a plume of milky gametes that hangs in the water like smoke, then drift apart without acknowledgment. You can watch one for a long time before you realize it has gotten smaller in the distance. Time slips.

Higher, where the canopy breaks near peaks, are the Canopy Rays. Five at most together, though older clearings show faint circular scars on the False Surface where larger flocks once rested. Black against the bright membrane when seen from below, wingspans wider than a boat, so thin you can see sun through them. They flap perhaps once every ten slow seconds — a single, enormous downbeat that pushes a visible pulse through the leaves below — and otherwise hang motionless, riding the heat rising off stone. They feed by skimming: opening a cavernous, jawless mouth and flying — if you can call that patient gliding flying — just under the membrane, straining the spore-broth. They never call. The only sound they make is the displacement of water over leaf when their shadow passes and the forest briefly darkens.

Lower still are the things you almost never see. In the troughs and calderas where light goes truly dim and warm vents breathe threads of bubbles that take an hour to rise, there are beds of glass. Not mineral — animal. Colonial cnidarians that lay down no shell but a lattice of silica spicules, building brittle, vase-like towers a meter tall on horizontal rock where nothing else will root. Inside each vase lives a blind, shrimp-pale guardian, legs gone filamentous, whose only job is to fan water through the tower. They are the closest thing to a crab this sky-sea has managed yet, and they are soft. If you brushed a tower it would shatter into needles and reform over months.

And the hunter, because even a young, quiet ocean eventually makes one: the Hang. You mistake it for another curtain of red weed until you have already pushed past it. It is weed that chose animal. A mottled, ragged sheet as big as a blanket, with dozens of trailing tendrils tipped in adhesive pads. It does not swim at all. It hangs among the rope-weeds and waits for a Pale to blunder, for a shoal of lily-shrimp to be dislodged. Then it folds, very slowly — you could pull your arm free if you were watching, but nothing here watches — and envelops. Digestion takes days, during which the Hang glows faintly, visibly fuller, still pretending to be weed.

### The Unseen Majority

For every drifter you see, there are ten million you don't.

The water itself is alive in the microbial sense: warm-bath bacteria turning fallen leaf ghosts into silt, cyanobacterial films making every sun-column glitter, fungi that are not fungi threading through bark and sponge alike. The smell — if you could take off a mask and smell, which you can't, because you would be breathing water — would be iron and warm rot and green, like a greenhouse after rain.

Nothing here sings. Nothing here swarms. Reproduction is broadcast, tentative: tap-detaching seeds, popping bladders releasing spore, milky plumes. Even death is slow suspension.

And underneath it all, always, the stone settling. The plateau is still young enough to groan under its impossible load. Low, subaudible creaks travel through trunk and water and bone, reminding everything rooted that this arrangement is temporary on a geologic scale. In a million years these cones will be softened, these cliffs crumbled, the basin breached, this whole perched sea spilled down to the real low oceans. For now, it holds.

What lives here lives as if it knows it has a little while and no reason to rush: rooted giants holding themselves down against their own desire to float, carpets that breathe once an hour, white grazers that kick once and drift, black rays that flap once and glide, all suspended in warm, bright, utterly silent green — a jungle that forgot which way was up, keeping an ocean balanced on a mountain in the sky.
