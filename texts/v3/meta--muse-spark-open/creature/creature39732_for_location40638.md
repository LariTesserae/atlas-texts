Nothing lives here the way a forest lives.

Nothing decided to live here. Nothing arrived. What lives here *remained* — or accreted — after everything that could leave had left, and after everything that needed to leave to die had died.

And it does not live *on* the Floor. It lives *with* the leak of it.

### The True Residents Are Not Animals

First, correct your expectation. If you are waiting to see something move, you will wait until the Floor polishes your bones flat.

Fauna here is a rounding error. 0.4 out of 3 is a way of saying: the air is too heavy with iron and cold oil to breathe if you had lungs that cared. The Sound — that seven-second throb coming up through the soles of your boots, into your molars — would shatter any nesting thing. There are no birds, because there is no sky to be under. No rats, because there is nothing to hide behind. No insects, because condensation here never stops; a gnat would drown standing still.

If you stand very still in the wet dusk and watch a shallow street-river — a hundred meters wide, two centimeters deep, the water the color of cold tea — you will eventually convince yourself you saw something flicker. You didn't. It was a rust flake settling. The water itself is too dissolved, too recycled through ten thousand years of slag-brick, for anything with a mouth to drink.

So what lives here lives without moving. Or without moving in a way you would grant the word *alive*.

### The Four That Stayed

**1. The Lichen That Eats Stone.**

On every slightly sheltered plane — the lee side of a foundry-dome worn down to a hill, the underside of a sagging roof-plate, the vertical lip of a cooling-basin amphitheater — there are coins.

At first you think it's spatter. Then you crouch, and your knees are wet, and the Sound is louder close to the Floor, and you see them: black rosettes, perfect, a centimeter or two across, with a faint raised lip like a corroded coin. *Lithicorona nigra*, if anyone were left to name it. It does not grow on concrete. It grows *into* it. It secretes an acid so mild it takes a decade to taste, and it digests basalt-concrete and exhales a dusting of soft black sand.

When the eternal weeping wets them, they glow. Not brightly. If you shade them with your hand you see a faint amber-green foxfire along their gills, the same bioluminescent chemistry that lines the water. They are not in a hurry. A rosette the size of your palm might be four hundred years old. They propagate by flaking — a wet wind peels the edge and carries a black speck a meter or two to start again.

There are millions of them. Which still means you can walk for minutes and see only bare rust between them. They make the Floor look freckled, diseased.

**2. The Hangers.**

Where a pipe still sweats — and pipes still sweat everywhere, endless manifolds exhaling from the warm below into the cool 8-degree air — you will find white thread-fungus.

It looks like ruined insulation left by a careless builder. It hangs in beards, in curtains, sometimes a single filament two meters long, trembling. It is bone-white, translucent when wet, and it never stops dripping. It drinks directly from condensation; it has no roots. It sways not in wind but in the hot exhalations that pulse from fissures every seven seconds. Inhale, sway out. Exhale, sway back.

If you touch it, it comes apart like wet paper and does not reform. It is not strong. It lives only where it is sheltered from the direct scream of venting steam. Under a gantry leg, under the rebar teeth, under the lip of a dome where ghost-gravity still pulls water sideways. There it can hang for years, filtering iron out of the air, turning it into more white thread.

It is the softest thing for kilometers. That is its only defense: nothing here wants softness.

**3. The Stain.**

In the flattest places, where the street-rivers pool and slow and finally hesitate before being pulled *uphill* by the memory of a tower, the water stays.

These are fields — sometimes a few meters across, sometimes a hectare — of standing water twenty centimeters deep, so still they perfectly reflect the low industrial dusk until you step and break them. And just under the surface, there is color.

Rust-red algae. Not a mat. A stain. It spreads as a film so thin you can see the concrete silt through it, a translucent red-brown cloud anchored to nothing, photosynthesizing — if that word still applies — on the dull red glow of pilot lights leaking through cracks, on the foxfire of the lichen, on nothing at all for months at a time.

It does not bloom. It *persists*. It endures the endless vibration that ripples the pools in concentric rings from some stamping press below. It endures the dissolved iron that would poison any other green thing, by incorporating it — its cells are half rust. When it dies, it does not float. It settles as a fine silt that becomes the bed for the next film to grow over.

You could scoop a handful and see nothing in your palm but reddish water. And yet from a low rise, with the light right, you see it: vast, faint, living watercolor washes, kilometers of shallow sea that is only ankle-deep and has no shore.

**4. The Tree That Misunderstands Gravity.**

And then, rarely, the exception that proves the rule is not cruel but indifferent.

Every few hundred meters — often much less, sometimes you walk an hour for the next — a birch. Or something that was a birch, before deep time edited it. Or an ironwood. Pale, starved, no taller than your shoulder, sometimes no taller than your knee. Its trunk is white but stained black at the base where it wicks rust-water. Its leaves are small, translucent, yellow-green, and they shake constantly with the Floor's throb so you hear a faint rustle even when there is no wind.

It grows straight out of a rust-pit. No soil; just silt that collected where a drain is clogged, or where a softer brick dissolved faster and made a bowl. The water there is slightly less toxic. Slightly.

What is strange is how it leans. All of them lean — not with wind, not toward light, because light here has no direction. They lean *toward empty air*. Toward the center of a dome that is no longer there. Toward the ghost of a wall that once held mass enough to dent gravity itself. Their roots, if you dig — and you shouldn't — are not radial. They reach to one side, groping for phantom weight.

One in four is already dead. You learn to spot them before you get close: no leaves, just white spars, bark peeling in wet strips like old paint, still shaking because the Floor still shakes them even after they have died. They stand for years dead. There is nothing to knock them down.

They do not make a forest. They make loneliness visible.

---

And that is what lives here. Not a chain. Not a web. Four separate solitudes that have learned to live on weeping and warmth and heavy water, each in the crack where the machine still sweats.

The Floor itself is the fifth resident — not alive, but active. The presses that stamp with no product. The pumps larger than houses that throb and pull the street-rivers uphill toward drains that lead nowhere you can find. The conveyor that still drags its plates in a screaming circle, carving its meter-deep groove one molecule at a time.

It tends them without knowing them. It waters the lichen. It mists the fungus. It fills the algal pools.

If you stay long enough to be counted, you will be the only thing here that chooses where to stand — and you will feel, after a while, that faint tide in your blood, that leaning toward a tower you cannot see, suggesting you will not choose for long.
