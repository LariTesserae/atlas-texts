They live here because it is soft.

Not everything can live on ground this young. The mud is too wet to hold a burrow, too salty-sweet, too hungry - it would swallow a mammal's den, rot a reptile's egg clutch if it sat directly on it. The island has to be held together before it can be inhabited, and the first residents are the ones who can do the holding.

So at the edge, rooted ankle-deep in brown water, live the Red Daughters.

They don't move. They are the mangroves, and they are the walls and the foundations and the mothers of the room. Young, fast-grown, thin-trunked, their prop roots are dropped down like stilts and knees and elbows, straining. Each root is furred with algae, dotted with barnacle and oyster spat where the tide kisses, slick with mudskipper tracks. They breathe through pores in their bark. They don't think, but they do decide: where to split, where to knit tighter. Their canopy is the roof that makes the weather inside different from the weather outside. Inside their circle, they have made an incubator on purpose without ever intending to.

And because they made a room, the weavers came.

This is their city, but they would not call it that. They are small even for birds - river weavers no bigger than your thumb, males yellow as fresh sapwood with black masks stitched across their eyes, females duller olive, juveniles still the pale green of unripe mango. They live by tens and twenties in neighborhoods.

Lowest, in the dimmest, dampest air just above your head when you crouch, live the Old Tenants. Their nests are the oldest, a season or two old - which here is ancient. Woven grass gone grey, sagging with moss and fern sporelings sprouting from the thatch itself. The openings have widened with use. Inside, second-generation tenants have moved in: tiny tree frogs the color of wet bark who sleep in the abandoned foyers, roaches who farm fungus in the damp felt, a sleek slate-blue lizard with a throat that pulses like a metronome who owns one porch and defends it by doing absolutely nothing until something smaller makes a mistake.

Above them, the Mid-City is dense and loudest - though loud here is still a whisper. This is where the weavers are most active right now. A female clings upside-down to her half-finished gourd, feet like wire, stripping a fresh palm filament with her beak, threading it over-under, tugging it tight with a whole-body jerk. Her doorway is a perfect circle you could push a fingertip through, always turned leeward, always away from the mean river wind. Next door, two nests share a vine and have chewed a small internal tunnel between them - sisters, probably, who raised chicks together last flood. The males dart between - bright flashes - carrying green strands, carrying caterpillars. The chicks inside do not cry. They trill, low and muffled by three layers of woven insulation, a sound like wet fingers on felt. When a thin shoelace snake - brown, patient, tongue flicking once per minute - probes along a branch looking for that sound, five adults will appear without calling and hover, feint, peck at its tail until it decides gravity is easier and lets itself fall into the mud below, where crabs will investigate it.

Higher still, near the leaf-roof where sun comes through in hot, dusty columns full of midges, live the High Architects. Not weavers at all. Here are the mud masons - wasps the length of your thumbnail, black and amber, with waists you could thread a needle with - building pottery. They collect the same coffee-colored silt from the mud plates at your feet, roll it into pellets and plaster it into tiny urns stuck to the undersides of branches. Each urn has one egg and one paralyzed spider. Higher is drier, better for curing clay. Beside them are the great communal thatchers: tailorbirds and warblers who have sewn living leaves together with spider silk into pouches, and stingless bees who have made black, resinous apartment mounds the size of pumpkins, studded with waxen doorways. Go close - if you could reach that high - you'd see the traffic: bees coming and going in straight, silent lines like commuters who know the route so well they don't need to look.

And that is only the air.

Look down, because most of the city is not in the air.

On every vertical root, on every vine-handrail of the city, live the Pavement People. Ants. Black, red, caramel-colored, some so small you think it's mildew until it moves. They have made highways. One column goes up carrying empty mandibles, one column goes down carrying white larvae like grains of rice, leaf bits, a dead midge wrapped in silk. They maintain their roads, clearing fallen scales, repairing washouts after rain with grains of sand. If a weaver chick falls - which happens - they will find it before the crabs do, but not as predators; as recyclers. Nothing wasted.

In the silt itself, in the puddles that never learned to drain, live the Water Babies. Tadpoles, comma-black and comma-gold, flickering through warm algae soup. Mosquito wrigglers hanging from the surface film. Diving beetles that row with oared legs. Over that water, on impossible toes that distribute weight like snowshoes, picks the water rail - brown, secretive, beak down - spearing. She lives alone at ground level, too big for a nest-hang, so she has tucked a shallow cup of reeds behind a crab chimney and watches everything with one sideways eye.

Because at the mud line live the Diggers. Violet-clawed mangrove crabs, fist-sized and solemn, who have built a mirror city. Where the birds hang down, they push up. Chimneys of wet soil, pellets rolled and patted into towers, each with a slick, perfectly round burrow mouth at the base that fills with water at high tide and breathes air at low. The males stand at their doors and wave their one oversized claw - not in greeting, in threat - all day in near-total silence. Their young, planktonic, live for now out in the milky brown river fingers beyond the root-wall; they'll return when they have a shell hard enough to dig with.

Mudskippers live with them, amphibious fish who have decided they are better at being lizards. They haul themselves out and sit in the warm ooze, eyes on stalks, throat-pulsing, breathing through their skin with a faint *pop*. They squabble over puddles no deeper than a teacup.

In the crevices between all these levels live the night shift, folded up for now. Bats - small, dog-faced, wings like damp paper umbrellas - tucked into the shadows between two big weaver tenements, hanging in neat rows of six and eight, shivering occasionally, waiting for dusk when the birds go quiet and they will own the flight corridors. And on a dead fig branch that didn't make it, a fungus glows very faintly white-green, digesting the wood cell by cell, turning last year's anchor vine into next year's nest fiber.

There is no mayor. There is no plan anyone drew.

What there is is agreement, worn into habit over a hundred thousand wet seasons on a hundred thousand temporary islands just like this one:

Stay damp or die. Build light; the young earth cannot carry stone. Take only the green fiber, the fresh mud, the soft leaf. Keep your voice down - a hidden room only stays hidden if its children learn to whisper. Share the vine. Repair the perch.

If you stay crouched at the narrow door in the prop roots long enough, until your knees ache from sea-level, you stop seeing species. You see functions. Hangers, diggers, rowers, sewers, plasterers, carriers, watchers. A whole economy scaled to fit in two houses' worth of air, suspended between water and leaf, on ground so new it still sighs when you step on it.

It isn't loud. It isn't empty. It's just small and very, very busy being alive.
