Nothing lives here.

That is the first thing you have to understand, and the last thing you will believe.

Nothing with eyes lives here. Nothing with legs, with wings, with a mouth that opens on purpose. Nothing that would register on a census. No rats, no roaches, no sparrows that got lost down the ramp and couldn't find their way back to the sky. No ants. God - especially no ants. In any other hot, wet hole in the earth the drums of standing water would be black with larvae, the air thick with mosquitoes. Here the water is glassy and untouched. A fallen leaf can sit in it for a week and never ripple.

The pit has sterilized itself with heat and patience.

If you stand still long enough, you will realize *you* are the fauna here. 0.2 out of 3. Marginal. Vagrant. The system barely counts you. You brought the noise of your boots and your breathing down the switchback, and the pit tolerates it the way the sea tolerates a single thrown stone. You will leave, or you will stop moving and be edited out.

What lives here instead is vegetable, and mineral, and procedural.

### The Islands

It does not live *in* the pit. It lives *on* the pit, the way lichen lives on a skull. In archipelagos. In three, four, five violent little nations that do not trade or war.

The first and oldest nation is **The Sump-Tangle.**

In the northeast corner where the concrete has cracked and subsided into a shallow sump, everything that drips finally arrives. The water there is warm as broth, faintly oily, stained tea-brown with tannins and rust. From it rises a clump of elephant-ear philodendrons that should not exist. Leaves as big as riot shields, as big as the hood of a car, black-green and so glossy they reflect the one work-bulb above them like wet plastic. They grow straight out of each other, stalk swallowing stalk, with no soil to speak of — only the compost of their own fallen sisters, a deep, steaming black peat that has been made entirely out of themselves. Nothing ever carried anything in. Nothing ever carried anything out. It is a closed economy.

If you part the outer leaves — they will be hot to the touch, sweating — the air inside the clump is five degrees hotter and stops moving. It smells like a greenhouse and a battery. In the center, where there should be darkness, there is a faint, sourceless amber glow. One of the leaves grew around a fallen work-light. It didn't die. It enclosed it. The bulb is still powered, somehow, fed by a root that found the cloth-wrapped cord and grew along it, and now the light shines *through* the leaf, making the veins light up like a hand held against the sun.

That light has never been seen until you saw it. It doesn't need to be seen.

The second nation is **The Engine-Garden.**

This one is not green. Not quite.

The old diesel on its plinth in the center is the hottest thing in the room, hotter even than the pipes. Its green paint is blistered. Its flywheel turns once every twenty seconds — *huh... huh... huh...* — with a shudder that rearranges the dust on the floor. Nothing has turned it off. Nothing remembers to.

And *it* is growing.

Not moss on top of it. *In* it. Seeds fell into the grease packed around its dynamo, into the black, warm, mineral-rich sludge where an oil leak meets condensation drip, and germinated. There is a fern, pale as bean sprouts, growing straight out of the inspection hatch, its fiddleheads unfurled around spinning copper windings. There is a vine — that pale, translucent kudzu — that has sent a runner *into* the engine's air intake, so that every slow piston wheeze ruffles its leaves. Its new shoots all point inward, toward the thrum. They do not phototrop. They audiotrop. They sonatrop. They lean toward vibration and heat, because there is so little honest light to lean toward.

The most unsettling one is the philodendron on the far side of the plinth. It has dropped a curtain of aerial roots as thick as your wrist down into the dynamo itself. You can watch, if you have twenty seconds to spare between turns of the wheel, as the charge builds. The roots pulse. The veins in the leaves flash amber, in time with the current, a slow heartbeat of light. Then fade. The plant is drinking electricity. It has no pollinator, no predator, no purpose for that light.

Or perhaps it does. Perhaps it is signaling.

The third nation is **The Shed-Fig.**

The foreman's hut is one cinderblock room, desk, chair, calendar from eight years ago, coffee mug with a fossilized ring. The roof is gone. A fig tree took it.

It didn't grow *next* to the hut. It grew *through* it. Its roots entered through the window, left through the door, cracked the back wall and drank the sump on the other side. The walls now are just a trellis the tree is wearing. The desk is inside the trunk. Figs — small, hard, green-white, never ripening — hang in the humid dark like unlit bulbs. They never fall and rot. They just hang there, indefinitely, because there is nothing to eat them. No wasp to enter them. No bird to carry them. They will hang until they mummify and become part of the wood.

Under that fig, in the deepest shade, is where the real inhabitant lives, if you want to call it that.

### The Under-Living

Lift a dead frond. You will jump at the slap it makes — the loudest thing that's happened in an hour.

Underneath, the floor is not floor. It is a mat of moss so thick you can press your thumb into it and leave a print that slowly fills with warm water. And threading that moss, marbling it, is the fungus.

It is not a mushroom. It is a *sheet.* A pale, waxy bracket here, a luminous thread there, a crust that looks like dried soap scum across an oil drum. It is the only thing in the pit that is actively digesting. Leaves fall — *slap* — and lie steaming on the concrete. Without insects, without bacteria from outside, they would lie forever. But this slow, white, brainless mouth finds them. It veins them, bleaches them, turns them to peat. It has no spores you can see. It does not need to travel far. It already is everywhere the green is not.

It and the green are one system. The plants make leaf; the fungus unmakes it; the water cycles it; the heat cooks it. No animal mediates. No animal is required.

This is what lives here: a plant-fungus-machine that learned to close its loop.

It doesn't think. It doesn't want. But it *attends.* Put your hand near the hot pipes and the ferns there will be bleached at the tips from growing too fast, too hot — cancerous with abundance. Put your ear to the vine on the steel joist and you can feel it leaning, microscopically, toward the flywheel's next turn. Leave your boot print in the moss and in three days the moss will have erased it, not out of malice, but out of sheer, thoughtless growth.

It has no face. It has a thousand leaves that do the work of a face: turning toward, drinking, being.

And it has you, for now. Another warm body dripping salt and carbon dioxide into air that has not had either in a long time. The philodendrons will barely notice you until you leave. Then the faint disturbance in humidity where you stood will close over, the fungus will take the skin cells you shed, and the engine will make its next turn —

*huh...*

— and nothing will have happened at all, except the pit will have kept living, quietly, without witness, as it prefers.
