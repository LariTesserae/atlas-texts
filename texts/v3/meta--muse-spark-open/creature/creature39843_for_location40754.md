What lives here doesn't move the way living things are supposed to move.

At first it looks like no one lives here at all. That's the trick the town plays. You stand on the grate over the hot channel and you listen for the dash or the scurry or the wingbeat and you hear nothing, so your brain files it under *empty*.

Then something unfurls six inches from your elbow and you flinch.

The fern does. 

There are dozens of them — *Nephrolepis* gone feral and giant, Boston-fern stretched by heat and dim amber light into something Jurassic. They live in the only real estate that matters: the crack where brick fails to meet steel, where condensate runs in a permanent trickle. Each one is a rosette the size of an open umbrella, fronds arching out over the catwalk. They don't sway. They *lean* toward the gutter, toward the sweat on the pipes. While you watch one, a fiddlehead at its heart, tight as a fist yesterday, loosens. You can hear it: a dry paper whisper as the crozier unwinds, pale fur falling away, new leaflets trembling out wet and lime-green and already beaded with water. They grow fast enough to hear because nothing eats them. No caterpillar has taken a crescent from the margin. No beetle has laced it. Every frond is perfect, unbitten, obscenely whole.

Under them, lower, shyer, is the moss.

It doesn't live *on* things so much as it *is* the surface of things now. On the north face of every brick, on the levee wall, on the fat asbestos-wrapped pipes where the temperature is kind, it has built a city of its own — *Bryum* and *Marchantia* and species that have no name outside this bowl, all stitched into a felt three inches thick. It is emerald so dark it's black in the dim, and it holds water like a sponge wrung almost-dry. Put your hand near it and it cools the air. Press it — if you dared — and it would give under your palm up to the knuckle and spring back, leaving your skin damp and faintly acidic. It fruits constantly in this hothouse. Tiny stalks rise a millimeter a day, capsules swelling red-brown at the tips, popping with an almost inaudible *pfft* to throw spores into the steam. The spores don't travel far. The air is too wet and too still. They settle on the next pipe, the next bolt, and start again. The moss is eating the town slowly, not by dissolving it but by covering it, keeping it wet against itself until the iron softens.

The water belongs to two things that are really one thing.

In the channels — those black-green concrete runnels that circle the Mill clockwise, bathwater-hot, clear as gin until it deepens — there is duckweed and watercress and, in the wider cistern sluice, water hyacinth. The duckweed lives as a perfect green scum, a million lens-shaped bodies that never get thinned by ducks. Each one buds a daughter from its side, then that daughter buds while still attached, so the mat thickens exponentially. It doubles while you breathe. At the edges it piles against itself and buckles into soft ridges. 

Beneath it, watercress roots hang in the warm current like pale nerve fibers, filtering nothing, feeding on the Mill's exhalations — dissolved nitrogen, iron, a faint sulfurous tang that would kill fish but makes the cress peppery and absurdly vigorous. Stems as thick as your finger thrust up through the duckweed, flower white, go to seed, collapse, and root again where they fall, never grazed.

And the hyacinth — that is the town's showpiece, its undisputed citizen. In the outer basin by the cistern tower, where the water is still and steams in a low sheet, they have formed a floating island. Bulbous petioles swollen with aerenchyma, leaves varnished green-black and cupped to hold rain that isn't rain. Their roots dangle a foot down into the hot water, black and feathery, breeding biofilm. The flowers are a violent lavender, trumpets opening in the damp heat with a sigh you can almost hear, only to be smothered days later by new leaves shoving up from below. They jostle. They creak. They make a sound like wet leather stretching. Nothing lands on them. A flower should be visited. These are not visited. They open and wither untouched, lavish and pointless.

Over it all, the vine.

One vine, really, though it wears a dozen shapes. It came in through the south sluice years after the workers left, or was always here waiting under the concrete for the right wet heat. Now it is the street-map of the town. Kudzu for its speed, fig for its strangle, bindweed for its patience. It has found the path of most water: up the sweating pipes, across the sawtooth window frames, through the teeth of the great idle gear by the Mill's side door that hasn't turned in a decade but is still oiled by condensation. Its leaves are of every size — heart-shaped small ones near the hot pipes where they scorch and curl, dinner-plate giants in the shade behind the washhouse where they overlap like shingles and dump their collected water in orchestrated pours. 

It grows while you look. Not metaphorically. You hear a tendril tap against tin, questing, then tighten with a soft *creak* as its cells swell on one side. It flowers sparingly, waxy white blooms that open at no particular hour and fill the hot air with a cloying vanilla-rot that has no pollinator to answer it. The seed pods — green bananas yesterday — have swollen overnight to forearm length and now pop with that soft artillery thump, spattering black seeds onto hot metal where they sit for exactly one day before splitting to show a white root-hair, already searching.

And underneath, invisibly, the things that actually run the town.

Because the plants wouldn't be this lush on water and rust alone. On every submerged surface — the undersides of grates, the insides of the hot channels, the film on the taro leaves — there is a skin. Biofilm, algal felt, bacterial velvet. Cyanobacteria the color of old bruises, thermophilic slime that thrives at 100 degrees, turning the Mill's waste heat and mineral sigh into sugar. It is the digestion of this place. It fixes the nitrogen the stack breathes out. It makes the water faintly iridescent when you stir it, an oil-slick that is not oil but life. It smells faintly of hot iron and wet earth after lightning. It too is uneaten. In any other marsh it would be grazed down to nothing by snails and larvae and protozoa. Here it thickens unchecked, a living varnish.

There are no mouths. That is the law. The Mill — the other living thing — made sure of it.

The Mill is not alive the way the fern is, but it lives. It has a metabolism: cold water in, hot vapor out, condensate down the pipes, back again, driven by that arrhythmic heart of piston and flywheel behind brick. THUD — pause — THUD THUD. It inhales through the levee door, exhales through the stack in white, wet breaths that never quite dissipate but become the ceiling. Its sweat waters the vines. Its heat incubates the hyacinth. Its tremor shakes pollen from the ferns. It doesn't need workers anymore. Its automatics — relays clicking in the switching house, paddles turning the channel clockwise — keep its cycle going. It is a low, broad animal of brick and steel that learned to farm moss.

It and the plants have an agreement. The plants keep the Mill from drowning in its own water, drinking the condensate, transpiring it, shading the brick so it doesn't crack. The Mill keeps the plants from ever wanting for warmth or wet or dissolved food. It is a closed loop, a terrarium the size of a town, production without consumption, growth without decay — or rather, decay without scavenger. When a taro leaf falls it doesn't get eaten. It sinks into the hot channel and is taken apart slowly, exquisitely slowly, by that bacterial varnish and by the heat itself, until it becomes peat-black water and feeds the cress again.

You are the only animal to stand here in a long time, and the town does not know what to do with you. The moss at your feet is already turning its rhizoids toward the new shade of your shoe. The vine's tendril, an inch away, has changed its helical drift very slightly in your direction, tasting the extra carbon dioxide you exhale. The hyacinth has not moved, but another daughter bud has appeared on its mat while you weren't looking.

If you stay, you will not be eaten. There is nothing here with a mouth to eat you.

You will simply be grown over.
