What lives here is not one thing. It took that shape slowly, while you stood at the central lock listening to the pumps.

At first you think: people. And there are people.

Not many. Not enough to fill the Row if you count windows against laundry lines. A woman in oil-black coveralls with the sleeves torn off at the shoulder — forearms mapped with old steam burns gone pale — who has been oiling the same gantry chain since you arrived and will be oiling it when you leave. She does not hurry. She knows the chain does not need her to hurry. An old man fishing in the north canal with a hand-line wrapped around a chipped brick, not for sport — he pulls up a catfish every twenty minutes, gill-hooked, fat as a forearm on warm water and spilled mash, guts it on the concrete lip and tosses the head back for the herons. Two kids in bare feet that are permanently grey with cinder, throwing cinders not to hit anything but to hear the *plot* they make hitting the weir's glass curtain.

They are not residents the way a city has residents. They are attendants. Keepers. They have the loose, quiet movements of people whose work is maintenance rather than production — check the pressure gauge, tap it even though it hasn't moved, sign a sheet where the last signature is still damp, sit. Their bodies have acclimated in small mammalian ways: sweat that never quite evaporates, skin faintly stippled with heat rash along the jaw and collarbone, hair that stays lank no matter how often it's washed in canal water. They speak rarely because the roar doesn't pause for speech and after a while you stop trying to compete with a room that is humming at the exact pitch of your throat.

They belong here the way a wick belongs to a lamp.

Then the birds correct you. You thought the herons were decoration — statues put on the weirs by a sentimental architect. They aren't. There are twelve, maybe sixteen, white and slate-grey, each holding a concrete corner as its own territory. They fish without impatience because the fish never stop coming. Beneath them the water boils without weather. Tilapia — pink-white, scarred, mouths working — mosquitofish in sheets that turn on a coin, armored catfish that scrape algae off the fresh pour and make a sound like a tongue on teeth. The fish are all fed by the same thing that feeds the boilers: heat and excess. Warm water holds less oxygen but grows more slime, and the outfall from the Pump House is always two degrees warmer than the rest, so the whole cross becomes a farm without a farmer. The herons know this. They are engineers now. They have learned the exact delay between gate-lift and the surge of stunned fry and they strike on that beat, precise as the pistons. Rats do the same along the pipes — brown, big-shouldered marsh rats gone slick and semi-aquatic, using the insulated mains as highways a foot above the flood.

Above them, under the eaves, the swallows. Hundreds. They have mortared their cup-nests into every gap in the brickwork, every joist hanger, every bulb cage. They live on the column of midges that lives on the permanent dusk. At no point in the day do the midges disperse — the light never tells them to — so at no point do the swallows stop stooping, so the air over the basin is always cut with sickle wings and that dry, ecstatic shrieking. Higher still, the dragonflies hold station over the center — Anax, Pantala — big as wrenches, territorial over ten feet of canal the way a dog is over a yard. Their wings are loudest of all when they go by your ear; you feel the pressure change.

Lower down, at your ankles, the true citizenry:

Frogs. *Ranidae* that should have been seasonal and became permanent. Bronze, throat-sac white, eyes like brass rivets. They coat every weir lip, every spillway edge, every patch of cinder where water stands without having fallen. Thousands. They do not call in chorus bursts. They call continuously, a metallic trill at 1.4 kHz that sits exactly under the pump whine so you cannot separate one from the other without deliberately listening for it. When one jumps, another fills the gap before the plop fades. They eat the mosquitoes before the mosquitoes can quite become a problem; the swallows eat what the frogs miss. Nothing wastes.

And over all of them, the cats. Six, seven, maybe more — you recount and get a different number each time because they sleep in shifts on warm places and look alike when curled. Lean, brindled, half-feral, ears notched by fights or machinery. They belong to the boiler house dynamos more than to any person. They drink condensation off pipe lagging, hunt rats when the rats get bold, and otherwise lie flat on humming steel, letting the vibration massage their bones. They blink slowly at you with industrial tolerance. You are also warm. You are also temporarily part of the heat budget.

But none of these, alone, are what lives here.

What lives here is the loop.

The basin breathes. Seep comes up through young concrete from the coastal aquifer — relentless, brackish, pressing at one atmosphere too low — and the sump lifts it. The boiler turns it to steam, throws half into the air as haze, condenses half on every cold iron surface, drips it, ticks it, returns it to the cinder puddles where it finds the cracks again and slips back down. The fish convert algae converted from coal smoke filtered through lime water. The frogs convert flies converted from fish offal. The herons convert frogs. The cats convert rats converted from crumbs. The people convert grease into motion into oiled chain. Heat never accumulates, water never leaves, sound never stops. The flywheel is a heart with a four-second diastole. The canals are atria. The haze is a lung.

If you stay long enough, the boundary blurs. You cannot tell where machine ends and marsh begins. The green-black algal slick is as much a gasket as the rubber ones on the lock gates; the dragonfly's wingbeat does the same cooling work as the fan in the pump house. The buildings are not housing this community. They *are* the exoskeleton this community secreted around itself on the day the concrete cured, and now the community is polishing the inside smooth just by living.

It has no predators. No season. No reason to grow. It achieved homeostasis within your lifetime and then edited time out of its contract. Leave for a year and come back and the same heron will be on the same bollard, the same rust flake will be half-fallen, the same girl — older — will be oiling the same chain, not because nothing happens here but because happening is distributed so evenly that change becomes invisible.

That is who lives here: one animal made of many animals plus brick and water and current, perfectly damp, perfectly warm, perfectly dim, holding itself — patiently, loudly, without ambition — exactly as it is.
