Nothing lives *in* it. That was the first adaptation.

There are no interiors here. No caves behind the doors, no halls behind the towers, no burrows that go deeper than a hand. The concept was tried and rejected. On this height, with the wind arriving first and the cold living in the stone, to go inside would be to die in the dark and flood. So everything that lives here lives *on* it, or *as* it. The town is not a shelter for inhabitants. The town *is* the inhabitant — a single, dispersed, patient animal-mineral-vegetable that learned, over an eon of being sanded down, how to be a hamlet.

You have to start with it, because otherwise the rest makes no sense.

### The Town Itself

It is a lithobiome. Not a coral — coral still believes in skeletons — but something older and more confused about the difference between growing and eroding.

The blunt knuckle-towers, the U-canal streets, the perfect too-regular terraces are not built. They are secreted and licked and breathed into shape by a consortium. The bedrock weeps a thin solution of dissolved silica and calcium when the cloud sits on it; the consortium drinks it and lays it back down as a slightly different rock. Century by century, a lip is extended here, a gutter deepened there. On approach every wall seems to breathe a little too much because it is: the surfaces are covered in a paper-thin biofilm, half lichen, half something that has no name yet, that expands minutely when wet and contracts when dry. That endless swell and shrink is what polished the hollows into thumbprints. That is what carved the doorways.

Those doorways — all two feet deep and no deeper — are its gills. Or mouths. Or ears. The distinction eroded too.

Each niche is lined with a soot-black liverwort, glossy as wet leather, with fronds like folded lungs. When the wind is idle the fronds hang slack and damp, and the niches look empty. When the wind hits the rim and organ-pipes through the streets, the fronds stiffen and bristle, standing out into the current. They harvest. They catch not insects — there are too few to matter — but droplets, spores, dust, ions, sound itself. They shiver. They make the howl into a chord by giving the wind a thousand soft throats to catch in. If you press your palm very close to one on a gusty afternoon you will feel it inhale: a faint, cool tug on the hairs of your hand, a sigh inward, and then, minutes later, a slow exhalation of slightly warmer, slightly spore-sweet air. This is why the niches seem to breathe when the wind is loudest. They are.

The north terraces are warm because the consortium is fevered there. A second species dominates — the red spiral moss — and it grows clockwise always because it is building tiny exothermic chimneys. Each tight rosette is a slow compost-heater, breaking down its own dead lower leaves and wind-blown grit, raising its center by three degrees. Enough that snow cannot settle. The terraces step down toward the center precisely because the consortium is creeping inward, following water as it abandons one gutter for another. You hear ice form at night with a *tink* and melt by noon with a rush because the town is moving water around in its sleep.

### The Upholstery

On top of that slow animal-town lives its upholstery, and the upholstery is not decoration. It is the working population.

The ankle-deep, sound-swallowing moss in the center is not one moss but a city of cushions, each a tight dome you want to press your palm into. Each dome is an individual — clonal, centuries old, male or female — that has learned to live as a bog without a bog. It stores the morning cloud in its hairs. A square foot of it can hold a gallon of beaded water and release it all day in silver threads to the gutters below. When you step near, the star-flowers close because the dome shudders. When you leave, they open again because the dome is listening for shade and has decided you are not cloud.

Among the mosses, the true gardeners move so slowly you have to watch for seasons, not moments. The cushion-lice: pale, translucent woodlouse-things the size of your thumbnail joint, blind, with feathery legs that never touch stone, only moss-hair. They spend their lives walking in circles over a single dome, grazing the dead tips, ferrying spores from dome to dome, pruning. Last year's growth as a paler ring is their haircut. They are the reason the town looks tended between plazas — it is.

Lower down, in the amber plaza-pools and the dark wells under overhangs, is the other lushness. No fish. No frogs. Water pockets here are too ephemeral, too cold, too likely to freeze solid at night. So the water learned to live fast. In each shallow cistern blooms a tea-colored soup of rotifers, tardigrade-cousins, and glassy copepods that hatch when the cloud arrives, feed on spores for six hours, lay eggs, and die when the sun lifts the cloud again. Their eggs can dry, freeze, blow away, wait years. The pools reflect stars at midday because they are not reflecting at all — they are remembering. On their undersides lives a film of magnetotactic bacteria that aligns with a field no compass here registers, and late at night that film ripples in the pattern of a different sky. The pool-things navigate by it. If you look straight down at noon and see slow-moving stars, you are seeing their map.

### The Transients

Fauna at 0.4 density means life is an event. The town notices when something moves besides water and wind, because sound has so much room.

There is, most days, the raven. There is only ever one. It may be the same one for years — a high-plateau morph, smaller, fluffier, its beak rimed white with silica dust from prying in niches. It does not nest here; wind would never permit it. It commutes up from the unguessed distance below, appearing over the rim like a ripped piece of cloud, to work the town like a street market. It knows which niches are breathing in and which are breathing out, and it snaps up cushion-lice with a surgeon's delicacy. It drinks from the gutter-threads by dragging its tail in them. When it cries, the town answers — the towers throw its voice back and forth until one bird sounds like five. It is loud enough that you have to raise your voice to be heard by yourself, and so the raven has learned to speak in that loudness, letting the town amplify it.

There is the moth-thing. You will see one if you are patient, clinging flat to a vertical face, the exact chartreuse-and-rust of lichen, wings like veined stone. It does not fly in wind. It waits days for a lull, then flutters — absurdly delicate — from one tower slit to another, to lay a single egg in a damp niche. Its caterpillar will not be a caterpillar but a slug of living felt that burrows *into* the moss dome for a year and eats nothing but condensation.

And very occasionally, there is the hermit. Because there is room here for a hamlet, structure for a city, food enough barely for one human if he knows how.

He — or she, the town does not distinguish — cannot live in the town as humans intend to live. There is no inside to retreat to, no fire that will not be taken by wind, no straight wall to lean a roof against. The hermit learns to live like the cushion-lice. A shallow niche, deepened a little with a piled windbreak of loose stone, lined with the driest moss domes for bedding. A route, memorized, from warm north terrace to central plaza to southern cistern that follows water as it finds a new route every few days. A diet of the red spiral moss tips — sour, peppery, faintly warming — and the amber pool-soup strained through cloth, and once a week an egg stolen from the moth-thing if he can find it, which he almost never should.

The hermit does not stay long. No one does. The cold, hovering just above freezing even at noon, gets into the hands first. The loudness gets into the mind. The light is too honest. But while he is there he becomes part of the density. The town makes room for him as it makes room for the raven: one more sparse, loud presence moving between towers, harvesting droplets. He learns not to speak, because the streets are flutes and his voice comes back wrong. He learns to watch the mist in the evening, when condensation thickens and the moss releases its spores in silent cathedral puffs, and the empty streets fill with suggestion — shapes moving between towers, crowding the empty place with the form the architecture suggests. They are not people. They are not spirits. They are the town trying on inhabitants the way you try on a coat in a shop, briefly inhabited by wind and spore and the memory of a grid that wanted to be a city.

He watches them until the wind turns and empties the streets again. Then he tucks deeper into his two-foot-deep doorway that never leads inside, listens to the creak of the ancient plateau settling a fraction — or the moss city respiring, or ice heaving — and understands, finally, the rule most clearly:

What looks like architecture is biology, and what looks like biology is architecture, and what lives here is the agreement between the two to keep waiting, damp and cold and strange, without needing a door that opens.
