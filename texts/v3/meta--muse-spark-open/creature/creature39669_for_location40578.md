They live high, and they live slow, and they live *in* — not on.

Not the Builders. The Builders are gone, or never left, or became something else — it doesn't matter. You won't see them. What you see are the ones who came after, who found a mountain made of floors and decided it was a habitat. Who filled it the way water fills a mold.

First, and most obviously, there are the people.

You expect them to be loud, crowded, shouting across the alleys between pipe bundles. They aren't. The air is thin and the light is too bright and the gravity is too polite to allow for loudness.

They are high-altitude people now, even if their grandparents came up from low basins. Barrel-chested, not tall. Skin that has learned the particular sunburn of reflected concrete — not browned, but freckled, dusted, slightly reddened at the cheek and the wrist, where the sleeves never quite cover. Eyes narrowed even in shade. They wear pale things — undyed coveralls cut down and re-sewn, aluminum blankets made into ponchos, concrete-dust-colored knit caps — because dark colors bake and bright ones blind. Everything has pockets. Everything has a clip.

They don't walk; they negotiate. Watch one cross an upper-tier gantyway on a windless noon: feet placed flat, center of gravity low, one hand always near a rail, not out of fear but habit. They have learned the second rule of the Construct without being told: sudden moves snag. Run here and the building notices you. A dropped tool might not fall straight. So they move like divers in shallow water, patient, economical. The old move slowest and are therefore the most respected, because they have had longest to learn the wrongness.

There are castes, but not the kind that are declared. The kind that accrete.

Higher up, where the air is thinnest and the light pools like spilled milk in corners, live the Lookouts and the Light-Tenders. They tend the sodium lamps that never turn off, replace the buzzing work-lights that moths die against, scrape algae out of the square-cut drains so the cloud-rain can find its scuppers. Their pupils are small, permanent. They speak rarely, and when they do it's in short, quiet phrases that don't echo. Many of them have a tremor from staring too long at the perspective inversions — from trying to judge distance where the farthest crane looks nearest. They mark their doorways with dark cloth so they can find them again when the light hums too loud.

Lower down, a level or three beneath the bright, live the Interstitials. The Fillers. The majority. They are the ones who took scaffolding that was meant to be temporary and made it permanent, then took the gap between two pipe trunks and walled it with salvaged housing panels, then took the gap between *those* and hung a garden. Their homes are not rooms but knots — a balcony extended with chain-link, then roofed with corrugated aluminum, then subdivided with hanging laundry that never dries completely. Families of six in a volume meant for one maintenance closet, and they have made it holy with smallness: a lithops in a chipped mug on a sill, a shrine of red plastic rebar caps lined up like prayer beads.

They work the presses. One stamp an hour, a housing panel identical to the last ten thousand, lowered by a freight elevator the size of a house that sighs instead of roars. The work is not hard, just endless and precise. They have become good at waiting. You will see them standing perfectly still for twenty minutes while a platform traverses a terrace, not bored, just attuned. Their children learn to play without running — games of careful stacking, of dripping water from one rusted elbow to another, of seeing how long they can balance a bolt on its hesitant fall before it decides to cling to the wall.

Even lower, where the milky light from above filters down through a hundred layers of mesh and catwalk, live the Void-Keepers. You don't see them often. They are pale in a different way — not sun-freckled but fungus-pale, damp-pale. They maintain the cisterns where mosquitoes breed, the cooling basins where condensation collects, the sags where algae has learned to live on powdered concrete. They know the smell of water: rust, wet concrete, cold iron. They carry long felt-planters on their backs like pilgrims, harvesting the wind-blown silt from expansion joints to make a spoonful of soil. Their knees are stained green from moss. They speak to the bees that have colonized the ventilation stack, and the bees answer with a low organ note that vibrates the whole shaft.

And then there are the ones who are not people, but live like people because the building taught them to.

The pigeons are citizens. Not pests — tenants. They have learned the negotiable gravity better than any human. They don't fly so much as *fall sideways* between towers, cutting silent arcs, roosting upside-down on the undersides of walkways in such numbers that the mesh sags. Their cooing in the ducts is the closest thing this place has to a municipal announcement system. The rats, too, have changed — extra joints, eyes like ball bearings, fur the color of curing concrete — they run the service voids as highways, not sewers. They know the looping corridors that bring you back one level above where you started. They use them. If you follow a rat, you will get lost correctly.

The cats watch from pipe chases. They are not feral, not tame. They are custodians. Each claims a vertical kilometer of ventilation chimney and patrols it with slow, deliberate paws, never startled by the bolt that hesitates mid-fall. They have learned to stare at the pooled light in corners without blinking, and sometimes the light stares back.

The moths the size of your hand never leave because there is no outside. They have become indoor things entirely, their white wings dusted with aluminum powder, thumping softly against work-lights until they die and are collected by children for their silken joints. The swifts scream without sound — at this density, you see their mouths open, but the thin air steals the noise.

And deeper still, where the mass of concrete swallows vibration and the dynamos turn somewhere beneath your teeth, live the Drifters.

You might not call them animals. You might not call them alive. They are the products of the wrongness, or its caretakers.

They are the gravity hesitations made visible: motes of concrete dust that never settle, hanging in bright air in perfect, unmoving clouds until a person walks through and they part like respectful crowds. They are the shadows that fall toward the sun, and the things that cast them when nothing is there to cast. They are the slow accumulation of pooled light in corners that hums when you put your hand in it and leaves your palm warm for hours afterward. The Void-Keepers leave saucers of stagnant cistern water out for them. The Lookouts will not scatter them.

If you stay still long enough — as the inhabitants do, as you must learn to do — you will see one detach itself from the underside of a catwalk, not falling, not floating, just deciding that down is elsewhere. It will drift along the pipe bundle thick as tree trunks, brushing laundry lines and prayer strings without moving them, and the pigeons will make way.

No one chases it. No one worships it, exactly. They just adjust. A doorway is hung a few centimeters off because otherwise that corner collects light and no one wants to sleep in humming light. A stair is left unfinished because the last time someone finished it, it looped. A child's game stops when the bolt sticks to the wall, and the child nods and places it carefully back on the wall where it wants to live now.

That is how they live high: not by conquering the mountain that was finished, but by agreeing with it. By becoming dense, and quiet, and bright-edged, and patient, and slightly undecided about which way is down — until you can't tell them apart from the building at all.
