Nothing *lives* here.

Not in the sense of inhabiting. Not in the sense of staying. This room was not designed to be habitable and the water is making sure it stays that way.

If you stand long enough for the thunder in your chest to stop being interesting, and you get your face close to things — close enough that the drizzle off the iron vault beads on your eyelashes — you'll see what little has learned to tolerate it.

First, what doesn't.

There are no residents. The conditions that read as 2.9-out-of-3 Made are lethal in exactly the quiet ways you would expect:

The air never dries. It is at or near 100% humidity, 48-50 degrees, and every surface that isn't actively sluicing water is filming over with condensate. Anything that needs to breathe air has to breathe *wet* air, under a constant arrhythmic drip that never lets spores settle, never lets silk dry, never lets an insect wing unfold without getting stuck to itself.

The light is wrong. 0.9 out of 3. There is one amber filament and a white slit ten inches wide full of shredded fog. No direct sun ever touches this chamber. Photosynthesis here is a rounding error.

The chemistry is wrong. This concrete is young. Less than ten winters young. It is still curing. It weeps calcium hydroxide, pH 11, 12 in the fresh beads — mineral-white, caustic — that blooms down every board-formed seam and leaves a sharp alkaline crust. You can smell it when the sump burps: wet cement, metallic. Touch the trickle and your fingers feel slippery, not from algae, but from lye. Nothing with soft tissue likes to root in that.

And the sound *is* a physical thing. 3.0 out of 3. The penstock behind the far wall is moving ten thousand gallons a minute three hundred feet straight down. Your handrail hums. Your jaw aches. The steel grating under your boots rattles with a low-frequency pressure that makes small arthropods lose their footing and abandon. Even bacteria form thinner biofilms when you vibrate them like this. Flies will not breed where they cannot hear their own wings.

So the room remains 0.4 out of 3 for animals for a reason. It empties itself.

What you will actually find, if you call it living:

### 1. The seam-moss.

In the one place where the system leaks just slightly wrong — the mortarless joint where the windward louver meets the raw concrete — there is a sparse, miserable fur of it. Not green. At this temperature, in this light, on concrete leachate, it can't afford to be green.

It is olive-black, dusty even while wet, the color of old army canvas left out in snow. It's stunted to maybe three millimeters tall, pressed flat by wind pressure through the slit. It only looks alive if you brush it with a gloved fingertip and see a faint, spongy give before it springs back. It has no soil. Its roots are holdfasts sunk into microscopic pores in the concrete, drinking fog-comb water directly as it sheets past. This is vegetation 0.7 out of 3: not a colony, a concession. A rumor that plants exist at this elevation.

Beside it, lower down where the drip is heaviest, two or three pinpricks of lichen — ash-pale, almost white, crustose — cling to the iron plate. Not growing on the iron so much as on the thin layer of lime scale the iron has acquired. They are maybe the size of pencil erasers after two or three years of trying. They will not get larger. The ice skin that forms on leeward bolts each night scrapes them with micro-expansion and the constant condensation never lets them properly photosynthesize, then properly dry.

That is the entire plant kingdom in this chamber.

### 2. The film.

You won't see it unless you know to look for absence of shine.

Where the concrete walls stay perpetually damp but not directly scoured, there is a translucent, slightly yellowish slick — bacterial biofilm. Mostly chemolithotrophs, alkali-tolerant slime working the high-pH water for trace minerals, maybe a few iron-oxidizers where rust bleeds from a tie-hole. It has no structure you can name. It just makes a handrail feel a little tackier, makes a concrete wall look a little duller. If you wiped it with a swab it would come away faintly brown-green and smell faintly of wet stone and nothing else.

It is not thriving. On young concrete the alkalinity is still murderous; the film establishes, gets washed off by a surge from the harps outside, tries again. It is life reduced to maintenance — holding on between sluices the way the valve stem holds pressure, hunting, hunting.

No mushrooms. No algae mats. Too cold, too dark, too caustic, too loud.

### 3. The visitors who do not stay.

The 0.4 fauna is not a population. It is a ledger of failures.

No nesting. No breeding. The roar keeps birds away entirely — you will never find droppings in here, never hear a swift cut through the louver. The spiders try. You'll see, in the corner above the amber cage light, the ghost of one: half a dozen anchor points of silk still beaded with permanent droplets, web broken, owner gone. That silk lasted a morning before the drip and vibration tore it. No fly ever hit it. There were no flies to hit it.

What you do find is what the fog harps comb out of the sky along with water: a wind-blown midge, dead on the grating. A single fungus gnat walking in tight, confused circles on the underside of the handrail before it gives up and dies in the sump. Springtails, perhaps, if you knelt by the louver seam for an hour — one or two, grain-of-sand white, grazing the moss and then vanishing back into the crack when the penstock hammers THUD.

They are not *of* here. They are *through* here. Blown in, washed down, killed quickly. The sump churns brown-white and if you stared into it long enough you might think it was muddy with life. It's not. It's rock flour and concrete fines, abiotic, ground to silt and suspended forever.

### 4. You. Briefly.

And the other kind of animal. Steel-toed, hard-hatted, earplugs turning the roar into a deeper bone hum. You check the valve stem rotation — quarter turn a minute — you tap the caged light, you note the ice on the bolts. You don't linger because the cold makes your hands stupid and the noise makes thought difficult and there is nowhere to sit that isn't wet.

No one lives here. The electric screw that turns that thigh-thick valve lives here, if anything does. The water itself lives here — ocean that became cloud that became mesh-caught drops that became channeled sluice that became roaring penstock — pausing in this little equalization bubble before it falls.

Leave the steel man-door ajar behind you when you go. The room goes back to what it was built to be: a small, sharp-edged lung for a mountain that pretends to be a mountain, breathing cold fog in and thunder out, with only a black moss mustache and a slime sheen to watch it.

It is perfectly normal that it is empty.
