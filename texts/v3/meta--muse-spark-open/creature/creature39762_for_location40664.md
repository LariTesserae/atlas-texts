What lives there is what could get in, and then could stand to stay.

Which, at first, was almost nothing. 

### The First Skin

When the cone was still bare and hissing, too hot to hold water for more than seconds, the first thing that lived there was not green. It was black-green, oil-slick green, a bacterial velvet that learned to drink the weep.

You can still see it where the walls are steepest and nothing else can root. Fifty meters of vertical basalt that never dries, pleated like drapery where the rock cooled too fast, and every pleat is furred. Not moss — under the moss. Under everything. A biofilm thick as felt, cyanobacteria and algae and lithotrophic bacteria in a living paint that holds the stone together. It smells faintly metallic when you brush it, which you will, coming down the ladder. It comes off on your hands as a dark smear that keeps growing after you wipe it away.

That was the anchor. Everything else needed that to soften the edge enough to hold.

### The Jungle That Folded In On Itself

Nothing that lives on the floor belongs at 4,000 meters. That's the point. It doesn't live at 4,000 meters. It lives in the bowl's lie about altitude.

So:

**The moss** is the ground itself. There is no soil, not really — just ash and tuff turned to sponge, and over it *Sphagnum* and *Breutelia* and species that have no business without winter, piled ankle-deep, knee-deep in hollows. Saturated always. It doesn't grow so much as bloat. Step and it sighs and takes your bootprint full of tea-colored water, then slowly erases it. Underneath, warm water braids invisibly. On top, it fruits constantly in a low, endless sporing — a haze of spores that never leaves the bowl, that rains back down in the drip.

Out of that, **the tree ferns**. *Cyathea* — or something that was *Cyathea* before it got trapped. They are the architecture. Trunks not really trunks but columns of old rootlets wrapped around each other, shaggy, dripping, completely upholstered in epiphytes. Their crowns meet at about six meters and make a second floor. Each frond is a door, dark waxy green, serrated exactly as sharply as the basalt behind it — rule 3 holds even here — and they overlap until the light has to fall as shafts. Nothing is lanky or alpine. The UV at this height should have bleached and dwarfed them. Instead, sheltered by steam-haze and by their own crowding, they went stunted in a different way: thicker, leatherier, darker, fronds curled slightly inward like cupped hands to catch drip.

Between their legs, **the broadleaves that hitched a ride**. You can read the accident history in them. Aroids with leaves as big as serving platters, glossy, pocked with condensation burns where mineral water sat too long in the sun. Stunted heliconias that will never be pollinated by the birds they were made for, so they propagate by rhizome alone now, clonal patches of fierce red and orange blooming day and night with no one to see except small amber flies. Ginger to your shoulder, its crushed stems smelling violently of lemon and pepper when the automated flood knocks it sideways every seven minutes. None of these should survive frost. There is no frost. The mountain breathes out and keeps the nights at 16, 17 degrees. So they persist, jammed stem-to-stem, fighting not for water — there is always water — but for a literal inch of light. Ten square meters holding more biomass than a hectare below does not look abundant. It looks desperate and exuberant at the same time, a crowd that has been told the room will never get bigger.

**The vines** are the only things that remember the outside. Thick as your wrist, pale and strangler-pale, they found the rim where wind-blown dust lodged and sent roots down the inside of the wall, down fifty meters through open air. They hang straight, plumb-bob straight, because there is no wind in the bowl. Midway down they sprout aerial roots that never bother to reach the ground; they just drink from the air. Some have reached the canopy of ferns and then turned sideways and rooted again in the fern-trunks themselves, making suspension bridges that quiver when the pump house exhales.

### The Things That Eat Industry

Then there is what learned to live *on* the works, not just among them.

On every ceramic insulator, on every lagged pipe ticking with heat, on the single high-tension line still humming its patient 60-cycle hymn across the bowl, there is a second, subtler jungle.

**Cable fungi.** Pale, phosphorescent at dusk, the color of dead rubber. It grows nowhere else. Not on rock, not on wood, only on the cracked neoprene and gutta-percha where the old cables sweat plasticizer in the heat. At night, if you turn off your headlamp, you can map the whole industrial nervous system by their faint green foxfire: the line from the turbine house on the north wall to the pump house in the center to the winch house half-collapsed under ginger. They glow because they are digesting the industry itself, breaking hydrocarbon long-chains. They are the bowl's most honest endemic — evolved, or mutated, in thirty years of selection to eat what fell in.

Under the sorting shed, where a bucket on a cable creep-creeps once a day and dumps warm silt, **the thermophiles live**. In the shallow, almost-pond in the center — that troubled sheet of water no larger than a courtyard — gas bubbles constantly through black mud. Lift a handful of fallen fronds there and the underside is orange, screaming orange, a bacterial mat of *Chloroflexus* and sulfur-reducers thriving on geothermal hydrogen sulfide. At the edges where pond becomes braid, where water goes over warm stones and actually hisses, the stones are slick with dark maroon slime that is too hot to touch but fully alive. It smells like rotten eggs and hot iron. Nothing larger lives in it. It would scald a frog. But it fixes nitrogen for everyone downstream when the pump spills it in a controlled flood over the moss beds.

That flood is the heartbeat everything else sets its clock by. Every seven minutes exactly, the central pump draws from deep under the floor — water that has sat against hot rock for who knows how long — and vomits it warm over the channels. The aroids lift slightly. The moss gulps. The insects time their molts to it.

### The Almost-No-Animals

The description said almost no animals. That's true, and what lives there proves it.

High over the rim, swifts cut the thin cold air and never drop in. Once in a while a juvenile loses its bearings on the warm column rising from the bowl at night and circles once, twice, inside the serrated circle of sky, bewildered by green and hum where its instincts say there should be scree and snow. It peels away. It cannot stay; there is nothing for it to hunt up there except echoes.

What stayed had to come in stupidly, accidentally, and then endure loudness.

**The clickers.** You hear them at dusk when the turbines' throb and the drip achieve a kind of equilibrium. A dry *tik... tik... tik-tik...* from under fern crowns, from inside rolled heliconia leaves. Small crickets, maybe katydids blown up as nymphs on a storm, now flightless because wings are useless in a 500-meter-wide room. They've gone darker, smaller, their chirp flattened by the constant industrial drone — they call at a frequency that cuts through 60-cycle hum. You will almost never see one. You will see chewed holes with perfectly sharp margins in ginger leaves.

**The frogs.** Perhaps two species, perhaps one species that arrived as two separate accidents: spawn stuck to a worker's boot thirty years ago, tadpoles in a sampling jar that got knocked over and never cleaned up. They are tiny, brown-black to be invisible against basalt mud, and they have no voice left worth using against the machines; males inflate a throat sac and make a thin, reedy buzz you feel more than hear. They breed not in seasons — there are no seasons in the bowl — but continuously in the warm basins between roots, in the footprints that fill with water and stay warm. Their tadpoles are black commas in tea water, grazing on cable-fungus spores. The adults live their whole lives under a single broadleaf, eating clickers that blunder by.

And **the slime things.** Trails everywhere in the morning after the mist burns off. Silver mucus ropes as wide as your thumb crossing warm concrete, up the side of a rust-red pump housing, vanishing into moss. Amoeboid? Flatworm? Probably introduced slugs and nemerteans that have lost pigment in the filtered light. They are nocturnal because even the soft sun through haze is too strong. By noon their trails have dried to faint salt-lines. They are the largest predators in the bowl, if you can call a creature that envelopes a cricket and digests it over twelve hours a predator.

There are no rodents. No birds nesting. Too recent, too enclosed, too loud. The food web is short, flat, circular: moss makes mass from steam-sun, ginger and fern steal light from moss, clickers eat ginger, frogs eat clickers, slime eats everything dead, bacteria eat slime and rust and make moss again. Nothing exports. Rule 1. Water, seed, heat, sound — recirculated.

### The Other Resident

But if you're honest on that rim at noon, looking down at the whole perfect green cup steaming and humming to itself, you know there is one more thing living there that the inventory misses.

The works.

Not alive, but not dead either. The low, riveted power house straddling its steam fissure, boilers long cold but turbines turning slowly, slowly, on the planet's own pressure. The pipe organ of geothermal piping up the north wall going *tick... ping... tick* all day and night as it expands and contracts. The pump house in the center that has never missed its seven-minute exhale. The bucket on its cable creeping an inch per second, dredging, dumping, building its little sharp hill that roots take instantly.

They are tended by nothing and therefore tend themselves. They grew a skin of mineral crust where weeping rock flash-boils; they grew a coat of fungi that keeps their insulation from cracking quite as fast; they grew a schedule that the frogs now breed to.

They don't grow. They persist — which, in a place where rock cools a degree per decade and a vine thickens around a conduit a millimeter per month, is the same thing.

In a hundred years the basin will be a meter higher with silt and root. The turbines will still turn, a little slower. The ferns will have swallowed the sorting shed entirely, but the sorting shed's roof will still be there under them, channeling drip in the way the ferns now rely on. The slime trails will cross it. The click will go on against the thrum. The sky will still be a serrated circle, far above, sharp as cut steel.

If you climb down the service ladder — already half-colonized, already velvet — the fronds close over your head and you are not visiting. For as long as you stay in that bathhouse-warm air smelling of wet iron and green, breathing the same humid loop that has gone round and round since the cone blew, you are briefly part of what lives there: another warm body that fell in and, for a little while, stayed.
