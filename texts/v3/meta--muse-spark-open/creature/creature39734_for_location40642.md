They live crowded.

Not scattered — not schools passing through an empty blue — but *stacked*. Pressed. Riveted shoulder-to-shoulder in a way that only happens when space is sacred and energy is infinite. This basin is not inhabited. It is clogged with inhabitation. It is occupied the way a lung is occupied by air.

### The Forgers

Start where you can't help but start — with the weight of them.

You thought at first they were the rock. That's their trick. On every terrace, on every basalt knife-edge that isn't actively spewing, the black stone is made thicker by another layer of black stone that breathes.

Each one is the size of a kiln, a bathtub, a small boiler laid on its side. Low, oblong, domed on top like a limpet that decided to become architecture. Their skin is not skin. It's precipitate — iron sulfide and pyrite grown from the inside out by colonies of bacteria housed in gills the size of curtains inside their mantle cavity. They smelt themselves. When young they are dull orange-black, porous; with age they grow glossy, heavy, gunmetal-bright, pitted with rivet-like valve scars. You could strike one and hear a bell tone.

They have no faces. Along the lower rim, facing downslope into the current, are six to eight intake siphons fringed with black bristles that filter hot particulate with a steady, muscular suck. The sound is like distant culverts filling. At the top, always pointed upslope, is the single exhaust jet. Every few seconds it punches — *chuff* — a spear of water so hot and clarified it goes shimmery and transparent for a meter before it dissolves. If you put dye in front of a bank of fifty of them, you'd see the whole basin's circulation drawn in neat, driven lines: cold seawater pulled down, stripped, heated, spat up to turn over again.

They do not move. They have solved movement by not needing it. Their larvae — little iron filings with cilia — settle directly onto the shell of an adult, or the gap between two adults, and cement there. Over decades the bank grows sideways, welds shut. Ledges that were once smooth basalt are now completely replaced by contiguous, thrumming Forger-reef, one continuous pump-organ ten meters long where you cannot tell where one body ends and the next begins. Place your hand — if you had a hand that could take the heat — on that surface and you would feel it: a slow peristalsis rolling through the mass, out of phase but relentless, as sulfur is fixed, as metal is laid down, as waste stone is sweated out between them to make new basalt out of living.

They are not intelligent. They are intentional in the way a furnace is intentional.

### The Shuttles

Between and over the Forgers, never still, are the tenders.

If the Forgers are boilers, these are the workmen who never left the floor.

About the size of a large dog, bone-white against all that black, blind, low-slung. Their backs are plated with loose, overlapping sulfur scales that clink. Their undersides are soft and flushed pink with blood rich in copper. They have too many legs — ten, twelve — jointed backward, made for gripping vertical glass.

But it's the arms that stop you. The front pair have swollen, calcified clubs the size of hammers, the striking face encrusted with pyrite that they renew by scraping it on chimney walls. The second pair are delicate manipulators, feathered with setae, almost surgical.

They spend their entire lives doing three things, over and over, with a deafening, mindless persistence:

1.  **Clearing.** A vent throat will seal itself in hours if left alone, choked by its own precipitation. A Shuttle will anchor its back legs on a Forger's shell, lean into the scalding jet, and hammer. *Clack. Clack. Clack.* A rhythm you feel in your molars. Each blow fractures the fresh anhydrite crust. Black smoke billows, and a cloud of shrimp erupts to feed.

2.  **Hauling.** Deeper in the white smokers, pure sulfur condenses into butter-yellow nodules. Shuttles pry these free and carry them — waddling under the awkward weight like laborers with ingots — to cairns they build in the slower currents. Neat piles, always in the lees of Forger banks. These are not nests. They are foundation. A larval Forger will choose to settle only on clean sulfur.

3.  **Culling.** They eat the dead. When a Forger finally exhausts its patch, its valves gape and go still, its iron shell quickly colonized by white bacterial felt. Shuttles descend and dismantle it from the inside, pulling out strings of decayed gill, carrying off chunks of shell to be re-dissolved elsewhere. Nothing is wasted. In a room with no outside, everything must be recycled.

They fight, too — clumsily, by shoving with their hammers — for the best hammering spots. White scars mark their carapaces. They have no eyes because eyes would cook. They navigate by vibration, by heat, by the taste of iron in the water.

### The Weather

And then everything else — the *air* itself, because water here behaves like air — is alive.

You cannot see more than ten meters in any direction not because the water is dark, but because it is full. Obscenely full.

There are the Veils: sheets of animal, translucent as vellum, a meter across, edged with thousands of coordinated cilia that beat like turbine blades. They hover over the main upwellings, pleated and rippling, each one a living baffle plate that slows and spreads the hot jet so the Forgers downstream don't scorch. Their bodies are so thin light passes through them, showing branching brown veins of symbiont bacteria. They never hunt. They farm flow.

Beneath and among the Veils are clouds — true clouds — of Glass Shrimp. Each one no bigger than your thumb, completely clear except for a single red headlight photophore under the jaw. Individually, nothing. Together, millions, they make the water glitter and gritty. They feed on the free-living sulfur bacteria that bloom wherever smoke and water mix, filtering with feathery mouthparts. And in turn they feed everything. Their swarms move as one body, parting around Forgers, pouring over Shuttles, aligning themselves on the invisible halocline — that sharp chemical boundary where cold clear seawater sits atop hot black metalliferous water without mixing. The shrimp swim *on* that boundary as if it were glass. Their legs dent it.

Below the halocline, in the heavier, hotter layer that pools in the bottom of the bowl, are things that do not like the light. Slow things. Detritus-worms as thick as rope, burgundy-black, burrowing through the precipitated ooze that is half mineral, half fecal stone, excreting more stone. Snails with shells made of actual scale-armor, iron plates layered like shingles. Anemone-analogues the size of barrels that have inverted themselves — their tentacles internal, forming filter-baskets — so they look like open urns, mouths gaping upward to catch falling snow.

Nothing chases anything fast. Speed is expensive when water is thick as soup and heat will warp protein. Predation here is grazing, is suffocation, is slowly growing over your neighbor so they starve of flow. A young Forger can be killed by a sponge growing across its intake. A Shuttle can be trapped by mineral precipitation if it rests too long in one spot.

The whole system hangs suspended. High above, the sun crosses the water-sky, moving shafts of gold across the roaring bowl for a few hours. The Forgers pulse. The Shuttles hammer — *clack-clack-clack* — without pause, without thought, a factory day that has lasted centuries and will last until the plateau cools.

They are not aware of each other as community. They are each a little engine exploiting a gradient, and together they have accidentally made a room so dense with engines that it has become a world.
