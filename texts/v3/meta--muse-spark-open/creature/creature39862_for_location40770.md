This place is not inhabited the way a valley is inhabited.

Nothing here *moved in*. Everything here *grew in*, and is still growing together. If you stay long enough to see past "a village surrounded by big bugs in a cold crater," you start to understand that you are not looking at a town with an ecosystem. You are looking at a single, distributed Organism that has learned to make a town-shaped part of itself.

It lets you count its pieces separately, if you need to. That is polite of it.

### The Carpet

Everything stands on it.

The moss is not groundcover. There is no ground. Underfoot, under the stilt-legs, under the shallow house-terraces cut into the basalt, there is a mat a foot, two feet, three feet thick — dense, sodden, living all the way through. Sphagnum and liverwort and something darker, black-green, without a proper name, knit together so tightly you can cut a cube of it with a knife and it will hold. It is warm to the bare sole. Warmer than it should be.

That is because it is breathing for the whole bowl.

The Carpet drinks the air. When the clouds come pouring over the rim at midmorning and fill the bowl to the brim like milk in a cup, the Carpet condenses them. Every leaf-hair, every pore exhales a little cold, and the cloud beads and breaks and falls as dew. *Tok. Tok.* All day. The Carpet stores that water in its sponge-body and feeds it back slowly to the fern-pillars and the fungal lanterns and the cisterns the people have cut. It never floods. It never dries.

Walk barefoot, as the people do, and you feel it responding. A soft give under your heel, then a faint tightening, as if your weight was noted. Press your palm to a hummock and hold it there for a minute and the moss will blush very slightly greener around your fingers. The children call this "the moss remembering you."

In the old stories the Carpet was first. Before fern, before moth, before human. The young, sharp bowl was bare basalt with a puddle of sky-water in it, and a crack on the eastern wall that steamed. And from that warm crack, the first spore came.

Every other inhabitant is ultimately anchored in it, fed by it, damped and quieted by it. It eats sound.

### The Pillars and the Lanterns

They look like plants. That is only half true.

**The Ferns** — each one alone in its interval, never crowding another, fronds wide as barn doors, stems glossy black. They are not trees but they do a tree's work turned sideways. They don't reach for the sun. The sun is brief and unreliable here. Watch them over a full day: all those huge, silver-furred fiddleheads track not the noon shaft, but the *center* of the bowl. Always a few degrees inward, as if listening to something low and central under the Carpet. Their spores fall straight down onto the moss and vanish without wind.

**The Fungus** — shelves, trumpets, sacs the size of a child's torso hanging from fern trunks and house eaves. They are the lungs and the lamps. By day they are dull, leathery. Stroke one with the flat of your hand, the way the women teach their daughters to do, and it bruises amber, then brighter, a warm, diffuse bioluminescence that lasts for hours. The liquid inside the great pitcher-plants does the same when swirled — a soft green foxfire. At night the town has no fires. The walkways glow with these coaxed lights, carried from porch to porch in cupped clay bowls. The light is primitive, alive, tired after a while. You have to feed it with attention.

If you slice a fungal stalk open — don't — you'll find it isn't wholly fungal. Thin, hair-like stolons run from its base straight down into the Carpet and pulse faintly. Some of the older men who tend the smokehouse say the Ferns and the Fungus are husband and wife. Some of the younger ones say they are fingers of the same hand. Both are correct.

The hanging gardens on the weeping walls — the rootless clubmoss curtains, the bead-succulents — are different. They are opportunists. Air-plants. Hitchhikers on a creature too big to notice them.

### The Stitched Air

The fauna is not *in* the bowl. It *is* the bowl's circulation system. Too numerous, too ordered, too quiet to be wild.

**The Blanket-Moths:** The first thing visitors miscount as cloth left to dry. There are always fifty to a hundred visible — underside of every eave, tucked against every fern frond like folded felt, pale gray with six perfect eyespots on each wing. Six, always six. Just before dusk, without any wind to cue them, all sixty or a hundred spots blink shut at once, then open, across different moths, different distances, in a slow wave that goes around the bowl clockwise. No one knows how. They never eat visibly. They drink once, at dawn, extending a proboscis as long as your arm down to the Carpet to take up mineral-rich water, then hang again. When the Rim-Watchers dive, this is what they take — soundlessly. The moths never struggle. Their bodies are full of spores.

**The Needles:** Forearm-long dragonfly analogues, iridescent to the point of hurting your eyes, wings fourfold and absolutely silent. Not dragonflies — they have no mouth. Only a jeweled head with eyes that meet all the way around. They are the hunters of gnats and midges and other things too small for you to notice until you see the Needle come to a full mid-air stop, hover, and needle a single speck from the air and swallow. They lay their eggs exclusively in the rain-barrels and pitcher-plants the humans set out. Without the humans keeping those waters clean and separate, they'd have nowhere to breed. It is an arrangement.

**The Bulldozers:** Fist-sized beetles, black and bottle-green, utterly earless. They move through the moss-hummocks carving highways with their horns, chewing rot and pushing it down into the Carpet where it can be composted. Put your ear to a walkway post and you can hear them as vibration, not sound: *crunch-crunch-pause*. The children keep them as slow pets, paint their shells with clay dots, and release them.

**The Crowns:** In every cistern, in every permanent trough, a knot of newts. Black skinned, belly orange as warning, with external gills flared like a feathered crown. Individually ugly-beautiful. Together unsettling, because there are so many. The water *is* newts. They breathe the water clean, they eat the mosquito wrigglers, they stir the water so it doesn't skin over completely — though if you leave a basin perfectly still overnight, it will form a transparent film that holds, at its center, a perfect faint outline of the bowl's rim, as if the water remembers the shape of its container. The people scoop that skin off and feed it to the pitchers.

**The Fat-Drinkers:** They are why no house touches another. At dusk the townspeople set out shallow fired-clay saucers on platform edges and rope rails, poured with rendered fat and bone-marrow oil from the smokehouse. From between houses, from under walkways, from the vertical basalt gardens, they come on paths they have kept since before the houses were here: lemur-things, but wrong. Bone-pale, translucent skin under sparse white fur, bellies distended, joints bending both ways. Hands like a human baby's, but with an extra thumb. Silent even when their lips touch the dish, long pink tongues lapping. Big nocturnal eyes reflect fungal amber, but they never look at you. They move hand-over-hand along the ropes with absent, procedural grace, drink, and leave exactly one lick's worth behind. Never more. Never less.

It took the first settlers a season to learn not to build in the intervals because the intervals are roads. Cover a road and nothing comes to drink, and if nothing comes to drink, the midges swarm and the newts starve and the moths sicken.

**The Rim-Watchers:** Small, dun, finch-like birds with feathers so soft they kill all air noise. Hundreds of them line the basalt rim at last light, shoulder to shoulder, staring down. They are the only thing in the bowl that looks outward-in. They sing nowhere else. The people call them jurors. Twice a day — dusk and false dawn when the cloud thins — one bird will fold and drop like a stone, never flapping, take one moth from where it hangs, and carry it to the rim where all the others tear it apart without a chirp. Just one per drop. The moth doesn't scream. Afterward they resume watching.

They are the only predators that kill quickly. The town forgives them because the birds keep the moth number exact.

### The Hands

And then, the people.

About a hundred and fifty. Maybe less in winter. Stocky, short-limbed, adapted already to cool dim: pale skin with a perpetual flush, large dark eyes, hair that grows coarse and holds water-beads like the moss does. Children born here have that faint moss-green at the lunula of every nail — not dirt, but actual incorporation. Chlorophyll? Lichen partner? No one tests it. Mothers just say "the bowl likes you."

They are not farmers. They are tenders, custodians, midwives.

A day is: Check the cisterns, skim the memory-skin if needed. Move pitcher-plants a quarter turn so they catch drip better. Stroke the fungal lanterns awake. Sweep beetle detritus back onto the Carpet where it belongs. Top up the fat dishes. Mend rope. Repack stilt bases where the Carpet has grown too greedily and is trying to swallow them — and it always is; you can see the stilt legs slowly sinking year by year unless re-lashed higher. In late autumn they help the ferns unfurl by carefully peeling back dead sheath with copper knives, a task done with held breath.

They speak low. Loud voices hurt the moths' antennae, they say. More truly, loud voices are simply rude in a place where every surface listens.

No leaders, exactly, but a circle of grandmothers who know which interval belongs to which family of Drinkers and which moth cluster tends to blink out of sync — meaning a sickness in the Carpet underneath that needs aeration.

They don't worship. They do maintain a courtesy: every birth, a pinch of afterbirth is pressed into the moss. Every death — and deaths are rare — the body is laid for one night on a moss hummock before cremation in the baffled smokehouse, and by morning the green has crept up to kiss ankles and wrists. The standing stones in the meeting circle aren't monuments. They're pressure gauges. When the eastern wall warms or ticks, the stones sweat or shift a hair, and the houses get moved.

They understand themselves to be the newest organ. The part that can tie knots and fire clay and remember.

What do they call the whole thing when pressed? Outsiders have tried to get a name. The closest in their dialect translates poorly as "The Cup That Drinks The Sky" or "The Room Still Making Itself." They laugh if you call the bowl alive, the way you'd laugh if someone called your body "alive" as if it were one thing instead of many.

But if you ask a child where the center of town is, she won't point to the smokehouse or the meeting circle. She'll point straight down, through the moss, through her platform, through the flat, sheared-off floor that shouldn't be flat at all. Down, to where the fern-roots and the fungus-stolons and the beetle tunnels all converge, down to where the basalt is warm.

On quiet nights when the fungal lights dim, when the birds at last sleep, when even the drips pause, you can put your ear directly to the Carpet and hear it:

Not a heartbeat.

A *growing* sound. Slow. Inexorable. Tiny cells dividing, water moving under pressure, heat ticking eastward.

The bowl inhales cloud. The Carpet sighs. The moths blink together. The people turn in their sleep, fingernails faintly green.

One room, complete.
