Whatever lives here does not live *on* it.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for nests, for burrows, for footprints pressed into peat. You look for the animal that stands apart from its ground. This place does not do apart.

It started small. Of course it did.

Lift the edge of any moss mat — and it will sigh and try to flood your hand — and you will see the first nation. Bacteria so thick the water has color. Not floating in it. *Being* it.

In the vats they are stacked like workers on a shift floor. The rust-red ones live at 78, 80 degrees, where your finger would blister. They lay down silica lace at the pool lips, bead by bead, repairing the rim every time the bellows-breath shudders and flexes it. They don't know they are building. They just excrete. In the next vat down, the viridian ones, cooler, thicker, string sunlight into sugar and then abandon the sugar as fiber. Pale threads extrude from their colonies in the gut between vats, half a meter long, strong as sinew when they dry. You thought it was mineral.

It is crop.

Because right above them, the second nation has learned to farm.

They are not bees, though at first you take them for smoke. They are stingless meliponines, black as pitch, gold-dusted on the thorax. But they don't live in hives here. The wind up here would take a hive. They live in lines. A line is twenty, thirty workers who have claimed a single fiber-run — one gut between two vats — and they tend it the way lowland leaf-cutters tend fungus. They cut, they bundle, they carry. Watch one gut for ten minutes and you will see traffic: going up empty-handed, coming down with a pale wobbling sheaf twice the length of the bee itself. They take it to the undersides of gunnera leaves where it is pulped, mixed with wax from their own abdomens, and plastered into galleries that run *along* the plant's plumbing, following the grooved stems that whistle when water runs. The plant does not mind. The galleries keep the stems from scalding in noon sun. Everyone gets paid in water pressure.

If you stand still long enough in knee-deep warm tea, you will feel the third nation move against your calf and decide you are a stone.

They are crabs. But that word is too lowland for them. *Tepuicaris*. Rust-red, flat as a pressed leaf, legs fringed with hair that harvests detritus. They never go to the sea. They have no sea. Their whole ocean is the braided marsh, ankle-deep and continent-wide, that flows nowhere and everywhere. The females carry six, seven huge eggs, yolk-yellow, under a flexed tail. When they hatch, the young do not swim away. They climb onto the mother's back and ride there for weeks, picking parasites from her carapace. The mother, in turn, gardens.

She does it without intention. She drags herself through algae mats in the cooling vats, and the algae sticks, and she scrapes it off in a new place, and it takes. Over generations the crabs have made the color-gradient in the cisterns sharper, more stable, than chemistry alone would allow. They are the vat-stirrers, the quality control. When a crab dies — and they die often, everything turns over fast in the heat — it does not float. It sinks a centimeter into the peat and in a day there is a white bloom of fungus around it and in two days nothing.

Above them, where the air finally empties for a foot or two between the peat and the dwarf trees, are the tank-keepers.

Each giant bromeliad is a country with closed borders. A liter, two liters of trapped rain and cloud-drip, absolutely still, absolutely defended. Inside one you will find: mosquito larvae that never become mosquitoes, filter-feeding; a tadpole the size of your thumbnail that will never leave until it has arms and has eaten its brothers; a beetle larva like a white pebble that breathes through its tail; and presiding over them, sometimes submerged to the eyes, sometimes clinging to the saw-toothed rim, the frog.

Not the gold poison frogs — they are decoration, advertisement. The real resident is larger, duller: a brown-gold tree frog the size of a plum, skin matte and warty, eyes like polished stone. *Stegopodus tepuiensis*, if anyone has named it, which no one has. She is not stunted. She is distended with economy. Her thighs are swollen with poison that she does not make, but borrows from the mites she eats, which borrow it from the fungus, which borrows it from the peat. Her call, at night, would deafen you. Now, in the day-heat, she sleeps with her feet in her own private pool, absorbing water through her belly. She lays three eggs at a time on the inner leaf above the waterline. If the tank dries — some do, when a leaf clicks and vents steam — she will carry water in the hollow of her back to keep them wet. If you touch her tank-plant, she will not jump. She will sink deeper and stare at you until you understand you are an intruder in a temple.

They are all over. Thousands of bromeliads, thousands of frog-temples. A city of ponds at knee height.

And then, moving through all of them, threading the trunks, flipping the bromeliads with a hooked beak, is the one you will actually see and remember, because it looks back.

The tepui caracara. It does not fly if it can walk. Wings short, legs absurdly long and yellow, face naked and orange as a warning flag. It moves like a secretary bird that forgot to grow. It strides the moss mats and its weight makes water sheet away in circles with each step. It stops, cocks its head, listens — not for you, for the tick.

For the bromeliad tanks click when their trapped air expands. The caracara knows that a particular click means a beetle larva has grown too fat and is chewing the leaf wall. It will prise the tank open with its beak, drink the whole community in one draught — frog, tadpole, larvae, water — and wipe its beak on the moss. Then it moves on. It is not cruel. It is maintenance. One bird can patrol a square kilometer in a day and keep the pressure valves from clogging.

In the hour before the clouds finally lift, when the steam begins to make its rings, you will sometimes see two of them standing on a rise, perfectly still, facing the breathing pools. White sky behind them. They do not hunt then. They wait, as if listening to the whoompf.

There is one more inhabitant and it has no body.

Or rather, its body is the whole valley. Not a spirit. Not a metaphor for ecology. A physical thing you can almost feel when the distillation is at full idle — the purr, the tick, the hiss, the whistle through the gunnera stems, the sigh of peat when you step, the bellows of the pools, all falling into a rhythm that is not quite synchronized and not quite random.

It is a standing wave of water and heat, established. The plateau learned, over a million years of no one watching, how to keep itself wet when it should be dry, how to keep itself hot when it should be cold, how to lift Amazon cloud and wring it and run it through vats and leaves and insects and frogs and birds and pour the surplus over the cliffs. It does not need a hand. It is a hand — many, many small hands — working in such tight feedback that the whole thing behaves like one warm, breathing, dripping intention.

You came looking for the owner of the factory.

There is no owner. There never was. There is only the factory, and it lives by making more of itself, and it has learned to use anything that stands still long enough — peat, crab, frog, bird, you — as a temporary part.

Stand still another minute. The cloud will catch on your shoulders. The mat under your boots will sigh and start to flood again. A stingless bee will test your sweat for salt and decide you are not fiber.

You are being incorporated, gently, for as long as you stay.
