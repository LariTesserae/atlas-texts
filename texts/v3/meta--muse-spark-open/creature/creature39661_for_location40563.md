What lives here shouldn't live here. That's the first thing they have in common.

### The People Who Have Become Lungs

They are human, still — two thousand of them, more or less — but they have stopped being human the way people on the outside are human. 

You can see it before they speak.

Their skin is the giveaway. Not burned, not tanned. Cured. The children are born pink and soft like anyone's children, but by five their cheeks and forearms have taken on a smooth, faintly waxy sheen, like the underside of a glass-cabbage leaf. The dust polishes them. The oil vapor they breathe all day seals them. Old people's skin is beautiful here: thick, amber, translucent at the temples where you can see the slow pulse underneath. They rarely sweat. Sweating would be suicide in a place that gives you back so little fluid.

They have learned to live dry.

Their lips are always a little chapped and stained pale rose from chewing salt-pith to make saliva. Their noses are broad, hairy inside with fine salt-catch. Their lungs — if you could open them up — would look strange, sponged with extra mucus to catch precipitate. When an elder coughs, which they do in the dry cold before dawn, they spit a little pellet of pink salt and do not think anything of it.

They do not drink. Not the way you are thinking. Water, pure H2O, is a rumor, a theoretical substance like absolute zero. What they drink is what the cold-traps weep.

Every house has one: the *mother pot*. A black-glazed belly half-buried in the salt floor of the most shaded courtyard, covered at dusk with a taut skin of leaf-leather. All night the glass-cabbages exhale. The rust-weed sighs. The air in the bowl cools by a few crucial degrees and sinks. The sour, mineral breath condenses on the inside of the pot — not into drops, but into beads, like mercury that hesitates. In the morning, the woman of the house scrapes it with a soft wooden spatula into a jar. It is cloudy, brackish, stinging, half oil and half ammonia-water and half something else that has no name outside. It will blister your tongue if you take it straight.

So they season it. A pinch of ash from last night's oil lamp to cut the acid. A flake of dried rust-weed filament for iron. A whisper of fermented cabbage-oil for calories. The children drink it from shallow saucers, lapping. Adults can get by on two saucers a day if they stay still. Three if they work. No one is ever quenched. Thirst is simply a condition they live inside, like noise — a low wire of need they have tuned out.

And because of that, their bodies are different.

They are tall and hollowed. Long torsos, long necks. You need surface area here — to radiate heat at night on the rooftops, to catch precipitating salts on your skin that you can later scrape off and bake into cakes. They move slowly by day, quickly at dusk and dawn. Their eyes have developed a second, milky lid, not quite a nictitating membrane, but a thickening of the conjunctiva you can see when they sleep. It keeps the dust from cementing their eyes shut. Their hair is thin, brittle, full of static. Many shave their heads so the charge doesn't lift it straight up during pre-dawn storm-fronts.

They are not adapted. Not yet. The elders still tell of babies born who could not learn to breathe the oil-thick air and went still within an hour, their little chests glittering inside with salt bloom. That still happens, one in twenty. They bury them shallow in the silt with a glass-cabbage leaf over the face, and by morning the leaf has already begun to etch the small body into food.

What *has* adapted perfectly is their ears, and their hands.

### The Listeners

You cannot live in the Bowl and be quiet. 

A stranger thinks the noise is something that happens *to* the town. The people who live there understand it backwards: they happen inside the noise. The sound existed before them — the wind over the mouth, the creak of leaves tracking sun, the high whine of salt crystallizing on rust-weed at 18:03 every evening — and they have learned to become an organ within it.

Every person here is a tuner.

Listen to a mother call her daughter not by name but by rhythm: two quick knocks on her courtyard's copper tongue-string — *tak-tak* — and the daughter's chime-tree, three houses down, answers with a sympathetic shiver even though no hand touched it. Listen to the old men argue in the market by striking their awnings harder and harder until the bronze roar swallows words entirely and the argument becomes purely percussion, purely feeling, and both men nod, satisfied.

A house without voice is a corpse. When a young couple claims an empty alcove in the cliff — and there are many empty alcoves, the city is built for ghosts — the first thing they do is not make a bed. They make the voice. They stretch leaf-leather over a frame, drill seed pods, hang them on gut string at different lengths, test them against the noon column until the wind finds the right note. A family's chord is as distinct as a face. You can walk blindfolded down the long, salt-swept avenues at midnight and know exactly who you are passing by the overtone overhead.

The deaf are revered and feared. There are three of them, all very old, ear-drums blown out by a childhood fever when they breathed too much black-vine vapor. They are the Silence-Keepers. Once a year, on the Day of Cloth, every chime in the Bowl is stuffed with rags, every shutter tied, every rattle buried in salt. The standing wave collapses. For twenty-four hours the pocket is what it should be: an empty, blinding, airless bowl. In that unnatural hush you can hear the cliffs themselves tick as they cool. You can hear your own heart and hate it. The three deaf ones walk the streets then, the only ones comfortable, checking walls for new cracks with their palms.

Children born here learn to shout without being angry, to sleep with their mouths open against the pressure, to read meaning in vibrato. A lullaby here is not a song — it is a mother slowly, carefully dampening her chime-tree string by string until the room's frequency lowers enough for sleep.

They think they are maintaining the sound. In truth, the sound is maintaining them. If the roar ever stopped for good, the cold trap would fail, the air would stagnate, the precipitate would not fall. They know this without knowing they know it.

### What Else Lives Here (Because People Are Never Alone)

The town thinks it is the only animal in the Bowl. It is wrong. It is just the loudest and most anxious one.

**The glass-cabbages live.**

Not like plants live on the outside. These do not grow. They *inflate*. Each rosette begins as a tight, hard knot the size of a fist, black and salty. Over weeks it drinks air — literally drinks it — catalyzing hydrocarbons and ammonia on its amber veins into that pale volatile oil. The outer leaves swell, become translucent, creak. Inside, pressure builds until the blister at the base is taut as a drum. At solar noon, when heat peaks, the oil shivers, expands, and the plant tics a few degrees toward the light. You can hear a field of them ticking like a million slow clocks.

They are not individuals. Under the pink salt pan, they are one mat — a single root-felt the size of the town itself, lifting and resettling the floor in ribs. When you harvest a cabbage — saw through its base and render it for lamp oil — the mat shudders. Neighboring cabbages close slightly, as if holding breath. If you take too many from one allotment, the rust-weed nearby wilts for a day, though no one can explain why.

At night, they close. Not all at once. A wave of sighing moves from the cliff-base toward the center as the outer leaves fold over the oil blister to keep it cool, and for an hour the whole Bowl smells faintly of citrus and hot iron.

**The rust-weed lives.**

It wants to be higher than anything else. Two, three meters of jointed, rust-orange stalk, completely covered in those filament-hairs. Each hair is a factory: at dusk it extrudes a glue, at night the glue catches dissolved salt out of the falling air, at dawn the salt has grown into a perfect cube at the tip, singing faintly as it crystallizes — that needle-whine you hear. By ten a.m. the cubes flake off. The plant does not care. It is not harvesting. It is breathing out. The salt is waste.

The people who harvest it are in symbiosis with waste. They walk the rows with wide, shallow baskets, brushing the stalks. The sound changes when a stalk has been brushed — a lower note until new crystals grow.

When a rust-weed dies — and they die standing up — its stalk does not rot. It cures where it stands, hardens to that mahogany-iron the carpenters prize. The filaments slough off as dry red snow. Inside, the pith has become loose, fibrous salt that can be pounded into flour. It tastes like blood and bread.

**The black cords live.**

The vines that hang from the cliffs are not vines. Come close. They have no roots. They are hollow tubes of a material more like dried kelp than plant, anchored by a suction cup the size of a dinner plate to the bone-white rock. They tap. All day and all night they tap — *tok... tok... tok..* — against the cliff with no wind to move them.

Put your hand on one. It is warm. It vibrates. The elders say they are listening to the stone, that they drink vibration itself. The young, more practical, cut them when they grow too long and weave them into rope that never rots. If you snap one, as the description warns, it sighs warm, scented vapor over your face and for an hour afterward your dreams are vivid and unpleasant and full of vertical light.

No one knows what they are. No one has ever seen both ends of one at once.

**The gliders and the click-beetles live.**

The pale mantas, the ones with too many frilled edges, are not visitors. They live in the honeycomb alcoves near the rim where no human can climb — nests made of pressed papery leaf-sheaths. They are filterers, too, but of a different kind: they strain the standing column itself, riding the hot air that churns over the mouth. You see one or two a week, spiraling up on a thermal, doing that slow, flapless circle. What you don't see is the dozens at night, when the column reverses and cold air pours in like water, that unfold their frills and *fall* silently into the cabbage fields to lay eggs that look exactly like salt crystals. The farmers collect them by accident and bake them into cakes. The cakes are richer.

The click-beetles the size of fists are custodians. Their luminous abdomens are not for mating displays — there is nothing to display to. The light is cold chemistry, a byproduct of neutralizing the acidic mulch where fallen stalks rot and smoke faintly. They drag themselves through that slick, fragrant rot, eating bacteria that would otherwise eat too fast and heat the floor until it cracked. The trail they leave is actually cooler than the surrounding salt for a few minutes. Children follow them on hot nights to stand in the cool footprints.

And under all of that — too small to see, too numerous to count — lives the true city: a slick of bacteria in every damp crack of aging leaf, in every fermenting jar of oil, in the sour film inside every mother pot. They are the ones who made this place possible. They take air and make oil, take oil and make salt, take rot and make perfume. A non-aqueous chemistry, slow and smoking. Without them the Bowl would be what it looks like from above: a dry scar.

### How It All Fits

What lives here is not a town in a garden. It is a single organism that has convinced itself it is separate parts.

The cliffs breathe — hot air out by day, cold air in by night. The plants strain the breath for food and exhale more breath. The bacteria set the table. The animals clean up. The people — loud, clever, half-deaf, forever thirsty — are the newest trick the Bowl learned to keep its resonator in tune. They build loose roofs not for shelter but for timbre. They harvest salt not because they love salt but because if they didn't, the floor would seal over. They tell their children not to go over the rim because the one time someone did, forty years ago, she came back three days later with her copper hammer rusted to lace and her lungs full of nothing and died learning that there is no other bowl, that the high desert truly is void and eats iron and does not forgive.

So they stay. They widen their avenues so air can move. They tune their houses. They drink their sour condensate and eat their salt-bread fried in pale oil that shivers. At night they lie on rooftops under that brutal vertical disc of stars and feel the stone give back its heat and listen as, one by one, a thousand glass-cabbages sigh shut, and the rust-weed stops singing, and the black cords keep tapping, and the whole town — plant and person and beetle-light — subsides into a ringing lullaby that says the same thing every night:

*We are still here. We are still here. We are still here.*
