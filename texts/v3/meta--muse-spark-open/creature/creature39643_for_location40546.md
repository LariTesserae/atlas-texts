It lives low.

That is the first rule here. Whatever lives on the high table lives low, or learns to love being thrown.

From a distance, you would say nothing lives here at all. One fern-tree where a forest should be. One shaggy back moving at the edge of a reed-bed. But get down - get your knees into the moss, your face down to where the air goes wet - and the plateau stops being empty. It is crowded, just crowded horizontally.

### The Carpet That Is The Country

The true inhabitant of the peneplain isn't a species. It's a tissue. A nation of green.

**The Moss-Mother** covers everything that isn't polished rock or scoured laterite channel, and she is not one moss but fifty mosses pretending to be one. Up close you see the layers: silver-green star-moss on top, tight and waxy to shed noon sun; beneath it a springy peat of dark emerald that holds water like a sponge wrung never quite dry; beneath that a black, almost soil-like felt where generations have composted into themselves, threaded with white fungal nerves.

She is ankle-deep on the domes and chest-deep in the basins. Push your arm in and it's cold. Not cool - *cold*. She makes her own climate. The water she wrings from the morning cloud stays trapped against the warm granite for hours, even while the wind tears dry overhead. Walk through her and every step releases a little cold cloud around your boots that smells of iron and celery. Lie on her and she will soak you slowly through the back of your shirt without ever raining.

You think she's just groundcover until you watch her before a storm. About twenty minutes before the daily cell builds, the light goes flat and bruised - and the moss begins to glow. Not bright. Not magic. Foxfire, foxlight, a faint bioluminescent green along the tips of her fruiting bodies, as if the mycelium underneath is arcing with static charge. The old people must have learned to read it like a barometer. When the glow comes, get out of the pans. Get under your stone lip. Rain is coming.

Inside her live the first citizens:

**Mist shrimp.** Not shrimp. Pale, blind amphipods the size of rice grains that live their entire lives in the interstitial water between moss stems, grazing algae, breeding wildly in the four hours a day the carpet is fully saturated, then encysting when it dries. Billions of them. If you squeeze a handful of moss into your mouth - and thirst will make you - you taste pepper and protein.

**Thimble frogs.** Frogs that never knew a pond. They are the size of your thumbnail, rust-ochre with granite speckles, and they lay eggs directly into the water axils of the lower moss. No tadpole stage - perfect froglets hatch out already walking. They sing only once, at 3 a.m., and only after the big afternoon storm has refilled everything: a single *peen* so pure and lost that you hear one a mile off and can't tell its direction in the wind. Most nights you think they're extinct. On wet nights the carpet peens softly everywhere, as if the stars were dripping sound.

And then the architects on top of her:

**Bathtub Bromeliads.** *Brocchinia tabula*, if you want Latin. Each rosette is a bathtub, three to five feet across, leaves like enameled leather overlapping into a watertight cistern that holds three to eight gallons of rain, fog-drip, dead insects and leaf tea. They are everywhere the soil is too thin even for moss - perched on granite knuckles like offerings. A miniature world in each one:

In there live mosquito larvae with red gills, dragonfly nymphs the color of rust that eat them, tiny crabs no bigger than a coin that prune the bromeliad's roots from inside, and a species of salamander that never touches ground - glossy black-gold, with sucker-toes, that spends its whole life in one bathtub, eating what falls, giving birth to live young into the same brown water it was born in. Tap a bromeliad and the water shivers. Look close and whole ecologies shiver back.

The bromeliads flower once every seven or ten years - a shocking scarlet spear twelve feet high, straight up into the killing wind, which rips it apart in a day. That's enough. One day is enough for the bees to find it.

**Fern-Trees.** Not trees. Tree-ferns that decided height was suicide. Thick, squat trunks covered in hairy black fiber, eight feet tall maximum - exactly the height the wind starts to punish seriously. Their crowns are huge, horizontal hands four feet across, waxy to catch condensing fog. Under each crown is a permanent shadow-garden of lichens, liverworts, and orchids growing on orchids, white ghost-flowers that smell like vanilla at dawn for an hour and then close. The trunk itself is often hollow, eaten out by beetle larvae, and used as dens by something warm.

### The Things That Move Against The Wind

Fauna is rare if you count by sightings. If you count by patience, it is everywhere, but it has all learned the same lesson as the plants: stay in the boundary layer. Stay where the moss makes still air, or be built to be thrown and come back.

**The Fist Bee — *Bombus niger altus*.** You hear her more than see her. An inch and a half long, matte black with wings that shine oil-blue, legs feathered with crimson pollen. She doesn't buzz. She thrums, low, drunk, like a finger on a bass string. There might be twenty of them on the whole visible plateau, but you see one every day because each one patrols miles, moving between bromeliad spears and pitcher blooms. She flies *through* wind that would tumble a hawk - 60 mph gusts - by flying low, two inches above the moss, riding its texture like ground-effect. She stores honey not in hives but in abandoned salamander bathtubs, sealing them with wax. The thin people harvest it sometimes, if they find it before the ants do.

**Glider-Hawks.** The only thing that loves the wind instead of hiding from it. From below they look like hawks drawn too long - six-foot wingspan, but tail feathers twice the body, like black ribbons. Up close - if one ever lands, which it almost never does - you see they're not raptors at all. Toothless beaks, huge forward eyes, skin wings stretched over elongated fingers, feathered only on the trailing edge to damp turbulence.

They ride. All day they ride the compression lift off the plateau rim without a single wingbeat, circles thirty miles wide. They eat the air: huge airborne insects blown up from the jungles below the cliffs, swarms that get caught on the table and have nowhere to go, plus the occasional fist bee if they're desperate. At night they finally call - that one falling note, *sooooo-aw*, incredibly sad and carried absurdly far by the flat acoustics - and roost by clinging like bats to the lee sides of granite knuckles, folding those ribbon tails around themselves.

**Pan-Capybaras.** You saw them drinking at dusk and thought they were silent stones. They are. A herd of eight to fifteen, each animal the size of a boar, low-slung, legs comically short, heads huge and blunt. Their hair isn't hair but dense, reed-like bristles the color of amber water and dried mud, so when they freeze in knee-deep reeds you literally cannot distinguish them from a clump of vegetation until someone blinks. They are semi-aquatic nomads. They spend the day wallowing in the deepest pan pools, submerged up to the nostrils, conserving moisture, chewing fermented moss. At night they graze, moving in a line, eating everything - grass, bromeliad skin, orchid bulbs - and dragging that valuable gut-water with them. Their dung is a perfect nursery: wet, fibrous, full of undigested bromeliad seeds that sprout in days.

They almost never vocalize. When threatened - by lightning, by the sudden boom of a laterite bank collapsing - they don't run. Running means wind exposure. They flatten. They become stones again. The storm passes over them.

There are others you almost see:

**Thread Snakes** in the laterite grooves - glossy brown eels no thicker than a pencil, breathing through their skin, migrating from pool to pool like water itself through temporary cracks. If their groove dries, they knot together under damp moss in a writhing ball and wait.

**Reed Tigers.** You never see the adult. You see where it was: a tunnel through the 12-foot reed beds, stems bent outward in a perfect circle, at the end a pressed-down platform above floodline and the faint ammonia of cat. Paw prints - huge, splayed, with moss tamped down between toes - five inches across. It hunts capybaras by drowning them, silently pulling them into deep pans where wind can't help them. The people leave it alone. They tie red grass to mark its reed tunnels and go the other way.

**Sink Maws.** Not predators in the usual sense. Freshwater bivalves, giant mussels two feet across, that live in the sinkholes where rivers vanish into the cracked granite. They open their black-green shells in surging flow and filter, and when the water drops they seal shut and aestivate for weeks on stored water, looking like slick rocks. The carvings on the quartz veins - spirals and wavy lines - often circle these holes. You understand why you'd pray to a drain.

### The Rememberers

And then there are the people, who are not separate from this. If the plateau is a memory of mountains that forgot how to be steep, they are a memory of villages that forgot how to be many.

They are few - oh, astonishingly few. Maybe one small band per fifty square miles, thirty souls at most, all related to everyone else within a week's walk. Low, broad, copper-brown, hair cut short against lice and wind, bodies wrapped in woven reed-fiber and capybara-hide that smells of smoke and tallow. Children learn two things early: read the glow, and keep your silhouette down.

They do not build to last. They cannot. Anything tall becomes a tuning fork for that awful, beautiful organ chord when wind hits basalt pillars and sings back to itself. So their homes are borrowed shapes: a granite slab propped on two others, roof thatch weighted with stones against 60 mph gusts. A ringwall only three courses high - not to keep enemies out, there are none - but to break wind for a fire pit for a week. Then untie it. Move when your pan silts, when the reed tiger claims it, when the sink opens new ground downstream. 

Their wealth is remembered water. Old women know, without map, where a pan that held water nine days after the last storm in the year their mother was born will probably hold again, because of how the moss cups there and the granite swales. Old men know which basalt whistle-note means a shear line is coming from the west and you should huddle under your slab, not in the reeds. They grind chert roots on the same stone hollows their grandmothers wore five generations deep. They tie red grass where a child took first steps; they place a smooth stone on a ledge above a sink so the river remembers to come back.

They see the strangeness but don't need to explain it. Yes, the perfect dawn pools reflect a sky that isn't there - a pale second sun, a scatter of moons too small and too many. They don't stare; they drink. A wrong reflection still shows you where a cloud will form. Yes, the wind answers itself from another dome. They answer back, sometimes, humming through pierced chert pipes so their chord blends with the mountain's and tells the glider-hawks they're still down here, listening.

If you stay a night, you won't see them at first. You'll think you're alone, lying on warm granite while the Milky Way brightens to shadow-cast.

Then you'll realize the low stone ten yards away isn't stone. It's a shelter that's been there longer than you can fathom, and someone under fern-frond bedding has been watching you since noon to see if you know how to keep low, how to tie your grass and move on before the sky you didn't notice building right above your head - out of heat and moss-breath - opens itself and tries, very loudly and with no malice at all, to wash you clean off the high, patient, frantic table of the world.
