They are not marsh people anymore, even if the marsh made them.

If you stayed long enough to see them move — and you would have to stay quiet on the stair for a long time before they forgot you and moved naturally again — you would think first that what lives here is not people at all but a kind of careful insect. Hands first. Always hands. Because you hear the hands before you see the faces in the trench-alleys.

The hands are what the bowl has made. Broad, short-fingered, nails clipped to the quick and rimmed black with peat and shale dust. The skin never quite dries. Even the old have a soft, pruned look to their knuckles, a gloss like the black wood of the posts. A child's hands at six already have the little white scars from shale flakes and the tar cord, and they already know not to wipe them on their clothes because the clothes will never dry if they do.

They are a small people, not by breeding but by accommodation. You cannot be tall in a place where second stories hang a foot over your head and third stories hang a hand's breadth more. So they learn to tuck. Shoulders in, head a little forward to watch the plank for the next bridge. The men who quarry are not thick like you'd expect; they are wiry, roped with forearms, because you split stone in this bowl by tapping, patiently, along the bedding line until it sighs and shears, not by swinging. The women who tend the channels have calves like river stones from standing on planks, knees bent, all day. Everyone's back knows the exact height of the eaves.

Their faces take a while to show themselves. For the first days you think they are all the same face: pale, damp, lidded. It is the light. In the long shadow hours the light is all reflected off wet shale and wet thatch, a cool, sourceless glow that erases cheekbones. But at that noon hour, when the white falls straight down and the water goes transparent, you can see them properly and they are not the same at all.

The old women who read the sweat have faces that have gone the way of the moss. Not green, but stippled. Tiny dark moles and pits from years of condensation beading and drying on skin, from shale dust settling into pores. They sit highest, where houses lean closest to the weeping wall, because they like the drip to be predictable over their cisterns. They do not govern — no one governs in a place you can walk across in ten minutes — but they keep the post. The notched post by the stair, where the water level is read. They are the ones who say *empty your cistern* or *bring the laundry in* or *south wall runs clear, two days*. The young men joke that it's just rock water, but they still empty their cisterns when the old women tell them to.

The young men and some of the young women work the wall. Twenty feet of raw, young stone. It is still falling in plates. Every week a new slab calves off and has to be split and either laid as a flat stone wall or as a bridge or as roof-weight. This is the only place that still has room to grow — outward, a foot at a time, into the bowl's own collapse. So their dispute is always about that foot. Whose house gets to lean a little further out when the fall is cleared. Who owned the air where the reeds shot up after the winter fall. They argue quietly, intensely, with their hands a few inches from each other's faces, because a shout would bounce off the bowl and wake the babies two alleys over.

The children are the only ones who live in three dimensions. Adults use the plank walkways at ankle level, turning sideways through the trenches. Children use the roofs. From the bowl's lip you might see them at noon, a flicker of movement across the thatch ridges, bare feet black with moss, running the high road while their parents call up in low voices that carry perfectly well — *slow, slow, the reed is loose there*. They are loudest at noon. The rest of the day they are taught to be water-quiet. They learn early that the bowl is an ear. A dog taught this too, after being hushed enough times, will whine instead of bark.

And it is not only people.

What lives here, really, is a negotiation between people and water and things that love water too much.

The moss lives here more completely than any of them. Felt-thick on the north wall where sun never hits, but also domesticated — patted onto thatch ridges to hold the reed down, encouraged on the shady side of every post where it acts as a gasket. The liverworts live here because they are allowed to, in a bright green line following every seep along the shale, marking where not to drive a nail. Watercress and marsh greens live in rows so disciplined they look wild by accident, each plant staked with a twig to keep it from washing away in the night-rise. A single alder seed that sprouts on a roof will be pulled by three hands before midday, because a tree has no business in a place that gets three hours of straight light.

The animals are tenants on strict terms. Rats in the rafters — tolerated because they stay out of the water channels, killed when they don't. Swallows that live in the high wall cracks and are never disturbed, because they eat the midges that hatch in the cisterns. Ducks, but not as you think of ducks: a few dozen, kept in stacked wicker pen-towers over the water, tiered so droppings fall through to feed the eels below. The eels themselves live in the channels, half-wild, half-kept, slick and cold as the seep. Every house has its eel-hole, a clay pot sunk under the plank bridge, and a boy or girl whose job is to know its temperament. Fish come in only as guests, trapped at the inflow sluice where the bowl drinks from the marsh, stunned by the sudden cool, scooped up before they can learn the channels.

Even the tools live here in a particular way. Iron cannot be left against the wall because the sweat will rust it by morning. So iron lives oiled, hung under the eaves in the drip-shadow, or set on wooden pegs away from stone. Wood lives black and glossy, too wet to rot, too cool to burn well. Stone lives clean, because the weeping washes it every night. You can tell how long a family has been here by how black their posts are.

What else lives here is the habit of listening. In a town where a conversation two streets over can be heard as a murmur, people don't finish sentences loudly. They leave spaces for water to fill. They knock on posts with knuckles instead of calling through walls. They sweep water off planks with birch brooms in a rhythm that matches the channel ticking, so it doesn't sound like an interruption. Quiet is not politeness here. It is architecture, like the low roofs weighed with stones against the rim-wind. If they were loud, the bowl would make them unbearable to each other.

And underneath all of it — not a people, but what the people have agreed to live *with* — the weep itself lives.

You could say no one lives in the walls, but you'd be wrong in the way that matters to them. At night, in the cold hours when the fog forms in place and hangs in heavy curtains and rolls down the shale, the bowl is exhaling. That's what the old women say, and you can see why they'd say it. The walls bead even in a dry spell. The cisterns rise an inch overnight with no rain. The thatch drips in unison. It feels like breathing. The young men say pore-water, fresh rock still settling. But they still read it the same way: clear and fast on the south wall means rain in two days. Slow means dry.

What lives here, then, is a people who have made a compact with a wound. They did not drain the marsh or dam the seep or try to roof the bowl. They braided their houses into the channels and their alleys into the drip-lines and their days into the hour of white light. They filled a fresh, edged, still-bleeding hole in the flat earth to the brim with a saturated, zero-clearance, water-ticking honeycomb, and kept it from rotting by keeping the water moving. Give it ten more years and the plates will have weathered round. The walls will have learned to be old. The sweat may slow. But now — now what lives here is damp, and quiet, and black-handed, and complete.
