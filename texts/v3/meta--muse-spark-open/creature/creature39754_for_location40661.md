Nothing lives here the way a valley lives.

If you came looking for an inhabitant — something with a face, a nest, a track in the dust — you will leave disappointed. This pocket does not support that kind of life. It *refuses* it. The fauna score here is as close to zero as a built place gets, and for good reason.

First, listen.

The sound never stops. A 2.9/3 wall of low thrum and high whine from the six black-iron hearts in the center of the room, running in perfect frozen equilibrium. There is no night to it, no pause, no season. No animal stays where the air itself vibrates in standing waves, where sleep would mean vibrating apart. No rat could breed here. No fly could land long enough to lay eggs. Even if you brought something warm and breathing inside and left it, the dry, -38C cold — a non-aqueous, machine cold with no water to buffer it — would mummify it before it starved. The preservation is the point. Sharp stays sharp. Dead stays legible.

And no human lives here either, not anymore. You can see where humans *were* — that is the sparse density, the 1.4/3 scattering of presence: one parka hung stiff on a hook, one tool cart with locked wheels, one clipboard where every box is forever checked, one row of analog needles blurred at 70% by vibration. This is a room built for many tenders, now tended by none. Or tended by engines. People pass through like you did, through the half-iced service door that wants to close, equalize pressure with their mouths open on the high thin air, and then leave before their ears start to bleed. No one dwells on a pedestal that thinks it's a mountain.

So what *does* live here?

The only things that can live in a totally made, totally sealed, totally loud, totally cold equilibrium.

In the seams — and only in the seams — there is a sparse, stubborn, almost embarrassing little flora.

There is a black-green felt of frost-lichen, coin-sized rosettes spaced a meter apart with a loneliness that looks intentional. They don't grow. Persistence is not growth. They sit where the ferrocrete meets the corrugated basalt-composite, where decades of human breaths left a ghost of mineral salts, and they hold that exact spot under the flat, normal industrial daylight from the caged tri-phosphor tubes. That daylight is the loophole — 2.5/3, ordinary enough to photosynthesize by — and because the dynamic never changes, they never have to decide to grow or die. They are frozen in place, year after year.

With them, under the light cages and around the maintenance grates, are the air-ferns. You know them: pale, etiolated, engineered CO2-scrubbers seeded into every habitat that was supposed to be occupied long-term. Here they were never needed, but no one told them to stop. They are etiolated not from darkness but from stasis — bleached and thin, holding a pose, not thriving, not dying. The cold keeps them. The dryness keeps them. The engines keep them.

And beneath even that, invisible unless you scraped a seam and put it under glass, is the real population: the mineral bacteria and salt-tolerant endoliths living *in* that hard, dirty glaze of leeward ice that was never really ice, and in the waxy factory grease on the bright bolt-heads that never flowed. A biofilm so thin it is more stain than organism. This is a dry chemistry world outside — no rivers, no lakes, water baked or blasted off the young basalt — and inside, any spilled water would sublimate before it puddled. So life gave up on water and learned to live on trace condensation, on electrostatic dust, on metal.

That is the ecology of the Hidden Scale: not a community, but a scattering. Not a forest, but a few patient dots.

If you want to name a true inhabitant, name the engines themselves — the six squat silos tipped on their sides, humming their one unchanging chord. They are not alive, but they are the only things in the room that *behave* alive: they metabolize power, they regulate temperature, they maintain the air, they preserve the sharpness of every edge. They are reason the room exists; the mountain outside was girdled and clad and turned into a plinth just to hold them. The lichen and the ferns are just hitchhikers on their equilibrium.

Stand there long enough and you understand the rule this place teaches: when everything is built, when nothing erodes, when sound and cold lock into a time-invariant roar, life does not colonize. It clings. It finds the one unsealed seam and stays there, sparse, still, perfectly spaced, waiting out an eternity of normal light in a room that will never change.
