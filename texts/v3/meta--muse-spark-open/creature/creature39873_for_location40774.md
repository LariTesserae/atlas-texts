They live here because nowhere else would have them.

Not the people first. The people are late. Start with what was already in balance.

### The Mat

It is not a plant and you should stop calling it that in your head. In the sheets that are too thin to swim in and too hot to touch for more than a second, the rock blushes.

If you crouch — your boots hissing faintly where the rubber meets the film — you see it is not a color *on* the water, but *in* it, under it, a living skin a few millimeters thick. Bacteria and archaea and fungi that agreed, a billion years ago, to eat light and heat and dissolved stone all at once. They don't need soil. They precipitate it.

Viridian where the flow is fast and thin and oxygen-rich. Copper-green where it slows and thickens. Down where the water pools and holds its heat, it bruises to a venous purple, then that obscene, chemical pink that looks artificial until you realize you are looking at photosynthesis that has never seen shade.

It grows a millimeter a day. That is its speed. A fur, a velvet, that advances over white travertine with a visible front if you mark it and come back after lunch. It smells like a struck match and a bleeding battery. Nothing in you wants to eat it - the pH would strip your throat, the silica would turn to glass in your gut - but everything else here does. The whole food web is built on that stink.

You can see where it has been grazed. Clean white etchings, surgical, as if someone took a razor to the velvet.

### The Cleaners

They make the etchings.

From standing height you think the pools are empty. Then your eyes adjust to the overexposure and you realize the surface is *crowded*.

Puddle-skimmers cover every sheet-flow and every warm shallows, so many that at first you take them for glare. White on white. Each one the size of your palm if you count the legs, the body itself no bigger than a grain of rice. Legs so long and hydrophobic they don't stand *in* the water but *on* its skin, bending the meniscus into dimples without ever puncturing it. They move like compass needles, all jitter and sudden perfect stillness.

They are mouths on stilts. They lower a black proboscis thinner than a hair and shave the bacterial mat, one cell-layer at a time. They do not compete. They do not fight. If two meet, they lift their legs impossibly high and step over each other without a ripple. A square meter can hold fifty of them and you will not hear a single one.

Watch them long enough and you see the discipline of the place: nothing hurry-scarfs. Even hunger here is quiet and precise.

### The Glasswings

Then the air above the water gives up its second population.

You thought it was steam-shimmer. It isn't.

Glasswings. The workers call them that, or sometimes just *needles*, but glasswing is better because you can see through them. Each about as long as your middle finger, with two pairs of wings that are not really wings the way a dragonfly's are - they are rigid, veined panes of chitin so clear they disappear when they bank, leaving only a faint rainbow at the edge. They don't buzz. They don't whine. They ride.

The heat coming off the terraces makes columns of rising air so steady you could set a watch by them. The glasswings lock into those columns and hang, absolutely motionless relative to the ground, beating their wings maybe once every ten seconds just to correct. Their abdomens are swollen clear tubes, and through them you can see what they've been drinking - mineral water that glows faintly pink-purple from the bacteria they've filtered. They are flying stomachs.

They hatch in the cooler rims where the mat frays, larvae like white commas that burrow in the soft carbonate for months, then all at once - always around 11 a.m. when the sun is most vertical - a whole cloud will lift. Not a swarm. A consensus. Hundreds, thousands, turning as one mind without a leader, drifting over a pool to dip and sip and never touching each other.

They are the air's plankton. Which is why the sky is not empty.

### The Soarers

You feel them before you look up. A flicker in the overexposed light, a cut of shade that is too sharp.

They are huge and they want you to think they are not. From the catwalk they look like kite-paper, like ash. Two to three meters wingtip to wingtip, but so thin, so translucent, that the white sun shines right through the membrane and you can see the tracery of black veins like rivers on a map. No head to speak of beyond a blunt, eyeless shovel. No legs. Just a vast, silent manta of skin stretched over elongated finger-bones, undulating once, twice, and then locking.

They are the only thing here that understands wind.

They live in the thermals that the factories and the hottest springs exhale - invisible towers of hot air that go up a kilometer and don't waver. The soarers stack themselves in those towers, a hundred at noon, then two hundred, all circling without a single call. Their shadows race over the white flats below, crisp-edged as cut paper, and the glasswings don't even flinch until the very last instant.

When a soarer feeds, it doesn't dive. *Dive* implies violence, a splash. It tips. One wing dips a degree, and gravity does the rest. It descends in a long, utterly silent glide, opens a ventral maw that is just a slit the width of its body, and *sheets* through a glasswing cloud. You don't see individuals taken. You see a brief absence in the cloud, a tunnel, and then the soarer is climbing again on the same thermal it left, never having flapped.

They excrete in flight, too - a fine mist of concentrated silica and salts that rains down and becomes tomorrow's terrace. Even their shit builds the ground.

The city men track them the way sailors track weather. If the soarers leave a factory plume, the vent is cooling. If they all tighten into one column, a pressure change is coming over the rim.

### The Rollers

And then, in the pools that are deep enough to have a real edge, the water itself moves wrong.

You were told not to go near them. Children here learn early - *white rock is safe, orange rock is hot, pink-grey moving rock will drown you without meaning to.*

They look at first like boulders that have rolled into the basins. Smooth, pink-grey, limbless, the size of a domestic pig, or a big dog curled up. They crowd the edges, a dozen in a pool that should only hold three, half-submerged, utterly still. No eyes you can find. No mouth you want to find. Just a dorsal seam that opens and closes, slow as a sleeping breath, venting steam that smells faintly of meat.

They are not amphibians. Not mammals. Call them rollers because when they do move - rarely, when a mat bloom is at its thickest or when another roller pushes - they do it by inflating one side of their body with hot water and deflating the other, rolling over with a soft *whuff* that is the loudest sound you have heard since you arrived.

They graze differently than the skimmers. They submerge their whole ventral face and suction. A square meter of pink velvet disappears in a minute, leaving white stone that will be pink again by tomorrow. They drink the soup itself, filtering silica through plates in their gut and excreting it as smooth, opalescent pellets that line the pool bottoms like pearls. The factory crews harvest those too.

Don't touch them. Not because they bite - they don't have a bite, not really - but because their skin runs at 60, 70 Celsius, and because if one startles it will roll, and a human leg caught between two rollers in a hot alkaline pool does not come out clean.

They lie there in their dozens, breathing steam, growing with the rock. You think they are stupid and you are right in the way a stone is stupid: perfectly adapted to doing one thing forever.

### The Tend-ers

And then - only then - the people.

They are not many. For a city that spreads kilometers over the bowl, you see remarkably few of them at any one time. Two in pale grey coveralls and knee-high white rubber boots on a catwalk a half-kilometer away, pushing a squeegee that is not for water but for cooling salt. One older woman in a shed with every wall open to steam, watching three automatic arms cut lithium glass, her hands folded.

They are quiet because the plateau demands it. Sound dies here. The wet air eats it. The steam muffles it. A shouted word goes twenty feet and falls down. So they have learned to speak with their hands, with taps on pipes, with headlamps blinked twice for *pressure high*, three times for *move clear*. Their voices when they use them are low and close, almost into the ear, as if the terraces might overhear.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with sun - though the sun is brutal at nine thousand feet, and they all wear broad hats and scarves that cover the nose against silica dust. They are pale because they never expect to be dry. Everything about them sheened: sweat, condensation, the perpetual film underfoot. Their coveralls are stained permanently at the cuffs with iron orange and sulfur yellow. Their boots are never quite white again after the first week.

They do not own this place. No one owns accretion.

Their job is tending, in the oldest sense. They walk the black steel causeways that have been half-entombed in new white stone - you can see the older levels fossilized below, rebar ghosts inside travertine - and they clear a vent, they lift a rack of salt cakes, they reroute a sheet-flow that has decided to build a dam where a doorway needs to be. The factories behind them hum at a frequency you feel in your molars more than hear, turning heat into current and current into motion without ever roaring.

They move at mat-speed. At terrace-speed. At soarer-speed. No hurry.

In the evening, when the sun finally thins and the white bowl goes pink and then bone-colored, you will see them gather on the higher causeways that are still dry, not to socialize so much as to simply stand and be dry together. They watch the rollers steam, the glasswings come down to roost on the cooling rims like dew, the soarers settle - not landing, never landing, but lowering until they are almost touching the mineral chimneys, hanging in the last warm air like laundry.

They don't farm animals. They don't plant. They harvest what the water leaves behind and they try, politely, not to interrupt the interval between presences - that vast, glittering kilometer of white between one clump of habitation and the next where the fauna own the light.

If you stand with them long enough, your boots filling slowly with warm water you stopped noticing, you understand the unspoken agreement: the town was not built *on* the hot wet high place. The hot wet high place grew a town the way it grows chimneys and bacterial velvet and glasswings - because in a mineral world that builds itself endlessly upward, something needs to remember where the walkway used to be.
