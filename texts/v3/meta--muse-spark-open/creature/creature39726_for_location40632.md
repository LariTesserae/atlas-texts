What lives here never left. There was never anywhere to leave to.

This isn't a habitat. It's a womb you had to climb into from the outside.

### The Builders Are Gone But Their Hands Are Still Here

The first inhabitants were people. Not many - a family, a cult, maybe a work crew of six or seven with stone hammers and baskets of clay carried up three days along a spine of rock that was still hot enough to blister feet through sandals.

They didn't live here long. No one can. They came up here to make the room and then the room made them unnecessary.

But you can still feel them everywhere because they built the way animals build - not by assembly, but by secretion.

Every brick in that throat was thumb-pressed. If you peel back the moss - and it resists, it has grown into the thumbprint like a fingerprint of its own - you will see it: the whorl, the crease, the little off-center divot where a thumbnail dug too deep. Twenty feet across, fifty feet down, ten thousand bricks, ten thousand touches. No two the same. They glazed them in a low, smoky fire right there on the rim, while the new mountain was still cooling, and they laid them while they were still warm.

They left their tools, too. Wedged behind a curtain of hanging fern near the bottom, you will find a stone flake, a fire-blackened digging stick, a shallow clay bowl now used as a reservoir by a vine that has drunk it full of roots. The humans are dead down in the lowlands, or moved on, or never existed before the mountain did. It doesn't matter. Their work lives on without them. The wall remembers being touched and keeps weeping because of it.

What lives here now is what they invited in to do the maintenance.

### The Wall Itself

The wall is alive, but not like a creature is alive. Like a *culture* is alive.

The bricks are porous lungs. They inhale the hot, thick air from the growing mountain below - that deep tectonic hum you hear is the planet still pushing new basalt up like toothpaste - and they exhale water. Drop by drop by drop. The weeping never stops. In a dry place this would be a miracle. Here, in this packed, sealed throat, it is a tyranny.

On and between the bricks lives a caulk of engineered wilderness. It is not wild. It was placed. Every crack was seeded on purpose.

*   **The black moss** - it is not moss. It's denser, more like felted wool soaked in green. It expands when wet to seal the joints and contracts when dry to let a breath through. It hums faintly when you touch it, with static.
*   **The thumb-vines** - thin, red, wiry things that follow the lines of mortar exactly, as if respecting the bricklayers' grid. They flower only in the dark. The flowers are waxy, white, and open with a soft *pop* you can feel in your molars. They live for twenty minutes, are pollinated by condensation, and then melt into a sweet rot that feeds the moss.
*   **The shield-ferns** - forearm-sized, fleshy, overlapping like the scales on the duckweed mat below. Their job is to catch. Every leaf is cupped to catch a bead of wall-water and funnel it sideways into a root channel. Nothing is allowed to fall straight. Straight falling would erode. Everything must be slowed, sipped, transpired.
*   **The curtain-root** - the hanging moss that brushes your face on the way down. It's the air's tongue. It tastes the layers.

Because the air here is stratified, like a failed atmosphere. A membrane of dry heat sits on top of wet heat and they won't mix. You have to push through it. The curtain-root lives exactly on that boundary, half in one world, half in the other, drinking the difference. When you pop through, the fine hairs on your arm stand up. The plant shivers.

Nothing here grows toward the sun. The sun is wrong - too large, too dim, too diffuse, filtered through gauze and bent by the pocket's broken light. Light here pools in corners and drips *up* the bricks. So the plants grow toward wet, toward warmth, toward each other. A vine will abandon the wall to twine briefly around your calf for heat before remembering its duty and letting go.

It is frantically, obscenely fertile. You can watch it. Kneel by the water-mat and hold your breath and you will see a tendril unfurl a millimeter. A bud swell and split. A leaf yellow, blacken, be reabsorbed by the moss in real time. The whole cycle - bloom, fruit, rot, sprout - happens while your ears are still popping from the pressure change. It has to. In a room this crowded, this sealed, if anything paused, everything would choke.

The puddle at the bottom is not a puddle. It's a stomach. Warm water under a seamless mat of duckweed and lily pads, all overlapping, all competing. Lift a pad and the water underneath is brown and writhing with root-hairs, with decay, with tiny white blind worms that are really just mobile roots. It digests fallen flowers and dead leaves and returns them as mist. The mist rises sideways - because gravity here is indecisive - and condenses on the bricks to begin again.

This is the true inhabitant: a single, closed, hyperactive green machine, twenty feet wide, breathing in and out, weeping and drinking its own tears five times an hour.

And it needs a gardener.

### The Keeper

You hear him before you see him. Or it. The sound is not a chirp or a buzz. It's a *tick* - the sound of a tiny stone tool tapping brick.

*click... click... click.*

He lives in the thicket of the wall, about two thirds of the way down where the wet is perfect. He has built a nest exactly the way the humans built the cylinder, but in miniature: a cup of woven root-fiber and fitted shards of fired clay pried from a loose brick. It is tucked into a hollow where three bricks meet imperfectly - a flaw the humans left for him, perhaps.

He is the size of your cupped hands. He is pale the way things that have never seen true sun are pale - not white, but translucent, like warm wax or unfinished shrimp. You can see his guts, which are green with leaf matter, and a darker knot that is his heart beating too fast.

He is not a bird. Not an insect. He has too many joints - an extra elbow in each limb, a knee that bends both ways. His skin is damp and dusted with fine hairs that collect condensation. When he moves, he moves in starts and corrections, as if negotiating with several gravities at once. One foot sticks to the wall. Another foot repels slightly. He compensates without thinking.

His head is mostly eye. Two large, black, wet eyes that do not reflect you, but reflect the light that falls *up* - the remembered glow leaking from the bricks. When he looks at you, you see yourself in negative, lit from below by a sun that isn't there.

He has hands. Small, clever, four-fingered hands. And in one he holds his inheritance: a flake of obsidian no bigger than your fingernail, sharp as a thought. The humans left it, or he knapped it himself from the mountain's fresh glass.

His work is endless and precise.

He trims. The wall would seal itself shut if he didn't. He cuts back the moss when it threatens to smother a weeping channel, snips a curtain-root that has grown too greedy, clears duckweed from the outflow. He pats mud back into place with his palms. He is a barber, a plumber, a priest.

He does not eat the plants. He drinks the weep. He presses his mouth - a soft, round, toothless mouth - to a bead of water on a brick and sucks. You can see the water travel up his throat.

He does not fear you. Fear requires a concept of open space, of predators that approach from a distance. There is no distance here. There is only packed closeness. To him, you are weather. A large, hot, temporary condensation event that has fallen into the pocket. You are in his way. He will wait, ticking patiently with his little blade, until you move, and then he will trim the vine you were leaning on.

If you stay too long, he may try to tend you. He will approach your boot where the leather is sweating and pick at a loose thread with his obsidian, as if it were an errant root. His touch is warm and slightly sticky.

There may be more than one. In the dense mat, under overlapping leaves, you hear other ticks, out of sync. Perhaps a whole silent clergy of them, each responsible for a course of bricks, never meeting because the foliage is too thick to cross. Or perhaps it's just one, moving impossibly fast through hidden root-tunnels, appearing wherever maintenance is needed. In this pocket, where shadows detach and pebbles orbit each other before falling, both are true.

He was not put here. He grew here. Or the room grew him, the way a wet pocket grows a specific mold and no other. When the humans finished the cylinder and sealed the physics-break inside - the bent light, the bickering gravities, the air that won't mix - they left it wet and dim and fed, and *he* condensed out of that, the way water condenses on brick.

As long as the wall weeps, he will work. When the mountain finally finishes growing and cools, when the weeping stops, he will dry out like a small, pale leaf and become part of the caulk.

Until then, the room breathes. Inhale - walls in, water up, leaves press. Exhale - walls out, ears pop, weeping intensifies. And in that rhythm, between drip and tick and tectonic sigh, the keeper makes his endless, loving corrections, keeping the impossible pocket from healing shut.
