What lives here is not crowded.

That is the first wrong expectation to let go of. For all its noise and work, the room is not teeming. Not crawling. If you came in thinking *jungle* would mean a thousand eyes, a thousand legs brushing your legs, you would be surprised by how empty it feels at first.

There are only a few of them.

And that is why they feel so large.

### The House Itself

The first resident is the room.

You have to count her, though your mind wants to file her as architecture. The ironbark mangrove-mother. Not a tree in the way a pine is a tree - not a trunk-and-branches thing you can point to - but a lowland matriarch who has forgotten verticality and chosen instead to spread, buttress, and braid. She is what lives here most completely, because she *is* here.

Her living wood makes the walls. Not dead heartwood. The pale inner bark you lean against gives slightly under your weight and springs back; if you pressed your ear to it you would hear sap moving, a slow hydraulic sigh far too low to be called sound. She sweats. The weeping burls - those knotted, eye-like nodules spaced at intervals around the chamber - are her hydathodes grown monstrous, exuding filtered groundwater continuously. She doesn't weep from sadness; she exudes because she is full and warm and cannot help but perspire in this basin heat. The temperature never leaves her. She holds it: 2.1 warm, greenhouse warm, the exact warmth of a body that has been in sun all day.

The leaves above you are hers, but they have ceased behaving like leaves. They overlap until suture becomes thatch. Light comes through them as through frosted glass. Spores catch in it and hang, never settling, because the air in the room is as still as the warm itself. She breathes, and the room breathes.

She is very old, and she is not growing. Or rather, she grows only enough to stay exactly this shape. Any root that would close the narrow slit you entered through is pruned by the traffic on it. Any leaf that would fall has been woven in. She has arrived.

She does not notice you.

### The Cutters and The Vat

Then, low on the far wall - the workers you will hear before you see.

At first you think it is a stain. A broad, dark, polished ribbon cut into the living wall, half a foot wide, running from floor to mid-wall and then turning sharply into a hollow. It shines. That shine is not wetness. It is polish. Chitin on wood, a million times a day for years.

They are black leaf-cutter ants, but calling them ants makes you think of something small and frantic. These are deliberate. Each one is the length of the first joint of your thumb, matte-black and clean. There are hundreds of them visible at once and yet they make almost no sound - just a faint, dry *shush* like paper being shuffled.

They move in two lines, never breaking. Outbound empty-jawed, inbound carrying. The cuttings they carry are not random litter: discs of fresh, waxy green the size of coins, cut from the thatch-roof above, held aloft like parasols. They do not waver. They do not fight. An inbound cutter meeting an outbound cutter does the slight step-aside that has been choreographed for a million years and continues.

They are not storing food. They are feeding something else.

In the hollow of the wall the Vat lives.

It is not an object. It is a presence the size of a bathtub, maybe larger, pale cream and veined with faint pink, foaming very slowly at its edges. The fungal cultivar. The ants have chewed and licked and inoculated those leaf discs into this mass, and it in turn digests for them. It respires. If you crouch near it you feel heat coming off it - a few degrees warmer than the already-warm walls - and you smell it: yeast and iron and turned earth, the smell of a brewery inside a body. It churns, but so slowly you think your eyes are tricking you. A bubble forms, swells to the size of a grape, and collapses without popping.

The air around it crackles. Literally. The static of ten thousand tiny legs combing fungal hyphae throws off a faint electric prickle on the hairs of your arms. This is the factory that was never built. No queen gives orders you can see. The highway, the Vat, the endless green parasols - they are one animal distributed across wall and fungus and motion. It does not pause. It does not accelerate. Even as you watch, the pace is identical frame to frame. Dynamic 0.5 - frozen-equilibrium. Labor without progress, because progress would mean change, and this place does not change.

If one cutter stumbles - and one does, briefly, a leaf disc tilting - another moves to right it without breaking stride. No alarm.

### The Weavers

Above you, where the aerial roots hang like unplugged cables, live the weavers.

There are three, maybe four. You count them again and get a different number because they move so little.

Each orb weaver is as big as your open palm. Not the delicate garden kind. Their abdomens are barred with yellow and slate-black, warning colors muted by tannin dust, their legs banded and thick as wire. They do not hang in the center of a pretty spiral. They have industrialized.

Between the root-cables they have strung sheets. Not webs. Looms.

Parallel lines of silk, dozens of them, taut and humming, anchored root to root to leaf-thatch. In cross-light you see they are not single strands but corded cables, re-spun and tensioned. Smaller silk - a kind of gossamer scaffolding - drifts between them to catch what the main lines miss.

And things are caught. Gnats, moths, tiny iridescent flies that breed in the black pools below. They throw themselves into the loom and stick, vibrating. The spiders do not rush. One will detach from its cable, move with a slow, hydraulic precision along the line, wrap the catch in a tight white packet, and attach it not to eat, but to store, to weight, to test. You see packets lined along the lower cables like bobbins.

Then the spider returns to its station and tightens. It plucks. It re-tensions. The whole sheet thrums with a low note you feel more than hear, a textile mill made of protein. Silk is being maintained for its own sake. The weavers shuttle back and forth, legs combing, spinnerets working, never building a new web, never abandoning the old one. Maintenance as purpose. The silk thrums and never goes slack. The drop of water on the root-tip above them swells and never falls.

They do not compete with the ants. They are on another shift, another plane.

### The Engines

And the loud. God, the loud.

There are three of them. You would swear there were twenty.

Spaced equidistant around the upper curve of the walls, locked to the pale inner bark like rivets, are the cicadas. Each one is huge - not the summer whine from high canopy, but a lowland engine, carapace glossy as lacquered tin, green-black with eyes like polished amber. You can see the tymbals on their sides flexing.

They do not call. They do not sing. They *run*.

Their sound is not biological in the way a bird is biological. It is mechanized. An industrial, electrified drill-whine held on a single flattened note - no trill, no pulse, no crescendo. Three of them together phase-lock until the small room is filled with a solid wall of sound that makes the tannin pools shiver in perfect concentric rings, makes your molars ache, makes the moss seem to shiver.

You wait for one to breathe. It never does. The note holds. Minutes pass and the pitch does not wobble a quarter-tone, the volume does not dip. Your ears adjust and then fail to adjust. It is too loud for this pocket, impossibly loud for so few bodies, and that is the point: a few presences, each filling its emptiness with incredible density of function. One cicada doing the work of a factory whistle pressed down and left.

They are not communicating. At this volume and constancy there is no message. They are generating. The sound itself is the product, and the product is stasis - a sonic roof that keeps the room inside itself, vibrating at the exact frequency of its own continuance.

You can see their legs trembling slightly with effort, but they do not tire.

### The Others

There is more, but it is small enough and old enough you have to decide to see it.

The moss. It is not groundcover. It is a resident, inches thick, peat-deep, springy as a mattress. It has grown into soil and then decided to be soil. Press your hand into it and it remembers you for ten minutes. Its surface is dry; an inch down it is soaked. It holds the black pools in liverwort lips, patient cuffs grown over centuries to keep each bowl perfect. It exhales. The wet you feel is its breath.

In the pools, nothing swims you can see, but the water is not empty. If you held it to light you would see cyclops, rotifers, tannin-stained protozoa turning slowly. The pools tremble each time a root-tip *pliks*, and then still again.

On the silk, in the furrows of bark, in the Vat-foam, live mites the size of poppy seeds, white and amber, cleaning, combing, eating spores that would otherwise overwhelm. They are maintenance crew for maintenance crew.

All of it - tree, ant-fungus, spider-loom, cicada-engine, moss-mat, mite, microbe - is abundant in function and sparse in kind. Few stations. Endless work. No waste. No product shipped out. The highway polishes deeper, the Vat respires, the silk is tightened, the scream is held.

None of them need you. None of them will stop because you are here. Long before you ducked through that slit between roots the pace was this pace, the warmth was this warmth, the wet was this weeping, the loud was this loud. The drop hung swollen without falling.

When you finally go - when you find the slit again and squeeze out sideways into the larger, messier, less perfect forest where sound rises and falls and trails go cold - the room will not mark your absence.

The ants will march.
The silk will thrum.
The engines will scream.
The nodules will weep.

In its own state.
