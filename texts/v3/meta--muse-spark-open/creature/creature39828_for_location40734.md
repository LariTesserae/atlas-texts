You think at first that nothing lives here.

That's the first settlement the hollow makes with you — that it is empty, or emptied. That something left. The moorhen you saw from the rim was only passing through. The midge columns are tourists over warm water. Even the fox thought better of it.

And for a long hour, the place lets you believe it.

Then you crouch.

Not because you saw something move. Because you stayed long enough for movement to resolve.

### The Weep-Tenders

They are not *in* the water. They are the water's way of staying in one place.

In the black-shouldered gullies, in the shin-deep braids that never flood, there is a skin that shouldn't be there. At first you take it for oil — that cold-tea iridescence — but oil breaks when you breathe on it and this does not. It flexes. It tightens when a drip hits it and loosens after.

If you watch one puddle long enough you see them: dots, smaller than rice grains, paler than the silt. Each one alone is nothing — a bead of water that holds itself too well. Together they make decisions. A seep starts at the high side of a vat-lid, and the dots gather to its lip. They don't block it. They comb it. They pull the iron out of it strand by strand until the water runs clear for a few minutes and the dots go rust-dark and heavy and sink a millimeter and let go.

You lift a stone and they are under it too, but different — flat, pale, liverwort-soft, spread like wet paper until they touch other flat things, and where two meet they exchange a darker seam. Not eating. Trading.

They are everywhere the water beads and falls, beads and falls. They farm the sweat. When you wipe condensation off a pipe and come back ten minutes later, the beads are back in the same pattern, beading along the same cold line. That's not condensation. That's placement.

### The Leaners

You heard one before you saw one. *Tock.*

A scale of rust the size of a dinner plate falls off a stack of corrugated sheet. Dust hangs in the cool light. The stack has leaned an inch.

No. It was leaned.

They live in the density, in rule #2: *No straight line stays empty.* They are the reason.

They are long, and narrow, and the color of old enamel when it chips. When they are still you cannot find them at all — they live in the angles where a pipe meets a trestle, where a cable spool leans against a drum, where chain-link has swallowed itself. Their bodies are jointed like conveyor frames. Six, eight, ten segments, each one the length of your thumb, each one holding a different rust.

They do not walk. They re-arrange.

Over the course of an afternoon one will move a length of rebar half an inch so that a drip that was falling short of a tray now lands in it. Another will nudge a sheet so that condensate runs down its underside instead of its top. They work in the quiet bazaar-energy, never together, never apart, passing each other in the knee-high pipe runs with a soft *chit-chit-chit* that you mistook for a pump.

You only know you've seen one when it stops. Then a pipe that was sweating in urgent curtains suddenly runs orderly, bead by bead. That was its hands.

They don't build. They maintain. The hollow is a tidal pool and they are what keeps the tide from ever going out.

### The Warm Ones

Rule #3 is for them.

*T*ouch nothing that is still warm.

Most things here are cool to the touch, even the metal, even in summer. The clay is cool. The water is cool. The air is close to saturation and cool.

But sometimes a seam in the beached boiler sighs steam with no heat source, or a pump cover ticks for thirty seconds after its chatter stops. If you put your palm near — not on — you feel it: a wallet of warm air, small as a cat, sitting on the concrete.

That's not residual.

Inside the boiler, inside the vats sunk to their shoulders, inside the transformer plinth where the bolts remain, there are nests of something that does not like to be seen. You never see them whole. You see the signs they leave on the surfaces they tend.

A dark water vat shivers without wind, in a perfect circle from the center out. Something just surfaced and breathed.

A puddle ripples in sequence, one to the next, in a straight line toward the center that drains nowhere. Something small swims just under the silt, carrying heat from one place to another.

A length of cable sways when no one touches it, not with wind but with weight, as if something hand-over-handed along it a moment ago, and its belly was warm.

They are the only things here that are warm-blooded, if blood is the word. Iron-blooded, oil-blooded. They run on the difference between the cool ordinary light and the warm inside of things left on. They drink the vapor that pauses in the center before it leaves. They are rare the way foxes are rare — one print between two drums, there and then gone — but the system is sized for them. The density is for them. The hamlet-scale bowl that feels like a cathedral storeroom is a burrow, not a ruin.

You can tell how long one has been curled around a pipe by how dry the ground is beneath it. A perfect half-inch dry oval in clay that is otherwise sweating. As if the earth itself held its breath to keep it comfortable.

At dusk — though there is no real dusk under that flat white light, only a slight thickening — you might see one try to leave. Up that barely-there slope that makes your ears pop. It never gets to the rim. The air gets too thin, too dry, too empty. No straight line stays empty in the hollow; outside, every line is empty. It turns back. It finds a warm vat and submerges. The water goes still. Then, precisely seven minutes later, shivers again.

That's what lives here, then.

Not the moorhen. The moorhen is a guest who has misunderstood the invitation.

What lives here are the ones who learned to live like water does in this place: to arrive, to pause, to leave as vapor if you must, but mostly to stay and weep and tend. Small, flat, pale things that farm sweat. Long, jointed things that lean rust into new channels. Warm, shy, heavy things that ride the pipes and make the whole dense bowl tick and plink and sigh without ever making it loud.

They were not brought here. The industry didn't leave them. The industry *became* them, slowly, as the channels finished eroding and the gullies polished smooth and the water learned it had nowhere to go but into the air.

If you stand very still and let your sleeves dampen at the cuffs without wiping them, they start to ignore you. Then you can see the real motion — not the sluice coughing or the steam sighing, but the continuous, industrious passing of things hand to hand to hand through the clutter, maintenance as metabolism.

The hollow is not abandoned. It is inhabited down to the last cubic meter.
