They live low. Everything here lives low.

It has to. Vertical space is expensive in a different way than you understand up there, under a sky. Here the sky hasn't finished being made, so there are only about six feet of usable world. Below your knees: rock and the warm skin of water. From knees to head: the breathing zone, thick air, condensation forming and falling again without ever becoming cloud. Above your head: a dark that eats light and holds it somewhere else. Nothing lives up there for long. Even the things that glide, land quickly.

### The Keepers

First, the ones who built the pumps and left them on. There are not many. There were never many.

You won't meet them in groups. They work alone, one to a station, and they move very little. You find a Keeper by the sound of slow work in the dark — the soft tap of a wrench against a gauge every thirty seconds, the creak of rubber boots shifting weight on a catwalk that goes nowhere.

They are damp people. Not wet, damp. Their clothes never dry because the air itself never dries. Young basalt dust and rock-flour has worked into everything — the seams of their jackets, the lines of their hands, under their nails. Their skin has gone the orange-grey of the lichen that cracks the stone, not from dye, from exposure. They don't wear lights on their heads. Lights are useless beyond a foot. They wear sound.

A bell on a string around the wrist. A loose valve left to tick. A pocket metronome going *tok... tok... tok...* at whatever tempo the basin allows today. They time their steps between quiets. They have learned not to trust their eyes, because a bulb ghosts to the side of itself, because a stone lands after its reflection. So they close their eyes often. They listen for the seam of absolute quiet and know that on the other side of that seam is another shed, another engine turning over once every five seconds with a *whup* you feel in your sternum.

They speak rarely, and when they do it's low and without sibilance, so it doesn't shear on the strange edges in the air. They don't call to each other. They leave an engine running to say: I am still here, I am still tending. When an engine stops, another Keeper will find it hours later by the absence and try to restart it, but restarting requires timing the basin won't give you. You have to turn the key a half-second before you mean to. You have to get used to causing before intending.

They are patient to a fault. They have to be. Nothing here is quick. Water breathes over hours. Rock expands a millimeter a year. Light arrives late. They eat standing up, directly over their task-light circle, chewing slowly, watching a needle that trembles inward toward the center of the basin because down is not quite down here.

They are not colonists. They are maintenance. Someone sent them after the rock came up, still hot, to see if the new flat would hold a road, a mine, a settlement. It won't. But the place makes a different sense, so they stayed to keep the pumps weeping, the transformers humming, the conveyors squeaking empty — because if they turn them off the basin gets too quiet and the fauna comes closer.

### The Crowded

The rest, the *most* of what lives here, lives in that quiet.

It is not a jungle. There is almost nothing green to support a jungle. The reeds like black wire, the stunted mangrove on stilt roots, the luminous puff of fern — they are garnish. The base of the food web is not photosynthesis. It's the sweat.

Warm stone perspiring. Mineral-rich condensation beading and falling three inches at a time, over and over, across a region the size of a town. Bacteria and biofilms eat the heat and the dissolved rock-flour. And everything else eats them.

So abundance stacks vertically in inches rather than in canopy layers.

**In the sheet, the swimmers.** You kneel and your eyes finally give up trying to see far and start seeing near. The water is maybe a palm deep but it is full. Boatmen the size of your hand, matte black, rowing with oars that don't make ripples because they row *under* the surface tension. Translucent eels — salamanders — tadpole-things that are essentially a gut and a gill and huge external lungs they flare like ferns, hanging perfectly still in warm water until they inhale with a soft *pip* you can feel on your ankle. Leeches that don't bite, that just anchor to a basalt blade and filter.

And everywhere: the frogs.

They are the census. Dozens to a square meter wherever a ridge breaks the water just enough to give them a perch. Small, black-backed, throats pale grey, packed shoulder to shoulder not touching — always that centimeter of courtesy space. They never chorus. They never need to. They breathe together. Watch their throats pulse: inhale, hold, exhale, all at once, a single distributed lung spread across the basin. They are listening to the echo that comes *before* the sound, and if one were to call, the others would have already heard its ghost. So they stay silent and eat whatever dimples the water, taking turns with a flick of tongue so fast you don't see it, only the afterimage of it a second later to one side.

Between them, the joints. A crab that is not a crab: too many articulations, legs that fold the wrong way, carapace the same black glass as the rock. It picks through frog-feet without disturbing a single animal. Isopods like wet seeds, rolling with the tide that isn't a tide but the faint inward pull of down-toward-center. They are all tolerant because there is nowhere to go and so much to eat in the sweat. Crowding is not stress here. Crowding is the strategy.

**At boot-height, the waders.** Things that look like they were recently something else. Long-legged, stilted. A bird that is not a bird — more a skin kite stretched over hollow bone, no eyes, beak open to the warm condensation falling into it. It stands perfectly still for minutes and then takes a step exactly as long as the interval between pump *whups*. You only see it when your task-light sweeps low and catches the oil on its feathers. There are tracks everywhere in the silt-skin between ridges, three-toed, four-toed, no-toed, overlapping until the ground is a script.

### The Gliders and the Watchers

Above — in the last two feet before darkness takes the light — is where the hunters live.

They have learned the delay. That is their whole evolution.

You feel one before you see it: warm air pushed down a fraction as something leathery the span of your outstretched arms goes from one unlit pylon to the next. It doesn't flap. It parachutes on a membrane between fore- and hind-limb, landing without sound because sound itself has seams here. Its head is all ears and whiskers, its eyes reduced to pinpricks. It doesn't look at where you are. It looks at where your heat *was* two seconds ago and intercepts. On the ground it folds into itself like an umbrella.

Perched under every gantry, along the rafters of the transformer shed, is the second kind: the Watchers. You thought it was eyeshine. It's not reflection. It's generation. A row of them, each the size of your fist, each with a belly that can hold a glow for a second and release it late. They open, hold, close in sequence — not blinking, signaling. They hunt the moths that navigate by delayed photons, by chasing the ghost of the filament that appears a hand's width to the side a few seconds after the real one. When you stare too long at a bare bulb, those afterimages crowding in — that's not your retina. That's them, hunting.

They are completely silent. Everything successful here is silent. To make noise is to make an edge, and edges in air occupy space. A mouse-quiet *chitter* can be a wall for a frog listening to the past.

All of them, wader and swimmer and glider, share one rule: never hurry. The basin punishes hurry. Throw that stone and its splash arrives late. Run and your shadow arrives early. The animals drift, row without ripple, glide on still air. The most common motion here is hanging: suspended in warm water, suspended in thick air, waiting for the world to catch up.

If you stand long enough, thigh-deep in bathwater with rock sweating under your palms, the distinction blurs. Keepers and crowded. The Keeper listening for the bell every sixteen seconds, the frog listening for the echo before the call, the glider looking for where you were — they are all doing the same thing. Learning to live in a place where light is slow, where down leans inward, where quiet is an object you can step around.

They've learned to breathe together, in a basin the size of a region that is still cooling, still making more black glass ridges knee-high and ankle-catching for the next warm sheet to cover. There is plenty of room, if you don't need a sky.
