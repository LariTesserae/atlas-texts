It lives in the spacing.

Not *in spite* of it. Because of it. Everything here learned a long time ago how to be far apart and still be a community.

The builders of this place, if you can call them that, are the pillars themselves.

They are not trees. They are not moss the way your book means moss. They are something that got to be this big because nothing ever told it to stop. Each one begins as a single shoot in the mat — a black-green tip pushing up through the sphagnum — and then it decides to be a column.

For the first ten feet it is just a trunk. Not woody. Fibrous, damp, as thick around as your thigh or your waist, wrapped in its own dead leaves that never quite fall. The surface is shaggy, vertical, a hanging garden on a vertical cliff: sheets of liverwort like wet leather, filmy ferns no thicker than tissue paper that drape down in blankets, and bead-moss that glows pale green in the gloom. If you put your hand on it, your palm comes away cold and faintly oily. It gives a little under pressure. It is alive all the way through.

Then at twenty or thirty feet it stops climbing and opens. The crown is flat on top, like a table, like a lily pad held impossibly in the air. Feathery fronds, each as long as your arm, spread and interlace with the next pillar's crown, and the next, until the whole grove has a ceiling. Not a canopy — you can see sky through it in a green, broken jigsaw. Light comes through diffuse and even. Condensation collects on the undersides of the fronds, beads, and falls. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* All day. All night. That is one of the rains here.

Nothing crowds them. This is the first law of the animals: keep your distance. The pillars exude something into the mat — tannin, acid, a signal — that tells any seedling too close: *not here. Thirty feet. Wait your turn.* So they stand like the columns of a drowned cathedral, each in its own circle of open air, each with its own warbler.

The mat is the second thing that lives. It is wrong to call it ground. Ground is what is under a thing. Here the ground *is* a thing.

A meter thick, sometimes more, it is peat that never finished becoming peat because the cold won't let it. Sphagnum, deep red and gold and waterlogged, growing forever upward from its own dead top — or downward, it's hard to tell — holds so much water that it sighs when you step on it. Under that are roots, not from the pillars but from everything at once: a felt so dense you could peel it up in sheets. Foraminifera live in the films of water between the leaves. Springtails by the millions, white as rice grains, flick and vanish when your shadow passes. The mat preserves everything that falls into it. A frond dropped three years ago will still be there, bleached to paper, legible, slowly becoming floor. A dead beetle will lie for a decade, polished by acidity, before it is accepted.

And because it preserves, it feeds very slowly. This is why the grove can be loud and empty at the same time. Food is everywhere but it is locked. You have to know how to unlock it.

The machines do.

In each of the five round pools sits an engine on its sled, rust-colored, sweating, breathing. They are not inhabitants the way an animal is, but the animals have made them so. The flywheel on the northmost engine — the one nearest the rim — has a polish on its upper spoke where warblers land to sing. Not one warbler, generations of them. The heat coming off the boiler makes the air above it shiver, and in that shiver midges hang in a column that never disperses. The engine is warm, the air is cool. The engine ticks, the midges rise.

They are the engine-birds. Small, the color of wet moss with a flash of sulfur yellow when they turn. One per pillar, never more. They claim a trunk and they do not leave it. From dawn until the amber hour they call: *tsee-tsee-tsee-TCHEW, tsee-tsee-tsee-TCHEW*, each answered a half-second later by another thirty feet away, around the circle, out of sync with the pistons but somehow in time with them. When you walk between pillars they look down at you, cock their heads, and keep calling. The sound is absurdly loud for their size. It carries the whole breadth of the hollow, bouncing off living wood, mixing with steam hiss until you can't tell bird from valve.

Below them, between your boots, are the runners.

They look like rails at first, until you see their toes. Mottled brown-black, long as your finger, with feet splayed impossibly wide to spread their weight on the mat. They never fly. They run — flick-flick-pause — through the sphagnum, parting it like grass, snapping up the springtails, the crickets that are not crickets. The crickets here have no wings. They are fat, pale, almost blind, living entirely in the root-felt, and they make their sound by rubbing their hind legs against their bellies without ceasing. A dry, papery *saw-saw-saw* that never peaks or stops. You think it is one insect until you crouch and realize it is hundreds, one every few square feet, all sawing at once. The runners know exactly where to probe.

In the pools themselves is the stillness that balances all that noise.

Lift the tea-colored water — never deep, six inches, clear to the white granite dish beneath — and you will see the bottom is moving. Black newts, orange-bellied, hundreds per pool, packed flank to flank in the cold, not swimming, barely breathing. They are the preservation made flesh. The water is acidic enough to sting a cut, cold enough to numb, and they love it. They do not call. They do not scatter when the bale-press drops its wet bale back into the pool with a soft *plosh*. They absorb the peat dust that the machines exhale, the spores that fall like rust snow from the crowns. In spring they lay strings of eggs on the submerged sled runners, and the larvae hang there like commas, sharing the iron.

And above the newts, in the open vertical air that makes this place feel so hollow, are the columns of midges. Not a swarm — a structure. Each pillar has one, a slow vortex of thousands rising from the mat's exhalation to the warmth of the crowns, falling, rising again. At dusk they catch the amber light and turn to smoke. The frogs eat them.

You will hear the frogs before you see them. They live up in the crowns, impossible tree frogs that should not be this far north, but the grove is always cool and always damp and the engines never let it freeze hard. Pale mint green with dark eyes, no bigger than a thumbnail, they wedge themselves into the crooks of fronds and make a sound like two wet stones knocked together: *tok. tok. tok.* Very slow. Very even. If you stand still long enough you will feel your own heartbeat try to line up with the piston chuff, the cricket saw, and the frog tok, and fail, because none of them ever agree and none of them ever stop.

There are others that do not sing.

On any pillar trunk, if you look close, you will find the carriers. Beetles as long as your thumb, dull black, with backs pitted like the granite, moving with insulting slowness vertically upward, grazing the film of algae and spore. Each one is itself a place. Mites, orange as the newt bellies, ride in the grooves of its elytra. Smaller white mites ride those mites. They do not fall off when the beetle hangs upside down under a frond. They have been on that beetle for generations.

And the spores that oil the engines — they live too, in their way. The pillar-moss fruits once a year all at once, though there are no seasons to tell it when. Fat capsules at the crown edge burst and release a cloud of rust-red dust that hangs in the cool air for days, settling on everything: your shoulders, the newts' backs, the flywheels, the open water. Where it lands in a joint it softens to a grease. Where it lands on the mat it sprouts.

Nothing rots fast here. Nothing grows fast. Nothing leaves.

If you stand in the exact center — equidistant from all five engines so their chuffs come to you as a round, rolling chorus, with the warblers over them and the crickets under them and the tok of the frogs stitching through — you will understand the equilibrium. The pillars drip into the pools. The pools feed the engines. The engines bale the peat that would otherwise close the pools. The midges breed in the peat, the birds eat the midges, the birds drop guano that feeds the pillars, the pillars drop spores that oil the engines to keep baling.

A footprint in the mat fills with dark water for an hour and then, very slowly, the sphagnum lifts back, erasing it. A fallen beetle is taken apart by springtails over months. A warbler dies and lies preserved on the mat until the runners pull it apart strand by strand.

You were not expected, but you are not intruding. The grove has reached a state so old and so stable that an extra observer changes nothing. The water will still seep. The flywheels will still turn. The pillars will still hold their thirty-foot courtesy, vast and empty and completely full.
