There is no one living here.

Not anymore. Not in the way you mean when you say *someone lives here* — a hearth, a name on a door, a path worn to the water. The sockets where their posts stood are empty. The hammers are still lined up, but no hand has lifted one in a thousand years. The bowl was made to hold people for a little while and then to hold something else for a long while after.

What lives here now does not live the way people do. It lives the way a held breath lives. Contained. Kept.

You have to let it resolve slowly, the way your eyes adjust to dim. At first you think: frogs and swallows, nothing more. Noise and wings. Then you stay long enough that the noise separates.

### The Occupants In Residence

The first residents are the quickest ones, and you see them before you understand them. They do not belong to the bowl so much as they borrow its geometry.

The swifts own the air. Not the sky above the bowl — the cube of air the bowl encloses. They come down at first light and they never leave it. Eight, maybe twelve, maybe twenty — you cannot count them because they are too fast and the walls throw their shadows in triplicate. They are soot-black with a white throat you only see when they bank vertical, and they scream as they work. It is not a birdsong. It is a tool-sound, a *scree-scree* of air being sheared. They live in the weep-holes. Every one of those perfect, square, fist-deep drains the masons left to keep the walls from bursting — now each one is a nest, a cup of mud and reed-stem and whitewash guano glued into the dark. If you climb close enough to look, which you shouldn't because the stone is slick, you will see them all in a row looking back at you: gapes open, skulls too big, waiting. At dusk they fold themselves impossibly small and disappear. The air goes empty all at once and feels larger without them.

Under them, on every vertical face that stays wet, are the frogs.

You will hear them before you ever find one, and even after you find one you will barely see it. Ten thousand. Maybe more. Each the size of the last joint of your thumb, the color of wet slate with a vellum-thin belly that glows amber when they cling to the light. *Peen. Peen. Peen.* Not a chorus — a lattice. Each male has tuned his note just far enough from his neighbor that it doesn't cancel, so the whole bowl rings like struck glass. If you stand still they resume around you, and if you move they stop in a wave that follows you. They lay their eggs not in the marsh but in the film that runs down the walls — long strings of clear beads in the trickle, trembling. Tadpoles with mouths like suckers cling there, grazing. They never fall. The frogs eat the midges. The midges rise off the water in columns that look like smoke and then vanish into those open mouths and the sound gets a fraction louder.

That is the contract of the upper bowl: too loud for how little green there is.

### The Occupants In Residence — Deeper

If you kneel at the terraces and put your face low, the second population shows itself. The slow one. The one that doesn't make noise.

In the clear skin over the outer rings, black salamanders live — broad-headed, yellow-eyed, each with two or three sulfur spots along the spine like someone touched them with a hot wire. They are impossibly still. You think they are shadows between flags until one blinks. They walk, not swim, under an inch of water, their four splayed feet finding the chisel-grooves as if the grooves were made for them. They eat the frog eggs that wash down. They lay their own eggs under the flags, in the dark silt where your hands can't follow without moving stone.

Between their legs, on the surface, needle-sized water beetles skate and make those bright Vs that point the wrong way when the echo comes back. On the bottom, leeches wait, fat and ridged and the color of old blood, folded under each lifted edge. They do not hunt. They simply attach to whatever pauses long enough — salamander belly, your ankle if you stand too long, each other. When they are full they drop off and bury themselves until the blood turns to more leech.

And then there are the things that are not supposed to be here.

In the center pan where the black water stands between reeds, if you scoop a clear jar and hold it to the grey light, you will see them. The shrimp-things. No bigger than an eyelash, translucent with a single black eye, moving in sudden, jerky spirals. Leave any container out overnight and in the morning it will be condensation-full and already inhabited. They were not carried in. There is nowhere for them to come from. They condense, the way the mist does. The water that beads on the walls tastes faintly of iron and sugar when you touch it to your tongue, and if you put a drop under magnification — if you had one — you would see why. It is not empty. It is soup. Bacteria in such numbers the water opalesces, algae the color of weak tea, animalcules turning in their small orbits, all of them feeding on minerals the stone itself is shedding. The stone is soft. Leave a flag lifted for a month and its underside will be furred with jelly.

This is the trick the bowl learned: it feeds itself. The hill bleeds water into it, the stone bleeds stone into the water, the microbes eat the stone, the shrimp eat the microbes, the frogs eat the shrimp before they even have a name, the swifts eat the frogs' flies. A closed loop, but not quite closed, because every morning there is a little more silt between the flags, a little more life per square meter than mineral logic says there should be.

And the reeds — that one stiff, jointed reed that learned the masonry pattern. Its seeds only germinate in mortar. Its roots secrete an acid that widens the seams just enough for more roots, and its hollow stems house ants that farm aphids that drink reed sap and excrete sweetness that grows the mold that the tadpoles eat. It stands in absolute straight lines, following the old flagging, because it is still following orders the builders laid down without knowing they were giving them.

### The Resident

All those are its tenants. They are not it.

It is what you feel when you sit on the lowest terrace and put your hands in that aching cold and the frogs go quiet for five heartbeats.

The bowl itself is alive. Not alive like a frog. Alive like a kidney.

It has an inside and an outside and a flow between them. It takes in — rain, spring water, mist, eggs on the wind, lost swifts, your breath — and it processes. It filters. You can hear it after heavy rain: a sigh and tick from deep under the flags as the maintenance conduits, those collapsed service tunnels around the rim that look too small for a person, exhale cold air that smells of wet iron and time. If you drop a leaf in the spring-mouth's pour, it does not just swirl. It is taken, tipped, sorted. Heavy particles drop to the outer ring. Light ones are carried to the center. In an hour the water is clear again, as if it never happened.

Stand in the center on a windless day and watch those reeds. You saw it already — they lean toward you, all of them, a fraction, then relax. The shrimp in your cup do it too. They orient. Not to heat. Not to shadow. To presence. To the small gravity of a living body in a place that has learned to collect living bodies.

That is the final inhabitant, the one that was made without ever being intended: a slow, stone, water animal a hundred meters across, shallow as a dish, patient as lime. It doesn't want anything. It keeps. In the sun it closes a little — evaporates less. In storm it opens — lets the conduits run. In winter it holds the cold so fiercely the midges never fully die, and in summer it keeps a mist at chest height so the frogs never fully dry.

It has been doing this since the masons left, and it will keep doing it after the liverwort curtain finally pulls free of the south wall and falls. The bats pour out of its throat every night and return. The leeches pulse under its skin. The swifts scream through its lung.

You are not its maker. You are just its most recent meal that it decided, for the moment, not to eat.

Sit long enough and you'll feel it hold you, total and primitive and weeping, not unkindly — the way a palm holds water — until you stand and shake off the droplets in your hair and it lets you go, a little heavier, a little wetter, echoing a little too long.
