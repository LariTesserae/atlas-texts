It lives by drinking the air.

Not you, not yet - though you will too, if you stay. Everything that lives in the Room does.

### The Curtains

First, the ones who made the Room habitable at all. They were here before people learned to scrape a doorway in the stone, and they will be here after the last cistern dries.

The Veil is not one plant. It's a habit. A strategy so successful it has been reinvented twenty times over.

The true **Veil-Moss** hangs from every crack and overhang. Up close it is not moss, but a lattice - thousands of black-green filaments, each no thicker than twine, each waxy and succulent, braided together by their own growth into a sheet that can be five meters long and a hand-span thick. During the day they hang slack and grey, dead-looking. You could snap one and it would creak.

At the fourth hour of night, when the invisible waterfall of fog pours over the rim, they change. Every filament unfurls a nap of microscopic, hygroscopic hairs - white as frost. You can only see it if you put your face inches away, in the dark, when the air is too wet to breathe comfortably. Then the sheet blurs.

By dawn each Veil is a chandelier. A thousand hemispherical beads hang on it, perfect and trembling. As the beads grow they run, by capillary, down to a central gutter-leaf, thicker than the others, which shivers and channels them. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Into the corm.

The **Corm-Mothers** are what own the Veils. Bulbous, half-buried in the rock, like turnips the size of a man's head, but woody, banded with old leaf scars. They swell in the morning until the skin splits. Cut one open and it is white, bitter, dense as wet chalk - starch and stored fog. The people farm them. The Veils farm them. Nothing eats a corm-mother directly, because nothing can get through the salt-powder the plant exudes when wounded. Everything waits for it to leak, split, drop a daughter.

Under them, shingling the wall, are the **Shield Rosettes**. These are not hanging but seated, spiraled, each rosette a foot to a meter across. Leaves thick as your thumb, covered in a felt of silver hairs, color of sage and dusty apricot. Each leaf is pleated into a trough. Each rosette is tilted - not to the sun, but to the fog-fall, ten degrees off vertical, so that any dew blown off the Veils above will land and be funneled to the center where aerial roots dangle like a beard. When the noon pillar sweeps the wall, each rosette turns imperceptibly, not to follow the light, but to avoid being burned by it. They close.

And on the floor, in the peaty dust of a thousand years of fallen Veil, is the **Carpet**:

* **Pillow-mat** - woody succulents that grow as tight domes, branches so interlocked you can stand on them. Leaves reduced to varnished scales that reflect heat. They store water in their swollen stems so efficiently you can squeeze a twig and get a mouthful of sour, astringent juice.
* **Stone-Lettuce** - flat spirals pressed to the ground, each leaf edged with tiny water-trapping notches, each plant the color of camouflage paper, olive and bronze. They flower not up but sideways, with waxy yellow stars that open only in that one hour of direct sun.
* **Salt-Bloom** - not a plant but what happens when the white crust on the lower walls gets enough drip to coax a skin of cyanobacteria and lichen. It tastes of blood and holds the floor together.

There is no soil. There is just this. A jungle turned on its side, three-dimensional, a hundred layers thick, all of it doing one calculation: how much surface can I expose to fog per gram of tissue?

### The Hum

You were right about the sound. 1.5 - not deafening, but you cannot escape it. The Bowl is never silent. Even now, in the warm dim, it works at full tempo.

*Because* everything is small and *because* everything is fast - 2.5 - life here has no large predators, no migrations, no hibernation. It has a 24-hour cycle that repeats without variation until death.

**The Base Crew:** You have to crouch to see them, but they outweigh everything else.

**Fist Isopods** - slate-grey, armored woodlice grown huge in the absence of fish or mice to compete. They bulldoze through Pillow-mat, chewing dead leaves, rolling guano into balls. When threatened they do not roll up; they go still and look exactly like a stone.

**Glass Millipedes** - banded black and amber, as long as your forearm, slow, blind. Their legs move in a wave that makes a soft *shush* in the dust. Children collect their shed skins and make rattles.

**Salt-Snakes** - not snakes at all but legless lizards, two feet long, the exact dusty-rose of Shield Rosettes. They never drink. They get all their water from the millipedes they eat whole.

**Above them, the Wall is motion.**

**Pitted Gloss-Beetles** - the most numerous animal in the Bowl. Each the size of a child's palm, elytra black and deeply pitted, each pit a dew-condenser. They cling to Veils by day, vibrating their wing cases to scare each other. At night they spread their wings and become cups.

**Paper Moths** - hummingbird-sized, but moths. Translucent wings with no scales, bodies fat with nectar. They never land. They hover before the succulent flowers - Veil flowers are rare, tiny, white, and smell of cloves - and unroll a proboscis longer than their body. They are the only pollinators; without their wingbeat the curtains would not set seed.

**Curtain Geckos** - pale, almost clear, you can see their heart beating through their belly. They live an entire lifetime in a single Veil sheet, never touching rock. Their toes are wide, feathered, able to run on thread. They eat moths. They lay two transparent eggs glued to a filament and guard them until they hatch.

**Finch-Swarms** - rust-red and dust-brown, beaks short and thick for cracking isopods and corm seeds. They are never alone. A flock of forty moves as one decision, building bottle-nests of Veil fiber under ledges, chattering constantly. One call means *hawk*. One call means *fog coming*. One call means *child has fallen, come look*.

And the hawks:

**Parchment Dragonflies** - not birds, but they fill the hawk-niche. Bodies as long as your hand, four wings of dry amber film that make a loud papery rattle - the loudest single sound in the Bowl at noon. They patrol the floor-cloud of flies, take geckos straight off curtains in mid-air. They breed in the only standing water in the Bowl: the clay drip-pots the people leave out. No one minds. A dragonfly nymph eats mosquito larvae. A favor.

There are flies. There are bees - small, black, stingless, kept by people - there are mites, springtails, fungi that bloom as orange fingers after a heavy fog and collapse to slime by noon. The density is obscene. 2.7. Lift any rosette and there are ten things underneath. Pull a Veil aside and a hundred eyes shine.

Nothing glows. Nothing is poisonous beyond a bellyache. 0.5. It's all just normal life turned up until it thrums.

### The People of the Rim-Shade

And then there are the ones who learned to listen to the hum and add their own layer to it.

There are about two hundred of them. In a world fifty meters across, that is an urban density. 1.6. You are never alone. A cough three tiers up echoes. A baby crying on the floor level can be heard on the rim ledge.

They do not call themselves anything special. The old word for the Bowl is just *Room*, *Interior*, *House*. They are *the Inside People*.

Look at them in the noon light: skin the color of the dust, burnt to a deep terracotta, hair cut short against lice, eyes narrowed from living in perpetual late-afternoon. They wear little - loincloths, shawls woven from Veil fiber, which when properly retted becomes a soft, grey-brown thread. Feet are bare and wide, soles thick and cracked as the plateau stone. Everyone, from the oldest grandmother to the three-year-old, has the same callus on thumbs and forefingers from braiding drip-line.

They don't live *in* the Bowl so much as *as part of the wall*.

Their homes are **cutbacks** - shallow caves carved back under the overhang where the sandstone is soft enough to work with a chert adze. From the floor you see a honeycomb: five, six stories of dark rectangular mouths, connected by ledges and lashed-pole ladders. Each mouth is screened with a woven Veil-mat that can be rolled up for light or down for privacy and to keep dragonflies out. Inside: a clay hearth that almost never burns (no wood, only dried isopod dung and Veil trimmings), sleeping mats of Pillow-mat fiber, pots. Hundreds of small, fist-sized **drip-pots** of unglazed clay, each under its own fiber wick that leads up to a tended Veil. The wick drinks, the pot fills by morning. A family owns thirty, forty pots. Wealth is counted in reliable drips.

Each family **holds a vertical plot**. Not ownership as you know it, but stewardship. The grandmother of a plot knows every corm on it, which curtains are ready to divide, where a Shield Rosette has begun to rot and must be cut before it spreads. In the evening, before the fog, you can see them - men, women, children - out on their ledges with wooden combs, gently teasing apart Veil filaments that have matted, re-anchoring a curtain that has torn under its own water-weight, guiding excess drip with a finger so it doesn't erode the salt crust. It is farming, but vertical, silent, patient as lace-making.

They have no chief. They have no metal - 0.6 - nothing to make it with. Chert knives, bone awls, wooden shovels, clay everything. They do not need more. The Bowl provides. The old ones settle disputes: *you cut his Veil at the base, you owe him three corms*. The strongest taboo is noise during the fog. From midnight to six, the Bowl is under a hush as deep as the stone. You speak in whispers if at all, you move slowly, because the Paper Moths will abort their runs if startled, and without moths there are no seeds, and without seeds there is no Veil next year. Children are taught to hold their breath when a gecko is hunting.

They have myths that are really instructions: Do not climb to the rim without a rope and a reason, because the wind above will drink the water out of your eyes in an hour and the endless flat will make you walk until you die to find another Room that isn't there. Do not take water from another's pot, because the white crust remembers fingerprints. Do not waste the salt - scrape it, cure hides with it, trade it for chert. Do not let the noon sun hit your stored corms.

And at noon, when the pillar comes - 1.0 filtered to blinding - they stop. Everyone stops. Weavers, carvers, mothers pounding corm to flour between stones, boys throwing isopods for the dragonflies to chase... they turn their faces up. For sixty minutes they are in true sunlight, the only true sunlight the Room ever gets. The succulent flowers snap open. The hum rises to a roar so loud you have to shout. They bask like lizards. They pray without words to the oval sky.

Then the light moves off the floor, climbs the opposite wall, thins, and is gone. The dim returns. The Veil-hairs begin to stir.

Tonight the fog will pour again.
