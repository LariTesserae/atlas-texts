What lives here has learned to live without being eaten, without being heard, and without being quite believed by the light.

### The Dominant: The Green Itself

There are no animals to speak of. That is the first law of the High Plain, and everything else is an answer to it.

No birds - the air is too thin and too empty to support a wingbeat for long, and there is nothing on the wing to eat anyway. No insects - or so few and so seasonal that you could spend a week in a basin and never see one: a few pale, flightless midges that hatch in the cold creek gravel, live for a day without mouths, lay eggs and die, never buzzing. No grazers, no predators, no scavengers. No footprints but your own in the silt.

In the absence of a fauna, the flora has become faunal.

It is not passive. It does not wait to be pollinated or pruned or dispersed.

**The Mat** is the base organism - though to call it one organism is wrong, and to call it many is also wrong. Over most of the plateau, the ground is not ground but a continuous, deep, springy carpet:

* **Rust-moss and Emerald-moss** - two species that grow interlocked - form a mattress up to a foot thick over the basalt. You can pull a corner up like hide. It creeps visibly after snowmelt, an inch a day, sealing over your bootprints by evening with new green fingers. It drinks fog directly through its leaf tips and makes a faint ticking as it swells and contracts with freeze-thaw.
* **Cup Lichen** like bleached coral grows on the moss, brittle in sun, rubbery in fog. It reproduces not by spores but by shattering - a caribou-less plain where the wind is the hoof that breaks it and spreads it.
* **Sideways Forest:** Dwarf willow and dwarf birch. Here they never become trees. They become a wood you can sit on. A single individual may be three feet tall and twenty feet wide, its trunk as thick as your thigh, lying flat, branching horizontal, huddled together with its neighbors into a dense, interwoven thatch that holds heat and moisture underneath like a living blanket. In autumn their leaves turn all at once - a shock of gold across a hundred miles - and drop straight down into the mat to be digested where they fall.

In the troughs and seep-lines, where the weeping cliffs feed the braided creeks, the Mat becomes **Jungle:**

**The Black Reeds.** Ten to fourteen feet tall, stems glossy black and hollow, leaves like knives. They grow in absolute thickets along every spring-line. They shiver constantly even when the air is still, because they are tracking heat. Pass your hand near one and the whole stand leans a degree toward you. They propagate by explosive joints - on a hot afternoon you will hear a sharp *crack* and see a plume of white fluff launch fifty feet into the air, hanging there, then corkscrewing with eerie purpose toward the next damp seam.

**Door Ferns.** Fronds as wide as doors, as heavy as wet canvas, unfurling in a single cold morning from fists of iron-red fiddleheads. They are carnivorous without animals to catch - their undersides are sticky with acid dew that digests wind-blown spores and lichen dust. They close at night with a soft, collective sigh.

**Bladder-plants.** Clusters of fleshy, pale celadon bladders the size of melons around every seep, each covered in hair-fine cilia that condense fog into droplets even when the air seems dry. At noon they are taut and shining; at dusk they sag as they feed their water down into taproots that split basalt. If you press one it sighs and does not reinflate for a day.

**Starflowers.** Low rosettes that should not exist at this altitude or cold - white and deep violet, five-pointed, waxy. They are the plateau's clock. When direct sun hits a hillside, thousands open at once with an audible soft *pop*, turning their faces to the paper-colored sun. When cloud rolls back in, they pop shut. Without bees, they self-pollinate in the closing: you can see a puff of their own pollen trapped inside just before the petals seal.

Between all of them, on windless afternoons, the air fills with **seekers** - clouds of spores and seeds that do not drift. They ride the plateau's weak electrostatic field in deliberate, spiraling columns, rising from the reeds, hovering, choosing. The sky over a seep will glitter with them for an hour. The Mat below waits with microscopic mouths open.

It is a prairie doing the work of a rainforest, at high speed, in silence. Growth, competition, seeding, death - all without a single cry.

### The Quiet Tenants: The People of the Basins

Human, or so near as to make no difference. There have been no new migrations for a very long time; the ones here are what high altitude makes when it has millennia to finish the job.

**Bodies shaped by thin air and cold damp:**
Broad, deep chests that hold the low-oxygen wind without hurry. Blood that runs dark and warm. Skin weathered to the color of the pink gravel, cheeks permanently ruddy from capillary bloom. Eyes that are used to seeing too far - slightly farsighted, quick to water, with lids that squint without thinking against the blue-black glare. They are short, heavy-boned, not from dwarfism but from compactness; heat kept close. They speak quietly because they must - lungs cannot afford shouting, and sound dies anyway. Children learn early to gesture: a palm flat for *wait, fog coming*, two fingers tapped on the throat for *water is uphill here*.

**Houses shaped by time:**
They do not build outward. The plateau does not allow it - the stone endures, the climate erases nothing, and there is no war worth walls for. So each basin-city is a set of nesting years.

The outermost ring is ruin that is still house: basalt blocks the size of a sleeping ox, fitted without mortar, now sunk to the knees in moss and lichen, lintels bowed under a thousand winters but unbroken. Goats would live there if there were goats. Instead, bladder-plants grow in the old hearths.

Inside that, a second wall, straighter, with houses shoulder-to-shoulder, reed-thatch roofs weighted with flat stones, low chimneys that smoke sideways before the smoke remembers to rise. Pink gravel streets, cisterns fed by miles of hollowed-stone aqueducts that bring the weeping cliff-springs home. Central green - always a green - where the water is divided by carved basalt sluices.

Innermost, where people actually live now, the rooms are small, half-sunk, sharing walls for warmth. Willow-wood doors, boiled and bent. Floors of beaten silt covered in woven fern fiber. Nothing tall. They learned long ago not to poke the sag.

**Culture shaped by a thin sky:**
They are watchers and listeners. Every basin has at least one **Bell Tower** - tall, narrow, open like a chimney - but the iron bells inside never ring in wind. They ring in *stillness*. When the air forgets how to carry sound, when gravity goes lenient, the pressure change makes the slate wind-chimes in every courtyard tinkle without breeze and the great bells give a low, sour tone all on their own. That is the signal to come inside, cover the cistern, do not walk straight lines.

Their maps are carved on cistern walls and they are deliberately wrong: streets that bow out around nothing, courtyards left empty because at the center your voice arrives before your mouth moves, a house with no north door because north sometimes slides east for an hour at dusk. Children are taught to throw a pinch of pink gravel before they step onto unknown moss - if it hangs too long at its apex, go around.

They are quiet, not from piety but from physics. You carry water, you carry cut fern bundles, you carry stones to weight a new roof. You nod. You do not call across a street. At night the basins glow amber, each a bowl of light in the vast dark, far from the next, and the talk inside is low, close, stories told almost into the ear. They tell time by flowers popping, by fog exhaling from the low places at dawn, by where the second sun is.

They have no word for *horizon* that doesn't also mean *deception*. The ridge you walk toward all morning that never comes closer is not a trick - it's where the world is tired.

### The Other Residents: What Lives in the Weakness

Nothing lives *in* the breakdown the way a fish lives in water. But the breakdown is inhabited - by afterimages that have learned to linger, by physics behaving like weather.

* In certain basins, **light pools**. Shadows fall toward the sun for a few steps. If you stand still at noon you can sometimes see your own shadow arrive a second late, hurrying to catch up to your feet. The Mat there grows in spirals.
* **Uphill water** for a yard, no more, where a creek crosses a basalt lip that has forgotten which way is down. The people put small prayer troughs there - not from religion, but to remind the water which way to go.
* The **second sun** - paler, a few degrees off, tracking yesterday's path for an hour at dusk - is not hot. Ferns do not turn toward it. But if you stare at it, you can feel the day before as a faint warmth on your other cheek.
* The sky at dusk develops **depth behind itself** - patches where the blue thins to a deeper night, and you see stars through day. On those nights the spore clouds rise higher than ever and hang motionless, as if the air has briefly become a liquid they can swim in.

The people do not worship these things. They accommodate them, the way you accommodate a damp corner of a house. You don't build there. You hang chimes there. You learn which empty courtyard hears your words before you speak, and you never make promises in it.

And over it all, the plateau itself lives in the oldest sense: it breathes. Every morning it exhales fog from every basin until you cannot see your hand; every late morning it inhales, tearing the fog to rags, rime on every leaf. Every afternoon it snows a dusting that never accumulates. Every night under stars so close they throw shadows, it settles - basalt ticking, moss tightening - a world so old it finished becoming itself an eon ago and now simply continues, green and damp and immensely quiet, doing its vegetable business under a sky that has worn thin from watching.
