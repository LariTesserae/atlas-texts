No one lives here yet in the way people mean living. Nothing *stays*.

What lives here *moves* through, because staying would mean drowning or freezing or being eaten in the open.

Start low, because everything here starts low - pressed into the rock like it’s trying not to be noticed.

**The Stitchers.**

They are the first and by mass still the most. Before any herd can exist, they have to make a kind of soil out of nothing.

In every crack where water beads and doesn't quite run off, there's moss. Not the soft forest kind. These are tight, domed pillows the size of your fist to the size of a sleeping dog, olive-black when dry, swollen bottle-green and translucent after the cloud wrings itself out. Press one and it weeps for a full minute - they are sponges first, plants second. They hold the plateau's water against the wind so it can't all sheet off at once.

Around their edges, lichen. Pale grey, powdery, still unsure of itself, stippled with those impossible orange fruiting bodies that only show true in this shadowless light. It doesn't grow *on* the rock so much as etch into it - acid-softening basalt a micron at a time, making the first dust that will one day be soil.

And in the pans - the inch-deep, foot-deep sky-mirrors with frost lace in the morning - the hair-sedge. One blade alone is nothing, thin as copper wire, shivering. In a mat they become a meadow that isn't a meadow yet. Their roots don't go into ground, there's no ground. They braid into each other, floating, anchoring only to the silt of rock flour. When the musk-oxen come they will tear whole rafts of it up, and it will regrow from fragments in a week because that's the only strategy that works here: be torn, be scattered, be everywhere again.

Under all of it, invisible, the rock flour water is alive. Every pan blooms with algae the water makes look blue but up close is brown-red, and bacteria that smell faintly of iron when you disturb them. You can see their colonies as a skin on still water, rainbow in the dim noon. They are what makes the pans not sterile.

Without the stitchers there is nothing to eat.

**The Hum.**

Despite the 0.4/3 cold - that constant just-below-freezing that lives in your ankles - the air over every pan vibrates.

Black midges. Clouds of them, each one smaller than a printed period. They don't bite. They don't need to. In this cold their whole lives are compressed into a few frantic days. They rise in vertical columns at midday when the clouddeck thins to pearl, males humming with a faint high voltage whine, females dropping like dust to lay eggs in the algal skin. Their larvae are the true population - glassy, red-gilled worms by the millions in every mat of sedge, every moss pillow. Scoop a handful of that wet "mud" and it wriggles.

They are the engine. They turn rock flour and algae into protein that can fly.

And because there are so many, the plateau is never silent, even at 1.1/3 quiet. The background is so empty that their hum carries a hundred meters, a faint crackle like static.

**The Tseepers and the Watchers.**

Where there are midges, there are the small birds. You hear the *tseep tseep* before you see them, because they move like thrown gravel.

Flocks of twenty, fifty, a hundred - snow-bunting things, grey-white with soot-tipped primaries, bodies puffed against the thin 2.9/3 air. They have to work twice as hard for every breath, so their chests are absurdly deep, their heartbeats audible if you hold one. They don't sing. Song would be wasted in wind and open scale. They seep-call, constantly, to stay together in the white.

They live entirely on the wing and the run. No nests in trees - no trees. They scrape hollows under the lee of a two-foot step where a moss pillow overhangs, line it with shed musk-ox wool caught on stone, lay speckled eggs the exact color of wet shale. Three days of still mist can kill a clutch with mold; a sudden clearing can freeze it. So they lay often, lose often, try again.

Above them, never seeming to flap, the Watchers. One, then two, then four, circling so high they are cutouts in the darker blue-grey. They are not eagles. They are paler, broader, the color of damp basalt with a lighter rump that disappears against cloud. Rock falcons, gyr-like. Their wings are built for hanging in thin air with minimal effort. Their eyes can see a midge column ripple wrong from a kilometer away, and their feet are feathered to the talons against cold. They almost never call. When they stoop, there's no scream - just a sudden absence where a *tseep* flock was, and a few feathers spiraling down to land in a pan and float.

They follow the herds, too.

**The Smear That Breathes.**

The herds are why fauna reads 2.3/3 on a plateau that looks empty from any one spot. Density 0.2/3 means if you stand still for an hour you might see nothing. If you walk for a day you realize the plateau is being lawnmowered.

They are musk-oxen the way a wolf is a dog - recognizable, wrong. Taller at the shoulder than any musk-ox that has any business living at this elevation, long-legged for wading. Their coats hang in heavy, wet dreadlocks, dark chocolate brown to iron-black, constantly dripping. Underneath, a dense qiviut so fine it never dries. They steam. Even when it's not that cold, every animal exhales a plume that hangs in the still, wet air for minutes, so a moving herd writes its passage in cloud.

A few hundred. Sometimes a thousand, strung out for a mile along a seep-step, moving north always north in this season because north is where the cloud hit last and the sedge mats are freshest. They don't have territories - there's nowhere to defend, no hill to own. They have a *front*. They eat a mat to stubble, churn the rock flour under it with splayed, dinner-plate hooves that spread on the slick stone and leave perfect, water-filled craters behind them, defecate loose and green-black and steaming, and move on. Behind them the pan is fertilized, the sedge fragments already re-rooting. They are farming in motion.

Calves are born right on the wet stone, already long-legged, already shaggy, licked dry in wind that never stops. They have to keep up within an hour or they are left - nothing hides here, everything happens out in the open on a table with nowhere to hide. Cow and calf call with low, almost subsonic moans that you feel more than hear under the midges.

Where they have passed, the plateau smells briefly of hot animal and chewed green, before the cold rinses it away.

At their flanks and far behind, at the precise distance where they stop being noticed, lope the pale wolves.

Not a pack the way forest wolves are a pack - no den, no rendezvous. Lean, long-jawed, coats the exact dirty white-grey of frost-rimed shale. In this dim flat light you can lose them at a hundred meters. They trot forever, tongues steaming, ribs showing even when fed because everything here burns calories to stay warm and oxygenated. They don't howl to proclaim. They yip, cough, whine - short contacts. They test. An old bull that stepped wrong on a slickenside and wrenched a shoulder, a calf that lagged, a pregnant cow that paused too long to drink from a seep.

When they make a kill it's not a chase - there's nowhere to chase to, no trees to cut around. It's a long, patient press until the herd decides that one animal is cheaper to lose than to defend. Then it's efficient, quiet except for the ravens - smaller, soot-black, that appear from nowhere for gut piles - and then the wolves move on, blood freezing quickly on their muzzles.

Their tracks and the herds' tracks are the only writing on this place that hasn't decided where it will be. Braided, overlapping, filling with water the second they are made. Reading them you understand the dynamic 2.0/3: this is not a still life. It's a cycle running at full speed, predator-prey-grazer-hatcher, hungry and wasteful and fast because summer at just-below-freezing is desperately short.

**And the Plateau Itself.**

The oldest inhabitant, if you count it, is the stone that is still arriving. It groans every hour - *Hrrrrrrrm* - deep in your molars as a slab the size of a county settles a millimeter. It exhales through the new eastern scarp in sudden factory-loud steam jets that howl for hours and shut like valves. Wind through fresh basalt cracks whistles at turbine pitch, Saint Elmo's fire crawling blue along every ridge before rain.

The animals ignore it perfectly. The midges don't care if the mountain is being born. The musk-oxen walk through the steam like it is weather, which it is.

For now there is no Built. No cairn, no fence, no mine adit that will hold. Erosion is still 0.3/3, embryonic, just learning to make a pothole. No human lives here because there is nothing to keep a human alive except constant motion and a coat that never dries. You are a visitor the cold will politely suggest you leave before night, when the water pans skin over again and all those sounds - drip, hum, howl, grind, distant yip, endless soft spatter of hooves through shallow water - become one mostly-quiet house that was never built for you.
