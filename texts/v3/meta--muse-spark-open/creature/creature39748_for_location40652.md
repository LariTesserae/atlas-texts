They live here. Of course they do. Wherever a table is set, something will come to sit at it.

Not many. Not ever many. On the Roof there is not room for many, and not water for many, and not silence for many. But what lives here lives *close* - layered, overlapping, within arm's reach of everything else, the way the Veil lives laced through the Barrels.

If you stand still in one of those stone alleys for a minute and let the dust settle on your shoulders, you will start to see them as the thicket does: not as visitors, but as one more growth form.

### The People of the Thicket Town

At first you might think there is no one here. That's because they are built to match.

They are human. Unmistakably human, in the way that a high-desert fox is still unmistakably a dog. Narrowed, honed, thinned out by air and light until only what is needed remains.

**The Body the Mesa Made**

You don't get big on the Roof. The children are small and stay small for a long time - 0.8/3 slow to grow, just like everything else - and the adults never get large. Four and a half to five feet is tall here. Bones are light, almost bird-hollow. Chests are deep and round, barrel-chests, because the air *is* thin and cool even while the stone underfoot is warm; every breath has to do a little extra work. They move like people who know breathing is work: no running, no sudden heaving. A quick trot down an alley to catch a child will leave an adult kneeling, hands on knees, for a full minute, steadying.

Skin: the sun should burn them, but the light is so diffused it tans instead of burns. A dusty, warm brown all over, even where clothes cover, with a faint waxy sheen. Not sweat - they hardly sweat at all. Their skin has gone a little succulent itself. If you press a fingertip to a grandmother's forearm and pull away, the pit stays for a second too long, as if there is pulp under there, not just flesh.

Mouths and eyes tell you the most.

Eyes are large. Not grotesquely so, just disproportionate, the irises almost swallowed by pupil even at noon. That dim 0.6/3 light under the Veil-curtains demands it. Colors here are washed out, so they have learned to see brightness inside leaves - the faint lumen in a Barrel leaf when you hold it up, the way a Veil beard goes silver just before it will give dew. Babies are born with a blue-grey film over their eyes, like cataract, that clears in their second year as their pupils learn to stay wide. Old people, whose eyes have gone pale with that same film again, see best of all in the twilight alleys. They do the finest stone-fitting, by touch and near-blind sight.

Mouths are wide, with thick, cracked lips and heavy cheeks. Cheek-pouches, really, though they are ashamed if you call them that. Everyone here can hold a wad of chewed leaf-pulp in one cheek for an hour, squeezing it slowly with tongue and jaw, extracting every bead of water, never swallowing the fiber. Spit is never spit. The dry fiber-ball is patted flat and laid on a roof-mat to become tinder, or chewed again by a child. Their kidneys are legendary - dark, concentrated urine passed once a day into a dust-pit where it vanishes, salt left to crust. They drink, but they do not *drink*. They *chew*. A stranger offering a cup of water would be met with confusion, and then gentle correction: you pour that on a wound, not down a throat.

Hair grows sparse and fine, bleached to the color of dust by mineral air. Most keep it cropped short or shave it entirely, because Veil snags. Ears are a little cupped, a little mobile; in a place that is 1.7/3 loud all the time - cricket trill, finch chip, glider squeak, *tok tok tok* of stone on stone, children's voices bouncing off close walls - you need to be able to turn your listening.

They don't get old quickly. They get *worn*. Faces furrow like Old Barrel bark, skin slackening in dusty folds, spines curving to keep low under the mats. Forty is mature. Sixty is ancient. An ancient's teeth are worn flat to nubs from a lifetime of chewing fiber, and they eat only the softest inner jelly, spooned to them by grandchildren.

**How They Arrange Themselves**

This is a hamlet, sixty to eighty souls at any one time, never more than a hundred. You could count houses, but you'd miscount, because a house is not a building. A house is a claim: one big Barrel - your Barrel - with your lean-to of dry-stacked slabs leaning against its north side, your roof-mat stretched from its crown to the next Barrel over, your terrace of two-stone-high dust-retaining walls where your sleeping platform and drying rack sit.

No one owns the Barrel. That would break the Rule. The Rule - Do Not Waste Pulp - is not a law with punishments. It is physiology, whispered to babies while they chew their first leaf-strip: *We close the wound with dust.* You are taught to take only the outer leaves, one at a time, with a flake-knife of basalt, and to immediately smear the pale, weeping cut with pink-grey flour-dust until it seals. A child who rips leaves for fun will not be beaten. They will be made to sit with the wounded Barrel for a whole afternoon, watching the sap bead and listening to the old ones say nothing. That is worse.

So families are not defined by blood as much as by *tending*. A hearth - *yeta* in their word, which also means "the gap between rosettes where dew collects" - is three or four adults and their overlapping children who share the dew-gourds hung from one roof-mat. Usually sisters or sisters and a brother, plus chosen partners who have been wed not with rings but with an exchange of wound-paste recipes. Men and women both wear the same dust-colored wrap, both carry flake-knives, both chew. The difference is small: women more often keep the long-term memory of which Barrel was last harvested and when; men more often do the high work of stretching Veil mats where gliders might tangle. But anyone will do either if the other is sick.

Children belong to everyone in earshot - which is everyone. Because the town is 2.2/3 dense - walls within brush-reach, voices carrying through Veil nets - a child shouting two alleys over is still *here*. They are raised collectively and, crucially, quietly. You never yell on the Roof. Sound has nowhere to go; it returns warm and near off stone. Instead they use a soft, layered creole of whispers and hand-signs, flicking fingers through Veil beards to mean *come, be careful, glider overhead, Barrel opening*. Babies who scream are swiftly picked up and pressed cheek-to-cheek, not to soothe them but to teach them resonance: we are too close to be loud.

At the center of town, where the oldest walls have sunk an inch into the stone and been buffed smooth as the mesa lip, live the Slow Ones. Not a council. Just the four or five eldest whose memory *is* the town's record. They remember when that sharp-edged wall at the eastern rim was built - thirty years ago, when the Veil there died back after a finch flock over-stripped it. They remember which terrace holds an inch more dust and will therefore sprout the copper-green beetles first after a heavy fog. They sit on the central sleeping platform most of the day, legs tucked, chewing slowly, listening to the cricket waves rise and fall. When a dispute comes - whose gourd was left uncovered, whose child took an inner leaf - they don't decide. They tell a story about the last time it happened, and the story decides.

Everyone works, because water must be coaxed, not fetched.

*The Wringers* - usually teenagers, light enough to clamber onto Barrel shoulders - go out at first light when the crown pores steam. They don't touch the Barrels. They tend the Veil. They lift great beards of the silver Tillandsia, heavy with condensed fog that never fell as rain, and wring it with a practiced twist into funnels that lead to gourds. Then they hang it back, spread carefully so it can breathe. Done badly, the Veil browns and dies. Done well, it gives a mouthful a day.

*The Pulp-Keepers* - most adults - harvest leaf edges, pound them to fiber on stone slabs, lay strips to cure, chew. The sound you hear constantly - soft, endless *thup thup* - is this. A woman's slab is an heirloom; its hollow is as deep as her grandmother's hands made it. She knows by taste which rosette is about to go sour, which Old Barrel is holding extra water after last week's pearl-grey fog-sea.

*The Beetle-Knowing Children* - all children, for a few years - are the foragers. Beetles drink first, pooling at the beaded dew on Basal rosettes. The children, bellies to warm stone, follow the copper-green lines, plucking only the three edible kinds - fat, nutty, popping with juice - leaving the two bitter reds that will sicken you. Lizards do the same. Children and lizards watch each other with perfect, mutual, mottled camouflage understanding and steal from each other constantly.

No one hunts the finches or the gliders. The finches hammer leaves open for everyone; the gliders, snagging and wringing Veil mid-air, shake more dew loose than any human hand. Instead every doorway has beetle-shell chimes that clatter in updrafts, not decoration but deterrent: *not here, soft-wing, tangle elsewhere.* The gliders learn.

At dusk - which is just a slightly dimmer dim - families gather on terraces, not around fire (there is almost nothing to burn that isn't also water) but around a shared gourd. No one pours. They pass the gourd, each tilting a few beads onto the tongue, then chewing a leaf to push it down. They talk low, or not at all, listening to the crickets' electrical trill stitch the whole Roof together in one unbroken blanket of sound. Overhead the Veil glows faintly, holding the last light. At the rim, the luminous void drops away, and if a child wanders too close the nearest adult hooks them by the wrap without looking up. The edge is not fenced. It doesn't need to be. Everyone has seen the shadow of the mesa on the haze below. Falling is not a story. It is the shape of the world.

When someone dies - rarely, slowly - they are not buried. There is no soil to bury in, only a centimeter per century of self-made dust. The body is laid in the oldest polishing hollow at the center, covered with Veil and dust, and left for the blind crickets and beetles. In a season the bones are clean, bleached pink-grey, fitted into the dry-stack of a new low wall. To live on the Roof after you are dead is to become a chock-stone, holding someone else's house level.

They do not dream of leaving. The concept has thinned out along with the air. Below is amber haze, the world that was left behind when the land stopped trying. Above is sky for a ceiling. Around is thicket, warm stone, the taste of leaf, the constant trill, the weight of near neighbors breathing.

They are not waiting to be rescued. They are not stranded.

They are what happens when a place decides to be a table, and something patient enough, small enough, and waxy enough learns how to live on crumbs of cloud.
