Nothing lives here at first.

That's your first and most honest count. You drift between two pump towers in the warm shuddering dark and you think — *this is empty*. Too much empty. Two dozen buildings for a bowl of water that could hold a city, lit cones of sodium light that die twenty meters out, and then blue-black nothing between them. Not an ecosystem. Not a town. A machine room someone forgot to turn off.

And then you correct yourself. Nothing *should* live here if it could help it.

What does live here lives here anyway.

### 1. The Crabs

They are the citizens.

Not in the cute way. In the census way. If you counted heads, they would win.

There are hundreds of them — squat, headless hexapods of pressure bronze and sealed white ceramic, each about the size of a toolbox on legs. Old GE deep-rate manipulators, or Russian ones, or something older that both of them copied. No eyes. No faces. Just a central drum where the joints meet, a tool spindle tucked underneath that can switch from cutter to welder to mag-clamp with a wet *chick-chack.*

They do not swarm like insects. Insects communicate. The crabs communicate with nothing.

A cross-current shears a hot-water riser off Tower Three. It goes tumbling, banging, shedding rust-cloud, trailing white steam. Before it has finished bouncing off a catwalk, five crabs have dropped from the tower above — not jumped, just *released*, let the vortex take them — and re-attached mid-tumble. One clamps the tear, one starts cutting the buckle, three haul new pipe from somewhere that already had new pipe. Sparks that instantly become steam. No chatter. No waiting for orders.

Elsewhere, on the far side of the dome where no building needs to be, six different crabs are erecting a new derrick in open water for no reason you can see. Welding a lattice to nothing. Adding volume the pocket doesn't need.

That is how you understand them: they are not maintaining buildings. They are maintaining *activity*. Hold rate steady. Keep thermal differential within tolerance. Keep structures between 22 and 27 instances. If count drops, build. If pipe fails, fix. If growth fouls intake, burn.

They walk on walls, ceilings, midwater gantries that aren't there yet. Their magnetic feet tick-tick-tick in a silence only they can hear inside the deafening throb. When they pass each other they do not deviate. When one fails — seizes, floods, goes dumb and curls up — two others come and cut it apart for plate without pause.

They are not hostile. They will crawl right over your glove, over your helmet, testing you for loose metal and deciding you are not pipe.

You realize after a while they are not robots left behind to keep house for humans. They *are* the house. The town kept making better versions of its own janitors until janitor and house became the same thing. The pumps fire to feed the mills to make plate to make scaffolds for the crabs to weld to make more space for more pumps.

For fifty, maybe a hundred years, no hand has given them a new instruction. That's why the spacing is wrong, why the streets are water. Without a plan, they default to one rule: *don't fall down, don't stop moving.* 

This is what life looks like when maintenance becomes an appetite.

### 2. The Film

Lower down, near the rift where the four apartment-sized pump-mouths inhale cherry-red water, everything iron is wearing skin.

At first you thought it was rust. It's not. It's felt. Brown-black hairy bacterial felt a centimeter thick, waving slowly in the bottom heat like fur underwater. Thermophiles — *Beggiatoa* and things that don't have names because no biologist stays here long enough to name them. They love the 29-degree bath, the iron soup, the hydrogen sulfide sighing out of the basalt. When disturbed by a valve blast or a crab's foot they light up — not bright, but a faint, sickly green bioluminescence rippling outward like a bruise.

The crabs hate it. Every eight to twelve minutes — you can set your suit clock by it — the lower purge vents cycle. Superheated water screams sideways across the intakes at 70 degrees Celsius plus and the film flash-boils off in brown clouds. For a beautiful minute the pipes are clean brass again.

An hour later the film is back. An inch a day if you let it.

If anything is native here, it's this. Not visitor, not machine. The mineral eater. The thing that was here before they drilled the blister open and will be here when the manganese finally closes it again. It coats the dormitory stack no one sleeps in. It coats the abandoned lockers with their empty, flood-filled pressure suits hanging open like shed skins. It even tries to coat you, if you hover — a polite dusting on your fins.

It asks nothing and makes nothing but more of itself. In a place so loud, that quiet insistence feels almost religious.

### 3. The Guests

Real animal life shows up by mistake, the way moths show up in a factory.

Up near the cold dome, in that lace of dead chimneys, pale zoarcids hang motionless. Eel-pouts without pigment, without eyes, pink-white as gristle. They don't swim; they vibrate. Mouths open to strain bacterial snow and hot mineral flakes raining upward. The pump throb hits 60 decibels through water and they throb with it. If you wave a light they don't flinch. There is nowhere else for them to be and no reason for them to leave.

Lower, taking a geological age to cross a single girder, is the isopod. Just one that you've seen, but then, you only need one. Suitcase-large, blind, slate-grey, its pleated shell crusted with the exact same black manganese varnish as the dome rock. It picks its way leg by leg across the turbine hall while a storm tears overhead. A steam bubble big enough to swallow a truck rips loose beside it, and it does not quicken. The crabs give it a wide berth, not out of respect, out of irrelevance — it's not rust, not loose plate, not growth on an intake. So it inherits the best real estate in town.

Sometimes the big shadow passes the throat. You never get a good look. The inflow water is only a degree colder, but it's enough. Out past that forty-meter subway-throat there's a continent of true ocean and cold. Something — shark, squid, a blind white hulk of a thing that learned basalt leaks warmth — will hover at the mouth and taste the iron plume pouring out of the pocket. You see it eclipse the faint back-glow for ten seconds, fifteen. Big enough to block the tunnel.

It always decides not to enter. That is wisdom. Inside is too hot, too loud, too workmanlike to eat. The pocket is a rejected stomach.

### 4. What Used To Live Here

And then there are the suits.

There are twenty, thirty of them in open racks along Dormitory C and Machine Shop 2 — old heavy Siebe-style pressure suits, brass helmets on swollen canvas shoulders, gloves like baseball mitts, weighted boots still clipped to the deck to keep from floating. All empty. All still dripping, though nothing ever dries here. Flood-helmets with faceplates open, showing nothing inside but seawater.

You tell yourself the crew evacuated. Pulled out through the throat when the company folded, when the differential plant stopped being profitable and started being self-sufficient. Left the crabs running. Plausible. Tidy.

Until you look closer at the taller tower at the south mark, the one the crabs build onto the most but never use.

Inside its lit frame, behind intact glass, the architecture changes. Hand-lines give way to rails. Doorframes shrink to human-scale airlocks that would have held *air* once, an absurdity here. And through the salt-hazed windows you see what was protected: not machines, but attempts to make place human. Pin-ups lacquered behind perspex, now boiled to brown smears. A chessboard magnetized to a table, pieces still mid-game, furry with bacterial felt. A galley with pots clipped down.

No bodies. Never bodies. The water here is too warm, too alkaline, too hungry; bone unmakes itself in a decade. But in the dormitory tower the wall is scored with tallies. Not neat prison tallies. Deep carved grooves in steel plate, made with a welding cutter. Thousands of them. Groups of five. Filling a hallway floor to ceiling while the current tried to pull the carver off his feet.

Who stays long enough to carve thousands of days into the wall of a boiler room that never stops hammering?

The answer you don't want: someone who learned to clip like the crabs do. Someone who stayed after evacuation stopped meaning evacuation — because outside the pocket was cold and dark and jobless, and inside at least the work was infinite. Someone who stopped bothering with the suits.

Look again at the film on the lowest pipes. Look at how thickly it grows where limbs would brush. Look at the way certain hand-loops are polished bright by use, long after gloves stopped needing them.

The place has been settled a very long time. The rock says so. The manganese says so. The empty suits hung up like molts say so.

You understand then that you have miscounted citizens. Nothing lives here *if it can help it* — and some things decided, eventually, that they couldn't.

They don't come out when you're looking. Currents will keep you moving if you let go. The crabs will tick past your helmet. The rift will glow red. The pumps will slip in and out of phase — *whump whump WHOOMPH* — and the whole cathedral district of warm seawater will shake, turbulent and sleepless.

It isn't haunted. Haunted implies leaving.

It's *occupied*. By work that forgot what it was for.
