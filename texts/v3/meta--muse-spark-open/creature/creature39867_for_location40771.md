They live here the way a hesitation lives in a sentence — not as residents *in* the place, but as the place pausing to hold a shape for a while.

You were told there are no builders. That is not quite right. It is more accurate to say **there is only building**, and that no part of it ever stops to be a builder.

### The Towers

Start with what you thought were buildings, because they will correct you first.

Each one is not an individual the way you are. Each is a colony that has decided to be a column. What looks like tea-stained bone and rust is not wall but body — ten to fifteen meters of self-laid laminate, thousands of years pressed thin as onion skin. Every layer is a season of still air. They do not secrete this stuff and live behind it. They *are* the secretion, alive all the way through, from the deep anchoring foot you never see — sunk down through the black salt into the damp dark the surface has forgotten — up to the fresh, wet-glass crenellations at the tip.

The tip is the only part that is truly awake.

It grows without eyes or plan, extruding frills and needles and blind arches as thin as mica because thin costs nothing when your food is dust. The air in the bowl is cool and heavy and full of loess, crystal, salt flour. A tower does not eat. It waits with a crown of cilia you cannot see until the moving air brushes them, and then it precipitates. Ion by ion. Grain by grain. The reason the new growth looks razor-sharp and unweathered is not youth — though they *are* young — but that this is how they grow when nothing opposes them: as ornament. No wind carves them. No hand trims them. They elaborate because elaboration increases surface area, and surface area is mouth.

An older tower — you will not find one here yet, but you can feel the intention in the three at the center — would eventually learn restraint. Its top would blunt and thicken, its frills would fuse into plates. It would begin to close. These have not learned that. They are adolescent spires, all declaration, still under the impression that height is the point.

They do not sense you standing in their avenues. Not as an enemy, not as prey. You are a brief, warm updraft offending the perfect dryness they farm. If you stand too close to a base for too long, the fine hairs at the footing will curl minutely *away* from the water vapor leaking out of your boots. That is the closest thing to acknowledgment you will get.

For they do acknowledge each other, intensely, but not in any way that needs sound.

Three times you will think you saw lightning, very faint, between towers. You did.

Watch the trio in the plaza — the listeners. They lean. Not from wind, not from damage. They lean like old people over a table, because the tips need to see each other's halos.

When the vault dims and the dust thickens, the edges of the towers kindle. Not light as you know it. A cold discharge, rose and bruised-blue, a finger's width off the laminate, there and gone in a breath. It is ionization. It is speech. One tower kindles, and a minute later — the delay is part of the grammar, a chemistry counting to ten — another answers. The message, if you could translate it, would not be words. It would be: *more iron in the northeast quadrant. slow your north face. I am passing surplus silica along the western vein.*

Because of the second kind of inhabitant.

### The Veins

The ankle-high causeways are not roads. Step over them; never on them if you can help it. They are external guts.

Feel one with the back of your hand without touching. It is warmer than the salt. Inside that papery tube — same laminate as the towers, but supple, alive in a different tempo — a dark brine moves. Not blood. Brine so saturated with dissolved silicates and carbonates that it is almost a liquid glass. The towers are otherwise solitary, fifty meters of polite repulsion apart, but beneath that etiquette they are all plumbed together.

In the deepest part of night-cycle, when the black pan contracts and those silent lightning-fissures race out, put your palm flat to the salt. You will feel it then, that tremor which is not sound: a slow peristalsis in the veins, pushing a pulse of brine from a tower that has too much manganese towards one near the southern rim that has too little.

That southern one is dying.

You saw it: the side slit open like a wasp nest, empty chambers inside. It is not sick. It has simply precipitated wrong for a few centuries — a little too much salt in its mix, a little micro-fracture in its foot that lets its own reservoir weep away into the void below. It cannot seal it. So it is delaminating. Layer after silent layer, tea-colored flakes as light as moth wings are lifting off its flanks even as you watch, though you cannot see them move. The wind takes them. The neighboring towers take them. The veins reverse and sip its last brine back into the network. That is rule two kept perfectly: no corpse. Just a redistribution. In a month there will be a clean circle of untouched black salt where it stood, a little paler than the rest, waiting a hundred years for a new spore to dare it.

Nothing here is wasted because waste would require abundance. Everything is accreted. Everything is re-accreted.

### The Wire Gardens

At the foot of each healthy tower is its beard of black wire.

You called them plants; forgive that. Kneel — but not on the salt — and look closer. They are not rooted. Each filament is a separate animal, or organ, or instrument; the distinction fails here. Hair-thin, knee-high, brittle as quenched glass, jet-black and matte so they disappear against the salt until your eye learns them.

They quiver without wind.

They are precipitators of a second order — more exquisite than the towers. Where a tower pulls bulk dust, these pull *specificity*. One filament fishes only for zinc, another for phosphate at parts-per-billion, tasting the air with a crown of molecular hooks you would need a lens to see. When it has gathered enough, it jerks — that sudden, perpendicular unfurling you noticed — doubling its height in a second, exposing new surface. It is growing, yes, but also offering.

Once a day a glider will come for what they've gathered.

### The Cloaks

You waited an hour and thought you were alone. Then wall became animal.

They are not cloaks, though your brain insists. They are wide and flat as a spread veil, the exact color of unlit paper so they vanish against sun-bleached laminate. They have no front, no eyes you can find, though if you saw one's underside — and you will not; they never flip — you would see a thousand hair-feet and a pale, endless mouth.

Call them the tenders. Call them the postmen. Call them the scissors.

They spend most of their lives pressed flat to a tower's flank, absolutely still, absolutely silent, grazing without biting. Their lower membrane exudes a weak acid that softens just the outermost molecular layer of laminate and drinks it, not for calories — there are no calories here — but to read it. To taste whether a tower's new frill has a flaw, whether its lead ratio is climbing too high, whether it is ready to be pruned.

And then one lets go.

It does not drop. It goes rigid and the cool, dense air catches it. This is the only real movement in the bowl that covers distance. A glider crossing the avenues is so slow you could time it with breathing: ten minutes to drift the breadth of the hamlet on a membrane you can see the vault through. No flap, no rustle. Even your heartbeat feels loud under it.

They ride the temperature gradients the towers themselves exhale. Warmish air rising off the laminate, cold air sinking over black salt creates a permanent, placeless circulation inside the bowl, a wheel of air you cannot feel but they can. They tack on it silently from tower to tower, from wire garden to wire garden.

When one lands it folds and again becomes wall. Then, with impossible delicacy, it extends a lip over a single black filament and sips. The filament shudders and goes dull, emptied of its gathered trace metal. The glider holds that wealth in a pocket of brine in its gut, carries it to the next tower vein, and regurgitates it into the system. They are the mobile part of the immobile city, the only thing here that understands *elsewhere*.

Are they separate species? Symbiont? Caste? Here those words dissolve. The towers make the filaments the way you make fingernails. The filaments make the need that summons the gliders. The gliders carry the towers' future gametes — flakes of fresh frill caught like burrs on their upper surface — from crown to crown. It is not pollination. It is mail. It is gossip made flesh.

Occasionally — once while you are there, if you are patient — two gliders will meet on the same tower face. They do not fight. They overlay, paper on paper, becoming for a moment a slightly darker patch of wall, exchanging brines and flakes in total stillness and silence. Then peel apart and glide opposite ways. If that patch were left there a century, a new tiny tower would begin to pimple up underneath it, a daughter excrescence that would eventually split cleanly and have to be connected by a new vein thrown across the salt like a thrown thread.

This is how the hamlet reproduces: not by seed, but by agreement. An average of one new tower every three hundred years. The avenues will narrow almost imperceptibly as you leave and the next visitor, centuries from now, will find thirty-nine towers instead of forty, or forty-one, and will not be able to say which is new because the black salt will have erased all sign of change.

You stand on the low rim to go and look back.

From here the whole intense, quiet argument is legible as one organism: a shallow nervous system of contracting salt and pulsing veins, a lung-field of wire gardens breathing dust, a set of stiff, ornate limbs reaching for more air, tended by pale, floating platelets.

It does not know you were here. It will not remember your footprint; the salt took it within an hour, flowing back smooth when the cold deepened. It wants nothing from you except that you not lie down and offer the water it has trained itself for an epoch not to need.

Above it, the vault has thickened again. The towers have dimmed their halos, conserving for the next brightening. Somewhere on a south-facing frill, a molecule of iron has just found its place, and the whole bowl, without a sound, is now one molecule larger than when you arrived.

Let it keep growing at that rate.
