They do not live *in* it. They *are* it.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn when you look down into the cup. You keep wanting to separate the builder from the building, the citizen from the city, the animal from its house. Here that seam never formed. What lives in the bowl is a single, layered economy of holding.

At the bottom of it all — literally underneath everything else — is the thing that made all the other things possible. Not a tree. Not a coral, though you thought coral at first and you were not wrong.

### The Weaver

The redweaver is not one plant. It is a continuous mat, a clonal colony that has been growing outward and upward for perhaps eight or nine thousand years, ever since the basalt was still warm from its birth. Where it touches stone, it dissolves it microscopically. Its holdfasts secrete a weak acid that pits the basalt, and then thread root-hairs finer than hair into the pit, anchoring and mining at once. No soil is needed because the Weaver makes its own soil out of itself: generations of waxy leaf-plates that have fallen, been chewed, excreted, and re-knit into a black, springy peat that never quite rots, because nothing here is allowed to rot quickly. To rot quickly would be to release.

In cross-section, if you cut one of the house-wide trunks — which nothing here does — you would not see rings. You would see a history of densities. The fiber is laid down where stress is felt. Where the gliders land a hundred times a day, the Weaver has thickened a buttress. Where the wind funnels over a gap in the canopy, it has grown a screen. Where a hunter wore a path along a ledge for three generations, the parapet has risen, lip-like, to keep her from falling. It remembers in wood. The older you get toward the center of the bowl, the darker and harder the growth becomes, until near the bottom the trunks are the color of dried blood and you can knock on them and hear stone.

The living skin over that skeleton is what does the work. It is not leaves as you know leaves. Each plate is a cistern. Velvety on top with a nap that catches the cold, waxy underneath to seal. At night, when those shutters in the canopy crack open to the stars, the plates cool. Before dawn, for twenty-three, twenty-four minutes, the cold air off the plateau rim pours in like invisible water and pools. The plate-hairs — each one a glassy, hollow filament tipped with a salt gland — stand up. You can see the whole city bristle at once if you are there early enough. Fog does not so much form as precipitate directly onto the city, bead by bead. Ticking. Millions of beads swelling. Then the hairs lie flat, the grooves between them capillary the water inward to the joints, and the great bladders swell.

You will find them everywhere if you know to look: translucent amber sacs at every fork and elbow of the Weaver, pulsing once every few minutes, each one a private well. The whole forest-city is a hydraulic machine made of patience.

And because the Weaver holds water, everything else can afford to be soft.

### The Middle Dwellers

The air inside the cup never moves fast. So nothing needs to be loud.

**The Tenders.** Start low, on what serves as ground — that deep, anastomosing mattress of old fiber and peat. There move the isopod things. They are not isopods, but the word fits your hand. Domed, pill-bodied, the size of a loaf of bread, with fourteen short, suede-soft feet that spread their weight so they never puncture the mat. Their backs are living gardens. Lichen and fungus are cultivated there in neat, circular plots fertilized by their own frass, weeded with nibbling mouthparts that can turn backwards over their own carapace. They are farmers, but they farm their own bodies. When they meet each other in the narrow hollow-ways between trunks, they pause and exchange spores, touching antennae that look like fern fronds. They do not fight. There is no scarcity worth fighting over, only moisture worth keeping. To injure another Tender would be to spill water.

**The Sailfall.** Above them, in the mid-atriums where the columnar trunks hollow out into cool, cinnamon-smelling rooms, live the things that hang. You saw them from the rim as pale laundry. Up close they are not bats. Their wings are not feather or membrane but two pairs of enormous, fused forelimbs covered in dense, downy scales that trap air and scent. They hang by hooked hind feet from the ceilings of the atriums, hundreds together, breathing in perfect, slow synchrony. Their eyes are covered with skin — they have no use for light. They navigate and speak by moving air. A Sailfall does not call. It *fans*. A single slow downstroke of those scaled wings pushes a parcel of warm, humid, fungus-spiced air across the chamber. Another answers. The whole colony stirs the air into letters. It is language as weather. At dusk they drop, one by one, without a sound, spreading those four veined wings — you thought they were insects, but each wing is as wide as your arm — and they do not flap. Flapping wastes water. They fall with control, steering by twisting their bodies, gliding from balcony to balcony to sip from the Weaver's bladders with a needle proboscis that leaves no scar. They never land on stone. Stone is too hot, too sharp.

**The Needle Hunters.** Where there are gardeners and fanners, there are pruners. They are the only sharp things that are allowed to be soft. Low and perfectly flat, the color of old wax, with a head that is all chelicerae and no face, they move across vertical walls at the speed of a shadow's crawl. So slow you doubt you saw movement until you blink. They do not chase. They wait at the joints where the bladders pulse and where the beetle-things come to graze. The beetle-things — lacquered emerald, dumb, abundant — scrape the waxy bloom with rasps and get fat on it. The Hunter extends a single, glassy filament from between its jaws, a thread of venom that hardens on contact with air, and tacks the grazer to the wall. Then it feeds at leisure, through a straw, returning every drop of fluid to its own bladders. Nothing drips. Even predation here is a closed loop. If you watch long enough you will see a Hunter after a kill carefully wipe the wall clean with forelimbs feathered like small brooms, reclaiming even the smear.

There are others — the silver ferns that are not ferns but colonies of animals that live like ferns, the lantern bladder-flowers that hold a single, buoyant seed aloft for years before popping — but they follow the same law.

### How They Hold Together

No rivers. No rain. No song. So how does a forest-city without flow stay a city?

It does it with memory and touch.

Because nothing can afford to evaporate a scent plume or shout, information is passed by contact and by architecture. The black-green cables that bind the towers are not a second plant. They are the Weaver's nerves, or as close as a plant gets. When a Tender finds a particularly good patch of lichen, it does not return quickly. It dawdles, pressing its feet harder. The pressure stimulates the fiber beneath to exude a faint sweet resin. The next Tender follows the sweetness. In a week, the Weaver has grown a faint ridge along that path. In a year, a low wall. In ten years, a covered arcade. The city literally grows the roads its citizens prefer.

The Sailfall do the same with air. Where their fanning is most frequent — in the great central atrium where three trunks fuse into a dome — the walls have grown porous, pitted with hundreds of breathing pores that humidify and cool the chamber, making it the nursery where their blind young pulse in clusters like pale fruit.

Even death is held. When a Tender dies — which it does rarely, folding its feet under itself like a closing hand — it does not bloat or stink. The Weaver is there within hours. Fine white root-hairs emerge from the mat and suture themselves into the joints of the carapace, drinking the body dry from the inside out, leaving a perfect, empty dome that will become a child's shelter or a fungus garden. No water escapes to the air. No calcium is wasted on the basalt.

They have learned the shape of sharpness, too. Because the bowl is still so young its rock has never been rounded, every creature that walks on it would be cut if it were not prepared. So nothing has bare skin. The Tenders' feet are suede. The Hunters' bellies are paved with overlapping chitin tiles. Even the Sailfall's wings are scaled. The only soft, exposed flesh in the whole cup is on the inside of the bladders, and on the undersides of new leaves still curled shut, where the Weaver keeps its own vulnerability folded inward, as you would cup water in your hands.

At night, when the leaf-shutters open that hand's breadth and the column of warm, resinous air rises straight up into the hard stars, the whole bowl exhales as one animal. You can feel it on the lip if you lie flat: a breath no warmer than skin, smelling of cinnamon and old stone, carrying on it not a sound, but the faintest pressure of many wings fanning, many feet stepping, many bladders filling — slow — slow — slow —

It is not silent because it is empty. It is silent because it is full, and has learned that to keep what little the sky gives you, you must never, ever spill it.

You came looking for a forest, or a town. What you found was a cupped hand that learned to drink fog, and then filled its palm with everything that could learn to be quiet enough to live there.
