Nothing lives here at first.

That is your first, honest impression, and for a long time — hanging on that rusted ladder with the wind cut off above you and the four-count beating below — you believe it. The basalt is naked where you can see it. The air smells like hot iron and old electricity. The lamps are too dim and too yellow to be a sun for anything. There is no water in any form you recognize as water. No drip, no film, no breath-fog that stays. You were told there would be nothing alive.

And then you see that nothing is not empty.

What lives here lives the way rust lives — not as a thing placed on the rock, but as a condition the rock has entered into. It does not sit *on* the cylinder. It *is* the soft lining of it.

Up close it stops being a color and becomes a texture, and then a population, and then — if you stay long enough to let your eyes adjust past "moss" and "lichen" and "fern" — something else entirely.

Each individual is hardly an individual at all. A hair-thin filament, black-olive and matte, stiff as wire-wool, rooted without roots. No leaf. No stem that means anything. Just a wire that grows because the air tells it to. At its tip, swollen like a gland, is a bulb no bigger than a salt grain, glassy, amber-caught — a nodule of precipitated metal. Iron. Copper. Manganese salts wrung straight out of the dry exhaust. That is its leaf and its mouth and its fruit. It doesn't photosynthesize. Light is incidental to it, a stain. There is not enough sky for that, and it wouldn't want it.

It eats the smog.

The four bellows — THUD-drag, THUD-drag — do not ventilate. They churn. They beat the same dead air over and over, warm, sulfur-laced, full of magnetite dust ground off pistons that have been running for decades. Anywhere else that dust would fall and be inert. Here it is food. The filament splits the fume the way other plants split water. It takes the metal out of suspension and lays it down as more of itself. Dust falls, is caught in the felt, is incorporated. The bulb grows heavier, clouds, seeds a new filament from its side with a soft, chemical click you will never hear over the beat.

Millions of them do this at once. So many that they cease to be hairs and become a pelt. A deep, sooty carpet thick enough to swallow sound, then amplify it. Put your hand on the railing-ridge under the felt and you feel it not as cold metal but as a faint, continuous shiver. The whole mass breathes with the pistons. When a bellows exhales its gust of hot, iron-smelling wind, the felt ripples. Not blown — *answering*. The filaments lean into the pressure wave.

Because this is the other thing it does besides eat. It listens.

It grows toward vibration, not light. That is why at first you thought it was lushest by accident. It isn't. The densest towers, knee-high, swaying though there is no draft except the machine's own exhale, are crowded right on the seams of the bellows housings where the roar is most physical. Each is a plume of filaments braided together by their own static, all leaning into the THUD like grass into prevailing wind.

And where the suspended control booth hums — that transformer buzz that has moved from your ears into your teeth — it has done something stranger. It has grown an antler. You can only call it that. A branching, velvet-black fractal that has left the wall entirely and hangs in empty air, dividing and dividing again into finer and finer tines, each tipped with a bright nodule. It is not climbing. It is not reaching. It is an ear. A lung's cilia made huge. It is the shape vibration takes when something has learned to precipitate itself perfectly in time with a 50 hertz drone. If you put your finger near it without touching, the finest tips will twitch toward the heat and tremor of your hand, tasting the different noise you make.

There are no animals here. That is not an absence you have to infer. You can peel back a slab of the felt — it comes away like industrial insulation, with a soft tearing and no bleeding, no smell but hot dust — and find nothing underneath but clean basalt with DEPTH and DANGER and PSI stenciled and half-overgrown. No mites. No springtails curled in the mat. No moths stupidly orbiting the sodium lamps. No spiders waiting for moths. The cold is too dry, the air too metallic. Even the bacteria that should live on everything have been edited out; the filters that were meant to keep the machine dry kept everything else out too, until only this one kingdom learned how to live on dryness itself.

So it has no predator. No parasite. No competition. It has reached equilibrium the way the machine has. That is what is most alive and most unsettling about it: it does not spread anymore. It does not colonize. It has already filled every inch that can be filled — ladder rungs, cables, the roof of the booth, the swallowed catwalk — and now it simply maintains. A filament dies, a neighboring bulb buds to replace it with exact precision. No spring growth. No die-off. No seasons. A held chord.

It is, in a strict sense, one thing. Not a forest. A single, distributed body that has turned the entire cylinder into its shell, its chest cavity. The bellows are not its environment. They are its weather, its tide, its heartbeat. It precipitates out of their breath, and trembles when they beat, and listens to them with its antlers in the dark.

If you stay until your ears ring, you understand that you are not the witness this place was waiting for. It has been witnessing itself, loudly and without change, with no water and no need for water, for longer than you have been alive — a hidden lung tucked into the summit, inhaling nothing but its own iron breath, exhaling nothing but more of itself.

And it keeps doing it after you climb back up the ladder into the wind that carries no moisture at all.
