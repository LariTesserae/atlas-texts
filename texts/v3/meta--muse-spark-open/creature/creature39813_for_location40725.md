They live here as stone lives - which is to say so slowly and so largely you have to be taught to see them at all.

Nothing else could.

### The Hearthbacks

There are no people here. No herds, no flocks, no packs to count. There is only one kind of animal that matters, and it occurs in the quantity that weather occurs.

From a distance you will mistake them for geography.

Each one is low, long, and terrible in scale — forty to ninety meters along its spine, twelve to twenty across at its widest, and never more than six high. A living barrow, an oblong hill with rounded ends. Their backs are not backs. Over centuries they accrete a carapace from the plain itself: plates of basalt and magnetite and slag, grown together, cracked and re-sealed, the same dark patina as every other stone here. Lichen colonizes them - that same black, circular lichen five feet across, thick as cured hide - and it does not seem to bother them. Snow collects on them. Fog pools against them. From half a mile away in that shadowless dusk-light, they are just another of the smooth hummocks, another knuckle under the skin of the peneplain.

Until you notice that the hummock is seated too perfectly in its saucer.

And that it breathes.

#### How to see them breathe

They do not have heads. They do not have legs in any way your body would recognize. What they have is a seal.

When a Hearthback chooses a basin — one of those broad, shallow saucers a mile across and thirty feet deep with the softly terraced rims — it settles itself until its ventral membrane makes a complete, airtight kiss with the stone rim. The terraces, you understand now, are not erosion. They are *levees*. Concentric rings of precipitated iron-oxide snow, of silica thread, of black glass grit, laid down year after year, century after century. The animal fits itself to its work-floor like a lid on a crucible.

Once sealed, it goes to work for years. For decades.

You cannot see the work. You can feel it. If you kneel three hundred yards out — and you learn not to go closer; the warm ground will burn through your boots — you put your palm to the stone and it hums. A subsonic, patient warmth comes up through basalt. Underneath that vast body, the stone is being cooked.

This is what they eat: cold.

More precisely: they eat the only things this high, cold, damp table reliably provides.

1.  **Water as a habit.** There is no river, no lake. But every relict braid-channel holds a black seam of frost. Every morning the whole plain sweats condensation that beads and runs for an hour before freezing. Fog pools six feet deep and flat as milk in the saucers. Three inches down, the ground is always damp and smells of iron. The Hearthback draws this in through countless cilia and pores along its underside, a slow inhalation that takes twenty minutes. You can see the fog near one thin and vanish as if drunk.

2.  **Dust as ore.** The wind that arrives here has traveled a thousand miles without interruption. It arrives tired and quiet and full of fine glacial rock-flour - basalt ground to powder, salts, iron, trace nickel. Over a decade, a saucer will collect millimeters of it. The animal vacuums it, filters it, holds it.

Inside, there is a furnace. Not metaphor. An organ that is a kiln.

Weirdness 1.1/3, remember? Just enough to feel the rules are off. The animal is not hot-blooded. It *is* hot. Its core runs at basalt-melting temperatures, sustained by some slow exothermal chemistry we have no name for - chemosynthesis that learned metallurgy instead of photosynthesis. It takes in cold damp and cold dust and boils the stone beneath it. The basalt under its belly vitrifies to glass. The iron sweats out of the rock in branching, root-like ingots. Volatiles sigh out as mineral smoke.

And then, on a cycle so slow you must time it with a stone to believe it — about one every forty minutes — the vents open.

#### The Exhalation

Along each flank, two to three rows of vents. Not mouths, not nostrils. Kiln-portholes, lipped with living ceramic, opening with infinite slowness. You have to watch a full hour to be sure you saw one move. There is no roar. In this thin, cold air sound itself is suppressed to 0.0/3; even a factory makes no noise if it is large enough and patient enough.

What comes out is a quiet, heavy sigh of steam and sulfur and hot iron, thirty feet high, dark as storm-smoke. It cools instantly in air that never warms. It precipitates and falls again as a fine, red-black snow: iron oxides and silica threads, still warm when they land. That is the ring. That is the terrace being built one silent snowfall at a time.

At what passes for night — that half-shade darker, stars never coming through the pearl-grey luminous ceiling — the vents glow. Not bright. Dull kiln-red from within, illuminating only the steam itself. From any rise of ten feet — and ten feet helps here, where scale is 2.8/3 and the horizon is a straight line hiding the curvature of the world — you can count them. Dozens. Hundreds to the true horizon. Each a soft lantern. Each alone. From orbit the plateau would look like a circuit board breathing, softly smoking, each light a foundry, each foundry an animal.

They pay each other no mind. No calls, no displays, no territorial shoves. There is no need. The plain is vast and the resource — damp, dust, stone — is everywhere evenly. Two will sit a mile apart for a century and never acknowledge one another except as faint extra warmth on the wind.

#### A Life at Table Scale

They move, but you will likely never see it.

Perhaps once a decade, once a generation, an individual breaks its seal. The sound is a long, soft *crack* as vacuum releases, as glass un-sticks from belly. It drags itself — not walking, but peristaltic, like a vast slug on a foot that is half living muscle and half molten glass — to the next saucer, or the next relict river trough, leaving behind a polished scar twenty feet wide and hundreds of miles long if you trace its life. The troughs you thought were dry rivers are in part their roads, polished round by bellies.

Where they were, they leave gifts without intention. This is the Tech 2.0/3 without builders, without building. In the center of the abandoned work-floor the stone is refined: glassy, crazed with cooling cracks, veined with fresh seams of native iron that branched as they sweated out, now cooling into ingots like metallic roots. Slag glints dimly everywhere. Not litter. The waste-product of a continent-sized, planet-old smelting that has been running since before mammals. The sparse vegetation knows it. The glassy black moss like frozen wire grows thickest at the edge of old floors where the iron snow has weathered to rich grit. The flat mat-shrubs — those waxy, ankle-high things that grow outward for decades, a fraction of an inch a summer — hug the vitrified stones that hold the day's faint heat a little longer. Even lichen prefers the slag.

They live a very long time. You can read it in the carapace. The plates thicken, darken, are overgrown. An old one is indistinguishable from bedrock except for the faint heat and the perfect levee around it.

They die rarely and without drama. A furnace cools. The vents stay closed. Snow does not melt on its back that spring. Lichen closes over the portholes. In a century it is a true hummock, a pillar that harder stone resisted — except this pillar is slightly warmer than the others for a few hundred years yet, and if you split it you would find concentric rings of slag and bone and magnetite inside, like a geode that once breathed.

How do they make more? Slowly. Embarrassed by scale, like everything living here. Every few decades, when the iron snow is thick, a Hearthback will exhale something heavier: a dense, hot droplet of iron-rich slag the size of a truck, shot a few yards from a vent, landing with a hiss that steams for days. It cools to a black, pitted boulder. Condensation beads on it. Lichen finds it. And inside, insulated by its own hot glass, something divides. Ten years later the boulder cracks. Something the size of a desk, soft and glowing, seals itself to the stone and begins its first tiny saucer, its first pale ring.

No one tends them. No one harvests the iron roots. There is no product, no shipment, no purpose you can point to. That is the slight wrongness here, the quiet weirdness: industry as digestion. They take the plain in, pass it through themselves, and exude a slightly more ordered version of the plain. Stone becomes glass becomes iron becomes stone again when frost shatters it. The atmosphere above the plateau is a half-degree warmer, a half-shade hazier than it should be, kept that way by millions of quiet exhalations. The planet breathes out through them.

If you stay here long enough — and the cold that has settled into the stone will try to settle into you — you will stop looking for animals and start looking for warm places in the stone. You will time your own breathing to theirs without meaning to. In for twenty minutes, hold for an age, out in a silent cloud that turns to snow and falls straight down in the still air, because there is no wind at ground level inside a saucer, only the faint tick of condensing steam freezing and falling, the soft *tink* of a cooling iron seam contracting somewhere a mile away in the dusk that lasts for months.

That is who lives here. Not on the table. As the table. A dispersed, silent, continent-wide organism that learned, very long ago, that the best way to survive on top of the weather is to become a slow furnace and gently smelt the world.
