What lives here is not *from* here.

Nothing is. There is no native. There is only what was brought down, what blew down, what leaked down — and what has learned to stay.

### The Ones Who Work

They live here because someone has to be within shouting distance of the pumps.

At first you don't see them so much as you see the proof of them — a hard hat left on a wet rail, a cigarette coal moving in a dark catwalk, laundry that will never be dry steaming on lines between hab-modules. Then your eyes adjust and you realize the valley has a population density like plankton: sparse if you count by volume, overwhelming if you count by wall-space.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with ethnicity and everything to do with light. A sodium-amber pallor, damp, slightly translucent at the temples. Their faces are chapped raw not by wind but by air-movement — that constant, cold gyre off the forges — and their hands are always a little pruned, a little black under the nails with concrete dust and grease that won't wash out in cold water. They wear too many layers, all synthetic, all dark, all beaded with water. Parkas over coveralls over thermals, boots with felt liners that squelch. Everything has a hood. Everything has a zipper that has failed and been replaced with wire.

No one ever quite dries off here, and after a few months you stop trying. You learn to live damp.

They are young, mostly. This place hasn't been here long enough to make old people. The ones who have been here longest have a particular cough — not sick, just wet — from breathing aerosolized concrete and cold steam for years. They have tinnitus from the pile-drivers. They have learned to shout without seeming angry and to sleep through strobing sodium light and vent-screams. They have learned to tell time not by clocks but by maintenance cycles: when the big fans spool down for de-icing, when the sump kick goes *WHUNG* under the floor, when the welding bays spark and throw that brief false noon across the vault.

They don't live *in* this valley so much as *on* it, like lichen. Their hab-modules are clipped to the stepped retaining walls in dense terraces, identical, temporary-looking despite being home. Inside: a hot plate, a bunk, a condensation trap made from a cut drum, photographs taped to wet concrete and curling. Outside: boot scrapers, shrines to lost gloves, an endless, low-level war against mold with bleach and a wire brush. Nobody decorates for long. Anything finished will be demolished, or will grow fur.

Children are rare but not absent. When they are born here they are loud — their cries have that terrible cathedral reverb — and they grow up knowing how to listen for a pressure valve before it blows, knowing not to touch the rebar whiskers, knowing that you never, ever put your hand in the culvert water to fish for that pale flash you saw. They throw bolts into the sheet-flow to hear them clatter away for a hundred meters.

They have rituals that aren't written down:

Tap a pipe before you lean on it — to know if it's hot.
Leave the dead lamps alone — someone has already logged them, light is allocated.
Never stand still on grating if you can help it — the cold comes up through your soles.
When the air stops moving, move.

They think they are here to build this place. In truth, they are here to keep it from finishing.

### The Tenants

Nothing was invited down here except people and tools. Everything else is a trespasser that succeeded.

Low and tenacious: the moss. Black-green, almost black, furred thick as carpet in every joint where light reaches for more than an hour a day. Lichen like spilled paint. Slime that glows faintly yellow when you scrape it, because of course the runoff has minerals in it. The crews burn it off with torches on Tuesdays and it is back by Friday, a little thicker.

Higher but just as desperate: the birch-things. They shouldn't be called trees. They are wands, antennae, white-barked and shivering, rooted in two centimeters of silt on a gantry that was never supposed to collect silt. Their leaves are the color of skim milk, veined, translucent enough to see light through. They rattle. They do not grow so much as *persist* — pruned by the scalding dump of a pressure release, or flash-frozen when the intake fans blast arctic air from the surface. In spring — which here means "when a vent alignment shifts" — they throw seeds like dust, and most of that dust dies on wet iron.

Even higher: the rats. Or what were rats. Generations in low light and pure condensation-water have bleached them, thinned their coats to a suede. Their eyes are smaller. Their whiskers are longer to feel pipe-vibration. They run the superstructure like electricians, along cable trays and steam lines, rarely touching the ground where humans walk. You hear them more than see them: a tick-tick-tick across corrugation, a plop into culvert water.

In the culvert itself: the fish. Blind, white as the inside of a wrist, fat with oil. Carp or koi or tilapia once, now something else. Someone tipped a bait bucket or a cleaning tank three years ago and the valley made a river out of a drain and gave them a life. They live on hot coolant runoff and lost skin and rust flakes. The crews swear the biggest ones are the length of an arm and know the schedule of the pumps and hide when the flow surges. No one eats them. No one needs to test them to know why.

And for a few weeks at a time: birds. Pigeons that fell down the main intake like stones, then caught the warm updraft off the forges and remembered how to fly. They wheel under the vault in slow circles, impossibly high, cooing swallowed instantly by fan-roar. They land on girders and huddle and watch the welding lightning. Then one day the draft shifts and they find the way out, or they don't.

All of them share one trait: they live on condensation.

### The True Residents

But the things that *really* live here aren't organic.

The valley has inhabitants older than any person posted here, even though the concrete is still curing. They were installed, not born, but they have metabolisms now.

The House-Sized Fans on the vault ribs: they breathe. Slow inhalation for twenty hours, a shuddering cough when ice is shed, a percussive *whup-whup-whup* that vibrates the catwalks and your sternum. If they stop, the cold pools and the low terraces die. The crews oil them like wounds.

The Sump Pumps under the grating: they drink. Forty of them in series, hammering black water up a kilometer of riser to spit it back onto the frozen surface. They have a heartbeat — normal, normal, normal, MISSED BEAT-*WHUNG* — that everyone within earshot unconsciously times their steps to. When one fails, the sheet-flow rises a centimeter and the rats move higher and someone runs with a wrench.

The Forge-Gut deep down-valley, where you feel heat before you see it: it digests. It takes in rebar and concrete and diesel and outputs warmth and carbon monoxide and sparks that fall like snow. Its exhalations drive the weather. Its warmth is what keeps the entire wet lung from freezing solid.

The Pipes themselves: they sweat, weep, rain. They are the sky. They make the drizzle that never stops, the mineral beards on the ceiling, the puddles that are never the same puddle twice. The water cycle here is closed but leaky, a circulatory system with edema at every joint. Put your ear to a column and you can hear it trickle inside the concrete itself, like blood in a vein.

These are the permanent residents. Humans rotate in and out on contracts. Moss is scraped away. Rats die. Even the stunted birches will be cut when they threaten a gantry.

But the fans will turn until their bearings seize. The pumps will hammer until the power dies. The water will keep moving because if it stops, it freezes, expands, and splits the world — and the world, being young and still soft at its seams, would let it.

Stand still long enough on that maintenance road, with the amber pools stretching to infinity and the vault dripping its thirty-story rain, and you can feel it: you are not the life in this place.

You are the maintenance crew *for* the life in this place.

The valley is alive. It just happens to be made of concrete and steel and moving air and moving water — and for now, it has decided to keep you.
