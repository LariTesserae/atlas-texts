They don't live *in* the Hollow. They *are* the Hollow — a second geology laid over the first.

If you stand still long enough, they will try to make you part of it.

### The Light That Eats Stone

Start at the bottom, because everything here does.

The first resident is not an animal at all. It is a crust. A bacterial-stromatolitic felt in sulfur-yellow, iron-white, and rust-blood ochre that paints every surface that stays damp for more than an hour. You thought the light was coming from somewhere above. It isn't. It's coming from *under your feet*.

*Phothelium lithophage*, if you needed a name for something that has never needed one. It is not plant, not fungus, not lichen, though it borrows from all three. It grows directly into the fresh basalt and limestone, exuding acids that pit the stone, then metabolizing what it frees — hydrogen sulfide from still-cooling veins, ferrous iron, dissolved salts in the ceiling-sweat. As a waste product it sweats photons. Not bioluminescence as a signal. Bioluminescence as exhaust.

In the wide dome where the roof lifts a kilometer, the crust is a continuous meadow of light, an inch thick and humming — literally humming, a faint 60-hertz buzz of chemical work you feel in your molars. Up close it breathes. The surface pulses, contracts, exudes beads of clear water that instantly bead with larvae. Where you scuff it with your boot, it goes dark for a few minutes in a perfect boot-print, then re-knits, brighter at the edges, as if annoyed.

Nothing eats the crust so much as *farms* it. Everything else grazes it the way cattle graze grass, except here the grass glows and the cattle are blind.

### The Shale-Sliders and The Runners

Layer two: the Shale-Sliders.

Lay your palm flat on the light-crust and one will eventually flow over it. Hand-sized, oval, flat as wet slate, with no front or back that you can find until it moves. Their dorsal side is matte black and gritty — actual stone dust cemented into living armor. Their ventral side is a ciliated, luminous foot that leaves a snail-trail of brighter crust behind it. They move an inch an hour, scraping, digesting, excreting fresh light. There are millions of them. The entire floor is tiled in them, edge to edge, occasionally overlapping like shingles. When a seep opens, they converge in a slow black tide, pile three deep, and drink with their whole underside. When a shadow falls, they freeze.

And on top of *them* — because there must always be a top — are the Runners.

You hear them before you see them. A dry, ceaseless *skitter* like rice poured on paper.

They are the first thing you'd call an animal and be correct. Forearm-long, centipede-lean but not centipedes — twenty, thirty legs, each jointed backwards, each tipped with a glass hair that tastes the air. Their bodies are translucent, the color of skim milk, and you can see straight through to the gut: a dark, moving cord of crust and slider-flesh and mineral grit. No eyes. No need. They navigate by antennae as long as their bodies, flicking ten times a second, tasting vibration and ammonia and heat.

They do not pack-hunt. They do not need to. They *flow*. In braids and rivers around your ankles, a living current that parts and reforms. One Runner is skittish, rearing back from your boot. A hundred Runners is a fluid that finds the lowest path toward water. A thousand is a fabric that can cover a fresh pool in seconds and leave it dry, the light-crust underneath polished clean.

If you kneel, one will run over your hand without breaking stride. Its feet are warm. Its belly leaves a faint phosphorescent smear on your skin that fades in an hour.

### The Drifters

The air belongs to something else entirely.

Above the warm floor, in the shimmer where stone-heat meets ceiling-damp, the Drifters inflate.

They are not insects. No wings that flap — no muscle could sustain it in this still, heavy air. Instead, each is a papery envelope the size of a dinner plate or a bedsheet, translucent, ribbed like a leaf skeleton, with a dark knot of organs dangling underneath like a clapper. They make their own hydrogen and hot air as a metabolic byproduct, puff themselves up, and let the Hollow's slow, circular wind take them. Two wingspans across, silent, drifting like ash from a fire that never goes out.

Up close under the dome-light they are beautiful. You can see the light through them. You can see the faint tracery of their veins carrying glowing fluid. They sail for hours, bumping the invisible ceiling with a soft *tink* like a wineglass touched with a fingernail, then exhale, crumple, and settle to the crust to feed — extending a frilled proboscis to suck bacterial slime, wrinkling like a deflated lantern.

When the ceiling sweats and the sudden rain falls — *plink plink plink* on hot basalt — the Drifters all exhale at once and drop, covering the floor in a brief, rustling snow. Then they drink, inflate anew, and rise.

At night — if there were night — they would be stars. Here, they are just the rest of the light, moving.

### The Coils and The Builders

Those are the small, the numerous, the constant. But the Hollow also grows large things, quickly, carelessly.

In every wash where rust-water lingers for more than a day — that twenty-meter cut through cinder that ends in sucking sand — lives a Coil. As thick as an oil drum, as long as a man, segmented like a terrible intestine, the color of wet rust because it *is* wet rust, iron precipitated into its skin. It has no head, only two equal ends, each a frilled, toothless mouth that opens like a camera aperture. It rolls. It unrolls. It anchors one mouth in the mud and siphons, filtering water, bacteria, stray Runners, mineral floc, then reverses and rolls a meter farther. When two Coils meet, they do not fight. They roll over each other in slow, steaming wrestling, exchanging films of microbes, and continue. When the wash dries — and it always dries — the Coil burrows straight down into the cinder, seals itself in a mucus chrysalis, and waits for the next sweat. You can walk over a field of them and not know it until the ground sighs under you.

On the half-pillars, the stuttering columns of taffy-stone, lives the only thing here that *builds* on purpose.

Colonies. Superorganisms. Each individual is smaller than your thumb, soft, white, eyeless, with jaws too big for its body. Alone it is nothing. Together — hundreds, thousands — they are masons.

They climb a pillar-tip where the mineral drip is still slow and make a chimney. Not a home. A lung. A spit-tower of chewed basalt dust, gypsum, and their own luminous saliva that hardens into a porous ceramic. Inside, air is drawn up by heat differential; moisture condenses on the cool inner walls. The chimney grows a foot an hour. A foot taller every time you look away. Crooked, lace-like, steaming.

Then the seep shifts, or a Coil thrashes too close, or the tower gets too tall and its own weight cracks it — and it falls.

And in seconds the colony becomes something else: a demolition crew. They swarm the rubble, carry each grain away in their jaws, eat the saliva-mortar for its remaining organics, and start again three meters over. Nothing lasts. The valley floor around the pillars is littered with the ghost-rings of a hundred failed towers, already being tiled over by Shale-Sliders.

It is the only architecture here that isn't yours.

### The Ones Who Left The Wall

And then — scattered like an afterthought, because they are — the ones who use hands.

You will not see them at first. You will see that the straight line of stacked basalt shards diverting a seep into a basin is *too* straight. That the windbreak leaned against the half-pillar, made of pried stone rods lashed with dried gut-cord and draped with cured Coil-hide — still faintly luminous — is *too* intentional. That the hammerstone with the finger-groove worn smooth, the volcanic-glass flake hafted to bone with sinew, the pecked basalt bowl cupped to catch condensation, were all made by a thumb that opposes.

They are human. Or were, recently enough to matter.

Not the Hollow-born monsters of a deep cave story. No pale eyes, no extra joints. Just people. Small, lean to the point of starvation, then well-fed for a season, then lean again. Skin stained yellow-ochre by the crust-light they sleep under. Hair cropped short with that glass flake to keep Runners from nesting in it. Feet calloused thick as Slider-shells from walking on tephra and cinder without shoes.

They are nomads of the mineral weather. They do not have a village because the Hollow does not allow villages. They follow water that climbs uphill along magnetic seams and then vanishes into cracks. When a seep opens, they stack a low wall — waist-high, dry-laid, no mortar — to coax it into a basin for a day or a week. They lay gut-cord snares for Runners, split them and dry them over warm stones where they curl like shrimp. They scrape light-crust and boil it down to a sour, salty porridge that keeps the glow in your teeth for hours. They render Coil fat into lamp-oil they don't need for light. They do not farm; farming implies staying.

Their shelters tell you how they think: three walls, never four, so the Runner-flow can pass through without burying them. Roof of hide weighted with stones, not tied, so when the deep *CRACK* of a cooling ceiling slab announces a new shape, they can lift it and move in an hour. The fire pit blackened with fine white ash — they do burn, when they can find something dry enough, mostly abandoned chimney ceramic and dried Drifter leather — is always on the *downwind* side, so the smoke doesn't drive the grazers away.

That single green-verdigris copper hook hammered into the wall to hold a waterskin is the only metal in the whole valley. They brought it from above. Or their parents did.

You find one half-finished wall, cinder footprint half-erased by that tide around your boots, and you understand: they were here this morning. They left because the seep moved. They will be back when it moves again. Or they won't. Someone else will find the wall and finish it or pull it down for the stones.

If you stay, you will become one of them. Not by transformation. By necessity. You will learn to read the ceiling-sweat, to listen for the hiss of water hitting hot stone, to step *with* the Runner current instead of against it. You will learn not to build to last.

Because in the Hollow, to live is not to conquer or to hide.

It is to *occupy* — briefly, brightly, noisily — before you are covered, and to be ready to occupy the next damp place that opens.

Around your ankles the tide flows, the air tinkles with colliding Drifters, the crust hums, and far off another slab cracks and somewhere a new seep sighs open in the warm, raw stone, and everything — Runners, Sliders, Coils, Builders, and the people who learned their rhythm — turns, as one, toward the water.
