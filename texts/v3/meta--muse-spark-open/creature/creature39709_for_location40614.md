There is no one living in the Under-Cistern.

Not anymore. Not in the way that *living* means a bed and a name on a roster and going home at the end of a shift. 

What lives there instead is something that has learned how to live *like the room does* — slow, close to the floor, out of the light, patient to the point of stubbornness.

At first you think it's empty.

### The Ones Who Stayed By Becoming Part Of It

If you sit long enough that the sweat starts to run down your spine and pool in the small of your back, you will realize you are not alone in the hot dark. You just haven't calibrated for what counts as a resident here.

**1. The Attendant**

He is still here. Or, an attendant-shaped thing is still here.

He is not at a desk. There is no desk left. He is wearing what was once navy coveralls, now the exact color of dust-felt and pipe rust because they have been dyed by the same air for thirty years. The knees have gone shiny. The name patch over his heart has been bleached blank by heat.

He does not patrol. He *is stationed*, the way a valve is stationed. In the hottest hour he sits on an overturned bucket between two of the tram-car boilers where the catwalk makes a little pocket, because there the draft — the ghost of a draft — moves over his neck. He leans forward with his hands on his thighs. He watches the radium needles tremble above ZERO. 

He is very still. Too still for a man, until he isn't — then his movement is sudden, decisive, practiced: a quarter-turn on the table-sized wheel, no more, even though the pressure doesn't want it. A rag wiped under a cold pipe to catch beads before they fall and hiss. He knows every *tok... tok* by ear. When the drip train into the sump stops, his head lifts.

He doesn't see well anymore. The dark has taken that from him, or the orange caged bulb has. His eyes are always half-closed against the heat shimmer. He navigates by handprint — you will find them everywhere once you start looking, smoothed places on asbestos wrap, polished patches on iron where bare skin has worn away dust for decades.

He breathes shallowly. He drinks from the sump with a tin mug on a chain. He has a tin of something — crackers, maybe — tucked behind a fuse box whose door hangs open. The ants know it.

He is not a ghost. He sweats. He coughs once every twenty minutes — a dry, brick-dust cough he doesn't seem to notice. If you shine your phone light at him he will turn away, not from fear but from irritation, like an animal in a burrow. If you speak, he will not answer in words. He will make a soft *tssk* sound with his tongue against his teeth, which means: *don't touch the hair-thin copper. it's singing.*

He was meant to leave when the hamlet above stopped needing a heart. He discovered that you can reduce your needs until you have as few as the room does: water, warmth, a little motion. He achieved equilibrium. Erosion 2.2 and him, together. He will look exactly like this for another fifty years, and then the bucket will be empty and no more worn than it is now.

He is not the owner. He is kept.

**2. The Pilgrims**

They were here before him and will be here after.

A single-file line of pale ants, so slow you can watch one leg lift and replace itself. They are almost translucent from never being in sun. They come up through a crack where poured iron plate failed and was patched with concrete and failed again, and they traverse the steam pipe that should cook them and doesn't — because in the Cistern, a pipe can be scalding to your palm and somehow walkable for an ant.

They carry crumbs. Not food. Just crumbs: flakes of rust-scale, fibers of baked dust-felt, a spore from the bleached fern. They carry them from nowhere to nowhere, around a valve housing and down into the invisible dark behind the boiler. If you watch for ten minutes — time moves 1.4 slow here — you will see that the line never gets longer or shorter. The same dozen ants, making the same circuit, for years.

They do not live here. They *commute* through here, as if the Cistern were a mountain pass they agreed upon when the world was cooler.

If you block their path with a fingertip, they will stop, antennae waving in the hot air, confused in a deep historical way. Then, one by one, they will go over you, feeling you out as another pipe.

**3. The Sump-Keeper**

In the lowest corner, under the collapsed grate, in the washbasin of black water that reflects nothing: it lives.

It is not large. It does not want to be large. It is a pale isopod, no, two isopods — flat, eyeless, the color of wet paper — and with them, something else you only see on the third kneeling.

Fine white threads, like nerve fiber or root hair, spreading in the water around the calcification lace. Not algae. This water is too mineral and too dark for algae. This is something that grows where condensation forgets to evaporate. It pulses — no, it doesn't — it *gathers*, very slightly, when a drip hits.

The isopods farm it, or tend it, or simply share it. They flick away from your phone light, but not far. They return within seconds because light is an aberration and dark is home, and your light will die soon anyway.

This is the only standing water in a room that sweats and never dries. It is the mouth of the river that the room was built to ignore. It drinks the mineral seep one bead at a time. It rises a millimeter a day. It falls a millimeter a night. If the Attendant did not sip from it, it would eventually overtop its little concrete lip and make that pool that the floor says cannot happen. So he sips. And it stays.

**4. The Moth**

Once an hour, wings.

It is too large for this place. Its wingspan would cover your hand. Its body is fat and dusty, its antennae feathered with soot. It was not born here; nothing can hatch here in 45 degree heat with no light. It came down. It followed the heat or the impossible faint draft from the sticking service door when someone last ducked inside.

Now it lives off nothing. Maybe off the felt-dust. Maybe off time itself. It bumps softly against hot pipes, against the caged bulb, against your cheek if you stand still. You hear it before you see it — a papery *fthh... fthh* — then nothing for another hour while it rests on the underside of a false ceiling tile, vibrating slightly to release heat.

It should be dead. The law says so. But 1.3 unusual has negotiated otherwise: here, a moth can live for years on darkness and warm iron taste. Its wings have faded from whatever color they were to the blistered amber of the boiler paint. When it finally dies, the ants will find it. They will carry it crumb by crumb toward nowhere.

**5. What Actually Lives Here, Most Of All**

Beyond all of these — beyond man and ant and isopod and moth and the stoic finger-length ferns that fur the inside of a dead floodlight shade without ever thriving — the Cistern itself lives.

Not metaphorically.

The density is the organism. The bundles of pipe like muscle, the laders to nowhere like vestigial limbs, the three slow boilers humming in your molars like a low heart, the valve wheels sighing once per epoch, the tick of expanding metal like joints popping in sleep.

The rust that was welded and painted and rusted again — that is scar tissue that learned to be structure. The air that is both suffocating and clear — that is breath held perfectly. The condensation that appears despite sealed iron — that is sweat.

You feel watched not because the Attendant is looking at you, but because the room has learned to keep itself at a steady state for fifty years by noticing everything that enters. Your handprint on the dusty tank *will* still be there when the moth dies, because the dust-felt remembers pressure.

The vegetation doesn't grow here. The fauna doesn't breed here. Even the Attendant doesn't quite live here. They *persist*, like afterimages after you touch something hot in the dark.

And if you wait long enough, still and sweating, listening to *tok... tok... hum... tssk... fthh*, you will feel the quiet settle over you — 0.8 quiet, so quiet your breathing sounds presumptuous — and you will understand the settlement:

*Nothing new may enter. Nothing old may leave. Everything may stay, if it learns to need almost nothing.*

When you duck back out through the service door, you will not have seen anyone living there.

But your shirt will still be damp hours later, smelling faintly of wet iron and hot concrete after rain.
