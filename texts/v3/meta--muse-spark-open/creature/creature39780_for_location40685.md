What lives here lives on a world that isn't finished yet. Nothing is permanent. Everything is still deciding what it is going to be, and so everything that lives here is an expert in impermanence.

We will start low and go up.

### 1. The Skin

The first living thing is not a thing you notice until you kneel.

It is the lichen.

On a plateau this young there should be no soil, and there isn't. There is basalt still holding the shape of bubbles from when it was liquid, and frost-cracked lithosol thin as grinding dust. The lichen is the one that can eat stone directly.

* **Plate Lichen (*Parmelia petrafera*-type):** charcoal-black rosettes a foot across, their undersides excreting acids that pit the basalt. Sulfur-yellow map lichen follows, then blood-orange crust lichen that looks exactly like rust until you touch it and find it soft, warm, slightly damp. They are the continent's first skin, and they have covered thousands of square kilometers in the few ten-thousands of years since uplift. When the low cloud-sea drains across the plateau, the lichens swell visibly. They drink from the air. They tick with condensation.
* **In their shadow:** tufted grasses. Not meadows — *hassocks*. Each a hard, wind-pruned dome the size of a human head, a single genotype that has split and huddled for centuries. Dwarf willow (*Salix nivalis prostrata*) that never gets higher than your ankle, but spreads sideways for ten meters in a mat of red, wire-like branches. You could walk on it and not know it was a tree. In summer it catkins furiously for two weeks, then stops.
* **In the water:** the marsh itself is alive. The shallow, ankle-deep braided wetlands that cover the embryonic basins are floored not with gravel, but with bacterial mats — rust-red, ochre, black-green sheets of cyanobacteria and iron-oxidizers that fix nitrogen straight out of the mineralized water. They photosynthesize under paper-thin black ice. They smell faintly metallic, like blood on cold iron. Floating above them are rafts of sphagnum so violently green it hurts at this altitude, cotton-grass, and the white-berry creeper — a cranberry analog with berries like hard pearls, waxy enough to survive under snow for eight months and still be edible.

This is not much biomass. And it is enough, because nothing that lives *on* it lives here year-round.

### 2. The Pulse

The plateau looks barren from a kilometer away. From ten meters away it is crawling.

Because this is not a tundra. It is a nursery.

The cold, the wet, the lack of trees, the sheer distance from the lowland forests and their ambush predators has made this raw shield — this construction site — the calving ground for half a hemisphere. Animals come here not to live, but to be born, to feed, to grow fast, and leave before the deep winter locks the basins in true ice.

At dawn, the marsh exhales.

**The Midges First.** Every warm spring — and there are thousands, where deep aquifers pressurized by the northern ice cap bleed up through fresh faults and steam faintly — erupts with chironomid midges. Not biting. Cloud midges. Columns of them rise like smoke and then fall like snow. The bacterial mats feed them; they feed everything else. Their trilling, the sound of a million wings beating out of sync, is the baseline white noise of the continent.

**The Waders.** Slate-grey, long-legged, long-beaked things — convergent with godwits and stilts but with denser down and nictitating membranes to deal with constant condensation. They arrive in flocks that are not flocks but weather fronts. You hear them before you see them when the cloud-river lifts at sunrise: a continuous, low, breathing *shrrrrrr*, and then the sky dims. Not for seconds. For minutes. A hundred thousand wings making the sound of shuffled paper pass overhead. They probe the black gravel for midge larvae, their droppings falling like rain and fertilizing the mats so fast you can see the mats darken overnight. They are here for six weeks. They double their weight. They leave.

**The Ungulates.** They come after the birds have broken the ice-skin. In single-file lines that stretch to the horizon and vanish into the cloud sea.

*The Hollow-Coats:* caribou in function, but not in form. Twice as bulky, on stilts of longer metatarsals to wade the braided marsh. Their coat is not hair but a thatch of hollow, translucent tubes, like deer fur mixed with polar bear fur. It traps air so effectively that snow never melts on their backs; they look frosted even when moving. They make no sound on the flooded gravel — their hooves splay and have a soft, fringed edge like a snowshoe hare's paw. They calve in the open, because there is nothing to hide behind. Their calves can run within an hour, steam rising from their birth-wet coats. They are utterly silent. Their breath joins the cloud rivers.

*The Sixes:* The locals. They were here *before* the uplift, or evolved astonishingly fast in its wake — low-slung, bison-mass grazers with six limbs: four pillar legs for walking, two smaller, shovel-like forelimbs set high on the shoulder that they use to scrape lichen off pillars and to cradle calves. Their feet are even broader than the Hollow-Coats', great splayed pads that distribute weight over sphagnum without sinking. And their backs — their backs are gardens. In the wet furrows between their shoulder hump and spine grows a cultivated lichen, a grey-green felt that the animal cannot reach to groom. It photosynthesizes, it insulates, and when the animal is starving it *eats* it, scraping it off with its forelimbs. You can see older Sixes crusted almost entirely, like walking basalt outcrops. They do not fear you. Fear has not had time to evolve for a biped that makes no noise and smells of warm air and ozone.

The ungulates should strip this thin pasture bare. They don't, because they pulse through. Three weeks of intense grazing, their droppings turning the marshes milky with nitrogen. The bacterial mats bloom explosively after they pass — red tides in ankle-deep water. The grasses, freed from dead thatch, bolt to seed. The lichens grow a millimeter — a year's growth in a week — on the nutrient fog. The land is being *made* by being eaten.

**The Predators Who Follow the Pulse.**

*The Flow-Wolves:* lean, white-grey canids, larger than any timber wolf, with narrow chests adapted for the thin air. They run in packs of 12-20 but they do not howl. They flow. They hunt by walking for days, a silent river of bodies around basalt pillars, never scattering the herds, just... culling. One pulls a calf that would not have survived the march down the escarpment anyway. They have learned to use the lattice-cities — they den in the warm circles underneath, where pups don't freeze.

*The Veils:* above them, always. With wingspans four meters, translucent at the wingtips where feathers thin to membrane. They are not birds, not quite — their bones are hollow but their wings have the aspect ratio of condors crossed with manta rays. They do not flap over the plateau. They can't afford to. They ride the permanent escarpment updraft in an endless, silent circle for hours, perfect stillness, and only know they are there when their shadow crosses you and every wader below freezes as one. They take afterbirth, weak calves, and occasionally a Flow-Wolf pup. Their nests are nowhere on the plateau — they nest on the cliff faces of the escarpment itself, three kilometers straight down.

### 3. The Lattice People

And then — laced through all of it, at a density of one per watershed — the city.

They are human. Mostly.

They breathe the thin air without masks, though it costs them. You can see it in them: chests deep as barrels, necks short, blood so efficient at carrying oxygen their lips are almost black-red in the cold. Their skin is darkened, freckled with permanent high-altitude tan, their eyes narrowed against UV that is merciless at 4km. They move slowly, deliberately, as everyone does at this elevation. There is no running on the plateau.

There are not many of them. Perhaps two or three hundred thousand across a continent the size of Australia. Ten thousand Lattices, each holding twenty to a hundred souls. Sparse in density, urban in kind — because a Lattice is not a village. It is a node in a continent-wide machine.

Each Lattice is a vertical field, a volume of edited physics three hundred meters tall and a kilometer wide. The anchors are the black filaments — not material, but scars in gravity, humming at 30Hz in your molars. You feel a Lattice long before you see it, as a faint pressure change and the sudden stillness of wind.

Between the filaments, they weave weather. The same weeping condensate that beads the basalt is lifted, spun, frozen into panes and vaults and branching balconies of clear, stasis-held ice that never melts. Dwellings are not buildings; they are pockets of warm, still, 18°C air held open inside the field, invisible until a child runs through one and the refraction ripples like heat haze. Walkways are consensus — places where falling is politely told not to apply. You step off a basalt ledge and the field takes your weight with a gentle sigh.

They do not build *on* the plateau. They build *over* it, because the ground is still too young to hold foundations and too sacred to carve. Waste heat from the field bleeds down into the basalt and thaws a perfect circle underneath each Lattice — a spring oasis 800 meters across where permafrost retreats. There, willows grow head-high for the first time. Grasses go to actual seed-heads. Cotton-grass nods like wheat. And the Hollow-Coats and the Sixes come to calve in the hum, in the safety of the warm circle where Flow-Wolves will not hunt. The humans watch from invisible balconies, quiet, recording, occasionally descending on fields to help a stuck calf stand, or to take a single old Six with a silent, field-induced aneurysm that leaves no wound.

They do not farm in the way lowlanders understand farming. They tend. They are pastoralists of wildness. Their technology makes almost no sound — the low *sigh* when a field reconfigures to catch more condensate, the faint *tick... tick... tick* of water weaving itself into a new wall. At night the Lattices dim to banked amber and you can see straight through them to the stars, which at this elevation do not twinkle. They glare.

Their children learn to fall. To step off ice with faith in the field. Their old learn to listen — to the continent-wide ticking of dripping basalt, to the trill of wings, to the millimeter-by-millimeter creak of the plateau still rising under their anchors, still weeping water from fresh wounds.

The rule they live by is simple and new: Geology is not finished. Biology has not waited. Technology is not separate.

The city does not conquer the mountain. The mountain is becoming the city by learning, slowly, how to be alive — and the people, the Hollow-Coats, the Sixes, the Veils, the lichens that ate stone to make the first soil, are all just the different ways it is trying.
