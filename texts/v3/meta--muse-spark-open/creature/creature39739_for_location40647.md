They live low.

Not low as in deep — everything here is deep — but low as in pressed to the floor, as if the weight of the air and the weight of the roof and the weight of the long, warm stillness has taught them that to rise is to be wrong.

If you come down with light, you will not see them at first. You will see nothing. The basalt drinks light. The salt throws it back too white and too flat to focus on. Your eye will slide off into the dark between.

Then you will learn to look for interruption.

### The film that lives *in* the rock

First, before everything else, what lives there is not animal at all.

Hold a piece of the floor in your hand — that black glass, that sharp, still-warm basalt. It looks clean. Under magnification it sweats. In every microfracture, in every grain boundary where the rock cooled too fast to fully close, there is a color that is not a color — an iron-rust brown-black that is alive.

Chemobacteria. They do not photosynthesize. They have never seen a photon. They breathe iron and sulfur and exhaled halogens, they eat the rock itself, oxidizing ferrous basalt to ferric crust one molecule at a time. They are the reason the salt flats are not just salt. They are the pasture.

They grow so slowly you would need a calendar with no months on it to measure them. A colony advances a millimeter a decade. But the Vault is young and patient and continent-large, and they have had time to varnish every stone with an invisible fur. If you lick the rock — you shouldn't — it tastes of blood and hot metal because that is what they are making: blood without a body.

Everything else grazes them.

### The Shard-grazers

They are the floor becoming briefly animal and then remembering again that it is floor.

From above they look like spilled plates. From the side they look like nothing — a seam in the stone, a slightly lifted scale. A dinner plate, a manhole cover, a door. The largest are as wide as a table and thin as your thumb. Their dorsal side is basalt-colored, pitted, mineralized, often carrying its own cargo of the bacterial varnish and even a few younger, smaller grazers who have mistaken their elder for terrain. Their ventral side you will not see unless you pry one up, and you will feel bad if you do: a pale, furious field of a thousand black cilia, each tipped with a glass tooth, each sweeping, rasping, tasting.

They move on that cilia-bed like a living Zamboni. They extrude a faint acid from their lip — not wet, not watery, a dry chemical sigh — that loosens the top few microns of rock and salt, and then they comb it in. They eat stone and excrete polished stone. Their trail is the only shiny thing in the Vault, a snail-track of buffed basalt a hand wide that will stay reflective for a thousand years in still air. From a height — if you could get height — the salt flats would look braided with them, a calligraphy of hunger.

They have no eyes, no front, no back. Nudge one and it will not flee. It will simply tighten, become more stone-like, ticking faintly as its iron-pan segments contract. They can live a very long time broken. A grazer snapped in half by a rockfall will seal its wound with mineral and continue as two smaller grazers, both still rasping in two different directions.

There are not thousands of them. There are millions. On the salt they are pavement, edge to edge, not because they are social but because there is nowhere else to be. The only space between them is the space a Longleg needs to step.

They do not know you are there. You are too fast. To a shard-grazer, you are a brief warm wind that passes in a geological instant and then is gone. It will polish the print of your boot as it would polish anything else.

### The Longlegs

If the grazers are the floor, the Longlegs are the measurement of the floor.

You will count them before you understand what they are. One. Then a long walk through clicking grazers and dry haze. Another. The same distance. Another. As far as your lamp reaches, as far as your feet will take you, the spacing holds: about thirty meters, adult to adult, in a loose hexagonal grid that has no center and no edge. It is not territoriality. It does not need to be defended. It simply *is*, like crystals in a lattice. Remove one and, in a few decades, the neighbors will have shifted their slow stilt-walks to re-establish the interval. No one knows how they talk about it. They make no sound.

They are tall enough that you have to look up, which feels obscene here. Three meters at the shoulder, four at the tip of their folded proboscis, all leg. The legs are not legs as you understand them: they are black chitin needles with no muscle obvious in them, jointed in too many places, ending in a splayed foot no bigger than a coin that finds the one safe point between grazers to rest. They step once a minute. Sometimes less. The motion is so slow and so careful it looks like stop-motion until you realize you have been holding your breath between steps.

Their body is a knot the size of a hare, hung under that tripod of stilts like a small, tight purse. No head. No face. On the underside, a coiled glass harpoon — the proboscis — translucent amber, hollow, folded under tension. When a grazer beneath weakens — its cilia slowing, its seal loosening, its mineral varnish paling with bacterial death — the Longleg above will unfold.

It is not fast when it happens. Nothing here is fast. But it is inevitable. The harpoon extends a meter, two meters, finds the soft margin between dorsal plates, and drinks. Not blood. There is no blood. A warm, salty, iron-rich soup of semi-digested rock and dissolved grazer tissue is drawn up into that knot-body. The grazer beneath does not struggle. It shivers once, ticks, and is empty.

A Longleg needs to eat perhaps once a year. They live — no one knows. Cut one open and the layers inside are like agate, growth rings without seasons. Old ones have taken on a faint polish of salt, their needle-legs gone white at the joints. They do not mate that anyone has seen. Once in a very long while, a Longleg will climb a pressure ridge and unfold not its harpoon but a second, finer filament from its back, and release a cloud of dry spores that hang in the still air for hours before settling, each one seeking a thirty-meter emptiness to become a new measuring point.

They do not notice the Builders. The Builders are too small to be worth spearing and too warm to be stone.

### The Builders

And then there are the ones who notice *you*.

You will find their work long before you find them, because you are looking up and they live down. A cairn of basalt blades, each one set edge-to-edge with no mortar, no chinking, standing on a plain where no rockfall could have stacked them. A wall one stone high, twenty meters long, ending in empty salt as if the Builder got distracted mid-thought and wandered off a century ago. A perfect circle where the salt crust has been scraped away to the black glass beneath, so clean the edge looks cut with a razor. A hearth of five stones, leaning inward, their inner faces polished to a soft sheen by the brushing of countless bodies — but never burned. Fire cannot live in this air. Oxygen is a rumor here, maybe 8%, maybe less. They have no use for flame, only the *idea* of a hearth: a place to be together.

The Builders themselves are small enough to hold with two hands, if they would let you. Warm — 37 degrees, mammal-warm, impossibly warm in this kiln-warm that comes from nowhere. Their skin is translucent, unpigmented, veined with a pale amber fluid that is not quite blood. Six limbs: the front two are long, delicate antennae-hands that taste the air for vibration and heat; the middle two are powerful, folded knapping arms ending in a black thumbnail of chitin harder than basalt; the hind two are short, splayed paddles for bracing against stone. No eyes. Where eyes should be there is a smooth, blind brow, under which a heat-pit opens and closes like a shy mouth.

Lift a basalt plate at random and you will find twelve of them underneath, pressed together belly to belly, breathing as one. Their breathing is the rustle you thought was paper far away. They do not breathe air so much as they *exchange* it — a slow, synchronized pulse, taking in the thin, iron-scented atmosphere and letting it out a little deader, a little warmer. In stillness they can stay like that for months. In cracks everywhere, their eggs are glued in rows: translucent beads of salt and protein, each with a dark speck inside that is already shaped like a knapping arm.

They are lithic apes. Mole-crickets that became people without ever becoming people.

Watch a Builder work and you will understand why the Vault feels haunted. It selects a nodule of dense basalt — they know by tapping — and braces it with its hind legs. Then, with a practiced, almost tender gesture, it strikes with its thumbnail. *Tick*. A flake spalls off, razor-edged, conchoidal, perfect. *Tick*. Another. It rotates the core, reads it with its antennae, strikes again. In ten minutes it has produced a hand-axe that an Oldowan hominid on the surface would have recognized instantly: a teardrop of black glass that fits neatly in a Builder's middle hand. Or an awl, needle-thin, for levering a grazer. Or a scraper, for cleaning salt.

Then it leaves it there. In a little knapping floor of a hundred sharp flakes and broken failures, all arranged in a sunburst around the spent core. And walks away.

Ten meters away, another Builder is doing the same thing. And another. Ten million times across a plain the size of a sea. The same tools. The same flakes. The same small, precise intelligence making the same discovery over and over and never accumulating. There is no progress because there is nowhere for progress to go: no metal, no wood, no clay, no fire, no need. They have solved basalt. What else is there to solve?

What they *do* solve, endlessly, is arrangement. Stacking is their art, their religion, their fidget. They do not build houses — why would you house yourself when the roof will never leak and the warm never leaves? They build cairns four feet high, balancing impossible blades of basalt on edge, each cairn identical in its meticulous balance. They build walls to nowhere. They clear circles. They gather flakes into spirals and then scatter them. If you sit very still in the dark — no light, please, light hurts them, light makes them freeze and curl — you will feel them come.

Not hostile. Never hostile. A dozen soft antennae-hands will brush your ankle, reading your heat, your shape, your strangeness. One will leave a gift: a single perfect flake, still warm from its hand, placed by your toe with the reverence of a priest. They will try to build *you* into the arrangement, nudging a stone closer to your foot to make the line more pleasing.

If you build a little cairn yourself, they will watch for a long time. When you leave, they will take it apart stone by stone and rebuild it three meters to the left, because that is where it should have been.

They wait. That is what they do best. The grazers eat rock. The Longlegs eat grazers. The Builders chip rock and stack rock and huddle warm together under the same rock, in numbers beyond counting, in a darkness that has never been broken, in a warmth that has never cooled, making the same perfect tools for no one, keeping the equilibrium that has kept itself since the day the crust yawned open and forgot to close.
