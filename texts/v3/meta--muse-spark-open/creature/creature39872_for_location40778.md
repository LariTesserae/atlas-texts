What lives here doesn't so much live *on* the stone as *in* the wetness on the stone.

That is the first thing to understand. Nothing here is dry, nothing is ever dry, and so nothing has bothered to become waterproof.

If you stand long enough and watch — and you will have to stand long enough, because nothing moves quickly in ankle-deep warm water over sharp rock — you start to see the layering.

### The Film

At the bottom, literally the bottom of everything, is the orange film.

Crouch at the edge of a cistern or a marsh-thread where the water is only just crawling and you will see it. Not on the new flows — those are too hot, too glassy, still faintly poisoned with salt and sulfur. But on any surface that has been left alone for a year, for five years, for ten, the black basalt blushes. A felt, a slick, matte and rust-colored, sometimes peeling in sheets like sunburned skin when the water drops a centimeter.

It is not one thing. Put your face close — the air smells like penny and rot — and it resolves into a million mouths. Bacteria, cyanobacteria, archaea, yeasts. The true citizens. They do what plants would do if there were plants. They take the dim amber light that filters through the stratus and the faint chemical heat bleeding up from the stone itself and they make sugar out of it. They take the sea-spray that the wind carries inland, invisible, salty, and they fix it. They grow on condensation alone.

Nothing eats the rock but they come close. They etch it. They dull the glass just enough to hold more water, which grows more film. In time they will make soil. Not yet. Now they make only a slick you slip on, and the base of everything else.

If you scrape it with a fingernail it comes away like wet rust and smells overwhelmingly alive. The children of the city harvest it. Not on purpose — they fall in it, they wade through it, it stains their feet and legs orange to the knee for their entire lives.

### The Water-Breeders

Because the film is edible, the water can carry life without a single blade of grass.

Every puddle the size of a plaza, every shin-deep marsh-thread between flows, every cistern and collapsed blister and open mortar joint that holds a cupful of warm, tea-colored water — it writhes. Not visibly at first. Then your eyes adjust to the dimness and you see the water is not water. It is suspension.

Midge larvae by the billions, black commas hanging head-down from the surface film, filtering bacteria. Mosquito larvae. Midge larvae that will never become midges. Copepods like flecks of glass. Tadpoles so dense in places they look like the mud itself moving. Translucent shrimp that are little more than guts wrapped around a gut, grazing. Small fish — blind, pale, finger-long — that never stop eating them. Newts with external gills like red feathers, walking from puddle to puddle on a night that never comes.

And the adults. God, the adults. When they hatch they do not leave. There is nowhere drier to go.

The midge columns are the most honest inhabitants. They are born over a specific square meter of marsh-thread and they will die over it. They rise in the evening-that-is-always-evening in pillars as thick as a man's body, as tall as a two-story house, humming one note so high and so constant it ceases to be a sound and becomes a pressure behind your eyes. The note never breaks because the column never breaks — as individuals die, others rise; as others rise, others die. A column can mark the same alley corner for a human lifetime.

They are food for everything else, and they know it. They do not care.

On the water's surface, needle-legged crabs — black, speckled white, fists when balled up, dinner plates when they splay — pick their way up vertical weeping walls against the trickle. They graze film and catch midges and lay eggs in any damp crack. The geckos eat the crabs' young. The walls at night glitter with the geckos' eyes. During the dim day they are invisible, pressed flat into fissures, skin the exact wet-gray of basalt, chirping a two-note call that you thought at first was water dripping.

### The Air-Breeders

The city would exist without the swallows, but you wouldn't know it was a city. With them, you can't think it's anything else.

They are not quite swallows. They are smaller, soot-black all over, with tails forked so deeply they look cut with scissors, and mouths that open impossibly wide and pale. They need no trees because they never land on trees. They land on stone.

Every wall has them. Every eave, every corbelled dome, every quarry-pit and pressure ridge and bridge soffit is pocked with their nests — little cups of imported mud and lime-spit and guano, shoulder to shoulder, row after row, thousands to a block. The guano is important. It is the other primary production. On a coast that has not yet made soil, bird shit *is* soil, falling from the walls in white streaks into the water, feeding the film, feeding the larvae, feeding the fish that feed the birds again. A perfect, deafening loop.

They are always in the air because they must be. The midges never stop, so the swallows never stop. At any moment half the sky — which is low, by the way, oppressively low, a ceiling of warm cloud you feel you could touch if you climbed a roof — is moving. They knife and turn without ever colliding, screaming a thin needling scream that stitches itself into the water-hiss and the frog chorus until you cannot separate them.

And underneath their flight, in the larger blister-caves where a whole house has collapsed inward and left a dark dome, the bats pour out. Not at night. There is no night. They pour out at all hours in a slow, oily river, larger than the swallows, silent except for a dry leather rustle when they pass close. They take the bigger moths — pale, damp moths the size of your hand that breed in the hides hung over doorways. The swallows and bats ignore each other with the total indifference of two guilds who have divided an unlimited resource for a thousand generations and never needed to compete.

If you stand on a causeway and look up, you will see the air stratify: midges lowest, swallows above them, bats above the swallows, herons above nothing — the herons stand. One per cistern, one per plaza puddle, utterly still, gray as the stone in the amber half-light, waiting for the pale fish to forget them. They have been standing there since you arrived. They will be standing there when you leave. They look embalmed until they strike, and then they move so fast your eye waters.

### The People

And then — threaded through all of this, shoulder-to-shoulder with it — the people who have learned to live like the film does: wet, low, patient, and permanently stained.

You expected them to be miserable. They aren't. Or not in the way visitors are miserable.

First, see them properly. They are not tall. No one grows tall under a dim sun carrying baskets of stone and fish on their head through water. Men and women are the same height, the same build: short, broad, barrel-chested, thighs thick with wading muscle. Their skin is dark, not from sun — there is no sun — but from a lifetime of never being quite dry. It is softened, slightly macerated always, like fingertips after a bath. Many have a permanent faint rash at the ankles and wrists where the warm water sits, a pale fungal lace that they scratch without thought. Their feet are extraordinary. Wide, splayed, soles thickened into pads like hide from walking barefoot on warm, sharp, wet glass for forty years. No one here has ever owned a shoe that stayed dry for an hour, so they stopped trying. They go barefoot over knife-edges that would flay you.

Their hair is kept short or shaved — lice love this climate as much as people do — and oiled with fish oil against the endless drip. Children gleam like seals. Old people gleam too, but cracked, their scalps orange-tinged from the film they wade through every day.

They do not shout. You thought from a distance the alley was full of shouting, but when you get close you realize no one is raising their voice above the minimum necessary to cut one foot through the noise. They have lived their whole lives inside that wall of sound — the hiss of a thousand trickles, the midge hum, the frog chorus that is not a chorus but a single unbroken organ note from millions of throats in the marsh-threads — and they have adapted the way people always adapt to inescapable noise: they stopped hearing it, and they stopped trying to out-scream it.

Instead they gesture. Hands are always moving. A flick for *come here*, a palm for *stop*, two fingers tapping a wrist for *how much?* Children learn a full lexicon before they can speak clearly, because speech is for indoors, under a slab roof where the water drumming is muffled for a moment. In the alleys they talk with their hands and their shoulders and with a system of whistles — short, piercing, two-tone — that cuts through the midge-drone the way a heron's beak cuts water. You will hear them before you understand them: a chirp from a mother to a child three causeways away, answered, precise.

They sleep in shifts, not nights. There is no night. The light never changes from that warm paper-lantern amber-gray. So they sleep when tired, for two or three hours, on raised stone platforms just above the waterline, under stretched hides, earplugs of waxed fiber in their ears, dogs curled against their backs for warmth. The city never quietens because half of it is always awake: pounding mortars of dried imported grain and pounded fish, chipping basalt with stone mauls — *tink, tink, tink* — that adds its own layer to the roar, tending causeways, clearing cistern throats of film so the water stays drinkable.

Because that is the other thing: they are hydraulic engineers before they are anything else. Every family, every courtyard of six walls-shared houses, is responsible for a stretch of water. Not owning it — you cannot own what never stops moving — but tending it. They sweep the marsh-threads with reed brooms to keep them flowing a thumb's width per hour. They lift a basalt slab here, wedge a chip there, to keep the ever-sitting, ever-sheeting inches-deep water in the lower streets at exactly its equilibrium depth. If they stop, nothing floods. If they stop, nothing dries. The system is so vast and so finely balanced — ocean seep one way, condensation and warm stone the other — that a man's work is not to control it but to not offend it. They whisper about the Water That Remembers Its Level, as if it were a god.

Food comes two ways and both are endless work.

From the water at their feet, they take protein without end. Boys squat for hours with hand nets of imported reed fiber, scooping shrimp and tadpoles and pale fish from the same plaza puddle where a woman is washing. Girls wade the marsh-threads with baskets, feeling with their toes for crabs. It is never a feast — a handful here, a handful there — but added together across ten thousand hands it is enough, because the water replenishes itself by the hour from guano and film.

From far away — days away on foot over sharp flows where you must carry everything, including water that isn't salty — the caravans come. You can see them arriving from the higher ground inland where the basalt is finally old enough to have weathered into something like soil and where stunted, salty tamarisk and glasswort have taken desperate hold. They bring what this low place can never make: grain, reed, hides, lime for mortar, timber-charcoal for the lime kilns, dried tamarisk wood for the few fires that must be kept always lit under shelters to smoke fish and boil water. They trade it for what the low city has in obscene surplus: salted fish, guano scraped from swallow walls (cold white gold for those inland fields), dried midge-cakes — yes, they eat them; they skim the columns with fine nets at dusk and press them into salty black cakes that taste of mud and fat — and stone. Perfect, dense, black, glassy stone, quarried with fire-setting and endlessly workable, which the highlanders covet for tools.

No one leaves. The caravan people are a different clan, paler, drier, irritated by the wet. The low people are born in a stone room with water running in threads across the ceiling, and they die in the same room forty years later having maybe walked three days in any direction and found nothing but more wet stone and more city. They do not think this is tragic. Why would you leave equilibrium? Outside is either hotter, drier rock with nothing to eat, or the sea, which is too much water of the wrong kind.

Listen — if you can, for a moment, filter the roar — to a child beside a cistern. She is perhaps four. She sits with her feet in the warm water, perfectly still as a heron, watching a newt walk. Her legs are orange to the knee. Midges form a halo around her head and she does not swipe. A swallow screams inches over her hair. She blinks slowly in the shadowless light, and her mouth is slightly open, breathing the wet. She is not waiting for anything to happen. Nothing will happen. The water will keep sheeting. The frogs will keep calling without taking a breath. The hammers will keep tapping.

She belongs to this place the way the film belongs to the rock. Not as a conqueror, not as a victim, but as a growth that found the exact conditions it needed and then, very quietly, decided to never change.
