It is not a big population. Not really — not if you count heads.

At first, when you step out of the mule-cut and onto the shelf, you think it's uninhabited. Because in a place built tight and planned like this, inhabitation should announce itself — laundry, voices piled on voices, smoke from a hundred hearths braided into one cloud.

There is one smoke. Perhaps two.

Then your eyes adjust to density, and you start to pick them out the way you pick out birds in snow.

There are maybe fifteen or twenty of them where there should be eight hundred. Old people, mostly. A young couple with ash on their hands from the cisterns. Two kids who know every alley-whistle by pitch. A man whose entire job is walls — just walking and looking at walls. They live spread out, one family per block, one occupied house, two empty ones, one half-roofed one where tools sleep under a tarp.

They are not survivors. That was my mistake for the first ten minutes — I catalogued them as aftermath, the last of a die-off, a plague, an exodus. They don't carry themselves like that.

They move custodially.

The old woman at the big firepit is not cooking for herself. She is feeding the pit itself, with split dwarf-wood laid starwise, keeping the stone warm and the draft correct. When the wind hits, she turns her face into it and waits, and tends again. She will tell you that the plaza will need heat when it is full, so it needs heat now, to learn.

The children are not lonely. They play the kind of games you invent when distance is the toy — run until the echo comes back late from the meeting hall and tags you, hide in a house nobody will ever look in because there are too many houses. Their shouting is practiced-loud, the volume of people who have grown up having to throw their voice across an empty market square to be heard. If one of them falls, the sound of it carries perfectly to the other end of town, and someone looks up.

Between any two warm bodies there is, as you felt, a cathedral's worth of nothing. That nothing is part of their housekeeping. They sweep plazas the size of lakes with brooms made of bound fern. In an hour the wind will stipple them with grit and wet leaf again. They sweep anyway. They re-lash a cord on a scaffold that scaffolds nothing. They scrape moss off a footing where moss wants to be, and leave it carefully on a roof where it is meant to be.

They are farmers, after a fashion, but not to scale.

Every terrace bed is a handkerchief — four feet by six at largest — boxed in by that soft, still-flaking basalt, fed not by rain but by what the air leaves behind. They harvest condensation the way lowlanders harvest wheat. A runnel directed here, a basalt lip coaxed there, a clay pot under a perpetual bead and fall. From that impossible wetness — that *2.4-to-3*, a fraction you feel in your lungs as abundance — they get too much life for this height. The potato leaves are dark and lacquered. Bitter greens spill over the walls. Tubers swell underground and crack the stone they are told to crack. One family's four table-gardens could feed seven families that aren't there yet. They store, ferment, dry, give away to each other and still have too much.

They do not hurry about it. Antler pick against infant rock, a shovelful a day, a wall-bulge watched for seven winters before it is pinned properly. You can work like that when erosion is still learning how to be erosion. The covenant here is *loud fast, slow slow* — wind forever, everything else in geologic time. Your tea will go cold before you cross the town. Their calendar goes cold before the town changes.

And then, slowly, you realize they are not the majority residents.

If you count biomass instead of heads — if you count *who keeps the town from blowing away* — the place is riotously occupied.

Moss lives here. That is the first citizen. Cushion moss three inches thick on every north-facing slate, on every shake, on the unused thresholds, turning abandoned half-houses into soft, green barrows. It holds water like a sponge holds prayer, ten times its weight, and releases it all night in drips.

Ferns live here, shoulder-high in every failure of flagstone. Cloud-tree dwarfs live here, impossible things, roots the thickness of rope sunk directly into warm vesicular basalt, splitting what has not yet learned hardness. Waxy vines live here, testing each lashing, each knot. Cyan threads in the gutters turn water brick-red or pea-green depending on the day's mineral sigh. Biofilm slicks the insides of the cisterns and makes the drinking water taste faintly of iron and earth.

They are not decoration. They are the town's first plumbing, first thatch, first soil-makers. Where the builders left grid, the green lives in alleys; where the builders left wall, the green learns to roof. At this height you expect scarcity. Here the altitude says desert; the new stone says womb; womb wins. The green arrived within months of the cooling, the old woman will tell you — spores on that very wind that never stops, that loud ocean-less wind.

For the wind lives here too.

Not as metaphor. As occupant.

It has addresses. It prefers Third Alley for whistling because the gap between the hall and the unfinished granary narrows just so. It booms in Doorway Seventeen, which is exactly deep enough to resonate. It uses the market awning posts, still strung with guy-cords, as a harp. You hear tenants shifting overhead all night, courteous but never leaving. The people talk to it by pausing for it. They do not curse it.

Water lives here alongside it, a twin tenant with opposite temperament. Steady where wind is gusty. It beads on every exposed crystal of basalt and runs as if chosen. Children divert it with palms. Old men read its color. It pools where they tell it to pool — especially in that wrong central plaza, the one with no outlet, that perfect shallow navel.

That water — that is the reason for the rest.

On a clear morning you can see your breath. On that same clear morning the basin breathes back. A thin veil lifts off it though its rim is cold. Put your hand inches above it and the air is just softer. Splash another puddle nearby, it crusts overnight. This one never does. Not because someone heats it. Because underneath, the shelf has not cooled. Put your palm flat on the bare rock outside the moss, where they've scraped it clean for you to feel, and the town stops pretending to be still.

*Thrum.*

Slow. So slow you think you imagined it until the next one comes. Eighteen beats a minute? Fifteen? Wide and patient. Not a machine. Not footsteps. Magma turning over miles down, squeezing last gas through a stone still plastic enough to transmit it. The mountain is not finished being a mountain.

And finally — the oldest resident, though you never see him whole — is the shelf itself.

It is alive in the way ice is alive just before it bears weight. Mineral blood still moving. Sweating, swelling, rising a thumbnail per decade. The people do not worship it. They listen to it the way carpenters listen to timber seasoning, waiting for the click that says *now you may peg me*.

That's why they built empty on purpose. Why sixty strong footings sit and wait. Why plazas were swept before feet existed to dirty them.

They aren't keeping the scale warm for missing friends. They are keeping it open for successors.

What lives here now is a pioneer household: a few humans practicing city-ness on behalf of a future city, a crowd of mosses and ferns and cold-loving tubers practicing forest-ness on behalf of a future soil, water practicing river, wind practicing street-life, stone practicing continent.

Ask them how long they'll tend, and they will not count in years. They'll tap the warm basalt twice and say, *Until it finishes arriving.*
