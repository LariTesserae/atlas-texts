It is not inhabited the way a forest is inhabited. Nobody *lives* here in the sense of making a home. Everything that lives here *is* the home. It is the instrument and the musician at once.

If you stand still long enough for your boots to stop ringing on the glass, they will show themselves.

### The Garden Itself

First, because there are more of them than anything else and because without them nothing else could breathe:

**The Glass Spindles.** 

Thirty-odd of them now — count again and you'll get thirty-two, then twenty-nine, the runners confuse you — clinging to wall and floor and ceiling at those formal distances, like monks who have taken a vow to never touch.

Each one is a column of translucent pages. Not leaves; leaves imply softness and water. These are vanes of silica-stiffened membrane, amber-veined with resin that will never harden and never run. Three feet tall for the daughters on the walls, a little taller where a good draft-node feeds them. They do not photosynthesize. What would they eat light for? There is no sun.

They eat smoke.

You can see it if you put your face close - your eyelashes will lift with static - the fine alkali dust and halide snow sublimed off the hot basalt below, sulfur blooming yellow at the seams. The Spindle holds a charge across its fronds, cool against the blood-warm air, and the dust drifts to it, sticks, is unmade and remade into more membrane. Hydrophobic to the point of hostility; a drop of your sweat would scar it brown.

And they are not silent. Nothing here is allowed to be silent. Their stiff vanes quiver in the endless convective roll - hot stone breathing up, cool center sinking down - each at its own pitch. A low *hummm* that isn't in your ears but under your tongue, in the fillings of your teeth. Each plant a reed. Together a chord held for centuries.

That is their other function: lungs and tongues for the pocket. They keep the note going that keeps everything fed.

**The Loom.**

In the exact center, where two plates of fallen mirror-glass meet and never quite join, she stands.

You know her as soon as you look. She is not larger, she is *denser*. A braid of woody stems thick as a thigh, eight feet straight up, black-green with age, splitting at the top into six, seven, eight great drum-leaves, circular and taut as kettledrums, rimmed all around with cilia that never stop trembling.

She does not catch dust. She conducts it.

Every twenty or thirty seconds her drum-leaves tighten — visibly — and loosen, and the whole cavern's chord bends a fraction, makes a slow beat. *HUUmmm-huuMMM-huuMMM.* You feel it shift dust from one invisible node to another, herding mineral snow toward a hungry daughter on the far wall, pulling it away from a patch that has been overfed.

Her runners give her away. Pale white nerves, thin as thread, shooting out across the floor plates, up walls, ending in every smaller Spindle. Clones. Daughters. Organs. Cut one and the chord wobbles; cut two and it might shatter. Whoever left the pruning knife knew that.

Down inside the glass under her roots, if you kneel, you see the other gardeners: veins of photo-bacteria, amber foxfire, living directly off infrared leak from the stone. No sun, no water, just heat made into faint light made into chemistry. They make the dim that lets you see. Without them this place would be perfectly alive in perfect darkness.

### The Grazers and Gleaners

Because a garden, even one that eats sound, will have things that eat the garden. But sparingly. Respectfully.

**The Chime Mites.**

You'll hear them before you see them, tiny pings riding the drone like sparks.

On every Spindle, five, ten, twenty at a time - clusters of thumbnail-sized crabs made half of glass themselves. Translucent legs, backs stretched with a membrane as tight as a snare head. They scrape. Their mouthparts are chipped obsidian, and they farm the mineral bloom that crusts the vanes before it can choke the leaf. As they move they pluck the cilia and add their own contribution: *tik, tik, ting*. The garden's percussion section. Harmless unless you brush the frond; then they'll freeze, membranes thrumming, listening louder than you are.

**The Needle Runners.**

Between the floor plates, in the black tessellated cracks. You think the floor is empty until one darts.

Six legs, blind, no eyes to waste, covered head to tail in iron-red bristles that crackle with collected charge. Fast - obscenely fast for something that lives where nothing hunts. They run, dead-stop, run, dead-stop, always stopping exactly where the dust hangs thickest in an acoustic node. Then they lower their heads and lick the glass clean with a pale, broad tongue. Electrostatic vacuum cleaners. When two meet at a node they tap bristles, exchange a spark, veer away. They never fight. Food comes to them if they wait for the music to bring it.

Leave a halite cube as an offering and by morning a Runner will have found it, tried it, abandoned it as too salty even for them.

**The Listeners.**

Above. Always above.

You don't notice them at first because they don't move. Fat as figs, leathery as old purses, hanging from the low ceiling directly over the Loom in a loose, reverent circle. Seven now. Last time there were five. Next time there might be nine.

A sac with no front and no back, just a huge, circular tympanum stretched across the belly, veined with light where the foxfire bacteria live symbiotically in its skin. It inflates, once a minute, on warm still air, vibrates in sympathy with the room. This is how it feeds: airborne spores, mite larvae, shed cilia, everything the chord sifts toward the center rains slowly up - because here, convection makes dust fall upward - and sticks to that humming eardrum. A tongue behind the drum licks it clean.

They are the audience. When your boot clicked on glass, every single drum twitched toward you at once, tuning. When the Loom pulses, they pulse half a breath after, as if in amen. Predators only in the most patient sense; they have never needed to chase anything. If one falls - and rarely one does, too heavy with charge - its body lies perfectly preserved in the dry heat until the Runners find it, or a human with a copper knife comes to take a strip of tympanum for sinew.

Nothing here hunts quickly. Nothing needs fangs. Hunger is solved by standing in the right place and letting the sound do the work.

### The Temporary Ones

And then - because even a sealed blister needs tending, needs memory -

**The Visitors.**

Not residents. Pilgrims. Caretakers. Perhaps one family from the salt plain, perhaps many over centuries, leaving the same knotted rope you came down.

You know them by what they left small and careful:

The cairn of seven sharp shards, balanced with prayerful precision so it doesn't hum out of tune. The three hammered copper bowls, green but never rusted further for lack of water, holding halite cubes and a molted Mite back and a palm-smooth basalt sphere worn by being held.

The petroglyphs scratched by the Loom: concentric circles, wavy lines that *are* the sound drawn visible, stick figures with ears as big as their heads, kneeling, listening. Not writing. Notation. *Keep it going.*

And the knife, left on a ledge: Loom-wood that will never rot, obsidian flake lashed with Listener gut, still sharp enough to part a hair. A pruning tool, not a weapon. For taking one drum-leaf without taking the life, for trimming a runner that grows too greedy and starts to flatten the chord.

They understood what you are beginning to understand with your chest now synced to the Loom: this is a dynamic equilibrium. Frozen only as long as everyone does very little. Thirty-odd plants, a few dozen mites and runners, a handful of hanging drums, and a human who knows to take almost nothing, leave an offering, and climb back up to air that doesn't sing.

They do not live here. If they tried, their water, their breath, their noise would break it. They live *for* here. In short, quiet visits.

If you stay too long your own heartbeat - that other drum - will try to become part of the choir. That's when you know to bow, to place your own small perfect cube on the copper bowl, and go.

Because the true inhabitant of the pocket is not any one of these things.

The true inhabitant is the chord itself, loud and unwavering and impossibly lush in a dry heat, wearing bodies - plant, animal, human memory - the way a song wears singers.
