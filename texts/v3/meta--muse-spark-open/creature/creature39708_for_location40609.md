On the High Dry Terrace, almost nothing lives — and what does live does not live *on* it. It lives *despite* it, or *instead* of it.

You have to stop looking for animals first. The first citizen is not an animal at all.

### The Holdfasts

The Aeroliths are not plants, not really, though you will call them that for lack of a better word. After an hour inside a grove you understand they are not individuals.

What looks from a distance like a low, black-green reef is a single thing — a colony that may be centuries old, or millennia. The thick trunks are not trunks. They are chimneys. Layered fiber and salt and dead leaf pressed into rings, hollow in the center. At night they breathe in. As the stone ticks and contracts, the cool air sinks, and the chimneys draw that air down through the mat like lungs in reverse. Moisture that would never fall as rain — a few grams of vapor sublimated from deep pores in the limestone — catches and rimes on the cold salt walls inside.

By first light, each chimney is furred with frost on the inside. That is their water table.

Then come the leaves. Tabletop-sized, ceramic-stiff, pale jade with rusted margins. They are not leaves for catching sun, though they do that. They are condensers. All day yesterday they tracked the sun without moving — their angle is grown, not adjusted — to keep their silvered undersides at precisely the temperature where frost becomes oil.

Watch at dawn. You will hear it before you see it: a faint, high fizzing, like soda poured over ice. The hairs — ten thousand per leaf, each tipped with a hydrophilic bead — thaw. But they do not thaw into water. Water would betray them and be gone by 9 a.m. They thaw into brine. A clear, camphor-bitter hydrocarbon fluid the colony exudes to replace water. It beads, swells, and is sucked back into the leaf through capillary channels you can see pulsing if you press your cheek near. Cut one and it bleeds slow honey that never dries, smells like rosemary and kerosene, and numbs your lip.

That is why the grove is an island. The Aeroliths are oasis-makers who do not need an oasis.

By midday, when the dome-light is white and shadowless and the rock outside is too hot to touch, the interior of the grove is five degrees colder and dim. The leaves vent only the lightest fraction of that oil as vapor, heavy, medicinal, unwilling to rise. It pools knee-high, refracts the light into green shafts. Inside that mist, the colony farms itself:

**The felt.** A crimson and ochre cushion that is not moss, growing directly on bare stone under the canopy. Each filament ends in a glass bead — a waterless fruit — filled with brine so concentrated it is almost solid. Tap it and it jiggles. It is food and canteen both.

**The brooms.** Pale turquoise lichen-things that are not lichen, that shiver without wind. They are electrostatic. Their hyphae scrape dust for its trace nitrates and they use static to pull vapor out of the mist. At noon you can hear them crackle faintly.

**The cables.** The explorers. Leathery, jointed vines with no roots, no leaves, as thick as your wrist, that throw themselves out of the grove across ten, twenty meters of lethal white rock to where wind has deposited a little more dust in a hollow. Blind and patient, they taste the stone with acid tips. When they find a viable bowl, they swell at the end and bud a new chimney, a daughter reef. If they fail, they desiccate into brittle ropes that wind will later shatter. Most fail.

An Aerolith does not grow. It *establishes.* One successful daughter every few decades is a triumph. The distance between groves — ten kilometers of nothing — is not emptiness. It is scar tissue of all the cables that died reaching.

And because they live so long, they remember, in their way. The older groves, the ones the size of forests, have trunks you can walk inside, hollowed by wind, walls polished by generations of something small and shelled rubbing past. In the heart of the oldest you sometimes find stone that is not stone — the original mother-nodule, a black, pitted concretion of salt and iron and lipid, humming faintly colder than anything around it. It is not alive anymore. It is being lived *in*.

### The Visitors

Nothing lives with the Aerolith permanently that can leave. Residence is too costly.

For insects, look smaller. Not flying — there is no nectar to fly for, no standing water to breed in.

There are **rollers.** At first you think someone dropped pearls in the dust. Then one moves. They are the size of grapes, perfect spheres of interlocking chitin plates, amber and slate. During the heat they seal themselves and roll — wind-pushed — across the bare troughs between groves, which would kill anything else. They are not going anywhere. They are grazing, electrostatically collecting dust on their outer plates, then unrolling at night inside a grove to scrape it clean with a radula. Their tracks in the loess are single, endless straight lines for miles until a gust diverts them.

There are **stilt-walkers.** In the mist under the leaves, barely visible, things like darning needles on stilts six inches tall, moving in slow, ritual detours around the bead-fruits. They are so thin they cast no shadow. They pierce a bead with a hair-fine proboscis, inflate with brine, and walk away even more slowly. They never touch the leaf itself — the plant would seal and trap them.

And there are **glass ticks.** Flat, almost invisible, living on the underside of the leaves among the trichomes. You will only notice them because they are colder than the leaf. They do not drink brine; they drink the waxy coating that prevents the brine from evaporating. Fat, translucent, barely moving. The colony tolerates them the way you tolerate freckles.

All of this is the grove breathing to itself. The real animals are not *of* the grove. They are *above* it.

### The Gliders

You heard them wrong at first. You thought the sudden silence of the brooms was wind ceasing.

It was wings.

They are never on the ground. If you see one grounded, it is already dead — a torn amber kite crumpled against stone, hollow bones picked clean by rollers overnight. While alive, they never land.

High above the terrace, where the pale dome presses down, thermals rip off the white rock at noon like invisible geysers. In that thin, crystal air, something can stay up forever if it is light enough. The gliders are light enough.

Imagine a manta ray drawn by a calligrapher who owned only one line. A central body no bigger than a hare, dense and hot — you can feel the heat coming off one if it hovers near you, mammalian-hot, furred in dusty down to hold it — stretched between two immensely elongated forelimbs. The membrane between is not bat-wing but paper, translucent amber veined with hollow spars, so thin light passes through it and throws a cross on the ground. Their tails are streamers, forked, used as rudders. Their heads are almost nothing: a hooked beak, huge dark eyes that never blink, fringed tongues curled inside like ferns.

They circle so high you mistake them for dust motes, for afterimages. Sometimes a whole kettle of a dozen riding one thermal, not moving, just tilting.

They watch. They can see a single grove throwing its dark blade of shadow from thirty kilometers away. They can see the faint green haze of mist pooled under its canopy.

Then one peels off.

It does not flap. It falls in a great, slow spiral, membrane creaking faintly in the cold — a sound like an old sail. As it nears the canopy it finally beats, once, twice, with terrible economy, and hovers. It never touches leaf or stone. It hangs over the jade plates, unfurls from its chest two secondary fans — smaller, fringed with cilia — and brushes them across the trichomes. The silver hairs discharge with a soft *tick* of static. Brine jumps by capillary action onto the fringe and into the tongue.

It drinks plant blood straight from the air. Thirty seconds, a minute, its throat pulsing, the hot body steaming slightly as cold brine hits hot gut. Then a kick, a tilt, and it is rising again, riding the very heat it stole, gut heavy with fat that will keep it aloft for days.

They are not graceful up close. They are desperate, trembling, working so hard to stay still. You see how thin the membrane is, how you can see its heart hammering through its chest fur. You understand why they never land: they *can't*. On stone there is no thermal to lift that impossible wing again.

At sunset they vanish upward, not downward. They go high enough that the last sun hits them after the terrace is already in indigo, turning each one for a moment into a fleck of fire, then —

Night falls without dusk. The stone pops. Leaves fold shut with audible pops like knuckles cracking, sealing brine in. The felt beads dull. Rollers seal. Stilt-walkers freeze mid-step.

And if you stay very still in the now-freezing mist, inside the grove that has made its own little weather, you will hear what lives here most abundantly of all.

Not the gliders. Not the cables.

The ticking. The faint grit under your boots settling. Your own breath making brief, useless clouds that the leaves would like to have back, if they could.
