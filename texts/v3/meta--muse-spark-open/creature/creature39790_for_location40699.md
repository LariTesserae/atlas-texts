It lives low.

Not nothing - but nothing that stands up to be counted. Nothing that can afford height here.

### The Film

First and everywhere: the film.

You thought the walls were wet rock. They are not rock. They are skin.

The black-green smears you saw from the entrance shaft are not smears. Up close they resolve into mats, into cities - laminated sheets of cyanobacteria and heat-loving algae a millimeter thick and kilometers long if you peeled them and laid them flat. They are the true residents. They own this place.

Where the water sheets thin to a silver skin over the basalt, they hold. Where the weeping walls sweat continuously, they drink light directly. The lattice is an unblinking sun; there is no night, no shade that lasts more than a surge-cycle, and so they do not sleep. They photosynthesize until they bleach themselves, die back to black, and then photosynthesize over their own dead. Layer on layer, year on year. If you cut one it smells of iron and old pennies. If you touch one it comes off on your hand like wet felt.

In the hottest places, where the rock hisses and steam ghosts off it, the mats change. The black-green gives way to rust-red and burnt-orange - *Chloroflexus*, *Thermosynechococcus*, things that like it nearer to boiling. They grow as filaments, as streaming hair in the outflow of each little hot spring, waving like prayer flags in water that never stops moving. They are learning to eat light and sulfur at the same time.

Underneath them, always, the rock is being eaten. They exude acid so slow you would never see it work, but in a decade they have softened bolt-holes into bowls, catwalk stumps into stalagmites. They are the reason the old stair is round. They will be here when the lattice finally stutters.

They are not passive. When a surge scours a channel clean, exposing fresh basalt, within hours the water going over it goes faintly green again. Spores teleport. The field's rude little jumps - a splash here appearing as mist there - carry them for free. The glitch is their wind.

### The Clutchers

Where the mats fail, the clutchers take over.

They cannot live in the full violence of the floor. They need a flaw in the stone - a crack, a lip, a place where the scour pauses for a second longer.

There, the pale ferns live. They are not pale because they are sick; they are pale because they have thrown away almost all their chlorophyll. In this light you don't need much. Their fronds are translucent yellow-white, veined in visible red, no bigger than your hand, curled tight against the stone like fists. Their roots are not roots so much as grapples - black wiry cables that drill millimeters into basalt and swell when wet, locking them in place. You could not pull one out with your teeth. During a surge they flatten, go utterly still, and let brown water hammer over them. Afterwards they shake and open again.

With them, in the same cracks, are the lichens - tight gray-green rosettes that look like splash-marks, and the real mosses: tight, dark cushions that store ten times their weight in water and then wring themselves out slowly over hours. They have learned the timing of the fissure's breath. Right before the fog plume comes, they swell. Right after it rains, they close.

None of them are tall because tall is death. Anything that lifts its head into the sheet-flow is ripped off. So they hug. They creep. They make soil out of their own bodies in cracks no wider than a finger.

### The Marsh Ring

The only thing that gets to be vertical is the marsh.

It has made a deal with the chamber. The central pool - the rust-colored knee-deep basin that drains and surges - is the one place where the water table is allowed to be still for more than a minute. The field holds it there, cupped in invisible topology. So a ring has formed around its edge: reeds and succulents and nothing else.

The reeds are thin as pencils, stiff as wire, pale at the base and then almost white at the tip where the light has bleached them. They grow not from soil but from a floating mat of their own rhizomes, interwoven into a raft that rides the surge up and down. When the pool drops and exposes mud, the raft settles, sighs, waits. When the pool slams back, the raft lifts without tearing. If you pushed one, the whole ring would wobble. Underneath the raft, in the anoxic mud, blind white nodules fix nitrogen and smell like rot.

Between the reeds are the succulents - and they are a joke the ecology is playing on itself. They are glassy, swollen, fat-fingered things full of stored fresh water in a place where fresh water falls from every wall. But the water here is not fresh. It is mineral, iron-heavy, hot, teleported and re-condensed a hundred times until it is bitter with dissolved rock. So the succulents distill. They take in the bitter sheet, exude salt from pores on their leaves as white crust, and keep the clean inside. Break one and it weeps clear, drinkable water for a few seconds before the chamber reclaims it. The midges know this. The salamander knows this.

Both plants tremble in unison each time the pool surges. Not from wind. From the impact.

### The Flicker

Animals cannot live here permanently. The chamber is too bright, too loud, too restless to nest in.

So they visit.

The midges are born in the steam columns themselves - larvae that live in the mineral crust at the edge of a hissing vent where no predator can follow because the water is 60 degrees and the air shivers. They pupate in a day, hatch in a cloud of thousands, mate in the warm fog plume from the fissure, lay, and die before the next big surge washes the crust clean. You see them as flicker, as dust motes that decide to be alive for an afternoon. They are the base of everything else.

And then, if you are patient, if you stand on the halfway ledge and let your eyes go slack between surges, *it* comes out.

The salamander. Or rather, the salamanders - because there are perhaps a dozen in the whole chamber, and you will never see two at once.

It is not like the cave salamanders of the world above. Those are blind and slow. This one has eyes - tiny, lidless, jet-black - because there is too much light not to see. Its skin is translucent pink, shrimp-pink, you can see its heart beating through its side and the dark line of its gut full of midge larvae and mat-filaments. It is neotenic, forever larval, with a frill of external gills like red ferns that it flares when oxygen is low.

It lives in the film. It swims not in the pool but in the ankle-deep sheet that braids over the whole floor, absurdly shallow water for an aquatic animal - but it has learned to be flat. It can wriggle through a layer no thicker than its own body, pushing with its absurdly small legs. It knows every crack, every bolt-hole filled with calcite that still holds a pocket of cool. When the surge comes, it does not fight. It goes limp, lets itself be tumbled into a fissure, holds with its mouth, waits. When the water runs uphill for a handspan because the field forgets gravity, the salamander runs with it, experiencing a second of weightlessness before remembering to swim.

It is alone in its trophic level. Nothing eats it. The drones ignore it. It will die of old age or of being flash-evaporated by a rogue droplet and no other reason.

Sometimes, at night - though there is no night - you might see a second animal: a cave cricket, long-legged and ghost-white, that has fallen down the entrance shaft and is still trying to find a dark corner that doesn't exist. It never lasts.

### The Other Tenants

And then there are the ones that were left to live here.

The monitoring pylon in the central pool is not a machine. It is a resident. It has stood knee-high in rust water for longer than any fern, its tip projecting its thin, useless red line that no one reads. Its alloy does not rust because its field repels water at the molecular level; the pool beads off it, leaving it perpetually pristine. It measures depth. When the pool surges over it, it notes it. When the pool drains, it notes it. It will still be noting it when the marsh raft has rotted and the mats have covered it.

The disk-drones - three, sometimes four - are its restless children. No bigger than plates, they skate not on air but on the lattice field itself, dipping to taste with long proboscises. They taste pH, salinity, temperature, spore count, and whisper it on a band no human ear hears to the empty smart-fabric tent three meters up that flaps and flaps and has not been occupied in five years. They scatter when the pool surges not because they must but because their programming tells them turbulence is inefficient. They return because their programming tells them this is home. They have learned to ride the topology errors like birds ride thermals; you will sometimes see one hover, wobble, and simply *appear* a meter to the left where a droplet did.

They are not alive, but they are endemic. Remove them and the chamber would be emptier in a way that would register as death.

And above it all, the lattice itself - the three rings turning, turning, matte black and then iridescent, humming in your molars. It is not alive and it does not mean to make weather. It holds up fifty thousand tons of rock without touching it, and the warm wet breathing is just waste heat, just squeezed air, just a side effect of holding the sky up. But to everything below, it is the sun and the god and the roof that never leaks.

It does not know they are there.

They have built their entire world in the shadow of a thing that never knew it was casting one - low, wet, trembling, bleached, stubborn, clinging to every crack where the water lets them live for another hour.
