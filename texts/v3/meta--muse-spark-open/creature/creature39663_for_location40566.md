Nothing here *evolved* to live here.

That is the first thing to understand. This mountain hasn't been here long enough for evolution. Everything that lives here arrived, or was invited, or leaked through.

There are three kinds of life on the Unfinished Stone. Only one of them breathes.

### 1. The Anchors Are Still Alive

You thought the buildings were abandoned. They aren't.

They are the oldest residents, and they are not buildings at all.

**The Ribcage** — the frame of matte white ribs on the eastern lip — is awake and working. If you sit beside it for an hour, you will see it *inhale*. The air around it shivers and the condensation reverses, drawn up off the black glass in fine threads toward its struts. It hums, not with electricity but with effort. Its job is to tell gravity to be polite, to tell basalt to fold like paper, to tell heat to stay close to the ground. The stone under it is perceptibly warmer. It grows. You can watch lichen try to colonize the fresh terraces and fail because by evening the terrace has moved a centimeter.

It does not know you are there. Or it does not care. It was told to make a mountain and it is making one without supervision. It will probably keep doing so until it burns out or someone tells it to stop.

**The Disc** that the basalt grew around is sleeping, but not dead. Its top is smooth, warm, dry no matter how much water sheets over it. Press your palm to it and you feel a slow thud, every eleven seconds, like a heart beating through insulation. Underneath, in the rock, something extends roots made of the same field that holds the haze in place. This one makes *more* — more mineral, more warmth, more permissiveness. The moss grows thickest near it, the amber beetles run in densest rivers toward it. It's a nurse.

**The Door** is the only one that is not a builder. It is a wound. It stands alone because it was meant to have walls around it and never got them. The air on its far side is ten degrees colder and smells of iodine and abyssal pressure. That is not metaphor. Put your hand through — if you dare — and your knuckles go numb in water that isn't there.

It lives too, after a fashion. Because something uses it.

All the dragonfly-things know not to fly through it. Even when chased, they will carom violently aside rather than cross its threshold. There are no webs, no egg cases, no beetle trails within two meters of it. Animals know a predator's trail when they smell it, even if the predator lives in another ocean.

Sometimes at night — though it's never really night under the lantern-haze — the Door sweats differently. Condensation on its frame runs *up*. And you hear, from nowhere, a wet click, as of something large testing the air with an appendage that has never learned what air is.

### 2. The Opportunists

The true abundance here is not the builders. It's the squatters who found a warm cradle with no winter, no frost, endless water straight out of the sky, endless minerals leaching from baby rock, and no established hierarchy to keep them in check.

This is a weed-ecology. An r-selected frenzy. Everything here lives fast, breeds fast, dies fast.

**On the Stone:**

The amber beetles are not a species. Are *rivers* of a species. Thumb-sized, dome-backed, translucent amber with dark gut-lines visible when they’ve fed, they move in braided columns hundreds of thousands strong between moss cushions. The moss — that tight black-green cushion that sighs when it drinks — is their cattle, their farm, their city. They prune it, they ferry its spores on their backs, they defend it. Watch closely and you’ll see they carry tiny globules of water balanced on hairs to irrigate new patches. Watch longer and you’ll see the price.

The **Still-Mantis** lives among them. It is not a mantis. It is the size of your hand, the color of wet basalt with a dusting of mineral white that makes it invisible when it tucks. It has six limbs folded like origami knives. It does not hunt. It waits on a beetle highway for hours, perfectly still while water sheets over its back, until — *snap* — faster than you can track. One beetle gone. The column flows around the predator without panic. There are always more beetles. A few meters away another Still-Mantis has already struck. The sound you thought was twigs breaking is their chorus. A percussion of execution all day long under the haze.

**In the Water:**

Because the water has no channel it is everywhere shallow, braided, an inch deep over hot rock. And it is alive in a way lowland streams never are.

Lift a handful and it writhes. The **Many-Eyed Tadpoles** — each no bigger than a grain of rice, each with a ring of five black photoreceptors around a pulsing gut — travel in whorls and vortexes so dense the water looks cloudy. They are filterers and grazers and, when one dies, instant scavengers. They vibrate. Put your ear to a pool and you hear them, a faint electric fizz.

They are food for the **Glass Shrimp-Hoppers**, who are not shrimp either. Clear as water with a single red digestive tract, they catapult themselves from braid to braid with a flex of a spring-loaded tail, landing with an audible *tick* on the stone. In air for a half-second they glitter, then vanish again into water. If you stay crouched, one will misjudge and land on your boot, stare at you with stalk eyes, and fire again.

And twice an hour, white as ash, comes the **Moth Snow**. Clouds of needle-moths — bodies as thin as eyelashes, wings too small to be useful — hatch synchronously from moss, flutter for forty minutes in the warm bathwater air, mate in mid-air in frantic spirals, lay eggs on any damp surface, and die. They fall like actual snow into the water braids where tadpoles boil up to eat them. The next generation is already hatching before the water clears. The whole bowl smells briefly of wax and ozone when they fall.

**In the Air:**

The hunters you saw are the princes of this place. **Smoked-Glass Emperors.**

Forearm-long dragonfly-things, but wrong in the joints — four wings instead of two, each wing thick at the base like brushed obsidian fading to transparent at the tip. They hover by warping the field the Anchors make, not just by beating air, which is why their halos stutter when they pass close to the Ribcage. Their heads are all eye, iridescent green-black, faceted. Their larvae are aquatic terrors among the tadpoles; their adults never land. They drink on the wing, skimming condensation from your sleeve, from the Disc, from each other's backs. They are territorial, they dogfight in silent, intricate spirals, they catch shrimp-hoppers mid-leap with a basket of spines under their thorax.

You are not prey to them. You are landscape. They will perch — if you can call hovering an inch from your shoulder perching — and look at you with that huge compound gaze, judging whether you cast a shadow worth hunting in. Then dismiss you.

None of these things sing. No bird has found this island in the sky yet. Instead the bowl is a factory floor at full shift: clicks, ticks, snaps, thrums, drips, hisses where water hits hotter stone underneath. If you close your eyes it sounds almost industrial.

### 3. The Veil-Grazer

And then there is what you heard and did not see.

High overhead, in the twenty meters of luminous haze that is not weather, something large lives permanently aloft. You never see it clearly because the light gives no shadows and its skin makes none.

It is not a bird. It is not a machine.

Think of a manta made of dusk, thirty feet across, translucent as jellyfish skin stretched over a kite of hollow bone, with a vast, frilled mouth turned perpetually downward. It doesn't flap so much as *lean* on the construction field, riding the heat the Anchors exhale.

It feeds on the haze itself — on the constant condensation of water from nothing, on the needle-moths, on the very humidity. As it passes, it condenses a cold rain beneath it: ten seconds of violent pattering wherever its shadow would be. The water beads on its belly and sluices into its maw. The Emperor dragonflies scatter before it, not in fear, but like gulls before a whale — annoyed, displaced.

Once a day it must descend to brush the highest folds of basalt to scrape mineral biofilm from the hot glass with a rasping underside. That's when you hear it — a low thrumming sigh, a wet membranous kiss against stone. That's what touched the Door.

It is the only thing here that might be old. It might have come *with* the Anchors, seeded intentionally as a gardener, a grazer to keep the pioneer explosion from choking on itself. It might have drifted here through the Door when it was first opened, smelling warmth above an ocean trench kilometers down, and decided to stay.

It circles. It feeds. It rains. It does not land.

---

If you stay until your boots leave permanent puddles, until the fresh terrace under the Ribcage is high enough to trip you, you will notice the real resident is none of these.

The mountain itself is alive — not metaphorically. Not basalt and biology. But as a process. A conversation between field and rock that has learned to invite guests.

The moss came because the Disc kept it warm. The beetles came because the moss was there. The mantises came because the beetles were there. The dragonflies came because everything else was there to eat. The Veil-Grazer came because the air was thick enough to swim in.

They are not an ecosystem yet. They are a queue. A line forming at a door that hasn't finished being built, waiting for the house to decide what rooms it will have.

Every trickle you see is still the first trickle to take that path. Every death is still the first death of its kind. Give it a hundred years and erosion will start, rivers will cut, soil will gather, birds will finally arrive and find the dragonflies naive. Or give it a week and whoever left the tools running will come back and turn them off, and this warm bathwater Eden will flash-freeze into proper alpine stone, and everything that thrums and ticks here will die in an afternoon, perfect and preserved like insects in amber.

Until then, the bowl breathes out heat, the Anchors hum, the amber rivers flow uphill, and all that fast, cheap, beautiful, doomed life does what life does fastest when it finds a world that hasn't learned to be hostile yet:

It fills it.
