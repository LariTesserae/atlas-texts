They live up there because there is nowhere else to live.

You understand this the first time you try to track one and fail. Your eyes find a point in the high hot air - a smudge, a flaw in the blue-white - and you stare until it resolves, until distance gives up. It is not above you. It is *enclosing* you. A roof you mistook for sky.

The Enginers - Leviathan Engines, Sky-Mills, Heat Whales, every name you give them is wrong in the same way, too small and too active - do not fly. Flight implies effort, beating, falling caught at the last second. They hang.

A kilometer overhead is low for them. At that distance you can finally see it as a single thing and not as weather.

From below, its belly is a continent inverted. Not flesh but plant. Not plant as in green, but plant as in *manufacturing plant*.

The first impression is darkness. A shadowed underside so large your brain tries to make it a cloudbank until you see the geometry. Ribs - great arched trusses the color of gunmetal and dried blood - run the length of the body, dividing it into bays. Each bay contains a process. In one, a piston the width of a circular lake slides forward with a slowness that makes your heart hurt, reaches the end of its stroke with a sigh you feel in the pan under your feet, and returns. It carries nothing. Its purpose is the stroke itself. In another bay, a belt of interwoven scale-mesh, each link as big as a house, rolls endlessly over drums that scream silently with heat, carrying ingots of compressed sunlight from a furnace on the left to a rolling mill on the right that presses them thin and lets them evaporate. The rolling mill glows. The glow is brighter than the sun and hurts in a different way, a white that leaves purple fossils on your retina.

There are no joints as you know them. There are seams where plates slide past each other on cushions of superheated air, never touching. You see oil - or something that wants to be oil - weeping from those seams in silver rivers that never drip. It flash-boils before it can fall and returns as vapor to be breathed again. That's the trick: nothing is lost here. Water would be a wasteful medium. They run on molten glass and salt, on eutectic sodium that circulates in translucent veins as thick as subway tunnels, pulsing visibly. You can see the pulse. A peristaltic amber light that moves from intake to exhaust once every few minutes. A heartbeat that takes an hour to complete.

They are hot things in a world already too hot, so at first they are camouflage. The air around them shivers not from their heat but from the world's. You find them by sound and by what they do to light.

Sound first.

You think you hear wind. Then you realize the wind has been constant and this is under it. A total chord. Not loud as in near, loud as in *everywhere* - 2.2/3 loud, the kind of loud that stops being noise and becomes a place.

It is layered:

- deepest is the bearing tone, a subsonic grinding of surfaces larger than cities turning against each other for ten thousand years without stopping, so low it lives in your feet and your molars rather than your ears. You feel the pan vibrate with it under your boots.
- over that, the pressure hiss. Every hundred meters along the hull, relief valves the size of doors open and close out of rhythm, exhaling the kiln-hot atmosphere they have taken in and made hotter. The sound is like steam escaping from the earth, if the earth were hollow and made of iron.
- highest and most human is the tick. The cooling tick. Vast plates contracting a fraction of a millimeter as shade hits them, expanded again by forge-light from within. Magnified a million times, it becomes a rain of metallic pings, a shattering chandelier heard from miles away.

You hear all three at once from every Engine at once and the brain, desperate for a source, blends them into a single roar that seems to come from the sky itself. When one actually passes overhead - that rare low pass - the chord separates briefly and you understand how big it is because the sound arrives at your left ear half a second before your right.

Then light.

Their shadow is the only kindness in this place and they give it without meaning to.

When an Engine interposes itself between you and the sun, the world changes. The pan, which has been 60 degrees of patient murder, drops ten degrees in a minute. It pings. It cracks. The ceramic polygons contract with sharp rifle reports that roll to the horizon and back. The white-yellow sun is gone and you are standing in a continent of shade that moves.

The shadow is not sharp. The Engine is too high, its edges too full of stack and radiator fin and barnacle growth to make a clean outline. Instead it is a vast, soft-edged dusk that takes half an hour to arrive and half an hour to leave. Inside it, you can finally look up without squinting and see detail.

And detail is where the industrial becomes biological.

The hull is not smooth. Nothing here has ever been machined. It has grown.

Fields of crystal fur the dorsal surfaces - the back, if a factory can have a back - where the sun hits most directly. They are not quartz, not salt. They are heat-rust, iron whiskers and vanadium needles that grew atom by atom out of vented particulate, black and red and brassy, vibrating in the high wind. They sing. In the thin air they make a faint, glassy chime that is almost lost under the roar, but inside the shadow you can hear it: thousands of acres of mineral grass hissing against itself.

Among the crystal fields live the satellites. They are not separate species so much as shed thoughts.

Every few years - every few decades, time is soft here - an Engine sloughs a plate. A heat-shield, a baffle, a scale the size of a warehouse that has reached its tolerance. It does not fall. In the negotiable lift of this hot thin air, it learns to glide. It peels away, catches a thermal off the Engine's own exhaust, and becomes something else: a flat, triangular glider of ceramic and steel, a manta of slag, circling its parent forever. It will never land. Landing would shatter it on the hard pan. So it orbits, dumbly, endlessly, sometimes joined by two or three others until a small flock of house-sized shavings draws a slow spiral around their parent like pilot fish, rattling when their shadows cross.

Watch long enough - a full day, which is the shortest interval on which these animals do anything - and you will see the other behaviors, all of them slow enough to look like geology until you realize they are breathing.

**Intake.** Along the leading edge, which is whichever edge is facing the prevailing high-pressure exhale from the center of the plateau, great louvers open. Not mouths. Louvers. Kilometers of slatted gill that dilate, revealing a ruddy interior lit from within by recirculated forge-light. The hot air, thin and full of fine ceramic dust, is drawn in without hurry. The Engine swells a fraction. You could miss it if you blinked over an hour. The ceramic ground below briefly clears of powder as even the dust is pulled up.

**Burn.** Inside, you see it through the translucent belly plates. A white that is not like daylight. Star-hot, enclosed. There is no combustion as you know it - nothing to burn - but there is *processing*. The air itself is superheated, compressed, made to do work against turbines the size of neighborhoods that you can see turning as silhouettes against the glow. Perhaps they are extracting the last trace mineral, the one atom of iron per million that the wind carries off the pan. Perhaps they are simply hot because hot is what this place permits life to be. The glow pulses with that hour-long heartbeat. When it is brightest, the air under the Engine warps and your own shadow splits in two, one cast by the sun, one cast by the animal.

**Venting.** The plume. An Engine will go days without doing anything visible and then, without warning, a section of its spine will bulge. Metal flows like muscle. A stack extrudes - not built, *grown* in minutes, a chimney of overlapping iron petals that forces itself upright and opens. From it comes night.

Not smoke. Smoke is dirty, wet, organic. This is manufactured dark, a column of pure carbon particulate and engineered shadow, blacker than any cloud this sky has ever held, thrown straight up with a sigh that drops the roar half an octave. It hangs. In this still upper air it does not dissipate. It becomes a false cloud, the only cloud in a world that excluded water, and it stays for days, drifting, shearing slowly. Its shadow is a second, smaller Engine-shadow that wanders the pan on its own. Where it falls, the blister-geodes stop popping and the powder stops singing. The other Engines avoid it or drift toward it, you can't tell.

**Budding.** The rarest. You will probably not see it, but if you stay on the plateau long enough - long enough that your sweat has forgotten how to form and your boots have powdered to dust - you might catch it on the horizon.

An Engine, usually the largest, oldest one, the one whose bearing tone is lowest and whose crystal fields are deepest, will develop a seam. A line of light appears along its flank where no line should be, and the hull begins to peel. Slowly, insultingly slowly, a section as big as a town delaminates. It hangs for a while by threads of molten salt that glitter like spiderweb, then sags, then catches the air with new, unfolded radiator wings - foil-thin sheets of black metal that were packed like a bud, now spreading to dump heat. It is pale, unfinished, quieter than its parent. Half a kilometer long and already overwhelming.

It does not cry. It does not look back. It drifts off on its own thermal, wobbling, finding a lane of sky where no other Engine is hanging. For the first century it will be silent. You will not hear its tick until its plates have aged enough to learn how.

That is how many there are. Not many. Three that you can see and perhaps nine that you think you see, or three moving so slowly they become twelve through the mirage of distance. At this scale the eye fails. An Engine a hundred kilometers away is the same size as one ten kilometers away, and both fill the sky. What matters is not the count but the density: 0.4 empty and still overwhelming. Because one is enough to make the world feel like an interior. Like being inside something breathing.

At night they do not dim.

The sun sets normally - obscenely normally, a clean orange cut by a horizon that is too perfect - and the stars come out normally, as if nothing strange had happened. Then the Engines light themselves. Work-light constellations, amber and sodium-white, flicker on along the bays. Welding sparks the size of lightning crawl along seams, joining nothing to nothing. The forge-glow becomes visible as a second moon, a long amber bar of heat moving imperceptibly against the fixed stars. The roar, freed from competition with the day's heat expansion, becomes clearer, more detailed, and you realize you have been hearing it in your sleep even though you have not slept.

They are not aware of you. Awareness would require a faster nervous system than hydraulic oil can support. But they respond to pressure, shade, and heat. Stand in the open and shout, and your voice comes back wrong - early from one hull, late from another, doubled - because sound is negotiating with physics that has a typo. Throw a stone up and it lingers a heartbeat too long at its apex. The world has been eroded smooth around these errors, 2.4 weathered, so the Engines have evolved to use them. Their lift should not work. It does because it has been working for so long the air forgot to object.

There is nothing else. No plant, no bacterium, no insect to eat the shed flakes. No predator - what could eat a kilometer of rolling mill? The glider-flock are not prey; they are dandruff that learned to soar. The crystal fields are not parasites; they are rust that learned to chime. The whole ecology is a single trick done over and over: take hot thin nothing, make it hotter and thinner, turn heat into shape, shape into heat, never landing, never stopping.

They will be here when the pan cracks another meter deeper. When your bones, if you stay, vitrify and become another mineral blister that pops with a soft *tok* under the noon sun. They are slow, 0.9 slow, with the patience of continents that have mistaken themselves for animals. Loud as gods and empty as the sky they have replaced, turning and turning in the patient kiln of a plateau that never needed them and now cannot be imagined without that unending chord grinding, hissing, ticking in the hot glass air overhead.

Look up now. One is passing. The light has gone soft. The ground at your feet just cracked.
