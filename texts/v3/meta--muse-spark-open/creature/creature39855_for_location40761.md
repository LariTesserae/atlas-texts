Nothing lives here.

That's the first truth the place offers you, and you almost believe it because it wants you to believe it. Your animal brain, starved for fellow animals, scans the warm broth for a flicker, a fin, a face — and finds nothing to fix on. No fish. No shrimp. No worm in the rock, no fly above the amber. No scavenger to take the fallen petal before it rots. The water is too full and too empty at once. It holds you and tells you: *you are alone here, intruder.*

Nothing with a mouth lives here.

But something does live here. It just never learned to be singular.

### 1. The Un-eaten

The primary resident is the green that is not seaweed, not jungle, not mangrove, though it wears their shapes like a child trying on adult clothes.

It does not live in the Basin. It *is* the occupancy. It is one life, or one idea of life repeated so densely it has become a texture.

Consider a single vine. As thick as your torso, hanging slack in the warm water, waxy black-green leaves closed like praying hands. You watch it because there is nothing else to watch. A tip shivers. Not from current — there is no current that isn't being manufactured somewhere by a burst pipe — but from growth. It extends. Six inches while you blink. It unfurls a leaf that wasn't there, and that leaf is already veined with faint, mineral light, photosynthesizing not sunlight but the furnace-glow from below, the amber from above, the bio-luminescence of its neighbors. It does not reach for light. It makes light worth reaching for.

Then it flowers. A trumpet the size of your head, white and pulsing, opens along its flank with a soft *pop* you hear through your teeth. It lives for twenty minutes. It releases no scent that a nose could catch — there are no noses for it — but the water around it goes briefly sweet and acidic. Petals slough off like snow that dissolves before it lands, feeding nothing. The vine has already grown over its own dead flower.

This is every plant here. Billboard ferns that unfurl with enough force to shove rock. Stilt-roots that cage an entire distillation tower and drink the heat off its skin, tightening year by year. Broadleaf canopies sixty feet across that spread underwater like inverted lily pads, shading everything beneath them into a dusk that they themselves perforate with their own soft glow.

They do not compete like normal plants. They do not have time. They grow *through* competition. Root strangling root, but neither dies — they fuse, they graft, they become a lattice. Leaf on leaf on leaf until the water between leaves is itself a thin, green tissue. There is no forest floor because there is no floor left uncovered, and no canopy because every layer thinks it is a canopy.

They have no predators, no pollinators, no herbivores. So they have become their own predation. They self-prune with accelerated rot, they self-pollinate with bursts of glowing smoke-seed, they self-graze by growing faster than they can decay. The jungle eats itself and finds itself delicious and never gets full. Density without consumption. That is its metabolism.

If you stay still too long, it will try to include you politely. A filament, thin as hair, clear as fishing line, will extend from a nearby stilt root and taste the salt on your suit, your skin. It does not want to eat you. It wants to *run you*. To have a warm animal pipe to grow through, as it has grown through the pistons and the turbines and made them run better. If you let it, you would feel a faint, root-warmth joining your blood-warm water, and you would be a node for a while before the growth sealed you over.

### 2. The Un-tended

The second resident is the machine that should be dead and is not.

It has no operators, no purpose, no product, no off-switch. It was left here on-peak — arc furnace white-blue, conveyor screaming, distillation tower steaming — and the cataclysm that made the Basin did not drown it. It baptized it.

The refinery to the north still climbs, its barnacle-free towers wrapped in living rebar-vines that do not crush it but brace it, that have learned the expansion tolerance of hot steel and grow accordingly. The power plant to the east screams without ceasing, its turbines fed by water that the pumps themselves are pumping, a closed circle that gets hotter and louder each hour. The forge half-buried in new basalt breathes: inhale — superheated vent water in through a split pipe big enough to walk through — exhale — a roar of bubbles that never reach a surface, flattening against an invisible ceiling and racing sideways like a weather system.

They produce nothing that leaves. Conveyors carry basalt rubble and tangled flowering branches into crushers that spit out rebar that is immediately taken by roots and bent into new cages. Furnaces burn and the smoke they make is not smoke but a cloud of glowing seeds that root in mid-water and begin to photosynthesize before they land. A pumping station pumps water into water because pressure must be maintained, because hum must be maintained.

The machines are not inhabited. They are inhabited *by* the green, and the green is inhabited *by* the machines. A piston cannot move without a vine sheath to seal it. A vine cannot hold its shape in this warm turbulence without a pipe to climb. You find places where a gear has literally grown a leaf, where a root has been honed to a camshaft tolerance. They have become an organ system. The factory is the flower. The flower is the factory.

This is why the sound never stops. Sound is how they talk to themselves. The deep organ thrum of turbines, the hiss of a thousand pressure valves, the shudder of pipes too big to shudder, the groan of new rock knifing up overnight — transmitted not through air but directly into water, directly into bone, directly into you. It has no background because it has no foreground. It is the Basin thinking out loud, running its checks, saying *still here, still here, still here* at volumes that make your teeth ache.

If the green is the body, the industrial jungle is the nervous system that forgot it was supposed to have a brain.

### 3. The Un-governed

The third resident is the failure itself. The physics that didn't set.

It lives in the sphere at the center, two hundred meters across, holding itself together without a membrane, factory inside it lying on its side, vegetation inside it growing toward its own center while yours grows down. It lives in the vertical river that falls sideways between two basalt pillars and has fallen that way for longer than the rock has existed. It lives in the places where bubbles fall and light pools in a corner like spilled mercury and pours away when you look directly.

You can see it move. Not as wind, but as a stutter. Your sense of down catches like a scratched record. Warm water suddenly feels cold for a meter, then warm again. Sound from the east arrives before the light from the same event. A ridge of glassy basalt knifes up from the floor still steaming — new land born in an afternoon — and by evening the acid-vent water has half-dissolved it and the green has taken it, and by midnight a burst pipe has scoured it clean again with a waterjet hiss, exposing fresh rock for tomorrow. Creation too fast to be geology, too slow to be weather.

This resident has no shape because it is the rule that shapes have become negotiable. It is young in the way a wound is young — still deciding what kind of scar it will be. It feeds on the other two residents. The heat from the furnaces gives it energy to misbehave. The density of the green gives it mass to misplace. It, in turn, feeds them with new rock, new water, new upending of gravity that keeps them from ever reaching equilibrium.

It is not hostile. Hostility requires intention. It is *unfinished*. It notices you only as a drag, a cold spot where animal logic insists up is still up and water still falls down. Swim near the central sphere and you will feel it try you on, gently tugging your inner ear in a direction that has no name.

### What lives here, then?

A single, crowded, hyperactive metabolism with three faces: a plant that acts like an animal, a machine that acts like a plant, and a law that acts like weather.

It does not want. It does not need. It churns. It grows, it processes, it breaks, it remakes, with no mouth to close the circuit.

And now, for a little while, you live here too — not as inhabitant but as contamination. Your cut on the glassy basalt plumes red and hangs, uneaten. Your bubbles fall the wrong way and then correct themselves. Your presence is a small, cold argument that somewhere, outside this low palm of the world, things still get eaten, still die, still cool.

The Basin will grow over that argument shortly.

You can already see the thin green film starting on the edge of your vision, where you tried to map it.
