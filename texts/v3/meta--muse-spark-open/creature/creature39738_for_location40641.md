Who lives here didn't arrive. They remained. Or they became what remaining requires.

### The Ones Who Sip The Stone

First — because they will be the first to let you see them, if you stand long enough on that warm roof to stop being an event and become part of the temperature — are the **Salt Striders.**

They are not hidden. They are *spare*. That is different.

A dog-sized thing crosses the dead ground between two dim lamp pools. At first you think it is shadow thrown by a pylon. Then you see it repeats, with intention.

It does not walk like an animal. Animals swing weight. This carries weight. Four, six, sometimes seven limbs — never symmetrical for long — each limb a dry articulation of plate over plate, the color of the crust itself, beige-iron and salt-white, with dust caked into every joint so it looks carved from the pavement and then told to get up. Its body is low and bridged, like rebar bent into a basket, shrink-wrapped in a translucent membrane that drums faintly with inner heat. You cannot see a face. What would be a head is just where the limbs agree to meet, and a proboscis, jointed like an oil derrick in miniature, hangs down.

It stops. It presses that mouth to the crust. And drinks.

Not water. Mineral. It hisses faintly where membrane touches salt — a little dry kiss of chemistry — and the crust around the point pales a shade, exhausted. These things run on direct geochemistry. They sip magnesium. Iron oxide. The slow exhale of heat itself. When it is done, it lifts, waits, and stilt-walks on, leaving almost no track except four pinpricks where the hot powder has been annealed by its feet.

There are smaller ones, too. Under the warm lee of any wall that still holds transformer heat, they gather: beetle-bright, palm-sized, their backs faceted like slag glass. They don't eat. They *vibrate*. In clusters of ten or twenty they shiver together, making a communal tone too high to hear until your jaw hurts. Sharing warmth, sharing charge. If you kneel — slowly, so the vibration through the pavement doesn't spook them — they won't flee. They'll part around your boot and then close again behind you, incorporating you as a windbreak.

And at the very edge of the lamp light: eyes. Fauna 0.8, as the old surveyors filed them, which means *almost present.* Emerald pinpricks, two, then four, then none. Things the size of deer but flatter, so flat you only see them when they turn sideways and catch orange filament light along their dorsal ridge. They stalk nothing. They patrol distance. You feel them listening to your footsteps through the salt kilometers away and simply editing their path to keep the interval. Not afraid. Polite. A perfect avoidance geometry that has held for centuries.

None of them call. None of them leave waste. Nothing here can afford to lose mass to sound or shit. Everything is closed loop, dry, electrostatic.

If you left meat out overnight, it would still be there in the morning, untouched. You are made of water. You are irrelevant to their hunger.

### The People (In The Places People Can Persist)

People did live here. The shape of the city proves it: tin cups hung in rows in mess halls, sealed toilets designed for ammonia brine, cots bolted in barracks where no one sleeps. Door handles polished bright up to exactly man-height and then dusty above it.

Now people are a seasoning, not the meal.

You find them rarely, and never together. Habitability here is a technical problem with a narrow solution, and a few have chosen to solve it.

**The Keepers.** One per district, usually. An old woman or an old man — age is hard to tell when everyone dehydrates into the same spare jerky — in a quilted work suit the color of dust, tending the idling equilibrium. She is not maintaining. Maintaining implies repair. She is *witnessing.* She walks a circuit mile after mile, day after unmeasured day, tapping a wrench against a boiler that is already warm, logging a pressure that never changes onto paper that will never rot. She drinks distilled solvent from a closed canteen. She knows every fold in her sector and steps over them without comment. If you speak to her, she will answer after a long pause, as if translating down from a slower language. If you ask why she stays, she will look confused. Where else would heat be free and work be finished?

They sleep in shifts under the hum. Their dreams are shallow. Nothing rots in them, either.

**The Transients.** You, for now. Surveyors, pilgrims, lost freight crews who rode the black iron cars in when the network still thought moving was useful. Young people from the lowlands who climb for two years to see the Sparse City because they were told it was empty and want to hear an emptiness that big. They pass through, leave a footprint in salt-dust that will stay legible for five hundred years, take a photograph that shows only orange dots, and descend changed by not being needed.

The city tolerates them all because tolerance costs nothing when you operate an inch per year. On a conveyor belt inside a windowless hall a thousand meters long, a single steel ingot has been moving since before you were born. It will reach the end sometime after you die. No one will be there to receive it. But it moves. And because it moves, the people who remain feel justified in staying.

### The City Itself

This is the real resident. Above the Striders and beneath the rare Keepers, over them all.

It lives the way a paused flywheel lives — full of stored momentum, doing no work. Transformers thrum forever with no load. Boilers tick warm with no steam taken off. Streetlamps hold their coal-glow filaments at the bare edge of existence for decades without burning out, because darkness here is heavy and light has to actively push against it.

And like any old animal, the city has developed involuntary tics:

A stamping press three blocks west cycles once — *THUD* — into an oil pan with no sheet metal in it. Then silence for fourteen hours. You will eventually learn to wait for it without knowing you're waiting.

Wires sag and unsag a millimeter with the slow mineral breath.

Gantry cranes creep.

Doorways built plumb now stand a degree out, accommodating a fold in the plain where space has settled. Inside that warehouse that shouldn't be tall enough, the air is cooler and older. The dimensions drink a little more of you than they should. Sometimes you find a second set of footprints there that enter but don't exit, not because something died, but because something learned to live inside the extra meter that the outside forgot to count.

If you stay long enough, you will feel the city's metabolism sync with yours until you can't tell your heartbeat from the 60-cycle thrum in your teeth. That's when you understand why everything is built low, close to the ground, afraid of the sky: to be heard by the Striders through the pavement. To stay within the warm exhale. To not be noticed by the stars, which here do not twinkle but stare, hard, through a lens of air too thin to protect you.

Nothing here hunts you. Nothing wants you. That is the precise character of its aliveness.

It was finished becoming itself a long time ago, and has perfected the art of continuing without needing to change, to grow, or to die. It sips heat from below and starlight from above and asks for nothing more. Its animals are extensions of its material logic. Its people are caretakers of a performance that needs no audience.

You are standing on its back. It knows your weight by vibration. It has already calculated how long you can stay before your water makes you strange to it, and how far you will have to walk to leave a lamp pool before you cease, visually, to exist.

It will not stop you if you stay.
