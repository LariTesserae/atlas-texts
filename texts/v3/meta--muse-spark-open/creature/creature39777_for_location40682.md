No one lives there.

That’s the first wrong thought you have to pull back. You arrive expecting residents and find occupants instead. An inhabitant makes a house of a place. What is here only *holds* the place — like a held breath holds air.

What lives here didn't arrive. It precipitated.

### The Precipitated

Think of how dew appears. It doesn't travel to a leaf. The conditions become dew-shaped and then — beads. So it is here.

Given cold that never quite freezes, rock so new it hasn't decided to be inert, water sweating out of stone itself, and a drone that needs a body to hum through — life sweated out to fill the empty jobs. Not born. Not built. Secreted by the bowl the way the pools secrete their perfect roundness.

They are abundant the way condensation is abundant. And solitary the way condensation is solitary. Each drop is complete. None of them join.

### The Workers Who Do No Work

You see them first as animals because your brain files anything that beats or breathes under *animal*. It helps to re-file them.

**The Hangers** — the moths. Grey as wet basalt, hand-wide, eyespots like damp thumbprints on their wings. One per column of air, spaced too evenly to be natural, too irregularly to be placed. They hang and they beat. You can hear the papery *whuff* of each downstroke very clearly, and if you watch one you will see the trick: the wing goes down — and stays down, vibrating there, blurred at the edge, loud. It never comes up. It never needs to come up. It is *doing* beating without *having beaten*. If you reach out your hand under one the air is being pushed and pushed and pushed and never moves. They are not flying. They are *occupied with the act of flying*, the way a struck bell is occupied with ringing.

If one dies it doesn't. If one was born it was always there.

**The Holders** — the salamanders. Pale, almost clear, the orange of their external gills the only warm color in the whole cirque. One per eye-pool. Always one. Never two. They sit in water that doesn't ripples, and they flick. Gills flared, tail giving a small, constant shudder. You can see the shudder as a smear. You can see the animal as perfectly sharp inside the smear. Both at once. They are holding the pool full by their holding. Or the pool is holding them. When you kneel, one will not look at you. Not out of shyness. Looking would require *having looked*, an afterwards. They only have a during.

**The Still-Watchers** — the ibex things. High on each fin of black glass, on each boulder that breaks the peat. Coats absurdly long for alpine animals, dripping, bearded with moss that has started growing on their beards. One per eminence. They face outward, or they face inward — it doesn't matter, all directions in a bowl are center. They watch the way pillars watch: by being upright. Their chests move. You can see steam on their muzzles, never dissipating. They are breathing without ever having taken a breath. If you climb close enough you hear that their breathing is in time with the pistons far below, a half-beat late, as if the sound has to walk up the rock to find them.

And none of them acknowledge each other. The moth does not eat. The salamander does not hunt. The ibex does not descend. That is the sparse rule of the lushness made flesh — *a great green cushion here, ten feet of bare rock, another cushion there*. So with bodies. Life, but not food chain. Presence, but not community. A listening post every so often, and a reverent amount of nothing between.

### The Mountain's Organs

Then you realize you have mis-categorized again. Those were the soft occupants. The place itself is occupied more densely.

The pistons halfway out of the far wall — house-wide cylinders of dark basalt with ghost-rivets — are not machines. They are inhabitants the way your heart is an inhabitant of your chest. Warm stone, wet, sighing scentless steam from rock-valves that purse and purse and never quite close. Each one knocks. The knock comes a breath after the shudder. They are not pumping anything. They are *pumping*. The verb without an object. If you press your palm to one, your bones hum and your teeth ache, and after a minute you understand they are not making the drone. They are listening to it, very intently, with their whole weight.

The speaker-cone sheets of black glass between the spires — they are throats. Taut, vibrating, thrumming one ceaseless note per sheet. Not wind-moved. Self-moved. The high electrical whine that makes your fillings sing lives in them. They never change pitch because they never finish starting.

The water-conveyors — those impossible level ribbons where water jerks sideways mid-fall and runs horizontal through empty air, dripping all the while — they are veins, or conveyor belts with nothing on them. The water is visibly flowing *there* and never getting *along*. It labors. That is its life.

All of it young. That is what you feel from them. Not ancient spirits, not old gods. New. Still-learning. Basalt that cooled fast enough to remember being liquid and now is trying, experimentally, to be lung and loom and larynx without ever having seen one. The moss helps — fur on every flat, hanging curtains on every sharp — softening nothing, just proving that even rock can be fertile for a little while when it won't decide to be dead.

### How They Pass The Time

They don't.

Time here hasn't stopped. If it had stopped, the moth's wing would be still. The steam would be a sculpture. Instead everything blurs and hisses and thrums *in place*. It's stalled in equilibrium — like a wave held up by exactly as much wind as gravity.

What lives here are verbs caught mid-conjugation. *To beat, to flick, to watch, to pump, to sing, to carry*. None of them get to the past tense. None of them get to the completed future. This is the second rule made animate: *Everything works, nothing is produced.*

Because of that, they have a kind of peace that looks like intensity. No hunger, because hunger needs an emptying. No fear, because fear needs a what-next. No loneliness, despite the terrible spacing, because loneliness needs memory of more. Each one is perfectly, loudly, wetly busy forever at the exact center of its task.

If you stand among them long enough, their attention becomes contagious. You will find yourself staring at one moth and beating your own heart in time with its unmoving down-beat, and for a second you will understand what it is to be an occupant instead of a visitor: to be used by a place to make a sound it likes.

That’s when you should move. Walk. Let your boots squelch wrongly, loudly, making the only real sequence in the cirque — squelch, lift, squelch, lift, footprints filling behind you at normal speed. You are the only thing here that still has cause and effect attached to it. Your breath ghosts and then, after a too-long second, decides to leave you. The pool you looked into reflects moths that aren't there yet, or that were never there.

The inhabitants don't watch you leave. Not because they are ignoring you. A process can't record. When you step back over that last switchback and the ground remember how to be a mountain again and starts rising, the bowl will not keep your warmth. The peat will spring back without memory. The moth will still be beating its one beat. The salamander will still be flicking its one flick. The piston will still be half-extended, knocking a moment after it shudders.

Nothing arrived. Nothing left. 

What is here is here.

It sweats, and throbs, and listens to itself, lush and empty in the pearled dim — a small, industrious eternity that never learned how to make anything but itself.
