They do not live *in* the basin so much as the basin lives through them. Everything here is a way for the salt and the dust and the cold air to hold a shape a little longer.

### The Choir

You heard them first, so you think they are separate. They aren't. The sound is not a chorus of individuals, it is a single organism with millions of mouths.

**The Salt Crickets** - *Lithogryllus basinii*, if anyone were here to name them. They are the size of hares, matte black, their backs not chitin but something closer to lacquered stone. They do not jump. Their hind legs are too heavy, too encrusted with excreted salt for leaping. They sit, one to a polygon on the white plain, exactly centered, as if placed. They saw their forewings together not to mate, not to warn, but to keep the air vibrating. Without that plane of pressure, the mosses cannot feed. A cricket lives for twenty, thirty years. When it dies, it does not fall. It simply stops. The salt ants will come and slowly take it apart, mandible by mandible, and carry it to a wall.

**The Veil Cicadas** - they hang. Dozens to a single air-moss curtain, their wings clear and veined like old window glass, each as wide as a spread coat. They do not fly except once, very slowly, from the moss where they are born to the moss where they will die. Their tymbals are enormous, stretched across their whole hollow abdomen. They click in phase. Not in unison - in phase, so the wave never cancels. The tone they make is at the exact resonant frequency of the salt flat itself. Stand on the plain at noon and you can feel the cicadas not in your ears but in the soles of your boots.

Under them both, **the Rollers**. Beetles the size of wheelbarrows, low and blind, their backs pitted and mossy. They harvest. They push perfect spheres of halite and silt and lichen-spore, three, four feet across, compacted by their own spit. The spheres hum as they roll. The beetles follow trails so old they have worn grooves an inch deep into solid salt, grooves you mistook for streets until you saw what made them.

### The Garden That Is Not Green

**The Lung Pillars** are not plants and not colonies, but both. Each one started as a single spore caught on a chip of stone ten thousand years ago. Now it is a tower of layered felt, of staghorn fronds and fungal mat and dead fronds turned to sponge, twenty feet tall, breathing. The outside is dry and gray and faintly warm. Push your hand in a foot and it is cool, damp, dark, smelling of cellar earth. Inside, water condenses out of nothing. Not collected - *made* - pulled molecule by molecule from the cold dusk air. Mites the size of rice grains live their whole lives inside a single pillar, grazing. So do eyeless white worms, translucent moth larvae, things like tiny shrimp that swim through damp air.

Between them drift **the Blanket Moths**. They are not fliers, really. They are sails. Six feet across, paper-thin, bleached bone-white, they detach from their pupal pillars and then never beat their wings again. The vibration of the choir keeps them aloft. They drift on sound-pressure, turning slowly, shedding scales that fall like snow and are caught at once by the liverwort.

**The Liverwort Sea** covers the slopes. *Marchantia perpetua* - a mattress, a carpet, a single continuous liver. You can walk for an hour and never step off it. It is springy, cool, slightly tacky. It exhales. Lie down and it sighs against your cheek, a faint sour-sweet breath of oxygen and geosmin. It has no roots. It holds itself down with sheer weight and time. Every few decades it flowers - not with color, but with a raised black bristle that bursts and lets its spores join the motes at the moss-tips, those sparks you see at night.

Everything that grows here grows *on* something else. Air-moss on vibration. Moth scale on liverwort. Lichen on beetle-back. There are no parasites. There is only slow, patient tenancy.

### The Architects

They are why you thought this was a city.

They look like ants only because your brain has no other category for a six-legged, busy, matte-dust-colored thing that carries. They are not ants. Their bodies are low, broad, segmented but soft-jointed, covered not in chitin but in a fine, salt-felted pile that holds moisture. The smallest are dog-sized. The largest, the wall-wrights, are pony-sized, their heads massive, their mandibles blunt as trowels.

They have no eyes. They have no queen. They have no war.

They walk. Always. In lines you can see for miles across the white, each line a faint dark thread. Each carries a pellet. The pellet is everything: chewed salt, digested lichen, crushed moth scale, their own feces, a little spit. It is warm when placed. They press it - *pat, pat* - onto the edge of a dome, a ridge, a terrace, a tower. No plan guides them. No foreman. The shape emerges because each ant smells where the last pellet was placed and where the vibration is strongest, and places its own there. In a hundred years, a wall curves. In five hundred, a dome closes. In two thousand, an avenue appears because no one builds there - the wind is wrong.

Inside the domes it is warm. Not hot. Metabolic warm. The walls themselves respire. Larvae, white and featureless as grubs, lie packed in salt combs, humming faintly in sympathy with the choir outside. Adults tend them by licking salt from their skins. They do not eat the Pillars or the liverwort. They farm them, in a way so slow you didn't recognize it as farming: they carry spores to new salt tiles. They clear dust from a frond.

When a building falls - and they do, slumping with a sigh you feel more than hear, over a season - no one flees. The liverwort is already there, softening the edge. The ants come and begin to carry the ruin away, one pellet at a time, to make something else a foot to the left.

And then there are the others, the ones who are not building:

**The Salt Lickers** - lizards like folded linen, blind, frilled, with tongues as long as your arm that lap not water but pure halite, extracting the trace brine trapped between crystals. They never drink.

**The Moss-backs** - what you took for armadillos. Low, patient domes themselves, their soft shells completely overgrown with living liverwort and lichen. They move inches per hour, plowing furrows through the moss-fields. The furrows become roads for the ants. The ants, in turn, clean the moss-backs' backs.

Nothing here hurries because nothing here can afford to waste. Water is made, not found. Salt is eaten and becomes house. Sound is food and map and blanket. Everything borrows and returns.

If you stay long enough - and the basin makes you want to stay, the blue dusk and the hum flattening your thoughts until time feels cupped like a salt polygon - you will see that there are no individuals here either. Just as you cannot say where one liverwort ends and the next begins, you cannot say where beetle ends and moss ends and ant-city ends. It is one slow thing, breathing out, grain by grain, note by note, waiting in its bowl while the world above wears away to nothing.

It does not notice you. Not unkindly. You are simply too fast, too loud in the wrong way, and too wet.
