They live pressed together because there is nowhere else to go.

Not one of them. All of them. The city is not inhabited — it *is* inhabitants, layer after layer, until you can't say where animal ends and architecture begins, because the architecture was never built, it was secreted.

Start at the bottom, where the stone is still warm.

You have to get low, face almost to the basalt, to see them properly. The foundation is not foundation at all. It is bacteria. A skin of white and rust-orange felt over the black glass, so thin you would think it was discoloration if it didn't move — if it didn't fizz faintly. Chemolithotrophs. Stone-eaters. They are drinking the heat. They take the iron and sulfur weeping out of that hairline fissure in the infant rock, the black water that is still technically magmatic, and they make sugar out of it, out of darkness, out of chemistry. There is no sun here, never has been. The light that filters down through miles of pressure-blue ice is decoration, not food. The food comes up from below. The whole city is standing on its mouth.

And standing on the bacteria are the first citizens.

The tube-worms. *Siboglinidae*, or something like them, but you won't find them in any book because no book has been down here. White as bone, white as new scar tissue. Each one no thicker than a pencil lead, each one taller than your hand, and there are thousands. Tens of thousands. They have no eyes, no stomach, nothing so personal as a face. Just a plume — a crown of red gills, like a tiny fern unfurled — waving in the almost-still water, and a tube of their own mucus and mineral that they have excreted around themselves like a house they will never leave.

They do not move. Not ever, once they choose a spot. Their choice is their life. A larva drifted for maybe an hour in this whole cosmos — three meters is a long way when you are the size of a grain of sand — and then it touched warm rock and *decided*. Here. And grew up. And opened its plume. And began to filter.

And filtering is praying, here. They strain the water for what the bacteria have made, for the snow of detritus that never lands, for each other's spawn. Their plumes beat out of sync, then, mysteriously, in sync, like wheat in a wind you can't feel. If you put your ear — if you had an ear that worked in this baffled silence — against the field of them, you would hear the very soft pop and sigh of a thousand tiny crowns opening and closing. They are the wheat fields and the tenements at once. Between their feet, you cannot see rock. Only more tube, more white.

It is on and between these tubes that the second city is made.

The amphipods. They look like shrimp made out of frosted glass, each one the size of your thumbnail, each one blind. They should be swimmers. Elsewhere in the ocean their cousins swim free. Here, they have given that up. As juveniles they cement themselves to a tube-worm's tube or to a lobe of basalt with a glue they make in their own heads. They add grit, they add their own shed skins, they add the empty spirals of the paper-shell snails, and they make a cell. A single room just big enough for one. And then, because the cells share walls, a neighborhood happens. Hundreds of little pallid commas in their little glued grit apartments, antennae twitching.

They never leave either, not really. They extend their feathery legs out of the doorway and comb the water. They farm. On their backs and on the walls of their streets — those worn current-paths where water moves a little faster — they cultivate the same white bacterial felt that grows on the rock, but thicker now, tended. They scrape it. They eat it. They spread it. They carry eggs under their tails that glow faintly opalescent blue, and when the eggs hatch the young don't swim away. They crawl a centimeter and cement themselves next door. The city expands not by planning but by budding, by filling in every gap.

And among them, traffic.

The isopods are the ones allowed to walk. Pills the size of peas, slow, deliberate, also blind, also pale, but with a faint mother-of-pearl light inside them, like they swallowed a little of the refracted light from the jellies above. They patrol. They clean. One will approach a tube-worm whose plume has fouled with too much bacterial mat and gently, endlessly, nibble it clean. Another will find an amphipod that has died in its cell — which happens constantly in a place this dense — and take it apart in a day, carrying the pieces away to other streets, where other mouths wait. There is no waste here. A death is a sudden widening of the street for a few hours, then new larvae settle in the space.

Above them, the hunters. Because even here, even in a pocket the size of a small bedroom, there must be hunters. Long red worms, thin as string, twice as long as your arm, that live in the interstices, in burrows made of old tubes. They have no eyes but they have exquisite chemical noses. You see one only when it decides to run — a quick, terrible ripple between the buildings, snapping an isopod off its route and pulling it under before the water has even stirred. They are the only thing that moves fast, and so the whole city's silence is tuned to listening for them.

Higher still, toward the cold. The solitary ones. Tunicates like lanterns, anchored to the upper basalt lobes where the water is a degree colder, pulsing once a minute, taking in water and squeezing it out, filtering indiscriminately. And clinging to their sides, the paper-shell snails, herds of them, grazing the algae that hasn't really taken hold — those sullen veils of black-red on the ice — rasp rasp rasp, a sound so slow you feel it more than hear it.

And then, inverted, the roof.

They are what you notice last because they are mostly light. The jellies. Each one clear as good water, with a sucker-foot planted into the ice dome itself — impossible, but there it is, holding against the cold that should kill — and a bell hanging down. And inside that bell, another smaller bell, and inside that another, like a nested set of parachutes, each one fishing with threads a hundred times finer than hair. They don't pulse to swim. They hang and they wait. The marine snow — that endless, slow falling of detritus from nowhere — sticks. They reel it in hand over hand, though they have no hands, and eat. Through their stacked bells the dim indigo dusk is broken into pieces and scattered, so they seem to glow from within.

None of them knows the city.

That is the strange, tender thing. The tube-worm knows its plume and its neighbor's tube. The amphipod knows its cell and the current in its street. The isopod knows the taste of clean versus dirty. The red worm knows hunger and lunge. The jelly knows fall and stick. None of them has a nervous system large enough to hold the idea of a pocket, of miles of ice, of an ocean that this place wanted to be and failed to become.

But together — together they know exactly how big the world is. They have filled it, wall to wall. Every warm centimeter is occupied. Every cold edge is patrolled. Every current is someone's road. What you are seeing, with your too-big body and your too-loud heart that finally went quiet, is not a collection of animals that live in a city.

It is a city that lives, by being animals, so densely and so completely that it has become a single warm thing, breathing very slowly in the dark, under a lid of ancient ice, waiting for nothing, needing nothing, making more of itself out of itself until the stone cools.
