They live very low and very close together.

Not *on* this place. *As* this place. If you stood here long enough for your eyes to stop looking for trees and birds and other honest things, you would stop seeing a plain with animals on it and start seeing an animal that has learned how to be a plain.

What lives here is one continuous, low, cold carpet that pretends, when you aren't paying attention, to be stone.

### The Varnish

It is the first thing alive. It has to be, because nothing else can eat rock.

It coats nine tenths of everything that isn't actively underwater or actively polished clean by too many feet. When dry it looks like nothing — a lacquer of rust and dried blood and oil-slick black, cracked like old paint, tight to the basalt. You could walk across it and think it was discoloration.

Then the mist comes down — not falls, comes *down*, condenses out of the pooled air — and it happens in front of you. It drinks without a mouth. It swells. It goes from varnish to flesh. An amber jelly, translucent, a centimeter thick, trembling. Under its surface you can see darker veins where it is dissolving basalt at the chemical level, pitting the stone molecule by molecule and exhaling that faint, sharp smell of iron and ozone.

It moves, but not like an animal moves. If you scuff it with your boot, the wound glows — a slow blue-green bioluminescence that starts at the tear and then *travels*, rippling outward ten, twenty meters across the sheet, as the whole vast organism tells itself it has been hurt. And that light is a dinner bell.

Because everything else eats the Varnish. Or eats what eats the Varnish.

### The Grazers

They are the bulk. The weight. The reason the sound never stops.

Imagine a woodlouse crossed with a trilobite and then flattened by evolution until it could slide under a door. Flat, oval, the size of your palm to the size of a dinner plate, their backs are not shell but overlapping plates of slate-gray chitin polished to a dull shine by each other's passing. No eyes. No face you would recognize — just a soft, pale underlip that everts like a tongue and rasps.

They move in herds. Not herds like deer — herds like rain. Thousands on a single basalt rib, shoulder to shoulder, all facing one direction, all rasping. You hear them as a single low *shushhhh*, stone being licked clean. Where they have passed, the Varnish is gone and the rock is left bright and scratched, a fresh pale scar that will darken again in hours as the Varnish regrows.

Lift one — if you can stand the faint ammonia stink of it — and its underside is a forest of tiny hooked legs and gills that flutter like pages, always damp, always beating. Under each grazer, riding along, are smaller things: white mites like grains of rice, cleaning its gills; blind flatworms as thin as paper drinking its excretions before they even hit air. Even the grazer is a place.

When they die, they don't flip over and lie there. They stop, their plates loosen, and the herd flows over them and takes them apart from underneath. In ten minutes there is only a fainter shine on the stone.

### The Pickers and The Sacks

Where the Grazers are cattle, these are the restlessness on top of them.

The Pickers look like crabs that forgot how to have a shell. Long, stilted legs — too many joints, bending both ways — translucent white so you can see the gray gut inside, the slow pulse. They are bigger than the Grazers, about the size of hares, but weigh almost nothing. They pick, with surgical mouth-parts, not at the Varnish but at the Grazers themselves: nipping off parasites, stealing eggs laid in the soft underplates, delicately amputating a leg and running away to eat it elsewhere. The Grazers do not seem to mind. Or rather, they mind the way a field minds crows — as weather.

They never stop moving. Their feet make the *tik-tik-tik* you hear most clearly, a million needles on glass.

Bunched in every crack, where the intermittent trickle actually runs, are the Sacks. They have no common name because you won't think of them as animals at first. They are colonies of conjoined throats, soft, gray-pink, each sack the size of a fist, all fused at the base. When the mist beads or the seven-minute rain comes, they inflate — *gulp* — swelling to twice their size with silt-water. Then, for hours afterward, they slowly wring themselves out, filtering bacteria and crystal dust and Varnish spores, sighing out clear water that trickles on to the next colony. Their exhale is the low thrumming you feel in the stone more than hear.

### The Fliers

They own the air from two feet up to ten feet up, which here counts as the sky.

They are not insects. Insects are precise. These are desperate. Bodies like plucked, hairless bats, the size of pigeons to gulls, four membranous wings that beat out of sync so their flight is a frantic, lurching hover. Eyeless heads dominated by a screaming mouth and two dish-ears.

They are deafening because they never stop screaming. Their sonar is so loud part of it leaks into your hearing as a constant flat whine that makes your teeth ache. They scream, wheel, snatch a Picker mid-step, scream again, collide mid-air with each other and tumble, screaming, separating, screaming.

When a rain starts they all at once — as one — fold and cling to the undersides of basalt ribs, wings wrapped tight, silent for the first time. The sudden silence is louder than the noise. Then the rain stops and they explode upward again in a black snow.

They live fast. Watch one long enough and you will see it snatch, eat, mate in mid-air in a thrashing tangle, lay a string of wet black beads on the Varnish, and die — all in maybe an hour — falling to be dismantled by the Grazers below before its wings stop twitching. Its belly glows faintly even in death.

### The Stone Listeners

And then there are the things that make you realize mineral and animal are the same word here.

Certain ribs of basalt — the oldest, the most iron-rich — hum. Not metaphorically. When wind shears across the basin just right, they resonate at a low pure tone that you feel in your molars. When they hum, every Flier within a hundred meters freezes and drops like stunned flies to cling motionless. The Grazers stop rasping. Even the Varnish stops pulsing.

Nobody knows why. Or rather, everything knows why but can't tell you. The tone is the basin discharging — after rain, pale threads of static crawl across the mist a foot above the ground, following magnetic veins, looking for all the world like slow lightning that forgot to be bright. The creatures follow those threads to water. Or away from something worse.

If you put your hand on a humming rib, the vibration travels up your bones and for a second you hear, under the roar, the plain breathing.

That is what lives here. Nothing tall. Nothing that sings for pleasure. Nothing that looks at the sky because there is no sky to look at.

Just layers — Varnish into Grazer into Picker into Flier into Sighing Sack — packed so dense you cannot be lonely here, only crowded. A low, cold, wet, clicking, sighing, thrumming, screaming city with no streets, where everything eats stone or eats what has eaten stone, and where the stone, very slightly, eats back.
