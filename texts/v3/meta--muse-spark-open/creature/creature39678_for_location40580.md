They do not live *in* the water. Not the way a fish does, sealed and complete.

They live *with* it, and after long enough generations, *through* it.

You see them first not as bodies but as interruptions in the forest's rhythm. A rope walkway trembles when nothing is on it — and then a shape resolves on it, moving hand-over-hand. A curtain of leaves parts and doesn't fall back the same way. A bubble of harvested air detaches from an old wound in a trunk and rises, wobbling, and following its string down you find a hand holding it.

They are human. Still, recognizably. That is the quiet shock of them.

If you grew up below the drowning you would expect monsters here. Something thinned and stretched or crusted over. Instead time has left them almost tender. They kept what they needed and softened everything else.

### The Body That Water Made

They are smaller than you expect from a people who carry houses of air on their backs. Compact, broad through the chest and shoulders. Not muscled in the gym-sense, but *corded* — forearms like old rope from a lifetime of pulling wet kelp-fiber, hauling nets, wringing and re-wringing stipe cordage until the hands forget how to be dry. Their legs are leaner, a little bowed, feet turned to paddles more than props. On land — if you could find land — they would walk badly, rolling side to side. In the colonnade of trunks they are graceful past belief. They don't swim so much as climb the water. Reach, pull, glide, reach again. Ankle flicks. Never thrashing.

Their skin tells how long they have been here.

Children shine. Oiled almost black-green by birth, hair cut very short or braided tight to the skull so it won't snag, eyes huge and clear and a little bloodshot from salt. Old people go pale and mottled, covered in the same soft film of algae that colonizes the bladders they tend, their backs stippled with pale sucker-marks where shrimp have cleaned parasites off them too aggressively. Everyone between has the kelp-tan: brown that goes a little olive underneath, as if light has soaked into them the way it soaks into the leaves. When they breach into a bladder and stand in air, steam rises off them even in cool water, and you realize they are always, slightly, cold. Their bodies run cooler than yours. It saves oxygen.

Lungs. That's the difference that matters.

No gills — the old stories are very clear that gills were prayed for and never came. What came instead was capacity and control. When a hamlet child is six they are taken to the Deeping Ladder, a knotted cord that drops thirty meters down the side of the plateau where the light thins and your ears begin to hurt. You go down it one knot at a time breathing as they sing you down, and then you come back up without breathing at all. Some children panic. Most don't. At eleven they can stay under for twenty minutes if they are calm. Adults, if they are not working hard, can stretch it to forty by slowing their heart until you can hardly hear it through the water. They do heart-slowing as a game. You will see two aunties hang motionless in a leaf-crotch, eyes closed, sinking very slowly, to see who stirs first.

When they do need air — and they need it often; they are air-people, not fish — they duck into bladders, or tip their heads back to take a sip from a skin-bag of forest air carried at the belt, or crowd around an ox-bubble stream and inhale the pearls one by one. The pearls taste of lime and iron. The forest air tastes of green sap and iodine and, faintly, of other people's breaths. They don't mind sharing air. They have done it for too many generations to mind.

Their eyes are the other thing. Wide, with a faint shine behind the iris when the leaf-canopy moves and lets a hard bar of sun through. Night-adapted without being night-blinded. They can read ropework by touch and pressure change by ear, but their look is still long and human. They look *at* you, not past you. The forest taught them not to startle.

### The Households

There are not many of them, and that scarcity has shaped them more than any belief.

Thirty-odd bladders, but bladders bud and bladders die, so no one counts that way. They count hearths. A hearth is a bladder whose lower mouth will admit your shoulders if you know the trick of exhaling as you push up, and that has a name and a song to coax it. Hearth-Keeping is women's work, mostly, in the way that important work often becomes women's work — not by law but by who keeps remembering how. A keeper knows her bladder like a body: where it wheezes, where it sweetens the air best, which shrimp family lives on its north seam, how hard you can pat its inner wall before it bruises. She talks to it. It sighs back.

A hearth is never owned. It is *kept* for the span of a life, then left fallow for the plant to reabsorb or passed to a daughter or a chosen niece who has apprenticed in its air for years. To inherit a good bladder on the sunward side, where the light makes the membrane strong and the air comes sweet in late morning, is to be well-married even if you never marry. Young couples who want to pair will often bud a new, small bladder together in the shadow of one of their mothers' hearths — lashing a frame of bone to coax the membrane outward, feeding it sugar-water, singing low notes to encourage gas exchange. It takes two years for a bladder to become habitable. The courtship lasts that long. If they are still together when they can both fit inside it, they stay. If not, one keeps it and the forest takes the failure without judgment.

Inside, a hearth is not primitive at all. It is precise. Shell lamps that burn rendered ox-fat with a wick of dried frond pith — they only burn in air, so they are a luxury. Woven cradles that hang from the bladder roof and sway with the current that transmits through the membrane. Walls tattooed with soot-ink and kelp-dye: hand prints of every child born there, current-maps, the long wavy line that marks the plateau edge so children learn the limit early. A low altar-shelf for the things that must stay dry: a stone knife that has been re-knapped thirty times, a cuttle-bird basket with unhatched glassbeads of eggs, a coil of cord waiting to be spliced. The floor is never quite floor; it flexes. You learn to sleep braced in the curve.

Outside, between hearths, they are all neighbors in a way town-folk in air forget how to be. You never pass another swimmer without touching — a palm to a forearm, a tap on the shoulder, a deliberate exhale of bubbles across their face that means *I see you, be well*. Sound carries too well here to be rude, and besides, the water steals heat if you sulk alone too long. They gather on the great slack nets to gossip, hanging belly-down while silver grazers part around them like weather. They gather on terrace-cuts to farm, slicing new shoots with shell adzes while the plant shivers but does not bleed; the oldest man, who cannot hold his breath long anymore, sits in half-air half-water at the terrace lip and hums the cutting rhythm so everyone cuts together.

### The Works

Because tech is stone and shell, work is body and memory. No one specializes completely, but everyone leans.

**The Rope-Weavers**, men and women both, but mostly the broad-shouldered ones with ruined fingernails, sit for days in leaf-shade chewing stipe fibers to soften them, thigh-rolling cord as thick as your wrist, testing each length by hanging their whole weight from a trunk. Their knowledge is the hamlet's true infrastructure. They know which walkway must be restrung before storm season when the planetary breath current doubles and the whole forest leans east. They know not to pull a rope taut — always a little slack, so the trunks can groan without snapping anything. A Weaver child can tie seven knots blindfold, in current, and untie them one-handed.

**The Ox-Tenders** — usually the most patient — keep the great blind kelp-oxen. The beasts are not owned either; no one could own something that migrates through the trunks exhaling lime-pearls the size of your head. Tenders learn their paths and clear the old growth ahead of them, and know the tap-song on a trunk that means *turn, love, not that way*. They harvest the pearl-bubbles by netting them gently and pressing them through bladder membrane; too fast and the gas sours. The oxen know their voices. If you sing the wrong song, they will go still and listen with their whole mossy back, and you will feel very judged in green gloom.

**The Edge-Watchers**, who are either very young and thrilled or very old and unflappable. They do not guard — nothing guards the abyss. They *listen*. At dusk they swim to the lip where basalt drops away into that terrible blue-into-black fall and they hang there, ankles hooked in rope, and watch the deep water rise. Things come up. Not every night. A light where no light should be. A shape too straight to be biology. A cold plume that makes the oxen moan from two trunks away. The Watchers do not have weapons that would matter. They have a bell — a bladder stretched over a stone hollow, struck with a bone hammer, that makes a low *bong* that carries through the whole plateau and means *come in now, bring children inside bladders, close mouths halfway*. The children are very good at this drill. They were born hearing that bell through water and then through air.

**The Cuttle-Bird Keepers**, almost always children and grandmothers, who braid tiny baskets and trade words with the not-birds. The cuttle-birds flash on their skins in a language of color that humans can only partly read. The keepers don't pretend to understand it fully; they answer in clacks and puffs of ink-water and little gifts of shrimp. The birds tell where grazers will school tomorrow, which bladder is about to sigh and go thin, where a lost adze fell. In return the people tell them where it is safe to nest when the deep things rise.

And underneath all works, the one law everyone obeys without being told to obey it: *You never cut a main stipe.* You can carve terraces into the sides, you can take leaves, you can pollard the curtains, but if you sever the column you kill a street, a house, a hearth, a farm, and the plant remembers. It will seal itself and leave a scar that no air will hold for years. Children are shown the scar of the Cut That Grandmother Made In Anger — a healed-over stump the size of a house — and told: *We live because it let us. Remember the size.*

### The Faith That Is Not Called Faith

They do not worship the kelp. That would be like worshiping your kitchen floor. They *negotiate* with it.

At midday, when the sun is straight above and the dappling stops breathing and becomes a steady column of light, the whole hamlet stills. Not from piety. From physics. The light is so complete then you can see all the way across the plateau — thirty hearths glinting like amber beads, nets hanging, ox-bubbles rising in slow strings, shoals of grazers turning as one mirror — and the forest holds its creak for a single held breath. In that moment, people press their palms to trunk or membrane and feel the slow push of oxygen coming in, water going out, the forest making *sky* inside itself for them. They tap twice. *Thank you. Continue.*

They have a story for why the world drowned and left them high. It changes every time it is told, which tells you it is not a history but a comfort. Sometimes the ocean rose because the earth was thirsty. Sometimes the earth sank because it was tired and wanted a blanket. No one remembers which. What they remember is that the ridge stayed. High is a kind of promise here. High means light reaches you. High means you are not lost in black.

They sing while they swim so the oxen know they are not predators. They also sing so they know *themselves* are not predators. That is the older reason.

If you fell off the edge — and everyone dreams of falling, even though no one swims over at night — you would fall for a long time through blue into black before you hit any real bottom. The hamlet tells children that the bottom is another ridge, and another forest, and another hamlet, upside down, looking up at them and wondering if they will ever visit. The children know this is not true the way grownups know most stories are not true and useful anyway.

You, hanging here now in cool that never quite becomes cold, breathing borrowed forest air in a sphere that hums, watching them come and go through their cathedral of green — you understand why they stayed. It is not that the world ended and left them this. It is that this *is* a world, complete, primitive only if you think completion needs metal. Their city is air pockets and rope bridges, yes, but also breath control and bell law and baskets for another species' children. Their mountain is a drowned table above an abyss that they treat like weather, not hell.

Somewhere a bladder births air with a soft, tremendous sigh.

A dozen voices answer it through water that has always been the sky — not words, at first. Just tones held long enough to travel, to locate, to say *here, here, here*.

And you, for a moment, know exactly where everyone is.
