Nothing here is alone. That is the first thing.

You expect a dim, wet, mineral place like this to be empty, or near-empty. A little algae, a tardigrade, a fungus. Instead the water is too full. The surfaces are too full. The air just above the water, when you breathe it, is full.

From a distance you see a sheen, a rust color, a stipple. Up close it resolves into bodies upon bodies, not swarming so much as *packed* — layer pressed to layer, each in its own film of water, its own millimeter of territory it will not quite cede, the whole basin tiled with life the way the stone is tiled with bacterial leather.

Start low, because everything here starts low.

The foundation isn't living in the way you came here looking for. It's the mat. The floor of every permanent pan and sump and boot-print that never dries is lined with it — not moss, not weed. A felt, a skin, a bacterial velvet in ochre and burnt-orange and wet matte black, iron-oxidizers and sulfur-reducers and nameless biofilm that has learned to make a tissue out of stone-water. In the warm shadow of the generator's sump it thickens into a pulpy mound that quivers when water runs over it, maroon-black and spongy as liver. Elsewhere it lies thin and tight to the rock, blistered with gas bubbles that never quite pop.

It is the soil and the pasture both, and it moves. Not by itself. Threading it are the worms — blind, white, as fine as thread and as long as your finger, in numbers that make no sense until you stop counting. They don't burrow so much as braid through the felt, thousands to the square foot, eating the mat and tunneling it and excreting it in the same motion so that the surface seems to crawl even when nothing is on it. Put your hand close and the mat tightens faintly. Put your knuckle to the blackest part and you can feel the cold, slow churn under the skin.

On top of the mat lives the crust.

Every pan that holds more than an inch of permanent water is boiling with them, but slowly, without heat. The amphipods and isopods — some no bigger than rice motes, some big as the last joint of your thumb — pale, almost translucent, their guts visible as a dark thread of eaten detritus. They tumble over each other constantly, antennae flicking, swimming in short blind hops and then righting themselves and grazing. The larger ones carry. Each has cemented a mosaic of sand grains, calcite chips, and flecks of rust from the pump-house onto its back, a coat of mineral grit carried like a thatch. When they stop moving you lose them against the grit they walk on. When they move the pan seems to glitter and right itself. They eat anything that falls — shed skin, bacterial slough, dead midge, dead brother — and they are in turn never not being eaten.

Above them, in the shallows proper, are the newts.

Not fish. Nothing here ever made it to fish. They are salamanders that have given up ever becoming land animals, paedomorphic, cool-blooded, frill-gilled things the length of your hand, flesh the color of wet stone. Their heads are blunt, their eyes small and lidless and milked over as if with cataract — they do not hunt by sight in this dimness. Their gills splay from each side of the neck in rust-red fern plumes, feathery and constantly pulsing, and they lie stacked. Quite literally stacked, two and three deep in the clearest pans, perfectly still, gills fanning in the cold current, one atop the next like spoons. You would think them dead. Then a cloud of midges dips too low, or an isopod molts, and the whole stack snaps. Not one. All of them, in a lateral ripple down the length of the pool — heads turning on the same frame, mouths opening to the same gape — as precise and silent as a trap shutting. Then stillness again. Their egg ropes hang from rebar stumps under the water, clear jelly with a string of black pearls inside. Their shed gill filaments drift free and in minutes are mobbed by the crust.

The white, the pale, the translucent — almost nothing here has pigment left. There was never enough light to keep it.

But the walls are dark.

The vertical world belongs to the shell-things. Every concrete flank of the pump-house, every iron stalk of the work-lights, every face of the crystal thickets is crusted with them, lacquered black shells overlapping like coins in a fountain, like fingernails pressed one over the next until no stone shows. They aren't snails as you know them — no spiral, no wander. Flat, limpet-like, with a pale foot sealed to the wet with a thin bead of water. Twice a minute the whole colony sighs. A synchronized lift of shell from surface, a thread of air taken, a bead of condensation left on the lip of each shell at the same instant, and then sealed down again. In aggregate it makes a soft, wet tick across the basin, out of phase with the dripping. Sometimes adhesion fails and one pops free with a *tok* and falls into the pan below, where the isopods are on it before it can right itself. The rock under them is clean. They have grazed it to a polish.

And over all this — a foot above the water, no higher — is the weather of midges.

They don't buzz. Or they buzz at the edge of your hearing, a pressure more than a sound. Clouds of them, non-biting chironomids in their thousands and thousands, in slow, dense vortices that never disperse. They turn dim to dimmer in the amber half-light, making a stippled shadow on the white sky even though each individual is nearly clear. At rest they make a black fuzz on the bacterial mounds. In flight they rise and fall in columns, males dancing invisibly to females. They live a day. They lay rafts of eggs on every still surface — brown, foaming rafts the size of a coin that tremble and then sink — and the newts and the crust tear them apart. The larvae are already in the water, red threadworms building silk tubes in the grit under the pans, filtering the bacterial soup with feathered mouths. The air gives to the water gives to the air without leaving the bowl.

There are places where the rules warp slightly.

The warm sump behind the generator is one. Its water is four degrees above the rest, enough that a bloom of purple-brown bacteria has turned it thick as broth, opalescent with oil. The smell is stronger there, mineral and faintly bloody. The isopods crowd three times as deep along its lip, the newts will not enter it but lie with heads turned toward it, waiting for the midges that breed there to lift.

The transformer is the other. No light, just a low, bone-felt 50-cycle hum from a box bolted to the pump-house wall that no longer does anything useful. Below it the crystal thicket changes. The white needle-crystals grow thicker, glassier, like a frozen spray caught mid-splash. And on them stand the elongated ones. Pale, many-legged, each as long as your forearm but thin as a pencil, with a narrow head and no eyes you can find. They never graze. They never hunt. They stand motionless in rows, all facing the hum, legs locked, antennae quivering to the vibration. When the generator coughs they shift an inch forward. When it resumes they still. They don't leave that radius. Drop one in a pan twenty feet away and it walks in a straight, urgent line back to the hum before going still again. What they eat, if they eat, is not visible while you watch.

Nothing gets out.

No dragonfly hawks the midge clouds. No bird stoops. No fish patrols the deeper sump. The rim is too high and too dry on its outside, and the basin floor is sealed — old clay and industrial tailing fines tamped and puddled into an impermeable plug. Water that weeps down the limestone lip has nowhere else to go. It ticks from overhang to pan to film to sump, it mists off the warm sump in the night, it beads on the cool rim and weeps down again. So do the animals. You can watch an amphipod swim its whole life's range in one pan. You can map a newt's territory with a single handspan.

It is not starving. It is not thriving. It is complete. Every mouth you see is fed by another mouth you can't quite see, which is fed by the mineral breathing of the mat, which is fed by the diesel hum and the slow corrosion of iron and the condensation that never stops. There are no weeds to be pulled, no predators to control the numbers except crowding itself. Things die here mostly by touching another thing.

Listen long enough and the basin tells you its density not by noise but by *nearness* — water on water on chitin on gill, the faint pop of a shell, the wet screech of the washer drum turning once, empty, for no one, and under it all the steady, sternal thrum that has taken the place of a season.

If you stay still, very still, the midge cloud will lower and include you.
