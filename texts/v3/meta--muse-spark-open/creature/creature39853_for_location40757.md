They are not hidden.

That is the first mistake you make, from the lip — thinking this green stitch in the earth must be empty because it is quiet.

Then a reed moves against the water's drift and doesn't stop moving.

Then a second reed moves.

Then the mat itself decides to stand up.

---

You see them first as absences — not creatures but corrections to what you thought was plant.

A trunk too straight. A shadow in the spruce-thicket where no shadow should hold. A patch of lichen that blinks.

They are the color of the mat. Not camouflaged — *made* from it. The people of the wound are small and winter-pale in a way that has nothing to do with sickness. Think of ptarmigan skin, of birch sap, of the underside of mushrooms. In summer, when the pale-gold noon drops straight down for its hour, they flush pink at the cheeks and forearms; the rest of the year they are the white of cold water.

They are short — most would come to your sternum — but not gracile. The rift does not allow gracile. Every body here is a cold-water body: broad chests like barrels to hold heat, legs thick and short to wade without falling, feet that are wide as spades, splayed, the toes webbed not quite to the nail with a translucent membrane that tears and heals and tears again. Their hands are what give them away — enormous for their frames, long-fingered, nicked and black-nailed, knuckles swollen from always being wet. A child's hand already looks like a root.

They have very little hair on their heads — a sparse, lichenous down that clings when wet, more moss than mane — and almost none on their bodies; hair is a liability here, it never dries. Instead they wear their insulation. In the cold greenhouse hush of the rift bottom, where your breath whites but the water never quite freezes, they move in layered, living clothes. A vest of felted musk-ox underwool, waterproofed with spruce resin until it shines. Leggings of woven horsetail fiber, tight as bark. Over everything, when outside the bole-houses, a draped cape grown — not made — from sheet-moss, still alive, still faintly green, stitched with living thread so that it continues to grow and must be trimmed every few days with a flaked shale blade. It drips. All of them drip. They leave no dry footprints because their feet never dry.

Watch one. She is thigh-deep in what you thought was a street and what is in fact a braided channel between two pleached larch walls. The water is 2°C and clear as glass over black peat, and you can see white fish the length of your forearm slide under her knees without startling. She does not walk so much as wade-part. With each step she feels ahead with her toes for the mat's give, for the sudden sink-hole where old moss has rotted through. In her arms is a bundle of cutting — willow withes as thick as your thumb, still leafed. She reaches a sheer place in the living wall where three trunks have been pulled together and forced to graft, years before she was born, into an arch. The apex is failing — a dead branch, the graft gone soft and punky, weeping more than the rock does. She doesn't repair it. She feeds it. She takes her shale knife — no metal here, metal rusts to nothing in a week, metal poisons the moss — and scores the bark below the wound, tucks the willow withes into the wet cambium, and ties them with a strip of cotton-grass root. By autumn those withes will have rooted, by next spring they will be the arch. If she had not come, the arch would have sealed itself with leaves and become just tree again. This is the work: endless, quiet, unfinishable tending. Nothing is built. Everything is persuaded to keep being a house.

That is why you thought the place was empty. There are no straight lines. A family bole — and there are hundreds, thousands, the density is urban if you count doors per acre — is not a building. It is a black spruce five feet through that some great-grandmother coaxed, when it was a whip of a sapling, to bulge around a hollow. Each year she widened the hollow with careful fireless rot — packing it with inoculated peat, letting fungi soften the heartwood, scooping it with antler spoons — so the tree grew around the absence. Now it is a round room, twelve feet across, the walls weeping resin that the family harvests with flat sticks to caulk the seams where wall meets living floor. The door is not a door: it's a curtain of aerial roots that have been trained to hang, still attached to the branches above, still drinking. You part them and they swing back and drip on your neck. Inside, the floor is slatted larch roots, woven while green, now hard, suspended over the mat-water so the house breathes. A sleeping loft is a hammock of living willow that has rooted into the opposite wall. In the central pit, where a hearth should be, there is no fire — fire drowns here — but a low stone, lipped, that holds coals under damp moss for a few sullen hours when a child is sick or leather must be smoked. Mostly they eat cold: cloudberries from the wall-gardens above, bitter kale that grows impossibly sweet after frost, raw white fish sliced thin and wrapped in larch needles to take the bog taste off, ptarmigan eggs sucked whole in spring.

They speak the way the rift speaks. Not loud. The walls return everything to itself, so a shout would come back and sit on your ears for too long. Their language is low and wet — clicks made with the tongue against teeth, vowels shaped around water in the mouth, words separated by listening pauses. A mother calling her child does not yell across the channel; she makes a soft *tok* with a wooden frog against a trunk, and the sound travels through root and water better than air. Children learn to be still incredibly early. You see a knot of them — five, six years old, bellies round with starch, naked except for their moss capes — standing absolutely motionless in a reed screen, watching midges. The midges at noon rise in columns that bend light, a deafening silent cloud, and the children open their mouths and let them land on tongue and teeth, harvest protein by the breathful. They chew solemnly, eyes watering with cold.

The elders are different. With age, their skin thickens and greens at the edges, not from disease but from symbiosis — a fine algal felt that grows in the perpetual damp of their elbow-crooks and behind their ears, helping with vitamin D in a place that sees an hour of direct sun. The oldest cannot leave their boles; they have rooted, almost literally. Sit with one and you see her legs have swollen, edematous, sinking into the peat floor where fine rootlets from the house-tree have found her dead skin and politely, opportunistically, grown in. She does not mind. She tends what she can reach — braiding reed, humming low songs that vibrate the water and call fish. When she dies, they will not bury her. The house will do it. They will lay her in the mat-water under the floor slats, cover her with sphagnum, and in a month there will be no distinction between grandmother and insulation. The peat here is fifteen feet deep and half of it is people, and half of it is spruce, and the distinction long ago stopped holding.

But they are not alone in the tending. That would be too lonely a city.

The musk oxen — or what were musk oxen before the rift took them — move through the avenues like slow, shaggy barges. They are smaller than their steppe cousins, their coats perpetually soaked and parted into dreadlocks of wet wool, their muzzles stained green from browsing horsetail. Their hooves are enormous, splayed snowshoe-plates that keep them from punching through the mat. No one owns them, no one herds them. They were here first, or came soon after the cut opened. The people have learned to follow them. Where an ox has trodden for generations, the water stays open, the peat compresses into a causeway. Where they rub their great heads against a pleached wall, they prune. Children ride between their horns, picking parasites and whispering. When an ox dies — and they die standing up, often, sinking slowly to become islands — a dozen families will move their boles to colonize the new hummock, grateful.

Above, on the young basalt walls that still flake and weep, the small predators do their own city-work. Martens slip along ledges no wider than your hand, foxes the rust-color of mineral seep dart between hanging gardens. Each hanging garden — peat mortared by roots to vertical rock, a furrow of cloudberries tended from a willow ledge hammered into a crack two hundred years ago — has its own scare-crow that is not a scare-crow but a hawk. The hawks nest on the wall-tops in the frost-feather, and drop each noon hour to scream once down the length of the rift, scattering ptarmigan from moss roofs in soft white explosions. The people look up, briefly, to count their birds. The fish under the translucent moss turn, showing a belly-flash twenty feet away, and everyone hears it.

At midday the pale-gold sun stands straight overhead and the entire wound lights. For one hour the weeping walls glitter, every thread of water catching. Mist rises off the living mat in a slow exhalation — the packed, wet, green city breathing — and the air itself becomes visible. You finally understand the scale: miles long, half a mile across, saturated. Not crowded. Saturated. Every cubic foot of damp air and cold water and grafted wood has something alive in it doing something alive.

Then the sun moves on. The walls shade. The hush returns like a cathedral after rain.

And in that hush you hear them again — not building, not cutting, not conquering. Just tending. A shale knife scoring bark. A root curtain dripping shut. A child's wet foot finding bottom. A willow withe being tucked into a dying arch so that tomorrow there will still be a door where yesterday there was a door, in the cold wet greenhouse at the bottom of the world, where nothing is taken outside and nothing is allowed to be empty for long.
