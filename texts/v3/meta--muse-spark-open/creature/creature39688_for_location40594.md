Nothing in the pocket lives alone. That is the first thing.

Even what looks like one thing is a crowd that has agreed to hold still together.

**The Green That Holds The Room**

It is not kelp. Kelp has holdfasts and stipes and blades and an idea of up. This has none of those for long. It starts with a root - if root is the word for a waxy green-black foot that melts directly into basalt and does not need soil because it eats cold itself. From there it puts out a frond that decides what shape a frond should be after it has already grown.

Some are broad as altar cloths, translucent bottle-green, veined with a paler light that brightens in your peripheral vision and dims when you stare. Some are hair-fine, black-red, branching and re-branching until they make a felt that traps water inside water. Some are bladders, some are tongues, some are flat ribbons that grew through another ribbon and kept going, so that two leaves share the same tissue for an inch and then separate again without a scar.

They do not sway. They press.

Packed so densely you cannot see where one plant ends and another begins, they make a single continuous leaning - a forest that is also a meadow that is also a single organism pretending to be many for convenience. Their surfaces bead with a dew that should be impossible underwater: perfect, lens-like droplets of *wetter* water that clings to wax, swells, and finally detaches as a pearl that rises slower than breath. An inch per hour. You could watch one for a whole visit and not see it pop.

When the basalt cuts them - and the young rock still cuts everything that touches it, glass-sharp hexagonal lips, needle-teeth along cooling blisters - they do not heal quickly. They bleed. A cloud of cold stars hangs exactly where the leaf was torn, luminous sap unmixing, each droplet holding its place for days. The fronds around it lean away politely, then over weeks, lean back in and stitch through the cloud as if it were just more water.

This is what lives there most. By weight, by volume, by patience. Not a population but a packing. If you listen for its sound it makes it once every long while: one leaf growing one millimeter pushes another and that pushes another all the way around the chapel-lung until the whole pocket gives a low, house-settling creak through the thick water. Then stops. It is the only way you know it is alive and not just lush.

**The Dust That Is Also An Animal**

The water itself is inhabited, because here water is never empty.

Hold still and look at what you first took for silt. It doesn't settle. It holds position, each mote equidistant from the next like careful work.

Some of it is spores, or pollen. Pale gold discs with a dark dot in the center that opens and closes slower than an eye blink. Some of it is skins - translucent molts no bigger than an eyelash, shed by something you never see molted, drifting with a faint curl still in them, as if they remember being worn.

And some of it twitches.

Tiny things - copepod, ciliate, larva, none of those words quite fit - as clear as the water but for a gut of green inside. They don't swim. Swimming implies current. They reposition themselves by extruding a filament too thin to see and pulling, hand over hand, through the thickness. If you put your hand out, the water remembers your handprint and so do they: they will collect along that invisible edge for a minute, tasting the shape of warmth, then forget.

There are more of them than anything else, and they are why nothing rots quickly. A dead leaf does not fall. It is held, and then slowly unmade in the airy dark, spore by spore, until the leaf is a lattice and then a memory of green and then just more motes.

**The Singular Tenants**

Larger animals are rare not because they cannot survive, but because the pocket has only room for one narrative at a time.

You might get the shrimp.

Blind, white, the size of a thumb joint, with antennae longer than itself that it moves like a person feeling their way through a dark house they built themselves. It does not walk so much as agree with a surface - floor becoming wall becoming ceiling without ever letting go. It picks at the waxy beads on the fronds and at the sap-stars when it finds them fresh. It has been there for generations, or it is the same one for a very long time; you cannot tell, because it molts rarely and leaves no shell behind - the dust eats it instantly. When it senses you - not sees, senses pressure-change - it stops and holds perfectly still until you are just another piece of slow green pressure, and then continues.

Or you might get the fish.

Never more than one. Never twice the same one, but always one. The color of dusk under water: mauve-grey with a belly that catches that peripheral leaf-light and returns it. Eyes too large, filmed, looking sideways at a vertical that isn't there anymore. It hangs in the center of the room where there should be open water and there isn't - among the motes - and it doesn't move its fins so much as adjust its decision about which way is down. It can hang inverted for hours above a sideways-growing frond as if that were dignified. It breathes once every long time. It eats the motes without hunting; it just opens its mouth and the thickness delivers.

You will not see both at once. The pocket corrects.

If you stay longer than you should, you may sense a third kind of tenant that is not an animal but uses animals when it needs eyes.

**The Hands That Left Marks**

On the lowest shelf, under a curtain of door-wide fronds that have learned to incorporate rather than hide, there is the cairn. Three stones, balanced, basalt on basalt, too deliberate for collapse. Beneath the cairn the rock is discolored, paler, where repeated handling wore the black off.

Nearby, the perfect circle carved shallow into the floor, filled with a silt so fine it feels like powder even wet. If you brush it, it clouds and then re-settles in the same perfect circle in an hour, grain by grain, choosing its shape again. On the far wall, the stair: three, four incisions forming a step up toward nothing, ending in uncut rock as if the maker sat back, remembered that exits are not needed here, and left the tool - not there, never rust, never plastic - but the intention.

Whatever lived here before the green packed in tight was human, or human-shaped enough to stack and carve and try to climb out of a place that has no out. They did not die here violently. There are no bones. The dust would have taken bones even, eventually, but not with such neatness, not leaving the cairn unknocked.

More likely they stayed until up and down became suggestions and then they forgot which suggestion they had come in with. More likely they are still here, thinned out, distributed. A warmth in the shrimp's pause. A habit in the fish's hovering. A tendency in the water to hold a handprint a little longer than physics allows.

Sometimes when the membrane-wall at the entrance shivers, it does not show the ocean outside. It shows this same pocket inverted, and in it your back is turned and your hands are busy doing something small and precise to the cairn - adding a fourth stone, brushing the circle clean - with a care that you have not yet learned but recognize.

If you reach for that double, your hand comes back colder, and the fronds nearest the doorway unfurl a fraction faster that week, as if fed.

But mostly, the living of the place is the green itself. Crowded, cold, unhurried to the point of stillness, luminous in its own damp way, holding its own thick water apart from the world. It does not notice you as food or threat. At 0.2 light it sees without seeing, grows through itself, bleeds stars when cut, and waits.

After you drop back out through the lip - if you do - the water will hold the shape where you were for a long moment, crowded with motes investigating an absence, before forgetting you were ever there.
