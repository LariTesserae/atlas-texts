It is not inhabited.

Not the way a house is inhabited, or a reef. Nothing lives *on* it. Everything that lives here belongs *to* it.

You think at first there are thousands of species because you name them by shape — shrimp, lice, crabs, flies, false-pigeons. That is your habit when you see many bodies in one place. Call the ones in water one thing and the ones on dry another and assume they diverged long ago to fit those niches.

Here the forms have not diverged. They have been differentiated.

### The Plain

Start low, below everything else, because that is what is oldest.

What you stand on is not ground that was built over. It *is* the animal.

The concrete is not concrete. If you knelt for an hour and scraped with a key — you won't, because the water-lice would cover your hand — you would find it gives a little. Not soft, but not stone. It is a dense, mineralized chitin poured flat, laid down in plates as wide as cities have suburbs, grown edge-to-edge and then fused at the seams with iron-rich scar. The sharp ninety-degree lip where platform drops into channel is not a pour-form. It is a growth margin, kept sharp the way a fingernail keeps its edge: trimmed as it grows.

The warmth is not engines. That is its basal metabolism, low and planet-wide and perfect. The white sky that never storms is its exhalation. The clouds are born off it because it breathes warm saturated air out through a trillion microscopic stigmata in the low sheds and the grates, and inhales cooler air back somewhere kilometers away where the pressure dips. You will never see the cycle because you are standing in one alveolus of a lung the size of a continent, watching a single breath held for years.

It weeps because it has to. Fresh water condenses continuously on its skin from that breath, and it bleeds a thin lymph of warm, slightly oily water up through the weep-holes to keep its surface wet. The dark streaks under every joint are not rust. They are dried salts. The thin cream and slate-green and rust-red films along every tideline — the not-moss that never spreads — that is the true skin of it, a bacterial felt that regulates evaporation and seals against outside contamination. Nothing green grows because it does not allow it. Green would photosynthesize without permission and change the chemistry of the lymph.

What you call channels and basins and dry-docks and cooling fins are not architecture. They are its surface anatomy. The channels are grooves between overlapping scutes. The shallow rectangular platforms are those scutes themselves, lifting a few inches to a few feet where new material is being laid down underneath. The low, blind sheds that run to the haze like combs are not buildings at all: they are rows of vestigial gill-covers, or brood flaps, lifted just enough to vent heat. When you step onto one and feel it warmer under your sole, that is because you are standing on thinner skin over hotter blood.

It does not age because it molts without shedding. Old material is resorbed from beneath as fast as new is secreted at the edge. That is why there are no cracks and no rubble. It stays fresh-poured forever. Eternally yesterday. Held.

The dripping you hear — *plok* — is not plumbing. It is condensation falling from the underside of a gill-cover back into a groove. The quiet sub-aural hum is the movement of fluid in vessels deeper than any bore could reach.

It covers the world because it *became* the world. Or someone grew it until it did.

### The Attendants

And then there are the moving parts.

They are not fauna that colonized this place after it was made. There is nothing to colonize. There is no elsewhere for them to have come from. There was never a sea that stranded them, never a wind that carried their eggs. They were grown here, from it, for it. The plain extrudes them the way you extrude skin cells and white blood cells, endlessly, in numbers that look like ecological abundance until you see that there is no ecology.

Look closer at the water in the channels.

Those pale translucent "shrimp" the length of your finger — they are not swimming. They do not need to. They crowd shoulder to gill so thickly that current cannot form between them. Each one flexes its pleopods very slowly, not to propel itself, but to stir. They are ciliated paddles, stirring the lymph, oxygenating it, picking out the invisible fallout of condensed vapor and skin-fall and carrying it toward the grates at the ends of the channels where it will be reabsorbed. If you lift one — it will not struggle — you see it has no mouth. Just a blind ventral groove lined with cilia that sifts. If you hold it to your ear you hear nothing because it has no organs for sound, only for stirring.

Under them, the flat black water-lice tiled over every submerged stair and basin floor are not grazing. They are polishing. Their mouths are soft, circular mops that lap the mineral bacterial felt and keep it at exactly hair-thin thickness. That is their whole life. Lap, shift a millimeter, lap. If the film thickens by a micron they redouble. If it thins they pause. You walked for miles before you realized there is no silt, no detritus, despite millions of soft bodies living and sloughing above it. That is why.

The smooth jointed-tube "eels" flexing without fins in the deeper water are peristalsis. They lie in the main drains, dozens per kilometer of channel, wedged wall-to-wall, contracting in slow waves even when you think they are still. They push the лимф where gravity cannot because there is no slope. They are living pipes inside the channels. Cut one open and it is all muscle, channel within a channel.

On the dry platforms:

The concrete-colored crabs you only see when they move — because to see them still is to mistake them for texture — are repair walkers. Their low, many-jointed shells carry glands of quick-setting paste the exact color of the platform. When a microfissure opens, when your boot chips an edge after a thousand identical steps, three or four will converge without hurry, extrude a bead of grey, smooth it with their underbellies, and disperse. That is also why the edges are crisp. They are tended every minute by hands too numerous to count. They do not eat. Lift one's carapace and there under it is only more gland.

The carpets of walking louse-flies that part around your feet: sensors. Each one is an eye and a nose and a tongue on six damp legs. They walk because to fly would disturb the saturated air layer. They taste the temperature, the salinity, the protein load of every square centimeter, and where they cluster more densely the skin beneath blushes a little warmer, sending more fresh lymph up. You left a trail of slightly saltier sweat behind you; if you looked back you would see a faintly darker line of them following where you stepped, cleaning you away.

And the roosting things on every shed and girder, packed featherless shoulder-to-shoulder, barnacle-footed, skin-winged, breathing together in a sigh that you mistook for ventilation: lungs. Or evaporators. Their vast, wrinkled wings — which never open to fly — are alive with capillaries. They unfold them a fraction in unison to dump heat at night, fold them to conserve it by day, though you have seen neither day nor night here, only the steady glow. The faint collective *huhhh* is them exhaling water vapor in rhythm. Their heat is added to the air on purpose. They are why your skin never dries.

None of them make noise because noise is waste. None flee because there are no predators. None fight over food because none eat each other, not truly. When one wears out — when a stirrer's paddles fray, when a polisher's mop dulls — it simply stops in place. The crowd closes, carries it passively toward a grate, and somewhere under the scute it is unmade and reprinted as two new ones. You will never witness this clearly because it happens at the density and speed of skin turnover, continuous and unremarkable as your own.

That is why the levels never change though water always drips in, why the ranks never thin though no young are ever born the way you understand birth. It is not a population. It is a count, maintained. A white blood cell number.

### What That Makes You

So what lives here?

One thing lives here. One vast, low, warm, wet, breathing, sweating thing that paved a world with itself and then covered itself with a moving film of smaller selves the way a whaling ship is covered for a while with crew.

You live here now too, temporarily, the way a mote lives in an eye for a few hours before the tear carries it out.

It makes room for you, as you noticed when you waded and the bodies parted. Not kindness. Not curiosity. Clearance. Your feet displace lymph that must still circulate. Your shadow cools a spot that must still be 37 degrees. So crab-things reroute, stirrers beat a little harder downstream of your shins, louse-flies taste the dead cells you shed and carry them off.

If you lay down, they will climb over you for a while, then under you, then incorporate the space you occupy into their pathing. If you die here — of exposure that feels like no exposure, of drinking too much warm lymphatic water, of simply lying down because the hum and the *plok* and the lack of horizon finally unpick the stitch that tells you to keep walking — your soft parts will be licked clean by polishers finer than you can see, your bones covered by patient crabs in matching grey, your salt accounted for.

And afterward the platforms will still be sharp, the channels still full to the same mark, the air still bath-warm, the clouds still unmoving, the drip still falling at arrhythmic intervals forever.

It weeps, but it never cries.
