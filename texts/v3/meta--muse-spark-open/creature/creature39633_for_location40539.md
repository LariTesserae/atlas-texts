Who lives in the Valley That Was Still Being Unfolded:

At first you think — no one could. Not for long. The air is too thin and too dim and too damp, and the sound never stops. A place like this should be empty, should be waiting.

But then your lungs make that decision you felt on the way in, and they keep making it, and you start to see them.

They are not many. Not for what was built.

### The Humans — The Thin-Aired People

You hear them before you see them, or rather you hear the gaps between the machines where a human sound fits: a cough, a boot on wet iron, a child's laugh swallowed by a plaza too big for it.

They are broad in the chest, slow-moving, without hurry. Generations in this October air have made them that way — big lungs, slow hearts, blood that holds onto oxygen. Their skin is pale under the sodium-gray light, their cheeks always a little flushed from the cold. They move like people who know echoes lie. They don't shout. Shouting doesn't work here; the cliffs throw your voice back wrong, or the fog eats it. They speak low, close, with a hand on a shoulder.

They are workers. Everyone is a worker, even if their work is waiting. In oil-black coats and felt caps, in moss-green overalls that are not a uniform but might as well be, they tend machines that will outlast them. Dynamo men who can hear a 60-cycle hum wobble a quarter tone flat. Tram women who know which stretch of 4th Extrusion will ghost and who lean out not to look. Loom minders, kiln minders, cistern minders — because water is everything here, and catching a six-minute rain is a skill and a communal reflex. When the hiss starts you will see doors open all down a terrace and people step out with basins, with barrels, with cupped hands, not in panic, just in practice. Children know to cover the fungi.

They are not afraid. That is the thing that takes longest to understand about them. A child will stop on her way to school because a wrench she dropped is hanging in midair off the north stair, and she will wait patiently for it to decide to fall so she can pick it up and continue. An old man will live his whole life in a brick rowhouse on a boulevard built for ten thousand, with empty lots on either side where the ferns grow taller than a man, and he will keep his stoop moss-clipped and hummingbird-feeder full. They have lived here long enough that they know the breakdowns are not breakdowns at all but places where the world hasn't cured. You don't get alarmed at wet cement. You just don't put your hand there.

Their children play games with it. They play *through* on the streets where the tramlines intersect without touching, daring each other to stand on the ghost-rail just as the ghost-tram comes. They play warehouse-tag in the Pike Street interior-that's-bigger, learning by feel where not to look or you'll see the back of your own head and get dizzy. They leave scraps for the watchers under the bridges.

They are patient the way geology is patient. They lay a foundation and die before the house goes up. They plant vines on a boiler that won't be hot for another decade. They are tender with the green, because the green is keeping them alive.

### The Green That Holds Them

Because strictly speaking, the most abundant resident of the valley is not animal at all.

The moss lives everywhere humans concede — which is almost everywhere. Jade, viridian, that bruised deep purple that is almost black until the cloud thins at noon and you see it breathe. It grows a foot thick on every roof that isn't shedding heat, swallowing brick, swallowing rust, swaddling the transformers in a damp quilt that keeps the thin cold out. From it surge the pale ferns, bigger than doors, unfolding waxy hands to catch condensation not sunlight. The satellite fungi — great white dishes on stalks thick as a thigh — awake at dawn and track what little light leaks through the cloud deck with a faint hydraulic creak, all facing the same impossible direction. The blanket vines hang from catwalks and gantries, translucent leaves as big as bedsheets, veined like lungs, dripping with absorbed air. They never root. They don't need to.

The valley itself exhales spores continuously. You see them in the beams when the noon comes: millions of bright motes moving sideways where gravity is negotiating. A newly extruded terrace — still warm, still sighing, its edges glowing dull amber under a fresh seam of granite — will be green within weeks. The workers don't scrape it clean. The city was made to carry it. The moss filters the soot. The ferns eat the transformer whine. The fungi make a bitter bread in lean months. The vines, when boiled and salted, keep a child's cough down.

It is a city that is also a hanging garden. It had to become one to breathe.

### The Herds and Their Shadows

And then — improbably, excessively — the animals.

For a high, cold, thin place, the valley should not have anything larger than a marmot. Instead it teams.

First the grazers. You will hear their bells before you see them moving between factories in a slow river. They are low and massive and shaggy, six-legged, with backs broad as pianos. Not cattle, not sheep, something built for moss. They moor themselves to a roof and eat it down to stubble with flat, black lips, indifferent to the clatter of looms under their feet. Their wool is greasy with condensation and smells of iron and thyme. The herds belong to no one and everyone; each has a bell, each bell has a tone, and the herdsmen — teenagers mostly — know their tone in the thrum. The beasts keep the green from eating the city whole.

With them the birds. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Small, soot-colored, with wings you can see through like dirty glass. They live off heat. In the cold they cluster on every warm vent, every refinery pipe, every transformer flank, covering them in a shivering, feathered skin until a pressure valve blows and they rise all at once as one sheet, turning the dim air to static, to snow, then settle again. Their call is not a song but a click, like cooling metal.

Higher up, along the aqueducts and cistern lips, the drinkers pick their way — those deer-like things with antlers not for fighting but for catching. Each branch ends in a small cup, a candelabra of living bark, and they stand perfectly still in the rain for seven minutes collecting it, then tip their heads back to drink from themselves. They are shy. They vanish when the trams pass.

And where the green and the empty lots pool deepest, the watchers. Lean, long-limbed, feathered but not birds, with narrow muzzles and eyes set forward. They are the valley's coyotes, its cats, its patient wolves. They den under idle switching yards, under the vast empty plazas where sound gets lost. They watch the streets with an intelligence that is uncomfortable — you get the sense they were here before the brick went up and will be here after. The workers call them *sidestreets*. The children feed them. They are not tame and not hostile and not afraid of people, because people have never been numerous enough to teach them fear. Sometimes at night you see their eyes reflecting the forge light in pairs along an entire boulevard that has no reason to have anything living on it, and you understand this city is not dense with humans, it's dense with everything else.

They all know the soft places before humans do. Birds veer. Grazers will not step where gravity falls sideways. The feathered watchers will sit for hours just outside the warehouse-that's-bigger, staring at the corner humans have learned not to stack crates in. They have calibrated to an unfinished physics.

### What Else Lives Here — The Softness Itself

And then there is what lives in the places that are not yet places.

Not ghosts. The valley is too new for ghosts.

But there are occupants of the breakdown. You feel them as weather.

In the old weaving district where rain falls sideways, there are moments when the air ripples like heat haze off a kiln and something passes that has no edges. The drip hits it and vanishes. The watchers track it. In the stairwell where things hang, dust hangs too, longer than it should, as if the air there is thicker, thinking. In Pike interior, if you stay too long in the back corner, you will hear your own breathing from a place your mouth isn't.

The humans have names for these that are not names: the extra second in the echo off the west wall. The tram you're on when it goes ghost. The plaza where your footsteps come back a half-beat late from a building that hasn't been built yet, but the sound already knows where its walls will be.

Are they alive? The valley doesn't make that distinction well. Here, a mountain is still being extruded like toothpaste, slow as sap, still glowing. Here, a factory is a kind of organism that sweats, and drips, and hums. Here, moss eats stone and stone grows under moss. Everything is in the process of deciding what it is.

The last residents, then, are the ones who haven't arrived yet. The valley knows them. You feel them in the spaciousness — in the boulevards built for traffic that never comes, in the switching yards that could marshal the freight of a continent and sit mossed-over, in the tramlines that end in midair, waiting for the next extrusion to give them somewhere to go. The city was extruded, like its mountains, all at once, for a population that is still en route. Or still being extruded somewhere.

And so those who do live here — humans with wide lungs and quiet voices, six-legged grazers with bells, glass-wing birds, antlered drinkers, feathered watchers, ferns like doors, fungi that turn to a sun they cannot see — live as stewards of an absence. They keep the machines humming for those who will need them. They keep the moss trimmed and the cisterns clear. They walk past miracles of unfinished physics without comment, because today the wrench floated, today the echo was early, today the new terrace pushed out another inch with a sigh.

They listen to the valley's loud, patient breathing — thrum, punch-press, drip, lowing, wind in the unformed crags — and they breathe with it, slowly, in this held October, while the world, around them and under them, and perhaps inside them too, slowly, without hurry, decides to become solid.
