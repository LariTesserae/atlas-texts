What lives on the High Sponge does not live *on* it.

That distinction matters, because after an hour of walking you understand there is no "on." There's no ground to stand on and thing to stand on it. There's only the Sponge, and degrees of being part of it.

Everything here is either mat, or moving through mat, or temporarily detached from mat with permission to return.

### The Mat Itself

At bottom, and through everything else, is the peat-mind. Twelve feet of black-brown compress, cold inside where it never sees sun. It's not soil. It's a body. A third fungus, a third half-rotted moss and birch root, a third bacteria so dense it holds heat.

The heat is the first clue it's alive. Put your hand flat on bare peat — where a hare has kicked it open — and it's warm. 88, 90 degrees. Not geothermal, not quite. Compost-heat, held and regulated. The peat is digesting itself at a controlled burn, wet smoldering without flame, and venting the excess through those domes.

If you lay an ear to it, on a windless morning before the forges start, you hear it tick. Not a heart. A million hearts. Gas bubbles percolating up through waterlogged fiber, popping at intervals too regular to be random. It's breathing out methane and breathing in fog-water.

Hanging through it, under it, over it: the Mycelial Loom. White threads thick as twine, thick as your wrist, branching down to hairs finer than spider silk. It is the conveyor belt and the bloodstream. Under a hand lens you can see ore dust moving along it — black magnetite grains, green-blue verdigris copper — not drifting, being *hauled*. The fungus plates metal onto itself like enamel to make stiff places, and keeps itself soft where it needs to flex. It decides.

Growing out of that, inseparable from it, is the Coral.

That's what the birch and stone-pine have become up here. Not trees. They gave up being trees thousands of feet below. Up here they are scaffolding that the Sponge extruded for itself. Trunks the diameter of your thigh, but only four feet tall, then splitting into a hundred interlaced, horizontal branches that graft together when they touch. You can't push between two of them because there is no between; the branches have fused into a lattice. Leaves are small, waxy, cupped to catch fog. Every leaf axil holds a bromeliad-cup, every cup holds water, every cup has something living in the water.

And on every branch, moss on moss on moss. The primary colonist is a feather-moss that grows only on living birch-bark and makes a felt an inch thick. On that felt grows a liverwort that only grows on that moss, that makes tiny white stars. In those stars lives an orchid the size of your thumbnail that never touches bark at all. It's a tower, eight stories high and three inches tall, and at each level the tenants are different.

This is the housing. Almost everything else lives *inside* the thickness of that felt.

### The Forges

You noticed the domes. There are so many you stop counting. From the air they'd look like blisters.

Each one is an animal and a plant and a kiln at once. Call it a furnace polyp.

Under the moss-felt, its wall is living chitin — not secreted and left, but still alive, veined, warm to touch. Inside, a chamber fills with iron-bitter water siphoned up from the weeping basalt by taproots gone deep. The chamber walls constrict — thud — once every ten seconds. That's not a pump. That's a hammer. A stalactite of magnetite, grown in place, swung on a cord of lignin-tendon, striking a floor of layered silica glass the organism excreted flake by flake.

Every strike spalls off a leaf of native metal.

Not molten. Cold-forged. The peat-heat has already concentrated the water, boiled it almost dry in a lower gut. The hammer just peels it.

And then the carriers come.

Open one of those chambers — carefully, it will seal itself with resin in minutes if you tear it — and you'll see the anvil glittering. Around it, a shivering carpet moves. At first you think it's mold. It's mites. Thousands of them, each no bigger than a poppy seed, each with a back pitted like a thimble. They swarm the fresh flake, abrade it with their own jaws, load the dust into those pits by static, and march it away along fungal roads to wherever it's needed. One line for copper, going to the leaves that need stiffening against wind. One line for iron, going to the vine-wires.

If you watch long enough you see traffic management. Mites giving way. No queen, no orders. Just chemistry.

Some domes are different. Quench-domes are low and wide, always full of perfectly rectangular pools — dammed by buttress roots grown at right angles on purpose. The water in them is black and still and cold, even when the peat around them is warm. They exist only to plunge hot silica sheets into, to temper them.

Wire-domes are taller, and they hum. Inside they extrude electrum wire, hair-fine, through spinnerets. The wire is alive for a while after it's made — fungal sheath around metal core — and it *crawls* to join the Grid. You can see it at night, faintly glowing, creeping an inch an hour toward the nearest main vine.

And storm-domes — you only find them on slight rises — open like flowers before a storm. They are lightning rods grown as living copper trees, ten feet tall but made entirely of plated air-roots. When the daily 3 p.m. storm walks across the plateau, and it always walks from west to east, these take the strike. You'd think they'd burn. They don't. The whole mat is wet. The charge is bled down through the wire-vines and stored in peat-batteries: layers of manganese oxide the Loom has laid down like a capacitor, black seams in the bog you can taste with your tongue as a metallic tingle.

That evening, those vines glow.

### The Small, Abundant Nations

Sound is eaten here, and size is punished. Nothing large can push through packing.

So everything is small, and everything tunnels.

**The Moss Hares:** Not hares. Convergent. Mouse-sized, with hind legs still built for hopping, but they never hop. Their fur has grown long and moss-like, green-tipped, and harbors living moss in it. They carve highways through the felt — perfect tubes, an inch and a half wide, kept open by constant nibbling. If you kneel you see entrances everywhere, like a sponge's pores. They eat new moss tip and carry forge-dust in their cheek pouches, not knowing why. Their droppings are little ore pellets the mites mine again.

**The Peat Weasels:** Longer than your hand, thin as eels, with whiskers that sense vibration in wet peat. They don't dig. They swim. The peat quakes ahead of them and closes behind, leaving no tunnel. You only know one's passed because the surface shivers and all the hare-highways tremble. They eat hares by listening for the heartbeat through the moss and striking sideways through the mat like a shark through kelp. Their fur is slick, almost slime, and sheds water constantly.

**The Barge Birds:** There are birds — you hear them before you see them. A short, loud *burr* of wings, ten feet of frantic flight, then crash-landing into moss. Wings are too short for real flight in thin, fog-thick air, but perfect for barging from birch-lattice to birch-lattice. They are drab, brown and lichen-grey, beaks like tweezers. They don't eat seeds. There are no clearings for seeds to fall. They eat mites, picking them off fungal roads with surgical precision, and glass-bees. In nesting season they weave nests from living electrum wire — stealing it warm from wire-domes while it still twitches — so the whole nest glints and faintly shocks anything that touches it.

**The Glass Bees:** Each one clear-winged, each one carrying a droplet. Not nectar. Fog-water, or quench-water, or iron-water, depending on which caste. You think a white flower on the liverwort felt is a flower until it detaches and flies away as twenty bees huddled together for warmth, sharing a single scent. They are the only true fliers here, and even they fly low, heavy. They pollinate nothing we would recognize as flower — they pollinate the domes, carrying chemical messages, spores, activation droplets from one forge-heart to another.

And under all of this, invisible until you look with the right light: springtails like flecks of ash that jump through water films; beetles with backs of hammered copper that were not born that way but were plated by mites while they slept; liverworts that close when a shadow passes because a tiny, blind shrimp lives inside their cup and kicks when light changes.

None of them lead. None of them rule.

The plateau isn't a hive with a queen. It's a factory town where every worker evolved to need the factory, and the factory evolved to need every worker, until no one remembers which came first. The thudding you hear is not industry imposed on nature. It *is* the nature. The Sponge lifts ore with roots, concentrates it with rot-heat, forges it cold, wires itself against lightning, quenches and plates and grows, all without a hand, without a plan, because for a million damp springs, the mat that did it a little better held a little more fog, kept a little more heat, and grew a little wider over the basalt.

If you stand still until the rain comes — cold, straight-down, hissing where it hits the warmest domes — you'll feel it pause. The thudding slows. The mites shelter. The birds tuck into copper nests. The whole high continent-wide felt takes a breath, drinks, and holds.

Then starts again.
