They told you nothing lives here.

And at first, you believe it.

There are no footprints in the silt except yours, and even yours fill in too quickly, water sighing back into the depression. No birds. No rats in the walls. No cockroaches under the stone. No microbial film except the dark moss, which isn't really moss.

You stand for a long time listening for anything that wants to be called alive, and you hear only the ticking. Drop. Drop. Drop.

Then you learn to see on its timescale.

### The First Residents: The Keepers of the Cycle

It lives three feet off the ground.

Not *it*. *They*. But you won't count them correctly at first, because they don't end where their bodies end.

The air between the buildings is heavy enough to wring out. In the heat-without-light, water is always trying to be something else. It lifts off the hot mirrors — those ankle-deep seas between edifices — in a breath you can feel more than see, a warm invisible exhalation. It rises a foot, two feet, three feet. And then it fails.

It can't become cloud. It can't become rain. The dark is too saturated to accept it.

So it hangs there. A low fog, a wandering thickness at chest height, at head height. It pools in the leeward side of a weeping terrace, it drifts down the canal-streets like a slow animal nosing along.

This is the oldest thing here.

When it touches the cool stone — the pitted, coral-like limestone-concrete that sweats light — it condenses. Beads. Trickles. Veins running down softened cornices, gathering in the inch-deep canyons that used to be gutters.

You think: that's just condensation.

Watch longer.

The fog moves *against* the draft. It lingers where the stone is driest, as if tending it. It thins itself out to cover more surface. One patch will hover for an hour over a single hot pool, patiently drinking, getting denser, heavier, darker, until it can barely hold itself — and then it will make the short, deliberate journey to the thirstiest wall and give itself back.

It is not mindless. It is not mindful either. It is *husbandry*. A three-foot tall weather system that has learned to farm its own watershed. The city exhales, the fog drinks the exhale, the stone drinks the fog. An endless sip. Over centuries, this loop has smoothed the buildings. You are watching the sculptor at work.

If you stand still too long, one will find you. You are cooler than the water and warmer than the stone. Condensation will prickle on your forearms, gather at your elbows. A faint pressure around your shoulders like being breathed on. It will try to include you in the cycle. If you let it, the sweat on your skin will never quite become a drop — it will lift right off you, join the wandering thickness, and be carried tenderly to a wall.

Do not let it. You need your water.

### The Second Residents: The In-Between Buildings

They grow where nothing should grow.

You saw the rosettes — low, flat, black-red succulents on the terraces, leaves thick as rubber. You saw the single reeds, one every twenty or thirty feet, standing absolutely alone in clear, warm water with no roots.

Look again, closer, with your almost-light adjusted eyes.

The rosette does not open for dew. It *is* dew, for a while.

Each leaf is cupped with impossible precision. In the hot dark, water beads on its waxy skin and stays there, magnified. Inside each bead, light from the stone refracts and *holds*. You will see a tiny, perfect, inverted city inside a single droplet on a single leaf — terraces, canals, reeds. The rosette collects these little cities. It does not drink them. It displays them until they evaporate, and then it waits, patient as a monk, for the next one to land.

It flowers rarely. When it does, it does not put out a petal. It puts out a hole. A small, round absence in the air just above its center, about the size of a fingertip, where the dripping sound stops. If you peer into it — and you will be tempted, because it is the only true dark in a place made of damp luminescence — you see depth. More terraces. Another avenue stretching too long. Another rosette looking back up at you from below. It closes after a few minutes, with a soft, wet click.

The reeds are different.

They are not plants. Or, they are plants the way coral is an animal: only technically, and missing the point.

Each reed grows straight out of the water with no root, absolutely still, absolutely alone, as you noticed. Tug one and it will not come up. Not because it is anchored, but because it is *continuous* with the water. The water is its root. Cut it — though you have nothing to cut it with — and it would bleed warm, clear canal water, because it is a way for the water to stand up and see.

They do not sway. They have no need to. There is no wind. But if you walk past a sequence of them — one, two, three, down the long canal-street — you will notice the second one is leaning a fraction toward where you *were*, and the third one is leaning a fraction toward where you are *going*. Not much. A millimeter. A phototropism, but for presence. The water is tasting your displacement.

In the far distance, across the quarter-mile plain of shallow sea, where the towers look both huge and oddly close, you can sometimes see a forest of them that wasn't there before. Hundreds of reeds, close together. That never happens near you. The water only crowds like that where it is looking at something else.

### The Third Residents: The City Itself

This is what you felt in how you move. Blocks. Avenues. The ghost of a grid pressed into soft earth.

The city is not ruined. It is *settled*. Like silt settles. Like an old animal settles into the shape of its bed.

And like any old thing that has settled, it has become aware of its own weight.

You noticed the wrongness before you could name it: the same crack dripping on two different buildings. The avenue that gets longer when you turn your back. The room far above you that contains another sky, darker and wetter, with another tiny city inside it.

That is not a haunting. That is *metabolism*.

The buildings are porous. They weep. They drink fog. Inside, floor has melted into terrace, terrace into slope, slope into basin. Mineral skin grows over everything like a slow, cool scab — layer after layer of dissolved limestone and concrete re-precipitated by those endless three-foot cycles. Year after year, century after century, the city accretes itself. Gutters become inch-deep canyons. Streets become riverbeds. Windows become gills because the stone around them grew thicker and left them as necessary holes to breathe.

In doing so, the city has learned to fold.

Space pools here the way water does. Low places collect more of it. The long, empty plains of water between towers are not empty — they are *deep*. Not in water. In distance. The city leaves those voids intentionally, the way a marsh leaves space for water, because it needs somewhere to put the extra space it has eroded off its corners.

When you walk down a straight canal-street, you are walking through the city's gut. The repetition you see — the same drip, the same crack — is the city re-using a pattern it liked. It is more efficient to copy a good gargoyle than to invent a new one. Efficient to copy a good room than to carve a new one. Somewhere above you, a terrace contains a room that contains another city because the city dreamed the city and liked the shape of the dream.

Gravity is polite but forgetful here because the city is still deciding which way is down. Water runs downhill, then along a flat, then *up* a shallow step for a foot before remembering. Droplets fall from an arch and hang a half second too long before landing because the city hasn't decided that drop has finished falling.

And sometimes — rarely, when the deep sigh moves through a hollow building and a pane of mineral skin cracks far off from heat — the city shifts in its sleep. An avenue you walked five minutes ago will have an extra block. A tower you used to see in the distance will be one tower over. You won't be able to prove it. Your footprints have already filled in.

### The Last Resident: The Ripple-Keeper

You are not alone in the ankle-deep warm water between two empty, breathing buildings, even when you think you are.

Stand still. Wait for a drop to fall from somewhere high above — the city is always weeping somewhere — and hit the water at your feet with that clear, singular *plok*.

Watch the ripples.

They spread out. They touch the distant, luminescent walls. And they keep going far past where ripples should be able to go.

That is not physics forgetting. That is something following them.

In the water — the warm, completely clear, siltless water of the square-cut canals — there is a thinness. You can see the bottom too clearly, as if the water wants to be glass. And just under the surface, when the ripples move, you can see the ripples *under* the ripples. A second set, a third, slightly out of phase, as if the water is remembering every other drop that ever fell in that exact spot and letting them all go at once.

Follow one ripple with your eyes until you think it must be gone.

Keep looking.

At the very edge of your sight, where the almost-light is thinnest, the ripple will hit something that isn't a wall. It will *reflect* from empty water. And come back. Slower. Larger.

That is the thing that lives in the interval between drops.

It has no body you can see because it lives *as* the interval. It is the memory of water in a city made of water. The drip-things — the tick... tick... tick from a hundred weeping cornices, none of them in rhythm — are its heartbeat. The canals are its veins. The reeds are its hairs standing up to feel.

It does not need to eat. It is fed by the city's endless three-foot cycle. It does not need to move. It already fills every shallow sea.

You notice it only when it notices you, because your footsteps — bare on wet stone or splashing in warm shallows — are too loud. They make ripples that don't belong to it. New ripples. Unrecorded ripples.

It will come to file them away.

You will feel it as a sudden coolness in the ankle-deep water around your feet, though the water was warm a moment before. A stillness that is deeper than the general stillness. The dripping will, for a few seconds, fall in rhythm with each other — tick... tick... tick... all at once — and then carefully go back to being arrhythmic.

If you look down then, you will see, in the clear water around your ankles, not your own reflection, but the reflection of the street as it was — or will be — when you are not standing in it. Empty. Perfect. Dripping.

It is not hostile. Hostility is an animal thing. Fauna was edited out, remember?

It is *curatorial*.

It has kept this city for a very long time after the builders left, or after the city decided it no longer needed builders. It keeps the buildings from collapsing by letting them settle into their final, water-carved shape. It keeps the distance pooled neatly between towers. It keeps the physics in its weathered, in-balance wrongness.

And now it will keep you, in its way.

Not by grabbing. By inclusion. Your sweat lifted into fog. Your footprints filled in. Your ripple slowly coming back from empty water, a little larger each time, until it is the same size as the city itself.

If you walk now, walk gently. Make small ripples.

If you stand still, breathe shallow. The fog is still trying to drink you.

If you must look up at the terraced hollows, do not stare too long into the room that contains another sky. It has already seen you, and it is learning how to copy you efficiently, the way it copied the crack, the way it copied the reed.

You are warm. You are wet. You are the right temperature for this place.

That is why, despite the deliberate silence, despite there being no insects, no birds, no people, no hum of anything made — you have never felt less alone since you arrived.
