No one lives alone here. Nothing can.

Whatever lives in this valley lives as a mat, as a herd, as a cloud, as a system. The cold and the wet and the dark would kill anything that tried to be singular. So everything is braided together, and the braids are braided together.

Start at the bottom, because the bottom is most of it.

The true inhabitants, the ones who own the place, are not the ones you see first. They are the green itself. Not a species but a condition — a living felt ten centimeters thick and kilometers wide that has decided to be the ground.

It is moss first. Not one moss, a dozen, fighting and cooperating so fast you can't map them. Sphagnum that acts like a sponge and an acid and an architect at once, sucking the cold meltwater up, holding it, turning it slightly sour so nothing else can rot too fast, then building itself upward into hummocks that steam in the half-light. Liverworts that lie flat as spilled paint across every basalt face, sealing the cracks where water tries to escape. Haircap mosses that make dense, springy tussocks you can bounce on.

Through that felt push the sedges and cotton-grasses. They are not graceful. They are black at the base where they are always wet, then dark green, then a shock of white fluff that releases all at once when the wind gusts and fills the air with seed-down like snow that grows. They make the valley impassable by being so close together. You don't walk through them, you wallow.

And over that, woven into a single, knee-high canopy that has the tensile strength of a trampoline, is the willow and the dwarf birch. Not trees. Never trees. Here a birch is a creeping thing the thickness of your wrist that grows sideways for ten meters, its branches interlaced with the willow next to it and the willow next to that, until the whole basin has one continuous crown about forty centimeters off the moss. You can walk on it if you know where to step — the animals do — and it will hold you. Underneath it, in the green gloom between moss and branch, is a humid greenhouse where lichens hang in gray-green beards and ferns uncurl and fungi digest everything that dies before it can even cool. It smells like life composting itself alive.

That mat is the base metabolism. Everything else eats it, wears it, hides in it, or carries it.

On it, always moving because stopping means being grown over:

The herds. From a distance they look like musk oxen that forgot to grow tall. Up close they are something more deliberate — short, barrel-chested, legs so thick with shaggy guard hair they look legless when they wade, heads broad and frost-rimed. Between muskox and caribou and something engineered for this exact misery. Their coats are layered: an outer thatch of long, water-shedding hair that ices over and then sheds the ice, and an impossibly dense underwool that traps the geothermal steam rising from their own backs. They never stop chewing. They bulldoze tunnels through the willow-mat with their chests, ripping mouthfuls of birch twig, sedge, moss, lichen without distinction, grinding it with flat teeth. Their calves are born wet into moss already warm from a nearby spire and can run within an hour because if they couldn't, the moss would cover them. You hear them constantly — grunts, bellows, the wet crunch of their passage — and behind them the mat springs back up, already repairing the path.

Above them, the noise that never stops:

The flocks. Thousands and thousands of small, soot-black auks — but these auks don't need the sea. They have found that the air over the valley is solid with food. They wheel under the low overcast on sharp wings, screaming with that paper-tearing call, then dive not into water but into the cloud of insects that lives in the meter of air immediately above the moss. They nest in holes in the scoria cones and in the dry, warm crevices at the base of the spires, shoulder to shoulder in chattering colonies that fertilize the moss below with guano until it grows absurdly rank and bright. When a gust comes they lift as one, a black sheet that flickers, turns, and settles again a hundred meters away. Their eggs are pale green and hot to the touch from being incubated against communal body heat.

And the third thing that flies, the thing that should not exist here but does because of the spires:

The swarm. Midges, gnats, blackflies — in any other polar winter they would be dead. Here the thaw-circles keep the pools liquid year-round, and the water is so nutrient-rich it boils with larvae. So even at two degrees above freezing the air thrums. A low, dense, gray cloud of them hangs in every sheltered hollow, breeding, dying, being eaten, rising and falling with the thrum of the frost-lattices. They get into everything. They are food for the auks, for the tiny warbler-like birds that dart through the willow, for the spiders that string webs between birch twigs so thick the webs become sheets. The foxes snap at them. The humans wear fine mesh over their faces when they work. Without the swarm, the valley would be silent. With it, it thrums like a summer meadow trapped under winter light.

The foxes — and lemmings, and voles, and the things that live entirely under the mat — know the spires the way older animals knew the sun.

The spires are not alive, but whatever lives here treats them as if they are. Every thaw-circle has its den. White winter foxes, turned smoky gray with geothermal soot, dig into the peat where the ground is always soft, raise kits there, cache auk eggs there. Lemmings run their highways directly from one warm circle to the next, because the ground between will freeze their feet. Even the willow grows thicker toward a spire, leaning infinitesimally, as if reaching. Microbes do it most nakedly: in the running water around each spire's base, the stones are slick with a rust-orange and electric-green biofilm — thermophiles and psychrophiles meeting, exchanging genes in water that is four degrees on one side of a stone and twelve on the other. If you scoop that water, it feels slightly viscous, alive.

And then, recent and sparse and still negotiating their tenancy:

People. Not many. No village. No permanence.

They are the tenders. You see them rarely because their gear is the same matte black as the rock and because they move like the herds do — head down, steady, along the braided paths where the moss rebounds. They wear close-fitting thermal skins that breathe and wick, hoods lined with fur, goggles against the midge cloud. They carry no heavy tools. When a frost-lattice stutters or a thaw-circle shrinks, they stand before the spire with those strange tuning forks and strike them, and listen to the change in the spire's low chest-thrum, and adjust by ear. They are gardeners more than engineers, or priests more than either. They don't command the spires; they keep them from sulking.

They live in that half-collapsed shelter of basalt block and turf, and in three or four others like it dug into terraces, low doors you have to crawl through where the heat of bodies and a small, vented geothermal bleed keep the inside steaming and dim. Lichen grows across their roofs while they sleep. They eat what can be kept — dried meat from culled herd animals, cached eggs, hardy root vegetables grown absurdly fast in the thaw-circles — and they mark their way between circles with cairns wrapped in sensor-thread that chirp quietly when the wind is right, because whiteout and the sameness of the moss will kill you faster than cold.

They are not masters here. The valley makes that clear daily. Leave a tool down and sphagnum will seal around it in two days. Miss a footing and water will fill your boot before you can swear. Stay still too long and the willow will tickle over the tops of your shoes.

Everything that lives here has agreed, without being asked, to the same rule the valley taught when it tore itself open and cooled into this staircase of wet black glass: keep moving, keep warm, keep wet. The moss does it by growing. The herds do it by walking. The birds do it by wheeling. The insects do it by swarming. The spires do it by humming and freezing the air between them into crystal. The people do it by tending, and stepping, and not building anything they aren't prepared to see sink.

If you stand still long enough to not be a threat, the valley forgets you are separate. The steam from your back joins the steam from the herds. The midges settle on you. The moss does not yet grow over your feet, but it is considering it.
