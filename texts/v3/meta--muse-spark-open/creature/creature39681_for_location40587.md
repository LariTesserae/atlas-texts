What lives here didn't arrive so much as it was allowed to remain.

That's the first thing to understand. Nothing in this valley colonized it quickly. In a basin this young, with edges this sharp, with sky this heavy — life doesn't rush in. It waits to be invited. And this city is an invitation, carefully worded.

#### The People

You have seen them at a distance and thought: people in dark clothes. From closer, you realize the clothes are not quite clothes.

They are tall — not dramatically, but noticeably — long in limb and neck, with a looseness in how they stand, as if their joints have one more degree of freedom than yours. That's the negotiated physics in their bodies. Children born here learn to walk in air that sometimes leans. Their inner ears are not like yours. On an MRI you would see the otoliths, the gravity sensors, grown large and branched like coral, able to tolerate contradiction. A child here can be falling sideways at sixty kilometers an hour along a transit thread and not feel dizzy. An outsider would vomit before the midpoint. They don't. They don't even close their eyes.

Their skin is matte in the same way the towers are matte. Not the same material — they are not machines — but they have borrowed the trick. In the dim, they seem shadowed even when you look directly at them. Most have very little melanin but their skin is dense with chromatophores underneath, a dusk-gray that deepens or pales with humidity. They don't tan. They tune. In the brighter bruise where the sun hides, their shoulders will freckle into a faint, polarized sheen, scattering heat.

Their eyes are the giveaway. Large, with irises that occupy almost the whole visible surface, amber-brown to nearly black, and pupils that stay wide open. You think of nocturnal animals, but they are not nocturnal. There is no nocturnal here. There is only dim. They see brilliantly in this amber-gray. Bring one to full sunlight and it would weep and go functionally blind within minutes. They keep the sky hazed on purpose. Rule four.

They wear loose black. Always loose. The fabric is not woven so much as grown — a second skin of hydrophilic filament that beads with dew by morning. You will see a person standing perfectly still on a high balcony at 5 a.m., arms slightly spread, not meditating, collecting. By 5:30 the shoulders and back will glitter with a hundred milliliters of water that runs in channels down the spine to a soft bladder at the small of the back. They drink from themselves. They water their balcony gardens from themselves. When two meet, they touch shoulders briefly, exchanging water. It is a greeting, an intimacy. To refuse is an insult.

They are sparse, by design. The valley could hold millions and holds maybe forty thousand. That isn't decadence. It's engineering. The towers need wind. The epiphytes need fog. Crowd the basin and the air stops moving and everything dies. So they keep distance as an ethic. A family might occupy an entire inverted delta tower — three hundred meters of vertical skin — and consider it barely enough room. You do not hear neighbors. You are not supposed to.

The children are taught early:

Do not build on the ground. The ground is for the moss.
Do not dam or pipe. Take only what the air gives.
Do not speak loudly in the transit threads. The air is listening for weight.

They are not religious about this, but it's close. The city will correct you if you disobey. A hard footprint on the moss mat will take twenty years to recover. The towers will tighten their pores above a place where someone has spilled chemical water. People learn politeness because the environment has perfect memory.

When they move between towers they do not call it travel. They call it *leaning*. You step to the edge of a platform — no rail, the gardens grow right to the lip — and you let go. There is a moment of vertigo where nothing happens and you think you will fall straight down into the moss, and then gravity catches you like a current and pulls you sideways. You fall horizontally, silently, feet forward or lying on your back, watching the hanging forests blur beneath you. They go with arms at their sides, eyes open. Some read. The light ripples faintly around them like heat above asphalt. No engine. No push. The city is politely telling that corridor of air that *down* is now *that way*, and air, for a few minutes, agrees.

They tend the buildings the way you would tend an old relative who does not need help but appreciates company. Each morning someone walks the skin — actually walks vertically for a time, held by the same negotiated lean — and runs a hand over the matte surface, feeling for dry patches, for pores clogged with pollen from the barrel pitchers. They sing while they do it. Not songs with words. Low humming that vibrates the weave. The tower breathes a little deeper when they do.

They rarely gather in more than threes. When they do, it's at the edge of the Stillness, that half-kilometer column between the four largest towers where rain forgets how to fall. They stand around it without comment, as you noticed, but not out of indifference. Out of respect for an old mistake. The Stillness is where the first field tests overlapped and created a standing well. It should have been decommissioned centuries ago. It wasn't. Now it is useful. They dry clothes in it. They teach physics in it, dropping feathers and watching them rise. Once a year they release paper lanterns into it and watch them never land, hanging golden with the dust until the charge eats them.

In the Stillness at night the captured dust glows. If you stand close, you will see the inhabitants' faces lit amber from below, wide-eyed, quiet. You will think they are praying. They are listening. Because in that well, the air never settles, and so every spore, every distant inhale of a tower two kilometers away, arrives intact. You can hear the valley breathe there if you are patient enough.

#### The City Itself

It is a mistake to call the towers uninhabited between the people. They are inhabited by themselves.

Each one extruded — not assembled — from a seed of programmable basalt-fiber and bio-silicate that was coaxed upward over years. Narrow base, wide crown to catch more fog. Inside: cisterns, dwellings, workshops, but also lungs, capillaries, lymph. That matte skin that heals is not cladding. It is organ. When it rains — those brief, warm, twenty-minute hammerings — the whole flank goes pitted and thirsty, drinking so fast you can hear it hiss. Water vanishes into it without rivulet or drip. In the crown, behind leaves, storage bladders swell. Later, at noon heat, it exhales the faintest cool mist from the leeward side, feeding its own gardens.

The towers talk to each other in gravity and heat, not radio. Their lean-threads are not just transit. They are tendons. When one tower is water-rich and another is in wind-shadow, they will shift a thread a few degrees wider and let moist air slide along it like blood. You can see, from the ground, a vine-heavy cable swaying for no wind. That is not a cable. That is a shared root in the sky.

They have no central intelligence. Or they had one and allowed it to dissolve, which comes to the same thing. If you ask a resident who manages the Stillness they will shrug. "It manages itself. We walk around it." If you ask who decides where to build a new tower they will look confused. "We don't. When we need one, we extrude one."

Need arises seldom.

#### The Other Residents

What you called animals — the glider, the salamander, the manta — are not leftover wildlife that learned to tolerate the city. They were invited too.

The glider: six legs, not for walking but for hanging. A flat triangular body the size of a dinner plate, wings translucent and veined like a leaf, wide as your outstretched arms. It hatches in a barrel pitcher, swims in condensation water for its first month eating the plant's drowned catch, and then climbs out and never lands again. It lives its entire life in the curtain of a single tower's garden — one tower, one glider — gliding from bromeliad to pitcher to vine, sipping nectar with a tongue longer than its body. If its tower dies, it dies. They live twelve, fifteen years. The people name them, but never touch them. To see one is considered a mild blessing, like seeing a tower inhale.

The salamanders: pale as limestone, as long as your forearm, with frilled gills that flutter like fern fronds. They live under the moss mat, in the black-green spring. Most of the year they are still, so still you can step within a centimeter and not know. After rain — that sudden drum that vanishes before puddles — the moss swells and they wake and move, fat and slow, eating the sudden bloom of fungus gnats that also woke. Children crouch for hours to watch them. The children are taught not to pick them up, because the oil of even a careful hand clogs their skin.

And high above, in the Stillness — the filterer. It is not a machine, not an animal, or both. Call it a manta because you have no other word: a vast, slow kite fifteen meters across, black on top and luminous gray below, with no eyes, no mouth you can see. It did not evolve. It was grown, or grown itself from stray bio-weave that learned to ride the well. It spends weeks elsewhere — no one knows where — and then returns to turn a slow, silent circle in the hanging dust, filtering spores, charged motes, stray skin cells, everything the valley is trying to say to itself. What it does with what it catches is unclear. Its underside pulses faintly. Sometimes, after it leaves, the air smells for days of wet stone and ozone, as if freshly made.

There are smaller things: moths that do not fly but sail on electrostatic charge. Vines that tighten before rain an hour before any sensor detects it. Pitcher plants that pop at dusk with a soft *thop* and release a sigh of digested perfume so sweet it makes your teeth ache.

All of it, people and tower and glider and vine, is doing the same work: combing water from air that has no water, making shade from light that is already dim, keeping the wind moving through a basin that wants, geologically, to be a still hot lake. It is not a city that was built to house life. It is a climate that was built to house a city that was built to protect a climate. The people inside understand they are the most optional part.

And yet you will see, if you stand long enough in moss up to your ankles with the towers rising like dark trees above you, their crowns lost in haze, a figure on a high balcony leaning out into nothing and not falling, but falling sideways home. You will see them pause mid-thread, just for a second, to let the filterer's shadow pass beneath them.

They are not loud. They are not many. But they belong here in a way that makes the basalt walls seem new and intrusive by comparison. The fault tore this valley open a few million years ago and has not yet learned to erode. The city, in its quiet, humid, half-gravity way, is teaching it how.
