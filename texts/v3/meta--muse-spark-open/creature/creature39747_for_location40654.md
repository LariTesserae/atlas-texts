Nothing here lives *on* the High Weir. Everything lives *in* it, or *as* it.

That is the first thing you have to understand if you want to meet the inhabitants. There is no ground and then life on top of it. On the plateau, the ground *is* life. A meter deep, three meters in the hollows, a soaked, breathing mat made half of living tissue and half of slow death.

So you have to start there, with the builders.

### The Holders

Sphagnum is the sovereign here. Not one species, but a loose republic of them - red sphagnum and green sphagnum, blunt-leaved and pointed, starry sphagnum that looks dry from above but wrings out like a sponge when you step. They are why 2.4 is not a statistic but a politics: each tiny leaf has a dead hyaline cell next to its living cell, a microscopic water tower. They drink the horizontal rain, they drink the weeping of the basalt, they drink the condensation that forms at 3pm without a cloud, and they refuse to let it go. They hold three, four, five times their weight and in doing so they acidify everything. They make the world cold, anoxic, sour. Nothing rots properly in sphagnum. It falls in and is preserved.

Underneath them, the dead do not disappear. They become peat. A millimeter a year, blackening, compressing, still faintly recognizable as leaf and stem for centuries down. The peat is the plateau's memory. Cut it open - as the old rail berms do - and you can see the stratigraphy of wet years and dry years, of ash from a turbine fire a generation ago. It smells sweet and iron.

Woven through that, like rebar, are the others that can tolerate the terms Sphagnum sets.

Liverworts sheet across the pools in dark liver-red films, so flat they look like oil. Clubmosses bristle like bottle-brushes, ancient lineage, older than flowers, because only something this old remembers how to live without soil. Cotton-grass lifts its white flags, and the only softness here belongs to it - each tussock trembles independently in the wind that never stops. Dwarf willow does not stand up. It decides to be ground. Its branches creep flat, wire-thin, rooting as they go, its leaves no bigger than a fingernail polished to resist UV. You could walk over a forty-year-old willow and think it was moss.

And the cushions. From a distance you take them for boulders left by the uplift. Closer, you see they *are* boulders, made of plant. Azorella principles, Bolax principles - tight, dome-hard masses a meter across and a half-meter tall, composed of thousands of tiny rosettes locked together. Their surface is so dense water beads and runs off like wax. Touch one and it is warm inside, a few degrees warmer than the air, because it has made its own microclimate. They live hundreds of years. They grow a centimeter a year if they are ambitious. The hares use them as windbreaks. The midges use them as continents.

On all of it, threading everything, is fungus. White fan-mycelium lacing the peat, mycorrhizae sheathing the roots of every willow and cushion, breaking down what even the acid cannot keep. If you smell earth here, you are smelling fungus breathing.

None of this forms a thicket. That's the key to the social life of this place. In a lowland swamp, plants crowd and climb and choke. Here, at this light and this cold, with nutrients locked in peat and water too pure and too acidic, life has learned to be sparse in order to endure. Each cushion keeps its distance. Each willow mat does not touch the next. There are acres of pure sphagnum where nothing interrupts except more sphagnum. It is fecund and empty at once, like a city where everyone has agreed to live alone.

### The Small, Patient Animals

Life gets rarer as it gets larger. That's not accident, it's energetics. There's almost nothing to eat that isn't soaking wet, acid, and poor in phosphorus.

So the real fauna is nearly invisible, and it lives *inside* the Holders.

In a single handful of sphagnum water - scoop it and let it drain brown through your fingers - there are thousands. Testate amoebae making perfect, vase-like shells of silica. Rotifers wheeling. Water bears - tardigrades - clinging to moss leaves, waiting out drought that never quite comes. Chironomid larvae, red with hemoglobin because the pools have so little oxygen, wriggling in the peat. Copepods like fleas of the water.

Above that scale, but not much: the pools themselves. Where the moss gives way to open water, glassy and iron-stained, they are not empty. They are nurseries. Alpine frogs - small, dark, slow, with legs too short for jumping - lay eggs not in strings but in clumps anchored to sphagnum stems. The tadpoles take a year to become frogs, sometimes two, because cold slows everything. On warm evenings, if you kneel and put your ear down below the roar of the Weir, you can hear the males call - not a chorus, a few isolated, patient *plips*, one answering another half a pool away. There are diving beetles, black and glossy, that live their whole lives in a pool the size of a bathtub, never knowing there is a quarter-mile waterfall half a mile east.

Then the air, in the lee of things.

Midges and dance flies and stoneflies exist here in such numbers they seem like weather. They cannot fly in the plateau wind - nothing that weighs less than a gram can - so they have learned the map of shelter. Every rusted pipe, every rail berm, every knee-high krummholz pine twisted into a bonsai by wind creates a cone of still air behind it, and in that cone the insects hang in a desperate, humming cloud. They live for days, mate, die. They feed nothing large except the moss, when they fall into it. And they feed the one or two creatures that have learned to hunt shelter itself.

The dun alpine hare - you will see its droppings, round and fibrous with willow, long before you see it. It is not white in winter, because there is no real winter here, just eternal cool November. It is the color of peat and rock, ears tipped black. It does not burrow; there is nowhere dry to burrow. It forms a shallow scrape in the lee of a cushion plant and waits. It moves mostly at dusk, when the wind drops a fraction, clipping cotton-grass and willow buds with incisors that must work harder than any lowland rabbit's because the food has so little nutrition. It bolts without sound on wide, splayed feet that spread on peat like snowshoes. There may be one hare per twenty acres. You are not supposed to see it. When you do, it looks at you as if you have made a category error.

Above it all, far too high to be part of this, is the lammergeier. Sometimes two. It does not live here. It lives in the air that belongs to this height. It turns on the thermals that rip off the cliff face where the Weir spills, its ten-foot shadow crossing the whole plateau in a second - over sphagnum, over shed, over you - without pausing. Occasionally it drops down to pick a dead hare or a stillborn lamb that wandered up from below centuries ago and was preserved in peat. Mostly it just watches. It is the only thing that can see the plateau whole, from ridge to drop, as a single green table. Its presence makes the plateau feel watched, and therefore inhabited.

No herds. No flocks. A pipit now and then, buff-colored, flicking between tussocks. Maybe a snipe, invisible until it erupts at your feet. The industrial drone has thinned what birds there ever were. The place is too loud for much birdsong to carry.

### The New Inhabitants

And then there are the caretakers, and the machines that outnumber them.

There were once perhaps thirty or forty people here year-round: pipe-fitters, turbine men, a sawyer and his family, line-men who walked the narrow-gauge in wind so hard they tied themselves to the rails. Now there are one or two. You will not find them unless they want you to. They live in the insulated huts that still hum - double-walled, small windows amber with light, smoke from a stack that is instantly torn horizontal. They have learned to be like the cushion plants: low, compact, heat-keeping. They scrape moss off intake screens with a tool like a garden hoe. They polish a turbine bearing. They wave rarely not from unfriendliness but from economy. Sound does not carry well under the water-roar except as vibration, so you communicate by gesture.

They are not separate from the fauna. They have the same sallow skin from lack of sheltered sun, the same patience. They drink the same water, filtered by sphagnum. They will stay until the peat takes the berms entirely.

But the more permanent residents are the Works themselves, and this is not metaphor.

Each turbine house, each headframe, each length of black pipe on stilts has become a habitat. The pipes run warm where they carry water under pressure, and on their undersides, in the permanent spray near the lip, a different moss grows - lurid, almost tropical green, fed by mineral mist. The corrugated sheds are streaked with living rust: bacteria that eat iron, orange and ochre, painting the metal. Inside, where it is dark and vibrating, swallows - or rather, the high swift that replaces swallows here - have cemented nests to the rafters because it is the only place out of the wind where the air stays warm through the night.

The power lines buzz - 60 cycles, a sound almost exactly the same pitch as a bumblebee queen - and lichens crust their creosote posts, measuring decades by millimeters.

The machines turn, turn, turn without purpose now. The sawmill's stack leans, its tarp flapping like a wounded bird, but the shaft still turns. The water that was going to fall anyway is made to fall through a copper coil first. The stations communicate by the thump in the peat. You feel them through your boots before you hear them. In a place where almost nothing moves fast, that thump is a kind of heart.

So that is who lives here:

A republic of mosses that made its own ground and its own sour ocean;

A hidden plankton of bears and amoebae in every puddle;

Frogs that take a year to be born, hares that live alone behind plants as old as they are, insects that live only in the shadow of a pipe;

A bird that does not live here at all but cannot look away;

One or two humans becoming lichen;

And a scattering of simple, stubborn engines that have learned, like everything else on the Weir, to drink endlessly and never quite drain.
