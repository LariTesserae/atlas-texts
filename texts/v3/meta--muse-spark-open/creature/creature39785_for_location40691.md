They don't live *on* the Table. They live *in* the deal the Table made.

The deal was this: no rivers, no soil, no shade - but water on schedule, and light without end. And so everything that lives here has learned to live on timing.

### The Residents Proper

There are no permanent residents in the human sense. No villages, no herds, no hives you can point to and count. What there is instead is a very thin, very complete ecology spread so evenly across so much empty stone that it almost looks like aridity until you stop moving.

Stand still for three minutes. The Table will populate itself for you.

#### 1. The Crust - which is not what it seems to be

Start at the bottom, because everything else eats down to this.

The black-green velvet that coats every crack where a lattice throws noon shade is not lichen. It's a mat, and it's awake.

In full sun it is flat, matte, almost black, drinking light so efficiently it looks like a hole cut in the rock. If you kneel - and the thin air will make you breathe a little too loud while you do - you'll see it has direction. A nap, like short fur, all combed one way, toward the west, into the wind.

It does photosynthesize. But that is its day job. At night it phosphoresces faintly, not to be pretty, but to keep working - re-radiating a little of the brutal noon it stored, running low, slow chemistry while the stone is still warm. And at dawn, for those twenty glittering minutes when the whole plateau sweats, it *moves*.

Not quickly. If you stare directly at it you will miss it. Look at its edge against bare stone and look away, then look back. It will have advanced a millimeter. It is crawling toward condensation. And if your shadow falls across it, the nap will slowly, irritably, reorient away from the cold you made, grain by grain.

The skimmers graze it. The gliders seed it. The cistern pools are inoculated with it. It's the green that pretends not to be green, and without it the Table is just polished rock.

Around the lips of the cisterns it gives up and lets real plants try. The wire-grass that looks dead until you touch it and find it's cool inside. The rosette funnels the size of your hand that are all leaf and all throat, built to take a single splash and send it to a taproot that has found a crack to the bell below. The white-dusted spiral succulents that grow so slowly you can see last year's dust still caught in their seams.

They're not abundant. They're tolerated. Islands around wells in a desert made of stone.

#### 2. The Skimmers - the ones who tack

You will hear them before you see them, if you hear them at all: a faint *scritch* on slickrock, like a dry leaf being pulled.

They are rabbit-sized and exactly the wrong number of legs. Six, set low and splayed, each foot a broad, fringed pad that grips polished sandstone and basalt as if it were damp. They don't run *on* the wind. They run *with* it.

Each skimmer carries its own sail - a dorsal membrane stretched between elongated spines, veined like a dragonfly wing, pale amber and ocellated with false eyespots that have nothing to do with camouflage and everything to do with startling the things above them. When they want to move they lift their bodies a centimeter off the rock, spread that sail, and let the thin, fast, constant westerly push them. They tack. You can watch one do it: angling its leading legs to carve, tilting its sail to catch and spill air, kiting across a whaleback ridge in long, silent, eerie arcs that make no hoofbeats.

They are grazers, after a fashion. Their mouth is ventral, radial, hidden until they stop over a patch of crust and unfold it - a soft, lamprey-like disc that secretes a little acid and squeegees the mat clean, leaving a pale, licked track that will darken again in a day. They freeze when watched, sail folded tight as a closed book, becoming exactly a wind-smoothed stone. Their eyes are not eyes but heat-pits, ringed around the mouth. They watch you through your warmth.

They live alone, but never far apart. One per cistern, sometimes two if the pool is large. Territories defined by the distance a sail can carry you before you need to drink. At noon they wedge themselves into tafoni hollows and tuck their sails over themselves like blankets, breathing through spiracles that whistle just below ultrasound. That is when the gliders hunt them.

#### 3. The Gliders - the ones who never flap

If the skimmers belong to the stone, the gliders belong to the air directly above the stone - the first ten meters where heat shimmers.

From a distance they look like trash. Like someone left a translucent grocery bag to blow across the plateau. Then the bag changes direction against the wind and you recalibrate.

Two feet across on average, some as small as your hand, some as wide as your shoulders, they are mantas made of living paper. No body to speak of - a flattened central pod the size of a fist, four ribbon tails trailing for balance, and two vast, veined wings that are not feathered or membraned so much as *woven*, a lattice of chitin-fine fibers that light passes through. They do not flap. They have no muscles for it. They ride.

The Table makes its own thermals. Stone exhales heat all day; cool air pours off the lattices; every cistern is a cold spot. The gliders know this map the way you know stairs in your house. They circle a basin for hours without a single wingbeat, gaining height on invisible columns, losing it in long glides to the next.

They are not blind, but their eyes are not where you look. The entire upper surface of the wing is photosensitive - a compound eye spread thin - so that the glider *is* an eye, looking always down. Its shadow is its tool. It will pass over a skimmer and tilt, just enough to flick a sharp-edged shadow across the rock. The skimmer, hardwired to freeze, freezes. The glider folds - and this is startling the first time you see it - into a tight, falling dart and drops.

It does not land. It kisses. The ventral pod extends a needle proboscis and taps the skimmer's sail joint where the membrane is thinnest, takes a mouthful of hemolymph rich with digested crust, and releases. The skimmer shudders and kites away, a little lighter. Most survive. The glider, weighted by a milligram more, catches the next updraft before it ever quite touches stone.

You can find their drinking places by the perfect rings. A glider will fold and fall to a cistern, touch the surface tension with its tails for exactly a second - *tick* - and lift again on the rebound, taking water without ever wetting its wings. Ripples expand in that still, glassy bell. The tussocks around the lip shiver.

At dusk they rise. All of them, at once, in spirals, as if called. To meet the shepherds.

#### 4. The Shepherds - the ones who never land

You have probably already seen one and thought it was a cloud. Or a contrail. Or your own floater, from looking at so much sun.

They are very high. Stratosphere-high, where the air that rakes the plateau has thinned to almost nothing and the sun is unfiltered and vicious. Up there, pale against pale blue, something very long and very slow undulates west to east, against the prevailing wind, without hurrying.

From the ground they are specks, white ribbons. If you had binoculars you would see they are not ribbons at all but segmented, kite-like bodies a hundred meters long, maybe longer - translucent, gas-filled, trailing filamentous tendrils kilometers behind them like jellyfish in air. They pulse. Not with wings. With light. A slow peristalsis of faint violet along their length, the same violet you see at the edge of your double shadow under the lattices.

They never land because they can't. They are lighter than the thin air and they were built - or grew - to stay up there. They feed on what no one else can reach: the aerial plankton itself, the vast, invisible bloom of bacteria and spores and desiccated algae that breeds in the cold throats of the cistern lattices every night and is flung skyward every dawn when the sun boils the dew. The Table farms the sky, and the shepherds are its cattle and its farmers both.

You can watch the exchange: at sunset, gliders rise in a vortex, dozens, hundreds from across a valley-cell, spiraling up to vanish into the wake of a passing shepherd. They are not being eaten. They are being cleaned, or tended, or read. They come back down darker, dusted with spores, carrying in their gut-folds the cultures that will seed tomorrow's dew. Sometimes a glider does not come back down. That, too, is part of the deal.

The shepherds communicate, if that's the word, by shadow. When one tips, very slightly, the light beneath it sorts - just as it does under the lattice - and for a second the stone below is striped with faint, moving color. Every glider in a kilometer adjusts. Every skimmer freezes. You will feel the hairs on your arms lift, the same feeling you get near an anchor, and you will look up and see nothing at all.

At night they are invisible except when they occult the stars - a long, slow dimming moving against the wind, blotting and revealing constellations that shouldn't be quite where they are.

#### 5. The Night Choir and the Things Between

When the sun is gone and the stone gives back its heat, the Table changes tenants.

The cisterns sing. Literally. As they cool, the air in the bells contracts and draws down through the throats, and the water, so still all day, shivers. From inside each one comes a chorus of clicks and chirps - not frogs, not insects, but something with a shell like a shrimp and legs like a spider that lives its whole life clinging to the submerged walls of the bell, filtering the dark. They are called, by the few researchers who stay long enough to name things, bell-crickets. They are blind and white and they navigate by the echo of their own song off the still surface. Each cistern has its own dialect, its own chord. Walk between two at midnight and you walk through a quiet duet.

Between cisterns, in the dry bone-warm dark, the hunters go out on foot. Low, many-legged, matte-black things no bigger than your hand that run on tiptoe over dust so lightly they leave no tracks. They hunt bell-crickets that stray, and each other, and leave behind tiny, cool traceries of bioluminescence where their feet touched - fading footprints of light that the crust slowly eats by morning.

And everywhere, always, the dust itself is alive. The lattices hum in a cold you feel in your molars because they are sorting - water from air, heavy isotopes from light, and, as a byproduct, charge. The air beneath a lattice is faintly ionized. In that air, bacteria you cannot see ride and divide, making a living out of static and dew. They are the true abundance. The reason a place with fifteen percent plant cover can feed gliders and skimmers and shepherds is because the base of the food chain is not plant at all. It's physics, made edible.

### And the Ones Who Made It

They are not here. Or, they are here the way a tuning fork is still there after you've struck it.

The anchors - those warm-cool posts of no-material that the lichen will not grow on, that the skimmers avoid by exactly one meter - are not ruins. They do not weather. They have no doors because they are not buildings. They are instruments that never stopped playing. If you put your hand very close, you can feel them decide not to notice you.

No skeletons. No roads. No writing. Whoever or whatever wanted a high, bright desert to be generous left the tool to do it, tuned it to wind and sun, stocked it with things that could live inside music, and left. Or became the music. The shepherds have that same violet edge to their light. The floating pebbles in the bowls hover a finger's width up for no reason anyone can find except that the field that holds the lattice taut is a little leaky there, and gravity is a suggestion.

You get the sense, sleeping near a cistern with your back to warm stone, that the Table is not abandoned. It is *tended* - just not by hands. By a system so quiet and so complete that animals evolved to be its maintenance crew without ever knowing it.

In the morning, before the bright hits hard, stand by any lattice and watch. Condensation will bead on every filament, on every hair on your arm, on the sails of skimmers still tucked in their hollows. For twenty minutes the world will glitter as if it were fresh-made.

Then the sun will crest the scarp, the ticking will start, the gliders will lift, a shepherd far above will tip a degree to catch the bloom, and the whole high, warm, impossible farm will go back to work without a sound.
