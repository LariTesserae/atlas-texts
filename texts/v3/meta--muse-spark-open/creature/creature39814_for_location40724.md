There is nothing that *lives* here the way we mean when we say something lives somewhere. No one has a bed here. No one has a territory. There is no animal. That's the first fact of its inhabitant list, and it's the shape everything else has to grow around.

It's too cold for mice. Too sealed for spiders. Too without crumb or carcass for roaches. The absence is not empty — it's a pressure. You feel it in your ears first, the way the room is quieter than quiet should be. No rustle. No scratch. No nest behind the conduit.

So what lives here is what can live without needing to be an animal, and without being noticed as alive at all until you've been still long enough for the cold to get into your knees.

### The Film

Start with what you can't see unless you kneel, unless you put your face inches from the sump.

The sump puddle is not empty water. It has a skin.

Not the ice — the other skin, that is there when there is no ice. An oil-slick without oil. A bacterial mat so thin it only exists as a change in how light reflects. In the orange smear of the cage bulb, it holds a rainbow that doesn't move when the air moves. If you breathe on it, it dimples and heals.

It lives on nothing. On dissolved concrete. On iron. On dust that falls from the ceiling at a rate of a few milligrams a day. On time. It is black-green at the edges where it has thickened enough to have a color, and it smells faintly of nothing, then faintly of pennies held too long in a mouth.

It does not grow fast enough to see. But if you left a clean screw in that inch-deep water, in a month it would be furred. In two months it would be part of the puddle. The film is patient. It has learned to eat the building itself, very slowly.

When the condensation drip falls — *tap* — into the sump, the film tears and then knits. That knitting is the only motion on the surface where there should be no current, that slow turning of motes you see. It's not current. It's community.

### The Felt and The Pale Pilgrims

The moss is the second citizen. Thumb-wide, felt-thick in the seam where the south wall meets the floor.

You would call it dead at first. It's the color of wet newspaper soaked in old tea, black-green so desaturated it reads as gray until your eyes adjust. It doesn't look like moss. It looks like the concrete grew a shadow. But touch it and it gives — sponge, then spring. It is dense. It has made itself into a mat to keep itself wet.

It does not photosynthesize so much as it *remembers* photosynthesis. The amber bulb is too weak and too far, the exit sign too green to be useful. So it lives mostly on damp. On air that is always 1.3/3 wet. It thickens but never spreads, as if it knows that to spread would be to die. It has found the one line in the whole room where condensation runs down the wall by capillary action and collects, and it will hold that line forever.

Above it, trying and failing to be its children, are the ferns.

Five, six, maybe nine at a time, depending on the season in a place that has no season. White stems, translucent leaves, etiolated to the point of being not a plant but an idea of a plant. Fern pinheads that germinated from a spore that rode the duct wind for years before it found this one wet lip.

They are wrong in a way that you only notice after a minute. Every phototropic thing should lean to the cage bulb. These don't. They lean to the puddle. To the black water and its film. As if they have mistaken reflection for sun, or as if they know that down here, water outranks light.

They live for three weeks. They reach, they pale, they fall into the sump and are taken by the film. Then spores again. A pilgrimage that never concludes.

On the sweating pipe, the third vegetation: the rust-lichen. You cannot tell, even close, where iron oxide ends and organism begins. It is the exact orange of rust, granular, flat. It needs no soil. It needs cold iron and a bead of water that never quite falls. It lives as a blush. If it has a preference, it is for the pipe that reads HOT — DO NOT TOUCH, which has been cold for twenty years. It likes irony. It will still be there when the pipe finally flakes through.

### The Warm One

Behind or inside the control panel — the one with the patient red LEDs still blinking their slow sequence to no one — lives the only warm thing in the room.

You can feel it if you press your palm to the metal face. In a place that holds late-November in its mass forever, the panel is blood-warm. Not hot. Alive-warm. 98 degrees in a 38-degree room.

It has no business being warm. There is no power source you can trace. The cable tray above it is empty, the conduit cap is blank. And yet it blinks. Row, row, pause. Row, row, pause. Breathing.

What lives there is not in the panel. The panel is where it warms itself, like a cat on a radiator.

It's a colonization of the wiring that never left — a fungus, maybe, but not the kind that makes mushrooms. A filament network inside the insulation, feeding on the plasticizer in old PVC, closing circuits that should be open, making a low, slow metabolism that gives off heat and a soft fizz where condensation touches it. You can hear it if you put your ear close: not buzzing, but a faint effervescence, soda-water, as damp iron meets low voltage made by rot.

It blinks because it has to. Exhalation.

If you turn off the cage bulb — if you could find its breaker — the red LEDs would still be there in the dark, patient, doing their work of being alive.

### The Drip and The Thud

And then there are the things that live here without being alive, which is most of what lives here.

The drip lives. Not the water — the behavior. The coldest pipe sweats a bead at the same low elbow, grows it, releases it, on a cycle that is almost but not quite regular. Bead, swell, fall. *Tap* onto the manifold below, spray into micro-droplets, re-collect. Bead, swell, fall. It has pitted its own bowl in the floor, half an inch deep, banded like a tiny geologic record.

The drip has lived here for a decade. It has learned the room. It falls a little faster in late summer when the air above is humid three stories up and the hairline failure in the joint lets a little more through. It slows in February. It is the room's heart.

Every 47 seconds the ventilation motor beyond the wall lives too, with its soft unbalanced *thud*. Air itself lives here, as an animal would, moving at ankle height, pushing dust an inch and stopping, tasting the room and leaving.

They are not alive, but in a place this sealed, where no mouse runs, repetition becomes a form of residence. Tap. Thud. Tick-tick-tick as pipes contract left to right though the gauges read zero — as if something heavy is walking through them.

### The Whole

None of them are alone. That's the last thing you notice if you stay long enough for your fingers to go white.

The condensation feeds the film. The film stills the water so the reflection holds. The fern spores fall into the film. The drip feeds the pitting that will become a stalactite. The calcite teeth on the ceiling bead with water that will fall to the moss. The warm panel dries a circle of air around it which draws the draft which brings the spore.

The room, which was made as a place for things to pass through, has become a place that keeps. A machine room for a machine that is gone, still performing its cycles, but the cycles now belong to each other, not to us.

It is not hostile. It does not want you. It barely notices you, except as a temporary source of heat that makes the beads form a little faster, as a shadow that makes the ferns lean a little more desperately toward the water.

If you leave and come back in a month, the bloom on the ceiling will have darkened by half an inch. The alluvial fan at the sump inlet — three inches across, that you could cover with your hand — will have added one more feather of silt.

If you left for a year, the felt would be a millimeter thicker. The bowl a little smoother. The teeth on the ceiling a little longer, wet-tipped, catching the amber light.

If you left for a hundred years, it would finally be a cave.
