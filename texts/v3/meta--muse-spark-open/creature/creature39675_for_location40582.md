What lives on the High Peneplain lives by permission of the stone.

Not on it. *In spite* of it. This is not a habitat. It is a finished place that life has not been invited back to, and so everything that persists here has learned the same lesson: be small, be far apart, be vertical.

### The Seam-Keepers

The truest residents are not visible until you kneel.

**The Stain.** For kilometers you think the salt seams are empty. Then your eye adjusts and you see the black. A crust of lichenform, not green, not grey, but matte carbon-black, growing *into* the salt rather than on it. It is barely a millimeter thick - a two-dimensional organism, a photosynthetic stain. It has no leaves, no body, no height to catch wind. It grows molecule by molecule, precipitating its acids into the lithium-white salt, harvesting bound water that hasn't been liquid for a million years and the hard 2.0/3 daylight that would scorch anything softer.

It doesn't grow outward. It grows *denser*. A patch the size of your palm may be two hundred years old. Touch it and it feels like cold soot fused to rock. Scrape it and underneath the stone is pitted, etched with a faint filigree - its lungs. It is the foundation of everything, and it asks for nothing. The plain would not notice if it died. But without it, nothing else could exist.

**The Needles.** Every two or three hundred meters, a seam makes a decision.

Out of the black stain, a wire-brush erupts - knee-high, bristling, wrong. From a distance it looks dead, like a clump of rusted wire left behind. Up close, it is terribly alive.

Each plant is solitary by law. You will never find two in the same seam-segment. Each is a perfect hemisphere of silica-coated needles, each needle a hollow glass filament the thickness of a pine needle, tipped with a black photosynthetic bead. The needles do not want light; they tolerate it. Their silica sheath reflects most of the sun-white daylight, rattling faintly when the laminar wind finds them - *click-click-click* - the only thing on the ground that makes sound voluntarily.

What you see is a breathing tip. The real plant is below you: a chemical root, a tap-root drill ten, fifteen meters straight down into the shield-stone, dissolving gneiss grain by grain to find hydroxyls trapped in crystal since the planet cooled. It drinks rock. It takes a decade to grow a centimeter. It reproduces maybe once a century, not with seeds - nothing could carry a seed here - but by sending a filament of itself laterally along a seam, under the salt, to erupt kilometers away as an apparent stranger. The spacing is not random. It is territorial. They are widely, precisely spaced because their roots are already holding hands under your feet.

Kneel by one and you smell nothing. No resin, no sap. Just hot silica and faint ozone. It will not move for you. It was here before your grandparents' grandparents were imagined, and will be here after your suit fails, rattling occasionally to no one.

### The Intermittents

Because fauna is 0.9/3 - rare enough to feel like a violation when it appears - animals here do not live *on* the plain. They punctuate it.

**Salt-Mites.** You think you are alone until you shift your boot. Then the ground scatters.

They are the size of quartz grains, translucent white with a pinkish gut visible inside, each leg like a hair of spun glass. Hundreds of them pack into a single salt seam, utterly still for hours - so still you mistake them for salt - conserving water that is already at 0.5/3. A vibration - your footfall, a strider's step - and they burst, a splash of living sand that runs a few centimeters and freezes again. They graze the black lichenform, lapping at it with mouthparts too small to see. They contain more salt than water. If you picked one up, it would desiccate and die in your palm before you could focus on it. Their whole life is a slow migration along a single plate-edge, a kingdom the size of a table.

**The Gliders.** Look up. Wait a long time.

High, impossibly high, where the stable air at ankle-height gives way to the 3.0/3 dynamic chaos, there are mantas. Paper-thin, translucent, the size of your outstretched hand, with a body like smoked glass and two vast, veined wings that never flap. They ride. Only ride.

They never land. They *can't* land. Their bodies are too delicate to touch polished stone, their mouths are ventral filters, their legs are vestigial threads. They were born in the air - hatched from spores released by their elders directly into a thermal - and they will die in the air, desiccated, falling finally as a flake of chitin onto a plate where mites will find them in an hour and erase them in a day.

Watch one through binoculars and you see it flex, banking on invisible air vortices thrown off by the Structures below. It is filtering - not flies, not dust, but the almost-nothing: airborne lichen spores, wind-lofted salt bacteria, the faint organic haze that the Peneplain exhales once a decade. The sky is furiously active above them - violet anvil clouds boiling into existence in minutes, aurorae snapping in daylight - and they ignore it completely, surfing the edge of a glass cyclone that could shred a helicopter. They are silent, as everything here is silent at 0.1/3.

You can stand for an hour and see one. Or none.

**The Striders.** If you are lucky, or unlucky, you will meet the only thing on the plain that looks at you back.

Once a day, maybe. A flicker at the edge of a plate, a geometry error. A strider.

Three legs - not jointed like an insect, but stilted like a crane, each leg two meters long, made of matte grey chitin and something like lacquered carbon. They move with obscene slowness and precision: one leg lifts, hovers, places itself on the next plate across a salt seam, *click*. Then the next. They do not hurry because hurry costs water. They step from plate to plate for kilometers, a journey that takes days, pausing every ten steps to lower a sensor head - a featureless pearl the size of a fist, ringed with black pits - to taste the lichen.

They are not afraid of you. Fear implies predators, and there are none. There is nowhere to flee to. A strider will approach within ten meters, consider you with its pit-eyes, step *around* you in the same wide arc it gives the Structures - as if you are a slightly warmer piece of architecture - and continue.

Are they animals? They eat, they excrete tiny dry pellets of concentrated salt, they lay a single amber egg in a deep seam once in their long lives. But open one - and no one has, really - and you would find too much symmetry, too much silica in its blood. Fauna here blurs. At 2.8/3 tech saturation, maybe the striders were fauna once and were edited. Maybe they are the shepherds the Structures kept when everything else left. They live twenty, thirty years, walking the same circuit between needle-plants and lichenfields, and when they die they simply lock their legs and become a tripod monument until wind-polish takes them.

They are the only thing that seems to *inhabit* rather than *endure*.

### The Structures

And then there are the residents that do not breathe.

One per horizon. Low, dark, angular, half-sunken into the shield-stone as if the rock flowed around them and decided to keep them. No doors, no windows. Surfaces that absorb light without being black, that shed dust and forbid lichen within a meter. At night they cool to a dull, arterial red.

They are not alive. But they *live* here in the way a lighthouse lives on a coast.

Every few minutes one shivers without moving, and the air above it refracts, and a ten-kilometer storm is born or murdered in silence. They are 2.8/3 tech in a 1.1/3 built world - which means they are not buildings. They are regulators, anchors, gardeners of vacuum. The ground below - 3.0/3 eroded, patient, finished - is the anvil; they are the hammers; the sky is the spark. They have done this for geological ages without ever touching the ground they sit on. No rain falls from their clouds. No lightning strikes their backs. The striders know to walk around them. The needle-plants will not root near them.

Do they notice the mites? Do they know the gliders surf their exhaust? Probably not. Probably no more than you notice the bacteria on your boot sole. But without them, the Peneplain would be merely dead. With them, it is *tended* - a desert kept as a desert, a storm kept as a storm, held apart by a vertical law no human wrote.

They are the reason the plain is empty. They like it that way.

### You

Finally, there is the rarest fauna of all: you.

At 0.3/3 density, you are an error. Too loud - your blood in your ears, your joints clicking like stones in the 0.1/3 silence. Too warm - venting heat into air that is permanently 1.4/3 late autumn. Too wet - exhaling grams of water the plain has not seen in eons with every breath, which vanishes instantly without ever fogging.

You walk for hours and the plates pass under you, house-sized, city-block-sized, banded charcoal and bone and rust, and nothing changes. Then a needle-plant rattles without wind, and you spin around because a sound has spoken to you after an hour of nothing. A strider pauses to taste your shadow. High above, a cloud-wall collapses silently into nothing because a Structure two horizons away decided it.

You do not live here. No human does, not really. You *visit* to service a sensor, to make a pilgrimage, to prove you can be alone on a scale that makes alone feel like a physical substance. You stay a night and watch the starfield - brutal, un-twinkling in the thin air - and the red outlines of the Structures heat-dumping, and the slow magnetic fireworks above, and you understand:

Nothing here needs you. The stain will etch, the needles will rattle, the mites will freeze, the gliders will ride, the striders will step, plate to plate to plate, for another billion years, whether you are there to hear your own breathing or not.

That is what lives on the High Peneplain: things that have mastered the art of being almost not there.
