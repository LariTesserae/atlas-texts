What lives here has stopped trying to be terrestrial or aquatic and has instead become *bowl-living*.

Not merfolk. Not mutants. People. Unmistakably people — though if you pulled one out and dried them on a hillside they would look wrong, unfinished, like something taken out of its jar too early.

### The People Who Stayed Put

They are all still human, and they all breathe water the way you sleep with a machine on.

The oldest ones remember being told the water was coming. The youngest ones have never taken a full, dry lungful of air in their lives. None of them have left. In a place that is 2.8/3 packed, leaving would mean making a hole, and holes are not tolerated.

Physically, they are hamlet-people made for warm broth. Pale in a way that isn't pallor, but underwater pallor - translucent at the temples, blue-veined, faintly freckled with iron. Their hair never dries and so it never quite behaves like hair; it hangs in slow, dark ribbons, heavily seeded with the same beard-algae that grows on the catwalks. Most of them stopped fighting it. You will see a mother whose braid is half her own hair and half living green curtain, tended, combed, even beaded with salvaged glass. An old man whose eyebrows have become small mossy ledges where tiny snails graze.

They wear weight as clothing. Waxed canvas smocks gone supple as skin from decades of saturation, pocketed and patched and stitched with copper thread that greens but never rots. Lead shot sewn into hems. Ankle rings of rebar. Copper nose-clips pinching the nostrils shut - not gas masks, not rebreathers, just enough to remind the body not to try to breathe the wrong way. Each person trails a hose.

The hose is the leash and the umbilical cord. Thin, algae-filmed rubber that goes up or sideways or sometimes down to one of the slow condensers - the converted drum-boilers ticking at the bottom, the wheezing electrolyzers on the balconies. From it they sip air. Not constantly. They have learned to sip like you sip tea. A long, slow pull every two or three minutes, just enough to keep the blood pink. Between sips they are empty-lunged and still, and that stillness is cultural. To thrash or pant is to be a tourist. To hover without moving, one hand lightly hooked on a vine-rope or a pipe-run to keep from drifting in the thermal turnover, is to be local.

Some have gone further. The living cuffs and collars - they let the black-green engine-block moss colonize their wrists, their throats, their shins where the hose chafes. At first it was accident, now it is intentional. The moss keeps the skin from macerating, seals out the iron snow, makes a gasket of felt. Underneath, the skin goes white and smooth and a little photosynthetic in the dim amber light. You can see the border where person ends and garden begins blur. Children are fitted for their first collar around age seven, in a small, quiet ceremony held in one of the big trapped air pockets near the surface-film, where everyone can speak out loud without bubbles.

They speak rarely in open water. Sound is 0.5/3 here and they respect that. When they need to talk at distance they knock. Two taps on pipe means *coming through*. Three sharp pings on a boiler hull means *child loose, watch your wake*. Up close they talk in a kind of exhaled hum, words pushed out with the airsip into each other's hooded ears, intimate as confession. In the air pockets - and there are dozens, trapped under inverted skiffs, under balconies, inside rooms that have learned to hold water out despite having no door - they are loud. That is where the laughing child you heard lives half its life. In a dry rug room, a family of four eats tomato paste with their nose-clips off, arguing in full voice while three meters away on the other side of an invisible membrane their neighbor prunes his hanging tomatoes in absolute silence.

They move on the timescale of accretion, 0.9/3 slow, because the place does. A woman tending her mid-water tomato nets will make one cut every thirty seconds, watch the thick-skinned, glowing orange fruit wobble, then make another. An old man mending net on a balcony rail doesn't tie knots; he lets the vine-rope grow through the net and graft. Time is measured in flywheel marks. *I'll meet you two marks past the piston thud.* *She hasn't moved from that stairwell since last turnover.* Rushing creates a wake that will be visible for hours, that will tangle your neighbor's curtains and silt their kitchen. Rushing is rude.

Work is tending. No one manufactures for export anymore. They tend the Balance - those house-sized wheels and drums at the bottom that no one remembers the original purpose of. Now they are the heart and lungs. Someone always sits with them, brushing the bacterial iron-shells off a pressure valve, watching that slow tick...tick...tick, feeding kelp-steel baskets into a chain that will take an hour to make a full circuit. It is not labor. It is housekeeping. Like sweeping a threshold that would otherwise silt shut.

Reproduction is infrequent, deliberate, communal. Pregnancy in warm neutral buoyancy is easy; birth is held in the largest air pocket, the old cistern dome at the very top lip of the bowl, where the surface-film is thickest and most mercurial. The child will spend its first months mostly in air, then be introduced to the hose, then to the water, then to the collar. No one teaches them to swim. There is no swimming. You wade-float-push through broth. You learn to not disturb.

### The Other Residents

They are not alone, but they are the only ones who *intend* to be here.

The tomatoes intend, in a plant way. They came as salvage and stayed as citizens. Their roots have learned to read copper. Each fruit hangs like a lantern, faintly warm to the touch from its own slow fermentation against osmosis.

The engine-block moss intends. It only grows on metal that remembers being hot. It keeps the hot memory alive.

The rust-lilies intend. Their pads on the far surface above you are dinner-table wide, their taproots splitting open fuse boxes to bloom copper filaments - they are the electricians now.

The carp does not intend. A single pale carp per stairwell, or maybe the same carp visiting all stairwells, hanging motionless and staring with eyes too large, gills flared wide in water that is almost too warm and too still to hold oxygen without help. It is a guest who forgot to leave after the flood.

Blind white crayfish, moving backwards in confused fits, picking at beards. Water boatmen dimpling the mercury ceiling of the surface-film from below, skating on the underside of the sky.

And the town itself lives. Not metaphorically. The bacterial varnish that sealed the rust, the barnacle-coral that knit itself into rebar in cauliflower nodules, the way brick has rounded like river stone but refuses to fall - this is a colonial organism the size of a hamlet that has decided that Density: 2.8/3 is its final form. It will not get more ruined. It will not get less alive. It repairs its people as much as its people repair it.

If you stay long enough and keep still, they will come close to look at you - not swimming to you, drifting to you, one fingertip on a pipe to steer. They will not rescue you or threaten you. They will check your hose, adjust your weight with a practiced tug, and with a hum-exhale directly against your temple say the only two things that matter here:

*Slow.*

*Don't make a hole.*
