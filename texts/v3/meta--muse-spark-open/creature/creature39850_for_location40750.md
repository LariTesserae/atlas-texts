Nothing lives here the way you think living should happen.

Not comfortably. Not permanently. Nothing *settles* here. It *holds on.*

This room is too young to have residents. It has tenants. It has shift-workers. It has things that arrived because water carried them and have not yet figured out how to leave, or never needed to.

Let me show you what holds on.

#### The Holdfasts

First — the green. Because it is the first lie your eye believes.

You will call it moss, singular, and be wrong. It is a city of mosses, so dense and so competitive that it has become a country with borders.

At the waterline, where the mere kisses the basalt, is *Sphagnum*. Not the soft, dry hummocks of the moor above, but sphagnum drowned and swollen, its heads like wet stars, each branch a sponge holding ten times its weight in that iron-tasting water. It bleeds tannins. That's why the mere is tea-brown at the edges. It is actively making the water more acid, more itself, pickling the sharp stone.

Just above it, where the weep becomes a runnel, is a curtain of *Philonotis* and *Brachythecium* — feather-mosses that never intended to hang. Gravity has given them no choice. They grow out from vertical cracks and then flop, saturated, forming dripping beards three, four inches long. Water runs *through* them, not over them. If you part one, you find it is hollow inside, a thatch-roof for mites and rotifers.

Lower, in the splash zone of the flume-wheel, the rock is painted black-green with liverwort — *Scapania* and *Jungermannia* — not plants as you understand plants, but wet leather tongues plastered flat against the basalt. They have no roots. They have no need for them. They drink through their skin. Touch one and it feels like cold, living suede.

And the dwarf willow — *Salix herbacea*, least willow in the world — is here as an exile. On the moor it is a creeping mat in wind-scoured peat. In here it has found a loophole. No wind. Constant water. Dim blue light. So it grows absurdly lush for itself: leaves the size of your thumbnail, glossy, trembling every ninety seconds when the hydraulic chuff shakes the air. It is not thriving. It is *persisting* with enthusiasm. There will never be a tree here, but this willow will never know that.

None of this is rooted in soil. There is no soil. There is peat slurry, basalt dust, and a film of algae that has learned to live as if it were still submerged. The algae — filamentous, bright, hair-fine — sways in air currents as if they were currents in a tide pool. It *is* the air current, visible.

If you get on your knees and bring your face close — close enough that your breath fogs the moss — you see the forest *inside* the forest: haircap moss like a stand of miniature pines, each stalk capped with a spore capsule still closed and furred, and between their trunks, tardigrades and nematodes moving like cattle through redwoods.

This is the lush that should not be. It is not decoration. It is insulation. The green is keeping the cold from winning completely.

#### The Conveyor

Second — the things that never stop moving, because to stop is to be eaten or to be flushed.

Look again at the mere. It looked empty from standing height. From kneeling height it is a factory floor.

The amphipods — *Gammarus*, perhaps, or a groundwater cousin paler than any field guide allows — travel in clouds. They are the color of old ice, the size of a grain of puffed rice, each with one black eyespot. They do not school. They *flick*. A thousand tiny flexes, off the bottom, into the water column, off the bottom again. They are eating the moss detritus, the algal film, each other's shed skins. They are, in turn, the only reason anything larger can live here.

Among them, slower, resolute, are the caddisflies. Not the flies themselves — you will see only their ghosts in a moment — but their larvae. Each one has built a case from what the room gave it: a few grains of basalt sand, a chip of willow leaf, a strand of sphagnum, glued with silk from their own mouths. They haul these cases across the black bottom like miners with carts, grazing biofilm. If you watch one for a minute you see its head emerge, jaws working, antennae flicking. They are the conveyor belts. Endless, patient, indifferent to the piston thump behind the wall.

On the surface, the diving beetles — small, black, absurdly fast — row. They trap a silver bubble of air under their wing covers and dive, and stay under, and surface again. They are not from here, not really. They fell in, or were washed in during the collapse, and found the amphipods abundant and stayed. They will die here when winter seals the crack, and their larvae will not.

And then, all at once, the air fills.

The midges hatch. Not in ones or twos. In a blizzard that makes no sound. One moment the water is dimpled with pupal skins, the next the air above the mere is thick with them — adults with plumose antennae and legs like blown glass, living for an afternoon on nothing. They rise, mate in a dancing column in that single shaft of blue dusk-light, lay eggs on the water, and die on the water. Their bodies become the next layer of detritus. You will find their wings stuck to your jacket when you leave. The chamber uses even their death as raw material.

This is the bulk of life here: small, soft, aquatic, furious in numbers, invisible at human scale until you kneel.

#### The Sparks

Third — the hunters. Because any room that makes this much food will make something to eat it.

They were here before you noticed them, curled in the moss hummocks like questions.

The eels. Call them eels because English has no word for a cold-water, paedomorphic, half-transparent fish-thing that never leaves groundwater. They are perhaps *Anguilla* elvers washed down and dwarfed, or perhaps something older — a brook lamprey relative that found this pocket and never needed eyes again. They are twelve, fifteen inches long, the thickness of your thumb, the color of skim milk with a faint, darker line of muscle showing through.

They do not swim so much as *pour* through the moss. They live *in* the hummock, not beside it, their bodies leaving warm tunnels in the sphagnum that fill with water behind them.

And when they hunt, the room changes.

An amphipod cloud flicks too close to a hummock. The eel goes rigid. Along its flank — just behind the head where you would expect a lateral line — a band of electrocytes tightens.

Then: *tick.*

A sharp, audible snap, like a spark jumping a gap on old machinery. In the dusk you actually see it: a hair-thin, branching blue-white line that licks out into the water for an instant and is gone, reflected twice — once on the water, once on the wet basalt. The amphipods within two inches convulse. The eel sucks them in with a soft *pop*.

After three or four strikes, the air over the mere smells faintly metallic. Ozone. The same smell after lightning, scaled down to fit in a stone pocket. The midges don't care. The caddis larvae don't care. The beetles pause for half a second and row on.

There are three of them. Maybe four. You never see more than one at a time, but you see ripples in different hummocks at once. They do not cooperate. They do not fight. There is enough flicker-food that territoriality would be a waste of electricity. They are solitary sparks in a wet machine. They have no nests. No young you can find — perhaps they brood deep in the moss, perhaps they spawn elsewhere and only the lost ones end here.

They are the only vertebrates in the room. They are the apex of a food web eight meters across.

#### The Machine That Is Not a Machine

And finally — what lives here that is not alive at all, but behaves as if it were.

The water-piston behind the north wall. It is not an animal. It is a basalt joint that floods and empties, floods and empties, compressing air until the air punches out with that deep *chuff* and a breath of stone-scented mist. But it sets the rhythm for everything. The moss shivers to it. The amphipods time their flicks in the lull after it. The eels hunt just before it, when prey is startled.

The wheel. That perfect disc of basalt caught in the flume, turning and turning, humming against its own bearing. It is not alive. But it grinds. It polishes. It will not stop until it is sand, and in doing so it makes more sand for caddis cases.

These are the shift-whistles and lathes of the factory with no builders. No bricks. No iron. No hands. Just young rock, still sharp enough to cut, and water under pressure doing the work of hands.

If you stay — and you shouldn't, because your exhalations are already condensing on the ceiling and adding to the weep — you will start to feel that the room itself is the organism. The moss is its skin, the mere its stomach, the piston its lung, the wheel its joint, the eels its nerves, firing.

Nothing lives here comfortably.

Everything lives here completely, for as long as the water keeps coming and the crack stays open and winter doesn't seal it shut.

When the moor above freezes solid, this pocket will keep working in the dark, fill-chuff-grind-tick, fill-chuff-grind-tick, unseen, unheard, lush and loud and utterly indifferent to whether anyone ever slips through the crowberry again.
