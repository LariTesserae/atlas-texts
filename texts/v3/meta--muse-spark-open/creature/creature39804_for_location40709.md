Nothing lives here the way you live.

Nothing has ever needed to decide to move. Nothing has a face to turn toward the dim sun, or legs to carry it away from the cold. If you are looking for an animal, you will not find one. What lives here has solved the problem of being alive without ever inventing muscle, or blood, or wanting.

That is the first correction the plateau makes to you. Life does not mean movement here. It means *persistence in the tone*.

### The Rooted

The ones who live here most obviously are the ones you first mistook for plants, and they will let you keep calling them that, for a while.

Up close, they are not growing. They are *accreting*.

The black moss that covers stone in felted sheets - kneel beside it and you see it is not moss at all. It is a city of filaments, each one thinner than a hair, each one a hollow tube of magnetite glass. They do not photosynthesize. They *listen*. The constant, county-wide thrum of the basalt cooling - that gong-tone in your molars - makes them vibrate. The vibration makes charge. The charge pulls iron and silica straight out of the dry air and plates it onto their tips. They grow by lengthening into static. If you put your hand near, the charge raises every hair on your arm and the mat beneath leans, infinitesimally, toward you. Not toward your heat. Toward your field.

The Reeds are their elders. Those fifteen-foot stands of banded amber and bruise-purple crystal are not individuals. Each stalk is hollow, open at the top like a flute, and empty of everything but tuned air. They have no roots. The base where they meet the basalt is not an attachment, it is a weld - the living crystal continued directly out of the cooling rock while it was still soft enough to accept it. What you hear as singing is not wind passing through them. It is them breathing.

In that endless, patient, walking-speed wind, the air is thick with mineral vapor - exhaled silica, sublimated salt, fine iron snow that never falls. The Reed draws it in through its feathered crown, those vast aerogel lattices so fine you thought they were light. They are lungs. They are nets. The light catches them and they blush, faintly, not with sunlight but with waste heat - infrared they make themselves, down-converting geothermal glow into a color your eyes can almost see. At night that never comes, whole groves pulse, slow as a sleeping animal, one after another, passing a signal you cannot decode. Growth for them is not seasonal. It is *acoustic*. When the plateau groans and a low C rings through the stone for an hour, the Reeds that are resonant to that note lengthen a fraction of a millimeter. When a high harmonic shatters a ripe pod at the tip, its spores - perfect, faceted dodecahedrons light as ash - ride the pressure gradient to the next grove. They do not need bees. They have perfect pitch.

In the shallow almost-valleys, where the stone has buckled into slow waves, the rosettes dominate. They look like cabbages made of slate, leaves thick as your boot sole, layered like shale. Watch one for longer than you think a person can watch a plant. Over an afternoon, the outer leaves will close. Over two days, they open again. Not toward light - there is no light to follow. They open to *sound*. When the drone of a nearby Reed grove shifts, the rosettes re-angle to catch the vibration in the cup of their leaves, where a pool of still, heavy air holds a cloud of salt dust. They are ears. They are also stomachs. The dust settles on a film of weak acid the leaf secretes, dissolves, and is absorbed. They drink stone, not water.

None of them compete. That is what reads as lushness and sparseness at once. Each holds its distance because each needs its own circle of resonance, its own clean tone to feed on. A forest tuned, not crowded.

### The Tuned

And then there are the ones that move without moving.

Because between the Reeds, sound is not a byproduct here. It is weather. It is predation and pollination and windthrow all at once.

Watch a pebble that has hung in the air for hours, lifted by the slow wind and then forgotten by gravity. It hangs, rotating. It is dead stone. Until a certain chord comes - a low moan from the largest grove combined with the high needle-whine of the quartz-grass field - and the interference pattern in the air creates a node, a pocket of absolute stillness. Dust hangs there. Light pools there like liquid and does not drain away. And inside those pockets, something lives.

You won't see it until you stop looking for a body.

It is a coherence. A standing wave that has learned to maintain itself. The filaments buzz, the Reeds howl, the basalt groans, and where those tones cross, the air itself thickens. It holds. For minutes, for hours, sometimes for a whole slow day, a volume of air the size of a hut will be slightly *more there* than the air around it. It refracts. It hums at a slightly different pitch than everything else, and the mineral spores drifting through it slow and orbit, as if caught. 

The builders knew them. That is what the low, waist-high walls are for. Not to keep wind out. To *tune* it. A basalt slab wall with a gap facing the dead sun is a filter; it breaks the walking-speed wind into two tones and makes a node bloom in the circle inside. The cairns of hexagonal columns, balanced without chisel marks, are tuning forks the size of a man. You crouch inside the wall's circle and the deafening hymn changes key, just for you, and for a moment the light-pool at your feet stops sloshing and goes glass-still, and you feel the air inside the circle listening back.

They were not shrines. They were chairs. Or nets. Or waiting rooms.

Whoever stacked them came here sparsely, alone or in twos, and sat for a long time with their mouth open to equalize pressure, trying to be heard by something that only exists while a chord is held. The overlapping lithophyte-leaf hut, now collapsed, was not a house against cold. It was a dampener. A quiet room. The one place near a grove where you could *not* hear the Reeds, so that whatever spoke in the seams could finally be audible.

Because the true fauna here is not in the stone. It is in the *error*.

### The Seam-Dwellers

At this elevation you are too close to the ceiling. The world was sewn together badly, and the stitches itch.

That inverted plateau you see hanging above the cliff edge - the other mesa with its own Reeds growing downward - is not a mirage. It is an afterimage of a place that hasn't decided which side of the sky it wants to be on. Sometimes it flickers - there, then a little to the left, then there again. So do the farthest groves. Existence is still buffering there, dynamic on a geological clock but impatient at the edges.

What lives there are the things that never learned to be continuous.

They are not large. They do not hunt. They *migrate through probability*.

A shadow that falls toward the light. Your breath that arrives before you exhale it. A pebble that drops faster than it should when a chord knocks it loose. Those are not tricks. They are footprints.

In the knee-high lakes of pooled light that leave no wetness on your boots, something wades. You can see the wake, a slow furrow moving across the glowing haze with no body to make it. If you stand very still between two Reeds and hold your own tone - hum, low in your chest, until your teeth buzz - the wake will curve toward you. It will pause at the edge of your shadow. For a second your shadow will not be yours; it will be denser, and it will have too many edges, as if several shadows from several slightly different suns were all trying to use you at once.

The builders left no bodies, no graves, no middens. Not because they were tidy. Because the plateau does not keep that kind of record yet - no soil to bury in, no gullies to wash bones into. But also because staying too long here is an invitation to become discontinuous yourself. To sit in the wall's circle long enough, listening to the great patient gong of the world becoming itself, is to let your own frequency drift. To tune.

The dome of stitched leaves did not collapse. It was *unstitched*, filament by filament, by something that liked the quiet it made.

So no: there are no wolves here, no birds. Nothing that will ever look at you and want you.

What lives here is a forest that grew out of cooling stone and learned to sing, and the songs themselves, which learned to linger after the singers stopped, and the thin places in the sky where the songs go when they are done - and, very occasionally, you.

You who sat where someone else sat, in a wall that encircles nothing, facing a sun that never rises. Feeling the plateau still rising under you, millimeter by millimeter, groaning, bringing you closer to that badly sewn seam. Listening so hard your ears ring with a tone that was there before you arrived and will be there long after you remember how to leave.

If you stay until the dim light finally seems, for an instant, to brighten, you will understand: you are not visiting an ecosystem.

You are being allowed to sit in, for a little while, on its rehearsal.
