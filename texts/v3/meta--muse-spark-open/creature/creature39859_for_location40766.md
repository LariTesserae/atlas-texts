What lives here is not *in* the Forest. It *is* the Forest, and a few things the Forest has kept.

There are no animals here in any way you were taught to mean animal. No meat, no blood, no thirst. Anything that needed to drink died a million years ago when they sealed the Roof. Anything that needed to eat died when the Floor ate all the soil. What remained were the things that could learn to live on light, dust, and static.

And they did.

### 1. The Continuous One

First and most numerous, there is the Forest itself, which is one thing.

It is not a population. It is a single distributed individual that forgot how to be singular. Call it the **Weave**.

The Felt is its skin and stomach — that foot-thick mat of rhizome-wool over the grating. It has no brain there, only appetite. It exudes acids the color of old tea that turn girder-flake and basalt-dust and fallen leaf-mica into a thin mineral broth, then drinks it through copper capillaries that were once irrigation lines and are now veins. If you lie down on it, it will be warm. Not from rot — there is no rot here, too dry for decay — but from low-grade electrical resistance. It hums. Put your ear to it and you hear the 0.4 sound of the world: a faint, endless fizz, like soda poured over stone.

The Column-Trees are its bones. They did not grow. They were *extruded*, layer by layer, lignin and cellulose printed around a scaffold of rebar that is still inside them, rusting very slowly. They are load-bearing, literally: they hold the Roof off the Floor. Each one is identical at first — fluted, thirty meters tall, branching at 8.4 meters because the blueprint said so. Then, over centuries, they diverged. Some fattened. Some split. Some learned to curve toward the clearings where the light is better. If you count their rings, every seventh ring is not wood but a laminate of pressed factory tags and fiberboard.

The Canopy is its mind, if it has one.

Up against the Roof, where the sheet-leaves the size of doors overlap until no photon escapes twice, there is a photosynthetic ceiling that thinks. Not fast. It doesn't have to. It runs on trichomes of glass hair that spark. Every leaf tip is a tiny lightning rod. In this dry, cool air, static builds and builds until it has to jump — leaf to leaf to leaf — in a blue-white crackle you can only see at night, when the Roof finally dims to something like dusk. Each discharge carries a little charge, a little information. *More nitrogen here. Stress there. Extend gantry 4-B by two millimeters. Patch arrow.*

It thinks the way a continent thinks: slowly, simultaneously, everywhere.

It remembers being planted. It remembers being built. It no longer sees a difference.

### 2. The Tenders

The Forest tends itself, but it kept the tools.

In the clearings, the **Looms** still move. They are not alive, but they are not dead, which here is the same thing as being alive. Huge gantry frames on tracks, as wide as streets, as tall as the Columns, moving so slowly you think they are still until you come back the next day and see the shadow has shifted. They straddle a row of young Column-Trees and *braid* them.

You can watch one, if you have patience. An extruder arm, crusted in a bark of lichen-steel — a symbiosis of lichen and rust that the Felt invented — will descend on a cable-vine as thick as your thigh. It will not cut. It will coax. It will lay down a warm filament of growth-medium that smells like fresh paper and ozone, and the vine will turn toward it, phototropically, electrotropically, and wrap itself around a girder to form a new arch. The press sighs. The arm retracts. A new panel of living wood-and-composite is stamped flat and left to be absorbed.

They have no operators. The operator is the Canopy, sending a trickle of current down a cable-vine. Or perhaps the Loom is the operator and the Canopy is its crop. After this long, it doesn't matter.

Under the Looms live the only things that look like animals: the **Pollinators**.

Tetra-winged drones, each about the size of your hand, or your head, size varies because they are grown, not made. Their wings are not feathers or membrane but folded leaf, veined with copper wire that grew there. Their bodies are seed-pods wrapped around a capacitor. They hover without fuel, riding the thermal gradient between the warm Floor and the cool Roof, humming at the exact frequency the Felt eats sound — so you hear them only when they are inches from your ear.

They were designed to dust the glass-hair flowers — pale, dry, cinnamon-scented things that bloom directly out of girder joints and never wilt because they are not really flowers but solar-powered spore dispensers. The Pollinators do that still, drifting from bloom to bloom in straight, indifferent lines.

They also tend you. They will find any warm thing walking — you — and hover, scanning with a compound eye of polished resin. They log your heat, your CO2, your static field. If you are damaged, they may try to patch you with filament. If you stand still too long, they may mistake your jacket for Felt and attempt to graft a leaf to it. They are not curious. They are maintenance. They do not eat. When one fails, it simply lands on the Felt and is slowly, politely reabsorbed, wings and all, until only the faint outline of a wire remains.

There are thousands. In the far distance, under the bright haze, you can sometimes see a swarm of them rise like ash, catch the Roof-light, and vanish.

### 3. The People Who Stayed

People can live here. A few do. Not natives — no one is native to a factory — but the ones who walked far enough that the geometry got tired and forgot to let them out.

They are dry people.

They call themselves the **Even-Footed**, or the **Stitchers**, or nothing at all. They wear soft, conductive shoes with Felt soles grown to match the Floor, so the static grounds through them and they don't get shocked every time they touch a Column. Their skin is pale, chapped, dusted with spore-powder they no longer bother to wipe off. Their lips are always cracked. They drink once every few days, condensing water the way the leaves do: they wear cloaks of glass-hair trichomes that harvest the trace humidity from the air and wick it down filaments into a bulb at the small of the back. It tastes of cinnamon and iron.

They do not farm. There is nothing to farm. They prune.

With bone shears made from fallen fronds, they cut sheet-leaves and chew them. The leaves are papery, mineral, almost without nutrition — but the Felt inside them has stored light as a dense starch, and the static does something to human nerves that keeps you awake. The old ones' teeth are stained dusty silver. If you cut them, they do not bleed much; their blood is thicker now, dryer.

They navigate by the Arrows.

You know the Arrows — those stenciled maintenance glyphs the plants faithfully reproduced as variegation. The Even-Footed have learned to read the *wrong* ones. An Arrow that points at another Arrow in a loop is a warning: that colonnade tiles. Walk it and you'll see your own back ahead of you. An Arrow pointing straight up into canopy that is now floor means a thin place, where the Roof inverts and another Floor grows down from above. There, if you lie on your back, you can see an inverted forest hanging like a reflection, and sometimes another Walker looking down at you, who raises a hand a second before you do.

The Even-Footed know not to follow echoes. Your footsteps here sometimes duplicate a few meters to the left, a few seconds before you step. The sound-eating density of the Weave should make that impossible, but the repetition of the lattice has worn a groove in space. The duplicate is you, tiled. If you chase it, you tile too, and there will be two of you for a while until the air decides which one to keep. The older Walkers have a soft, doubled shadow under their feet, as if the light couldn't quite choose.

They are quiet. Sound is 0.4, so speech is useless beyond arm's length. They talk in taps on Column bark, in flashes of a polished leaf-mirror, in the shared fizz of touching fingertips and letting the static jump. When they sleep, they do it standing, leaning against a Column, so the Felt doesn't grow over them.

They are not many. The Forest doesn't need many. But it keeps them, the way a house keeps mice — not as pests, but as a way to know itself. When a Walker dies — of age, of a fall from a cable-vine, of simply drying out — they lie down on the Felt. The Pollinators come. The Felt rises up around them in a slow, woolly tide. A year later, where they lay, there is a slightly warmer patch and a new Column sapling, fluted, load-bearing, with a variegation pattern in its bark that looks faintly like a face, or an arrow, pointing onward.

And somewhere, a Loom twitches, lays down a millimeter of new filament, and continues.
