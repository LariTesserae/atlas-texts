What lives here lives low, and close together, and does not waste breath.

First - the people. You hear them before you see them well, because they are the same color as the walls.

They are not tall. Not dramatically so; there is no separate race here, no fantasy of dwarfs - only the shape a body takes after forty generations breathing thin air and sleeping half-underground. Barrel-chested, short-legged, long-waisted. The children are pink with cold when they are small and then weather to the same pale grey-brown as everyone else. The men and women are difficult to tell apart at a distance by silhouette; both wear the same thing, which is wool over wool over wool, the natural felted grey of the plateau sheep, with cloaks that are almost mats, heavy enough to turn rain. Their heads are covered more often than not. Their hands are out, always - uncovered, stone-colored, the skin of the palms thickened and split like old leather, the nails short and black under the quick with lichen dust.

They breathe audibly. Everyone does up here, even the children. A faint wheeze on the inhale when they carry, a soft grunt when they lift. The air is thin and they have made peace with it by moving slowly. No one runs. A boy chasing a sheep on a causeway does it at a loping walk. An old woman with a bundle of sedge on her back goes at the same pace as the boy. Hurry would be a kind of rudeness to the lungs. So their whole city moves at the pace of stone repair - deliberate, continuous.

Up close their faces are broad and reddened at the cheek and nose where the wind takes them, eyes narrowed habitually against low cloud glare. They are not loud people. On the causeways they speak low, or not at all, and they use hands a great deal - a palm flat on a wall means *wait, water coming*, two fingers tapped on a lintel means *duck*. Children learn the gestures before the words. When they do speak the language is soft, consonant-light, made to carry a short distance in cold air without shouting. A call from one terrace to the next is not a yell but a clear, fluting *hoo* that the stone bounces gently from wall to wall. You can hear a mother call her daughter three cells away and it does not break the quiet.

They live by wall. That is the unit, not the person. A family is a set of shared walls - perhaps eight to twenty people, old and young, with adjoining round houses that have grown together like soap bubbles, sharing heat through the stone. You are born into a wall-family and you learn its water. Because every wall-family is responsible for the water that comes to it: the seep it catches off the scarp behind it, the troughs it keeps clear, the cistern it keeps covered against sheep dung. To neglect your trough is to flood your neighbor downslope, and to flood your neighbor is the nearest thing they have to a crime. So children are given a stone hoe as soon as they can hold one and taught to scrape silt. Not as punishment. As membership. An old man who can no longer lift slabs will still sit on his roof in the drizzle and watch his section of channel for leaves.

There is very little distinction to be made between living and maintaining, here. The sheep need moving from grazing cell to grazing cell before the peat poaches. The frost will have shifted a capstone, even if only by a finger's width; it must be lifted and packed. The cotton-grass must be cut for the byre floors. The cisterns must be skimmed. It is the same work your grandmother did in the same walls, and it never ends, and that is not despair to them. That is what it means for a place to be alive - that it requires tending.

They are healthy, in the way of well-kept stone. Not long-lived by lowland standards - the damp gets into joints and lungs by fifty - but dense with years while they last. Babies are fat and solemn, kept in deep wall-niches out of the wind, passed from lap to lap. Old people are small and bent, polished by weather like the stunted birches, and they are always handling something - wool, a joint of meat, a child's hair. No one is alone. The architecture forbids it. To go to relieve yourself you must pass two neighbors.

And with them, the sheep. You understand at once why there are no other herds.

They are the plateau made animal. The color of wet limestone when dry and darker when rained on, with faces like weathered wood, small black eyes. They are compact, low to the ground, their wool not fluffy but dense and felted close to the body, almost waterproof. They make almost no sound - no bleating chorus at dawn, only an occasional soft cough, a shuffle. They move in small knots of six or ten, guided not by dogs - there are no dogs here, nothing to feed a dog on - but by a child with a stick and a patient *tch tch* sound. They eat everything the plateau sparingly offers - sedge, marsh grass, the lichen scraped off sunward slabs - and turn it into milk and wool and, eventually, meat and bone tools. Their dung is gathered every morning, dried on wall-tops, and burned underground where it smoulders slow and hot. Each family knows its sheep by feel more than sight. At night the sheep are tucked into stone cells under terraces, and their breathing warms the houses above them.

Then what else - almost nothing, and that almost-nothing matters.

The small brown birds. They are everywhere and nowhere, the wind made quick. No bigger than your thumb, the color of peat, they live not *on* the walls but *in* them, in the chinks left deliberately or inevitably between dry stones. They dart out to take the brief, cold midges that hatch over the meres in cloud, and dart back. If you stand still long enough you will see a whole wall flicker with them, each hole occupied, each bird with its own exact door. In winter they vanish into the deep of the walls and do not die, quite - they puff and sleep, heartbeats slowed to nothing, waiting. The children know not to pry them out.

In the water itself, life is careful. No fish worth naming in these shallow, cold pans - only in the deepest cisterns, where no light reaches, something like a pale newt or blind minnow, left alone because it eats mosquito wrack. The real inhabitants of the water are invisible: the peat itself, which is not dead matter but a living mat, black and springy, made of centuries of sedge half-decayed; and on it, the cotton-grass with its trembling white heads, and the sphagnum that holds water like a lung holds air. If you pull up a handful of mire the water that runs out is tea-dark and cold enough to ache, and it smells of iron and time.

Overhead, very rarely, the hawk. It does not live here, not truly. It lives in the air above the plateau, where the plateau's broad back makes its own thermals. From below it is only a fleck, a slow turning that watches but never stoops often, because there is not enough below it worth stooping for. To the people it is a kind of clock - if it is turning, the air is stable; if it is gone, rain is coming.

And beneath and between all this, lichen. You would not think to count it as an inhabitant until you have been here a day and realized it covers more surface than people do. Grey-green leather shields as big as dinner plates on the windward boulders, rust-orange crusts in the seep-stains, black stitching in every crack. It grows slower than a wall is built. Some patches, the old people will tell you - touching them gently, not picking - were there when their grandmother's grandmother laid the first course of that terrace. Lichen is the city's other builder. It seals stone. It holds it. In places the walls are more lichen than limestone, living mortar.

None of these lives alone. That is the rule the plateau teaches.

The sheep cannot live without the walls that shelter them and the sedge the walls enclose. The sedge cannot live without the peat that the sphagnum makes by dying. The peat cannot live without the seep-water the walls channel. The people cannot live without the sheep and the cisterns. The small birds cannot live without the walls and the midges over the managed meres. The lichen cannot live without the stone and the mist.

So the city is not inhabited. The city *is* the inhabitant - a single, spread-out, low, wet, breathing thing made of stone skins and wool and grass heads and water moving through troughs and hands moving to clear them. If you stay until the stars come out, excessive and pitiless, you will feel it settle. Not sleeping - it does not sleep. Just breathing a little slower. A trough dripping. A sheep shifting in its cell. An old woman coughing softly in a sunken house and a young one answering without words.

You are, for a little while, part of its warmth, if you keep your feet dry and your voice down.
