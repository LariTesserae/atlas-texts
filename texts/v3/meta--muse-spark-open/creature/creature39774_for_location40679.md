No one *settled* here. That word implies arrival and decision. The people who live in the pocket were thinned out until only they remained.

There are fewer than two hundred of them at any one time, and you understand that number is not a census but a carrying function — like the number of trout a cold lake can hold. More would be a different city. More would fill the void, and the first law here — unwritten, unneeded — is *do not fill the void.*

### The Humans

You see them first as movement against stillness, because nothing else moves with intention.

They are tall and narrow, not by genetics but by habit. Living vertical does that. Their bodies have learned the economy of ledges. They don't swagger or stride; they place. A foot, a hand on a rail even when they don't need it, a pause to let a drop fall past their face before stepping through its path. Children are taught to move like climbers before they are taught to read, and the elderly never stop moving like that — there is no shuffling here. To shuffle is to slip. To slip is to fall forty meters into a net that will catch you and shame you.

They look older than they are and younger than they are. The condensate is mineral-soft and cold and it keeps their skin pale and slightly damp always, as if they have just come inside from rain. They wear the same thing with personal variations: matte, charcoal thermal weave that sheds water in beads, never absorbing. The cuffs and fingertips have a faint pearlescence, a second skin of assembler residue. If you work the vats — and everyone works the vats at some point, unclogging a channel on level three where the calcite beard grows too thick — the material kisses your hands and stays there for days. It glows faintly under the nails. They don't wash it off vigorously. It's useful. It remembers light.

They are quiet. Not silent; quiet the way people who live in a church are quiet, or in a library with a very high ceiling where every small sound returns to you. They speak in low registers, little breath spent. Laughter, when it happens on a bridge between habitations, is startling because it echoes four times, cleanly, *ha ha ha ha,* diminishing. They learn to laugh softly.

You will not find a mayor. You will find a rotation. The woman who checks the heat exchangers this week taught the children last week and will tend the lower drain next week. Jobs are not identities here; they are chores scaled to a house that happens to be sixty meters tall. Status is measured in useless knowledge: who knows where the swift nests, who can predict which basalt tooth will drip next by the change in air pressure, who has memorized the particular hum of each filament lift so they can tell by ear if one is fraying. That is wealth.

They sleep lightly. The pocket never goes dark — at night-cycle the ceramic dims to amber, the color of low sun through ice — and it never goes silent. Water ticks on metal roofs inches from their heads. The rock contracts once or twice a year with a deep *tick* you feel in the molars. Pressure doors sigh. So they have all become light sleepers who wake completely, instantly, without panic. A child wakes when an icicle falls three levels above because the change in the dripping pattern is information.

They do not reproduce quickly. Two or three births a year, often in the same habitation near the warmest exchanger. Pregnancy here is conspicuous, not because of the body but because of the movement. A pregnant person cannot be vertical in the same way. The community builds around them, temporarily: ramps extruded from the rock in a night, rails where there were no rails. Then taken away again. The architecture is solicitous but not sentimental. It accommodates and then forgets.

And they do not age out. There is no outside to retire to. The oldest among them is a man whose job is to sit on the market platform — that vast, mostly empty platform with three stalls — and watch the mineral beards grow. He can tell you to within a week when a sheet of water will find a new path down the western wall by the color of the salt it leaves. His eyes are the color of wet basalt. He hasn't left the pocket in forty years. He doesn't miss sky. He says the shaft of sun that comes through the cleft at noon is more sun than anyone needs if you really look at it.

What they share, all of them, is a peculiar attention. Outside, humans look out. Here, they look *up* and *across*. They are always aware of the volume. When they talk to you, part of them is listening to drips behind you, to the crab-maintainers ticking along a pipe, to the faint change in the thrum when the lower drain sighs mist. They have become an auditory culture. They know each other by footstep on different materials — alloy versus ceramic versus stone — long before they see a face.

They think of themselves as guests. Not tenants, not owners. Guests of the void. If you ask why they don't build more, pack the geode tight, make it warm and full and efficient, they look puzzled, as if you asked why you don't fill your lungs with water to use the space better.

### The Other Residents

The humans are the least populous thing that lives here.

**The maintainers** live here more truly. Small, dark, crab-like, with needle legs and bodies like polished river stones that have been folded wrong. There are dozens. Maybe hundreds. No one counts. They pick their way along pipes and conduits and the undersides of bridges, never on the walkways unless they must cross. They have no eyes you can see. They feel scale — the white crust of minerals that blooms where water runs — and scrape it with mouthparts that unfold like origami wrenches. They clear ice with a puff of waste heat that smells briefly of ozone. They ignore humans absolutely, and humans afford them the same courtesy. It is not indifference. It is coexistence between species with entirely different ideas of what the city is for. To you the city is shelter. To them the city is a warm rock that sweats useful salts.

If one falls — and rarely, one does — a human will pick it up on a piece of card and set it back on a pipe. Nothing more. No one keeps one as a pet. No one has named them. To name them would be to fill the void in a different way.

**The swift** lives here by mistake and now by choice. A high alpine swift, soot-black with a white throat, that blew in through the cleft one storm and never relearned the way out. That was eight years ago by the market man's count. It nests on a girder on the eastern wall, a messy cup of stolen insulation fibers and mineral dust, and it hunts what should not exist here: tiny midges that breed in the perpetual damp, that themselves feed on the only green that is not green —

**The beards.**

You thought the pale growths on the walls where water sheets were just calcite and salt. Come closer. In the thinnest films, where water never quite freezes and never quite flows, there is a living slick. Translucent, cream-pink, bacterial mats no thicker than paper, laying down minerals as they live and die. They are not lichen — lichen hasn't made the argument outside yet — they are something older and more patient, lithotrophic, eating rock and exhaling stone. They make the beards grow a millimeter a year. They make the water taste faintly of iron when you cup it. They are the true agriculture of the pocket, though no one farms them. The midges eat them. The swift eats the midges. The humans breathe what they exhale. It is a food chain that fits in your palm.

And underneath that, **the rock itself** lives in the way young rock lives. Four thousand meters up, sheared up last century, it has not finished becoming itself. It still holds heat from its making. It still ticks as it contracts. Water beads on its lips, runs in dark fingers, freezes overnight and splits again by noon — even inside, that cycle ghosts through in miniature. The chamber sweats because the mountain breathes. The people have learned to time their quietest hours to its exhalations, when pressure equalizes and the cleft sighs cold air inward and every hanging bridge hums at once, low and mournful, like a throat clearing.

If you stay until amber, when the filament lifts stop running and the humans have gone inside their small, bright containers that hang alone in immense, respectful emptiness, you will see what really lives here.

Not a population.

A negotiation: between water and cold, between rock and the things that learned to hang lightly from it without ever presuming to fill it.
