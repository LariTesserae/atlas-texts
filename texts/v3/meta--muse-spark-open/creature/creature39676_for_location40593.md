No one is on the street when you first look.

That's the trick of it. For a place so saturated with work — the mill turning, the water running, the audible tick of growth — it looks, at first glance, uninhabited. No crowd at the corner store, no traffic. Just the alleys dripping and the sodium lights buzzing behind their cages of vine.

Then you see that it has all been cut back.

Every doorway is cut back in a clean half-circle, the thatch of fig and philodendron shaved to the brick as if with clippers that morning. The stair that should be impassable is passable; the iron treads have been scraped free of moss. The gutter-river runs, but it runs *clear* — someone has pulled the water hyacinth out of the choke point. A broom leans against a wall where the sweeping stopped mid-stroke. A pair of rubber boots sits outside a door, full of water that was left to tip out and dry, which it never does.

This place is inhabited the way a beehive is inhabited. Not by presence, but by maintenance. You only start to count the inhabitants by the evidence of where they have just been.

Then they come back into the frame, because you have learned how to see movement at the speed they move.

They move slow here. Everyone does. You will at first think it is lethargy, or heat-stunned indolence, until you stay a whole afternoon and understand it as technique. Nothing dries here; exertion doesn't cool you. To hurry is to fog your glasses, to slick your palms, to slip on the algae between cobbles. So they have learned — over two, three generations since the plants took the brick without breaking it — to do everything at the pace of tending.

They are not strange to look at. That is the second thing that takes time to understand. You were braced for something feral or dramatically adapted and what you get is people who look almost exactly like the people on the agricultural plain you came down from, only softened and darkened and kept damp around the edges.

They are mill people still, mostly. The same families. Shorter than you expect, or maybe just compact, because growing up in low rooms under pipe-racks and in the perpetual stoop of weeding does that. Pale in winter, deeply tanned in summer except where the sun never touches — the insides of forearms, the hollows of throats — where they stay the color of wet paper. Their hair is almost always cut short, not for style but because long hair here mildews. The men and the women both. Children with shaved heads at the start of summer, showing the pale scalp underneath, already freckled. Older women with tight perms that hold despite the wet. Older men with mustaches that trap condensation.

Their clothes never quite dry. No one's do. You learn to stop noticing the dark sweat-patches at the back, the cuffs that are wet through. Cotton rots here in a season if you let it stay damp in a pile, so everything is thin, synthetic where they can get it, sleeveless, patched. Aprons made from old conveyor-belt rubber. Knee-high boots, always, even in 84 degrees — because of snakes and rust and the suck of canal mud — worn with the tops folded down. Hands that are never clean. The dye works never fully closed; someone always runs a short batch for trade, and it leaves a faint indigo under the nails that no scrubbing takes out, and a green-black in the creases from crushed fig sap and copper fungicide. If you shake hands with one of them your palm comes away slightly warm and slightly gritty.

And their lungs. You hear it before anyone mentions it. A soft, collective rattle on humid mornings. Not tuberculosis, though outsiders always think it. A gardener's cough. From breathing spore and brick dust and steam that has passed through jasmine for forty years. They cough into their shoulders without breaking conversation. The children cough too, less self-consciously. They all know which alleys to avoid when the mill vents the drying fans and the air goes white with lint.

There are not many of them for how dense the knot is. A half-mile bowl can hold ten thousand if you stack them industrial-tight. This one holds maybe two or three thousand now. Enough to keep every pipe lagged and every wall cut back, not enough to ever catch up. You understand the population has stabilized not at what the buildings could hold, but at what the cutting requires. One person can tend about one building-face and its gutters. Less than that and the root-mat wins and the mortar washes in the 3 p.m. rain. More than that and there isn't enough work and the food — taro from the ditches, beans from the roof meadows, bread from the plain traded for machine parts — runs short.

So they tend. That is the verb that has swallowed every other job description here.

There are no more “millhands” and “electricians” as separate castes. There is a rotation. In the morning, before the heat settles, the young men and some of the women go up. Up onto the roofs with machetes and sickles, barefoot for grip, to pollard the willows that have rooted in the tar. They do not pull them; pulling would rip the roof membrane. They cut them to stumps, paint the stumps with salt, leave the roots as reinforcement. The cut branches are tossed down to the street where kids strip the leaves for compost and stack the wood by the boiler house.

At mid-morning, when the turbines are at full thrum and the air is already thick, the sluice-keepers — mostly old men and old women who can no longer climb — take their hand-cranks and walk the creek-canal. This is a position of real status, quiet as it looks. One wrong turn and a low courtyard floods to the knee and a nest of mosquitoes hatches that will torment that block for weeks. They know the water by sound. They open a gate a half-inch to let the hyacinth flush. They close another to keep the taro beds wet. They do it without speaking.

By noon, everyone sweeps. Every stoop, every stretch of alley cobble where the banyan roots have heaved the concrete into ridges. Not because they are tidy in any puritan sense, but because leaf litter here becomes soil in three days and then becomes seedlings in cracks and then becomes a heaved foundation. Sweeping is structural maintenance. You will see a woman in her seventies sweeping the same ten feet of alley with a stiff palm broom, steady as breathing, while steam sighs off a valve six feet over her head wrapped in morning glory.

In the early afternoon, before the rain, they go inside. Not to rest, exactly. Inside is where the second tending happens — wiping condensation off the inside of walls with rags so the mat on the outside doesn't push through as black mold, checking the ceramic insulators where vine tendrils try to short the humming transformers, oiling the shears. They talk then, low. The buildings here are so close and the plant-mat so good at muffling that sound carries oddly; a normal voice on the second floor sounds like shouting in the alley. So they have learned to speak under the threshold of the mill's thrum, in short, practical sentences. *Need the north wall cut. Gate three is sticking. Your boy left his boots in the canal again.*

At three or four, the rain comes. Everyone knows to be under something, not because it is dangerous but because it is absolute. It hammers the roof meadows into mud, it turns every gutter into a roar, it drums on the corrugated sheds until you can't hear the looms. The inhabitants stand in doorways and watch it, arms crossed, expressionless, not with reverence but with assessment. Is the street draining? Is the wall weeping faster than usual? After, they all come out again and the ticking starts — water off leaves, off eaves, off power lines — and the evening shift of snails and ants resumes.

The children are the most visibly *of* this place. They know the town the way feral cats know a pipe-rack, which is to say three-dimensionally. While adults keep to the maintained corridors — door, stair, alley, roof-hatch — the children use the rest. You will see a boy disappear into what you thought was solid thatch on a wall and reappear two windows over, having used a fig-root ladder only he can see. Girls harvest air potatoes where they dangle from wires. They know which philodendron leaves will hold water to drink and which will burn your mouth. They are not wild; they are apprenticed early, given a small knife at six or seven and a section of wall that is *theirs* to keep. You can tell a well-kept child-wall by the neatness of its cut: not bald, but trimmed to a dense, even pile, like a crew cut.

The animals that live here live by permission of this human-plant pact.

There are the mosquitoes, clouds of them at dusk, bred endlessly in the taro ditches no matter how often the sluice flushes. The people do not pretend to control them; they burn coils of dried sedge, they sleep under nets that are themselves quickly overgrown and must be shaken free each morning, and they bear the bites as tax.

There are the ants, in highways an inch wide along every mortar line, farming scale on the kudzu. No one poisons them because they prune. The cats — thin, brindled, with tails like whips — hunt them half-heartedly and are tolerated because they use the pipe-racks as highways the way children use the vines, and they keep the rats to a sustainable few.

The heron in the canal behind the dye works is not a pet, not a symbol. It is just the single heron the town will support. It stands for hours because there is nowhere else for a heron to stand where the fish still run warm out of the sluice. When it leaves, another will come in a season. No more than one.

And under and through all of it, the other inhabitant that is not one thing at all.

The mat itself. The thatch. The ten to twenty centimeters of soil-that-is-not-soil living on every north and east wall: moss and fern and fig and vine root felted together into a blanket that holds the brick from washing and holds the heat from entering. You could argue it is not alive the way the people are alive, but here it behaves like a population. It grows, it competes, it is cut back, it heals. It sweats. On cool mornings you can see the heat differential where the pipes run behind it — a bright green line in a sea of duller green where the waste heat pushes growth twice as fast. The people talk about it the way farmers talk about weather, not with love but with negotiated respect. *That wall is hungry this year. That roof wants willows no matter what we do.*

They never say the town is theirs. If you ask a sluice-keeper who owns the mill now, he will shrug. The company that built it left papers in a drawer somewhere that have composted. Ownership here has reverted to something older and more literal: whoever keeps the water moving and the vine cut owns that stretch for as long as they keep it. When they die, a cousin or a neighbor's boy takes the crank. There are no fences left that are not also trellises. No doors that lock for more than politeness.

At night the bowl holds its warmth and its thrum. The sodium yard lights that never go out make the mist over the canals glow amber. The generators buzz behind walls of jasmine. And from every alley you hear the soft *shink, shink* of someone — always someone, in shifts — trimming back the edge of the green just enough to keep the brick breathing underneath. Not at war with it. Never at war. The sound is too patient for war. It is the sound of cohabitation, of a town that discovered the only way to survive drowning in a plant was to become the thing that gardens it forever.
