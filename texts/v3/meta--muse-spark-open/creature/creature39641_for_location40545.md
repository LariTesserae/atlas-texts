On the High Fen, nothing lives *on* the land. Everything lives *in* the act of keeping it from dying.

This should be a desert of cold stone. At two kilometers up, on a peneplain that old, with that temperature, you should get wind-scoured fellfield: lichen, stone, perhaps a stunted willow with its branches pressed flat to the ground like a frightened hand. Barren by physics.

Instead it is soaked, loud, and industrious, because life here solved a different problem than survival. It solved drainage.

### The Foundation That Is Alive

First, the continent that you walk on.

**The Mat** is the true resident of the plateau. It is not ground cover. It *is* the ground.

There is no soil. The basalt-gneiss beneath was planed down epochs ago until it became a single, gently undulating slab, polished and impervious. Water cannot soak in, so the Mat learned to be soil for itself.

It is a meter to a meter-and-a-half thick, a superorganismal felt of sphagnum, peat-moss, liverwort, black-green *Cryobryum*, and a dozen unnamed fungal-plant chimeras, growing over and through and into itself. The bottom layers are dead and compressed into peat; the middle layers are alive and half-drowned; the top is vivid and dripping. Everywhere it is supersaturated. Step down and your boot sinks three centimeters, and brown, iron-tasting water wells up around the laces and does not drain away for a long time. Step away and the Mat slowly exhales and rebounds.

It breathes. Literally. The whole plateau's peat skin moves in ultra-slow peristalsis, a ripple that takes an hour to cross a mere, driven by water pressure and gas from decomposition. You feel it through your soles before you see it.

It filters. Cold seep — water sweating directly out of the ancient stone from artesian pressure far below — pushes up through cracks in the peneplain. It is anoxic, heavy with dissolved basalt, ferrous iron, manganese, ancient salts. Unprocessed, it would be sterile. The Mat drinks it, strains it, oxygenates it with its own photosynthesis, and passes it on. Its rootless underside is a white tangle of rhizoids, like lung alveoli, hanging in the thin film between peat and stone.

It remembers. Cut a core and you get ten thousand years of pollen, ash, bacterial crust, all preserved in the cold. The Mat has been here since the plateau stopped being a mountain.

Nothing can live here without its permission, because there is nowhere else to stand.

### The Ones Who Hold Up the Sky

**The Crowntrees / Factory-Crowns** are not trees, though you will call them that because your brain needs a vertical word.

*Classification: Architect Megavegetal. Engineer without intent.*

There are not many — 0.6/3 density — and that is why they dominate. On a flat so vast and so empty, a single vertical object becomes geography. You might count four on the horizon at once, faded blue-grey in the mist, each alone in its own shallow watershed kilometers across.

Anatomy:

A trunk, 30-45 meters tall, 8-12 meters at the base, not wood but densely braided fibrous vascular columns, like a bundle of a thousand wet ropes fused together. They are not barked; they are banded. Layer after layer of living pressure-sheath grows, dies, sloughs off in great wet curtains that rot where they fall and are instantly colonized by the Mat. You can put your hand on a trunk and feel heat through it — not much, 15-20°C above ambient — and a deep, locomotive thud. *THUMP... THUMP... HISS.* They pulse. Each thud is a peristaltic contraction that forces seep-water, already pre-filtered by the Mat, upward at enormous pressure.

Inside is chemistry, not fire. Cavities lined with magnetite-rich parenchyma flash-boil the water by dropping pressure, precipitating minerals. You hear the churning, the clank of mineral scale breaking free inside a living pipe. Warm steam vents in rhythmic sighs from lenticels all up the trunk, condensing immediately on the aerial root skirt.

At the top, the trunk splays into a shallow bowl — a frond-rosette 15-20 meters across, thick, waxy, slightly concave, like a satellite dish made of living leather. This is the Crown proper. It does three things: catches falling cloud-water, acts as a photosynthetic array in dim light (1.4/3), and generates the electrostatic field. On overcast days you see it crawling: St. Elmo's fire, blue-white, lacing across the wet rim, snapping between root-tips with sharp ozone pops. That charge is how it forces mineral separation.

Every 7-9 minutes, it fires.

The entire bowl convulses inward. With a *WHUFF* you feel in your chest, it ejects its entire volume — 8 to 12 metric tons of processed water — 20 meters straight up. For an instant it is a perfect inverted umbrella, a column of nearly boiling, hyper-oxygenated, iron-and-ozone-laced water and steam. Then it crashes down as scalding rain.

Around each Crown is the proof: the Scald Ring. A perfect circle ~40 meters in diameter where the Mat has been killed back and replaced by a crackling crust of thermophilic, rust-red chemoautotrophic bacteria — *Ferriphagea coronae* — and iron-manganese scale. It steams in the cold air. It hisses under the hot rain. Nothing soft can grow there. It smells like a bloodied hot spring and a circuit board.

The Crowns are factories that make weather. They take cold, mineral-dead seep and return hot, mineral-fertile rain and new cloud. The plateau's perpetual overcast is half-natural, half-exhaled. They are loud — 1.6/3 — and their thudding carries for kilometers over water, so the whole fen is a factory floor: *THUMP... THUMP... HISS...* out of sync, then briefly falling into sync, then fracturing again.

They are not rooted. Not really.

They stand *on* the Mat, their base a broad, splayed holdfast that crushes the peat down to stone. The tangled white rhizoids beneath intertwine but do not bind. That is why, when the weirdness peaks, you can swear they have moved a hundred meters when you looked away. The peat shifts. The Crowns drift.

And now — as the cloud deepens — the oldest ones have stopped drifting. Their aerial root skirts, those thirty-meter curtains of black, hair-thick dripping roots that have always swayed meters above the Mat, are lowering. You watch one near you, sighing steam, and see its lowest root-hairs brush the peat.

And grip.

### The Herds That Tend the Machines

**The Shroud-Grazers** are the only large animals that belong here.

*Classification: Megafaunal Filter-Grazer. Maintenance fauna.*

Do not picture elk. Picture what would happen if an elk was designed by someone who had only ever seen a manta ray and a water tower.

Size: 400-600 kg, low-slung, barrel-chested, almost neckless. Six legs — not jointed like a mammal's, but stilt-like, multi-jointed chitinous pillars that end in broad, splayed pads that punch through peat until they find stone. They need the stone. Without it they'd sink.

Color: There is almost none. Their skin is translucent, thick, gelatinous, tinged grey-violet like cold fat. You can see the dark river of their gut running front to back, and the huge, slow four-chambered heart pulsing in their chest. Their blood is copper-blue and you can see it. On their backs, they carry a living mantle — the same rust-red bacterial felt that grows in the Scald Rings, but thicker, lush, almost fur-like. When it is healthy it fizzes faintly. When it is cold it dulls.

Heads: They have no head. The front of the body is simply a vast, horizontal mouth — a filter maw 1.5 meters wide, lined with baleen-like keratin plates. They walk with it barely above the water, vacuuming. Mere water, liquid peat, bacteria, dead moss, mineral silt — everything goes in. Inside that mouth is a radula-like tongue that sorts, a gizzard that grinds peat to pulp, and a complex fore-stomach where symbiotic bacteria do the same mineral processing the Crowns do, but slower. Behind them they leave a plume of fine, warm, pale-grey silt — processed waste — that billows in the shallow water and smells intensely of iron and wet paper. That silt *is* soil for the Mat. Without the Grazers, the Mat would choke.

Behavior: For most of the day they are solitary, wading for kilometers through ankle-deep mirror-meres, silent except for the soft *sluice* of their mouth and the *clonk* of their stilt feet on stone beneath peat. Abundance is 2.4/3 — there are many of them — but the plateau is so huge they seem sparse. A lone translucent back in the mist.

Until a Crown prepares to fire.

They *know*. Perhaps they feel the pressure change, the static buildup, the deepening thud. From kilometers away, they turn and converge. Ten, twenty, thirty of them crowd shoulder-to-shoulder just outside the Scald Ring, steaming, shivering despite their size, their back-bacteria dimming in the cold.

Then the *WHUFF*.

The column goes up, crashes down. The Grazers tilt. They push slightly *into* the scald, letting the nearly boiling, mineral-rich rain hammer their backs. The effect is immediate and violent: the bacterial felt blooms, fizzes, turns bright rust-red, releases spores in a visible puff. The Grazer's translucent skin pinks as its blood surges. They shudder, not in pain but in something like ecstasy, a whole-body tremor. For a few minutes after, they are incandescent with heat, steaming giants in the cold, and the bacteria give off a burst of oxygen and sugar that feeds directly back through their skin.

They are husbandry animals with no husband. The Crowns need the Grazers to keep the Mat from overgrowing and sealing the seep vents. The Grazers need the Crowns' hot rain to culture the bacteria that keep them warm in 0.5/3 cold and that provide half their caloric intake. It is industry: factory and worker, evolved together with no designer.

Young are not born. They bud. Once a year, a well-heated Grazer will swell on one flank and extrude a translucent, calf-sized polyp that drops into the mire, already filter-mouthing, already carrying a dab of its parent's bacterial felt. Most are eaten — not by predators, there are none — but by the Mat itself, which overgrows the weak ones.

### The Real Inhabitants of High Weirdness

And then there is what lives in the malfunction.

The plateau is turbulent (2.8/3) and strange (2.7/3) because an ancient, perfectly flat, perfectly wet, perfectly electrified factory-floor should not exist at altitude. Reality is thin here, worn by wind and age.

So:

**The Flicker-Herds.** Sometimes the Grazers glitch. You watch a group of twelve jostling at a Scald Ring, and then, between blinks, only their legs are there — twelve sets of stilt legs walking in perfect formation with nothing on top, still filtering water with absent mouths. A second later the legs are gone and just the translucent barrels hover, mouths working the air. They don't seem to notice. The rain that falls on them during a flicker falls *through* them and hits the peat hissing.

**The Hanging Water.** A Crown fires, and the column does not fall. It hangs, a glassy cylinder of churning hot water twenty meters tall, perfectly still, reflecting the dim sky. For twenty, thirty seconds it just hangs there while steam curls *downward* off it. Then it decides to fall — sideways, slowly, like syrup poured in low gravity, toward a mere that wasn't there before.

**The Draining Mere.** Perfectly flat water, a foot deep, stretching a hundred meters — then, without sound, a perfect circular hole opens in it, perhaps a meter across, draining straight down. For a few seconds you stare down thirty meters through crystal clear, impossible water onto dry, polished basalt that reflects no light — stone that should be covered in peat — before the water slams back with a concussive *thump*, leaving no hole.

**The Schematics.** The Saint Elmo's fire on the Crown fronds does not always wander. On high-turbulence days it *draws*. Thin blue lines race between root tips, forming intricate, branching diagrams: pressure vessels, turbines, lattices that look almost like circuit boards or chemical plants, but subtly wrong — pipes that loop into themselves, dimensions labeled in no math you know. They hold for a few seconds, bright and crackling, then dissolve into static. The next Crown over might be drawing a different half of the same machine.

The Mat records these too. If you peel back the peat after a storm, the iron scale beneath sometimes holds a faint, perfect imprint of those schematics, as if the plateau itself tried to build them.

None of this is alive, exactly. But it behaves like inhabitants — patterns that persist, migrate between Crowns, seem to watch.

You stand now as the light finally begins to truly dim, not because the sun is setting behind the permanent cloud, but because the Crowns are making more cloud than the sky can hold. The air fills with fine, warm drizzle that was inside a plant ten minutes ago. The thudding has slowed, deepened, synchronized across the whole visible fen into a single planetary heartbeat: *THUMP... THUMP... THUMP.*

The nearest Crown, the one whose root-beard brushed the peat, has taken hold. The Mat around its base is rising, bulging, climbing up the root-curtain as if being pulled. The water around your boots, which had been ankle-deep and cold, is suddenly warm.

All across the plateau, the other Crowns are doing the same.

The factory floor is putting down anchors.
