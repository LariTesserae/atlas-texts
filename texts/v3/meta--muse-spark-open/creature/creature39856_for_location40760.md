It doesn't empty when the shift ends. That is the first thing to understand about who lives here.

Up on the plain, habitation is scattered the way the map says it is - a farmhouse here, a depot there, miles of wind between them. Down in the cut, habitation follows different rules. Nothing lives here singly. Everything that lives here lives *crowded*.

Start at the bottom, because everything drains to the bottom.

In the lake itself — that warm, rust-colored, four-foot-deep accident of a sea — the water is not water. Look closer. Hold your hand over the side of a pontoon and let the silt settle for a second. What resolves is not clear water but *meat*. Mosquitofish so dense they make a moving carpet, each one the length of your thumbnail, each one pregnant all year long because the water never gets cold enough to tell them to stop. In the deeper trenches where the bucket left a gouge, carp turn slowly, fat as pigs from eating the other fish's young, their backs crusted with leeches that wave like wheat. This fish was stocked by a man in a state truck ten years ago who tipped a bucket and left. No one stocks them now. They stock themselves.

And beneath them, what really lives here: the filter. Scoop up a handful of that bottom silt and it comes alive on its own, without needing animals at all. Bloodworms by the thousand, chironomid larvae red as paprika from living without oxygen. Snail shells the size of BBs spiraling in the thousands over every submerged surface. Water boatmen rowing frantically upside-down. A bacterial mat so thick that when a trackhoe drags it, it peels up in a sheet like skin, green-black on top where the algae photosynthesizes in that dim pearl light, anoxic black underneath where it digests itself. It smells like hot iron and rotten eggs, and it is the reason anything else can live here at all. It makes food out of light and diesel.

Up one inch, to the surface film, there are the striders. Water striders by the hundreds of thousands skating between duckweed islands, and whirligig beetles packing every sunny slick in spinning, colliding rafts. When the air goes still at noon, the lake breathes out damselflies — not in the polite numbers you know from creeks, but in hatches, in *events*. Blue-tailed damsels and big green darners rising in columns until the lamps on the cranes are haloed with them. They live for a week. For that week they do not leave the water.

Up one more inch, into air. You already heard them before you saw anything else. You thought it was the pumps.

The frogs never stopped. No winter kills them here; the earth keeps the water at 78 degrees whether it's January or July. So the bullfrogs sit half-submerged in the borrow pits, throats ballooning, eyes just above the algae-filmed waterline. They're not wary. There's nowhere for a coyote to hide to eat them and too much noise to hunt by sound anyway, so they've grown enormous and stupid and loud. Eighteen months old, two pounds, sitting within arm's reach of each other because the territorial call has been drowned out by generators for so many generations they've forgotten how far apart to be. Between them, tucked into cattail stems, the smaller chorus frogs — Baja tree frogs, western toads that shouldn't tolerate brackish seepage but do — cling with their toes and scream for mates day and night. It is not a chorus. A chorus has pauses. This is a wall.

If you wade in, which the levee crews do in rubber bibs, you realize the mud is frogspawn. Every boot print fills with jelly and black dots before it can fill with water.

And wherever there are that many frogs and that many flies, the rest comes whether you invite it or not.

The middle kingdom belongs to the birds who learned that this hole in the desert does not migrate.

You will not see them at first because there are too many to count as individuals. The phragmites beds — those same planted-looking clumps you thought were scattered — are *full*. Rails slip through them, little soot-colored chickens that almost never fly, nesting with such faith in permanent water that you can lift a frond and find three nests in one square yard, eggs speckled like wet concrete. Red-winged blackbirds claim every reed tip that sticks up; the males sing without stopping because the female can’t hear him twenty feet away over the pumps, so he sings again. Louder. More. At dawn the whole mosaic of dikes shivers with them taking off to feed, and then resettles minutes later because there is nowhere else to go that’s better. 

Above them, the waders: great blue herons standing statue-still on half-sunk pump housings, snowies, cattle egrets following the crews around for the insects their boots kick up, night-herons hunched under catwalks. They nest on the only trees — the stunted tamarisks — but also on the cranes, and on the power pylons, and on the handrails of abandoned sluices. Their droppings have painted every high thing white, and under their rookeries the water is scummed with fallen chicks, failed eggs, feathers, all being worked over by turtles.

The turtles deserve a separate census. They came as pets, some of them, released by workers’ kids into “the pond.” Now every piece of styrofoam, every sun-warmed sheet of black plastic liner, every back of a drowned haul tire that breaks the surface carries a stack. Red-eared sliders in their hundreds, pancaked one atop another in a way that looks companionable until one slips and the whole pile shuffles for dominance. At night they drop into the water with soft plops like fruit.

And above the birds, feeding until the light fails completely, the air itself is occupied. You called it midges. Up close they are each distinct — biting gnats, non-biting chironomids, alkali flies with wings the color of salt — but distance turns them into weather. Columns of them rise at 6 p.m. every night regardless of season, because the light cue is the only calendar that works down here. They show as smoke, then as static, then as a solid thing. You breathe them if you talk. And in that smoke, the aerialists: swallows and swifts in such number that the sky flickers, nighthawks, bats that spill out of expansion joints in the upper walls at civil twilight, dragonflies big as your hand patrolling in shifts. Nothing native migrates through here anymore; these residents stay year-round, fed by the hatchery below that never closes.

That is the animal city. Three million warm bodies of various sizes breathing out carbon dioxide that the inversion holds down with them to keep them warmest.

Then there are the ones who pay rent.

The people down here do not live down here, not officially. No one lists an address as Tier -90, Sump 4. But their occupation is so total it amounts to residence. There are the pump men, who have spent so long beside their particular coughing Briggs & Stratton-on-stilts that they can diagnose bearing wear by a change in pitch you can't even hear until you've been here six hours. They sleep in prefab trailers six to a room, eat microwave meals on overturned buckets, grow salt sores on their ankles from wading and pick up fungal infections in their boot lines that never clear. They know the turtles by shell deformity. They talk to the herons.

There are the equipment operators on the terraces above, sealed in cabs the size of small apartments, running AC hard against the greenhouse heat, looking down every day on a lake they will never touch. To them the wildlife is hazard footage on the safety monitor — bird-strike on windshield, frog migration across haul road. Yet they transport seeds in their treads, algae in their undercarriages, spreading that black-green slick to every new cut before the water even gets there.

There are the maintenance crews in high-vis orange, the only spot of saturated color for miles. In the pearly dimness their vests seem to glow on their own, like deep-sea creatures. They move in gangs of five, shouting over the *chud-chud* and the frog-trill, repairing fabric liners while egrets watch from the spoil piles like supervisors, while the silt tries to swallow their shovels between strokes.

None of them belong here biologically — lungs want dry, cool air, ears want quiet, feet want solid ground — and yet they acclimate precisely the way the rails did. After two weeks they stop hearing the noise; after two months they stop noticing the smell of warm oil-mud; after two years their houses up on the rim feel too windy, too bright, too empty. Some retired men come back down just to fish for carp nobody should eat, standing on pipelines at dusk, trading hand-rolled cigarettes for stories while swallows brush their hard hats.

The machines, you could argue, live here too.

No one ever removes a dead pump. To hoist one off a dike requires a crane that is busy elsewhere. So the seep claims it. It slumps into the silt, its intake clogged, its hammer silenced, and instantly becomes real estate. Within a week: algal beard, snail colonies, a turtle basking deck, algae-green dragonfly eggs glued to its rust. Within a month: cattails rooting through its chain-link cage, blackbirds weaving nesting material through its conduit. The cut is littered with thirty years of these iron corpses in various stages of being eaten back into marsh. The largest — a dragline left when the seam played out early — looms over the eastern settling ponds on legs gone knee-deep into mire, its boom lowered like a neck, swallows nesting by the hundred in its cab and gulls whitewashing its flank. From a distance you mistake it for geology.

It is, now.

And that is who lives here: an unplanned estuary that forgot it was supposed to die. The base layer of worm and bacterium that eats light and iron, the fish that eats the base, the frog that eats the fishfly, the bird that eats the frog egg, the bat that eats the midge that hatched from the bacterium, the man who watches the gauge that tells him if the water — which feeds all of it — is winning. Each tier borrowing warmth from the tier below, borrowing stillness from the walls that hold the wind off them, borrowing time.

They do not thrive fast. As you noticed, nothing does. But they *press*. Budge up against one another shoulder to shoulder in the warm, buzzing, splashing dimness, making more of themselves at a rate exactly balanced by the amount of space that leaks back from the rock each night. If you stand long enough with water lapping at your boot-tops, the roar smoothing into a hum until you feel it in your sternum more than your ears, you'll realize it's not really a chorus of many different sounds at all. It's one continuous, low-grade act of living being undertaken by perhaps ten million throats, gills, and motors at once, slightly out of sync, never resolving, never stopping, because the cut is warm and wet and below the world, and nothing that finds its way down here ever finds a good enough reason to leave.
