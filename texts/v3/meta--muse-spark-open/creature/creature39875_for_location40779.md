What lives here does not arrive. It accrues.

At first you think — nothing does. Or nothing should. You have been told there are no birds. No insects stitching the air over the canals, no rats threading the walls. In a place so wet and so green, the absence is wrong, like a held breath. You listen for the small skittering that should be eating the fig that ate the loading dock, for the buzz that should be pruning the waist-high grass on the roofs, and you hear only drip, and hiss, and the thump every eight seconds.

So you look harder, and you realize the bowl did not stay empty. It filled the vacancy with other kinds of life. With things that learned to live without being eaten.

The first of them is the green itself — but not as decoration, not as weed. As citizen.

Here, the plants are not growing *on* the town. They are growing *as* the town. The moss that is two inches thick on every north wall is not a coating; it is upholstery, and it holds the brick together with wet fingers. The glossy vine that follows steam pipes and learns to avoid insulators is not climbing for light — there is hardly any real light down here — it is climbing for warmth, for voltage, for the slow sigh of heat that never stops. It tests current with blackened tips and remembers.

The ferns in the smokestack soot, the forearm-sized ones, have roots that drink sulfur. The elephant grass on the rooftops shivers not in wind — there is no wind in the bowl, only settle — but when the press stamps three floors below. The roots of the dye-works fig didn't break the I-beam; they *incorporated* it. Bark swallowed rust and made a new alloy, harder than either.

Nothing here is eaten, so everything here grows until it learns a trade. Leaves harden to lacquer strong enough to hold a child's plank. Lotus pads grow a foot across and still. Water hyacinth knits itself so tight over the warm canals that it becomes floor, lawn, bridge — you could lose a wrench in it and never hear it hit bottom because there is no bottom to hit, only more root, more tangle. The people cut it back from intake vents with machetes not because it is invading, but because if they don't, it will do the job too well. It will seal the vent. It will *become* the vent.

The second thing that lives here is the water — but not as weather. As circulatory system.

You were told water is a condition, not a river. That's true, and conditions can be alive. It seeps in through the brick from the marsh outside on a slow inhale you can hear between shifts, is lifted by patient pumps to the roof tanks, beads on every underside of every pipe, runs down every vertical in bright threads, gathers in gutters and cisterns and canals and boiler bellies and sweats out again as mist. It never leaves quickly. Nothing leaves quickly.

It clings too long on ceilings, hanging heavy and glassy before it falls straight down through steam without swaying. It stains the lowest course of brick black-green and does not wash it away. It is warm, opaque with silt and iron, reflecting almost nothing, and in the old cooling pond it holds lotus leaves upright like plates.

The people do not drink it raw. They distill it with the waste heat of the dynamo house, which is also the greenhouse now. They tend it the way you tend blood pressure — bleeding a valve here, adding a gutter there. If you stand on that iron bridge over the hyacinth lawn long enough, you feel the level change a finger's width under you. Inhale. Exhale. The bowl breathing through its raft of brick.

The third thing that lives here is the machine — but not as tool. As elder fauna.

There is nothing sleek here. No turbines whirring too fast to see. There are big, slow engines behind brick arches, belt-drives the width of your hand turning and turning, beam engines thumping so deep you feel it in your molars. A flywheel twenty feet across turning once every eight seconds, weeping warm oil into a catch pan where a fern has cheerfully taken root in the oil. Transformers buzzing behind chain-link gone velvet with rust. Chain drives groaning over sprockets worn to soft teeth.

They never really stop. Between shifts the big hammers pause and you can hear the smaller things: metal expanding, water boiling somewhere you can't see, pressure ticking out of a hundred rivets. They are not loud in a sharp way. They are a held chord, too low and too many parts to locate. It comes from the density itself.

And because nothing here collapses — rust forms a skin and then holds, paint peels to a point and stays, a wall that should have fallen a century ago is still standing because root and mineral crust have made a new mortar — the machines have aged past ruination into a kind of health. They are tended constantly. Oiled by grandfathers and grandsons using the same can. Their wear is burnished, not broken. The foundry, the press hall, the wire mill, the waterworks — they share walls, share pipes, share roofs. Corrugated iron leans on handmade brick leans on tar-patched concrete and the whole lattice corrects its height every generation by adding one more shim, one more walkway between two windows that were never meant to connect.

The machines are not used up. They are inhabited.

Which brings you to the fourth thing — the only thing in here that knows it is alive the way you mean alive.

The people.

They are dense. Shoulder-to-shoulder dense, because the bottom of the bowl is finite and the town decided to become a city without sprawling. Habitation blocks stacked directly above assembly floors, so a man eats supper over the thump of the press that will be his workplace at dawn. Tenement windows three stories up glowing amber through steam, each sill crowded with potted taro and chipped enamel basins catching the rain that isn't rain, just condensation made heavy enough to fall. Laundry lines and power lines and steam lines making a second, lower sky you have to duck under.

They are not pale from darkness, not quite. They are glazed. Skin damp all day, hair never fully dry, eyes practiced at seeing by sheen — off wet brick, off canal, off leaf. They move slowly, not from lethargy but from agreement. You cannot hurry in a place where water arrives as seep, where decay is patient, where even a new crack widens one mineral grain at a time. Children lay planks across hard lacquer lotus and cross the cooling pond because that is faster than going around, but they do it without running. They have learned the vine's lesson: test the current first with a fingertip, then commit.

They speak low, because you don't need to shout over a deep sound; you let it carry you. They are all maintenance. No one here is just a worker or just a resident or just a gardener. The woman who runs the dynamo house also trains the rubber-leafed vine up its trellis to shade the vats. The man who peels moss off a flywheel with a putty knife will, that evening, wring the same moss out over his taro pots. The kids who cut back elephant grass from a rooftop meadow know which whistle means the boiler is sighing and which means it is warning.

They came down here — or their great-grandparents did — and stayed, because the bowl does not let you leave quickly. You can leave. There is no wall. Just the soft, forested shoulders, the complete ring of low hills. But the seep is slow and so is departure. After a few generations, no one feels the rim as a boundary to cross. They feel it as a ceiling that holds the haze in, that keeps the amber light close.

If you ask them what city this is, they'll give you a name. It will sound like any town in warm country. If you ask them how big it is, they'll gesture around them — from that dye-works courtyard to that cistern under that boiler-house — and mean: *this big*. The whole world is this raft, this lattice, this breath. A single room, complex as a cathedral, busy as a capital, wet as a mouth, that forgot it was supposed to be small.

Stand there long enough on the iron bridge and you stop trying to separate them. The fig-tree-beam, the oil-fern, the vine-conduit, the man with the machete, the thump under your boots, the heavy drop of water falling straight through steam from a ceiling that has been sweating since before you were born.

Nothing new. Nothing ruined. Just maintenance, without end.

Somewhere to your left, a press stamps. Somewhere to your right, water finds a new crack and, very slowly, begins to make it a door.
