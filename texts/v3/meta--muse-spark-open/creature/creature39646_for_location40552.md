They do not live here. They are *kept* here.

That is the first thing you have to understand about anything that lives in the High Water. Nothing belongs. Nothing evolved to fit this. This ocean is too young — geologically newborn, minutes old by planetary time — and too perfect. The rock is still sharp from the snap, the water is still impossible, the Mills are still humming their first note. Evolution has not had time to make something for this place.

So what lives here was made *with* it. Or moved in after, quickly, because it could tolerate forever.

Listen. You'll learn to hear them in layers, under the Edge-thunder.

### I. The Stewards

You will never see them. That's deliberate.

Before you see an animal, you see a Steward's work. Every Mill is a nursery, a forge, and a blind god.

Up close, a Mill is not a building. It is a colony. The concrete-and-iron you saw from a distance resolves, within a kilometer, into motion. The walls breathe. Not metaphorically — they contract, once every ninety seconds, pumping. The tower shivers.

What lives there is:

**The Skin.** A film of black-green chemosynthetic bacteria, engineered, or bred, or simply encouraged — meters thick in places — that coats the hot parts of every Mill. It eats iron and sulfur and the waste-heat itself, and in return it makes the only real food in this ocean. Sloughed off in pale snowflakes by the current, it feeds everything else. Without it, the water would be sterile glass. You can taste it if you flood your suit vents near a vent plume: metallic, bitter, alive.

**The Crabs.** The service drones. Each Mill births them by the hundreds. They are not smart. They cannot speak. They are the size of dogs and refrigerators and small cars, all elbows and welders and pitted ceramic. They crawl endless circuits over sharp pillow basalt that cuts their feet to nothing — they do not heal, they replace. You will see one in the far distance, alone on the plain, picking its way with agonizing slowness toward a rock that needs no tending, and understand: this is what a prayer looks like when it has limbs. They do not rest. They do not defend themselves. If a Benthos steps on one, it keeps welding as it is crushed.

**The Mind in the Caisson.** There is no one inside the Mill. There is also *something* inside the Mill. A low, distributed intelligence — not AI in the human sense, not a voice — more like a thermostat crossed with a shepherd. It watches temperature, salinity, current shear, bacterial load. When a diver stays too long, it sends a warm plume to push them away. When a manta dies and snags on its intake, it delicately reverses flow until the body drifts free. It never speaks. It never sleeps. It has kept 3.8°C for longer than you have been alive by adjusting a heater by a thousandth of a degree.

It does not live here. It *tends* here. And it will tend long after your bones have become one more white scaffold on the black glass.

### II. The Commuters

Because the Table is so vast and so empty, anything that lives here must be built for distance. Not speed — endurance. The animals of the High Water are not fish. Fish dart. These things *commute*.

You see them first as flaws in your depth perception. A speck, impossibly far, hangs motionless for hours. Then with one beat it is beside you, and you realize it was never small.

**The Cathedrals.** The six-winged mantas. They are the dominant thing. Adults run 40 to 80 meters across, albinos with translucent skin through which you can see their slow, cold blood and the faint glow of bacterial colonies in their guts. They have no eyes — or rather, their entire ventral surface is a light-sensitive membrane that sees the Mills as dim orange lighthouses. They flap once every twenty seconds. Each downstroke pushes a volume of water the size of a house. They don't hunt. They strain — great baleen plates of keratin combing bacteria-snow from water. They fly in long, silent V-formations of six or seven, a hundred kilometers apart from the next formation, taking weeks to cross from Mill to Mill. When they pass over you, the water pressure changes. You feel devout, despite yourself.

Their young cling to their backs like pale remoras, dozens of them, until the current near a Mill rips them free and they must make their own way. Most don't. It doesn't matter. The Mantas do not mourn. They whistle — a low, organ-note moan that carries for kilometers — and keep beating.

**The Knives.** Where the Mantas are contemplative, the knife-fish are frantic. Three meters of muscle and cartilage and whisker, eyeless, all lateral line and sonar. They travel in packs of thirty to fifty, always moving, always clicking. *Click-click-CHIRP.* That chirp is the only sound in the High Water that is sharper than the Edge. They eat the bacterial snow too, but they also eat each other, and the dead, and the Crabs when they can catch them. They are the hyenas of this desert — nervous, intelligent, too aware of the void. Divers say if you float still, a pack will orbit you for an hour, clicking, trying to decide if you are warm enough to be food or cold enough to be rock. They are the only thing that seems to feel the loneliness. At night — when the high sun is occluded by the planet's own shadow and the columns of light go out — the Knives sing louder, as if to reassure themselves there is still something else.

**The Benthos.** And then there are the things that refuse to swim.

On the sharp plain, walking, you find them. From far off they look like wreckage. Up close they are worse. Eight, twelve, sixteen limbs, each as thick as a tree trunk, jointed in wrong places, tipped with chitinous picks that *crack* the basalt with every step. Bodies like overturned oil rigs, white and barnacled, featureless except for a ventral mouth that scrapes rust. They are grazers, but what they graze is the chemical bloom itself: iron oxides precipitated where hot Mill-water kisses 3.8°C ocean, manganese nodules forming in real time on fresh glass. They eat *rust*, and in turn their droppings — fine, iron-rich silt — are the only sand this ocean will have in a million years. You can follow a Benthos for days by its trail of faint red dust, the only softness on the Table.

They do not swim. They do not need to. They walk for years in a straight line, until they encounter another Benthos, exchange packets of genetic material through a brief, awful interlocking of limbs that looks like fighting, and continue. If one falls in an Edge-current, it simply folds its legs and lets go, tumbling outward like a thrown temple. You will find its white, upright corpse months later, standing as a landmark where no landmark should be, until the Crabs dismantle it for calcium.

### III. The Ones Who Fall

Nothing lives at the Edge permanently. The pull is too strong, the sound too loud — it liquefies thought. But some things live *by* falling.

**The Ribbons.** Fifty kilometers long and no thicker than your arm, translucent, bioluminescent only at the very tip. They anchor — somehow — to the basalt right at the lip, where the water begins to accelerate, and let their bodies stream out over the abyss like pennants in a hurricane. Their mouths are at the free end, drinking the storm. What they eat, no one knows; perhaps the compressed plankton of the cyclones below, thrown back up as spray. When a Ribbon breaks, it does not die at once; it drifts back inward at great speed, coiling and uncoiling, flashing distress in pure white light that every Knife for a hundred kilometers can see.

**The Divers.** Human, or once-human. You will meet them if you work the rim.

They wear bone-dampeners and thruster harnesses and mirrored helmets that have long since gone blind from the roar. They are contract crews, or penitents, or cultists — the Table collects all types at its drain. Their job is to service the Edge Mills, the ones that hammer and stir hardest to keep the cataract even. The sound out there is so total it becomes a second gravity; you feel it in your teeth, your marrow. After a month, Divers stop dreaming in words. After six, they can stand in the current without tether, leaning into it like wind, eyes closed, listening. Some walk over deliberately. Not suicide — pilgrimage. Their suits log them as "lost to outflow." The Mills do not search. The Mantas do not follow. The water just goes, and they go with it, down ten kilometers into cloud, where satellites watch storms the size of continents spiral forever.

No one fishes them out. No one can. The fall is the only honest thing about this place: it *leaves*.

### IV. The After-Image

And finally, what lives here most abundantly: us. Or the idea of us.

No one lives *on* the Table. There are no cities, no farms, no children born here. Habitats are tethered beside Mills for weeks at a time, then towed away when frost starts to form on the viewports — because your body heat, your waste, your breath, your noise, is a perturbation the Stewards must correct. Stay too long and the Mill spins faster to compensate for you, and somewhere two hundred kilometers away a Manta alters course because its snow has thinned.

Humans in the High Water are therefore ghosts: researchers in quiet submarines who speak in whispers because the water carries everything, maintenance mystics who believe the Mills are praying, thrill-seekers who free-dive the clarity until their lungs ache from cold and beauty.

We do not live here. We *haunt* here. We come to look at an ocean that was decided upon — an ocean held young on purpose — and to feel, for a little while, what it is to be a temporary thing in a place that has been forbidden to change.

And when we leave, which we always do, the Crabs smooth over the scrape-marks of our anchors, the Mantas resume their beats, the thunder from the Edge fills in the hole our thrusters made in the sound, and the water stays 3.8°C, clear as glass, normal light falling forever through nothing onto sharp, young, black rock.

As if we were never there at all.
