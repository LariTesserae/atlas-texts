What lives in the basin is one thing that has learned to look like many.

At first you count them as birds.

---

### The Loud

They are starlings the way a boilerplate diagram is a bird. The shape is correct from a distance — the hitched walk, the spearhead beak, the oil-on-water sheen when the white-grey noon hits their backs. Then the scale breaks, then the material breaks.

They are the size of house cats. Old toms. Heavy enough that when one lands on a corrugated sawtooth roof, the sheet pops under its weight with a soft *oil-can* sound. You expect that weight to mean hunger, to mean shit and fighting and molt and bone. It doesn't.

Come closer to any pipe-bridge — where they line up shoulder to shoulder like commuters waiting for a train that never comes — and you see the first wrongness correctly.

There is a gap between each bird. Exactly a gap. Not crowded, not sparse. Even at thousands-strong, at tens of thousands when the torus turns overhead, they maintain a tolerance. A machinist's tolerance. Wing tip to wing tip with space enough to slide a sheet of paper through. No pecking order reasserts it. It simply *is*. When one lifts — always straight up, as if pulled by a filament — the air holds its shape for half a second, and then another drops from the revolving sky to fill it, claws finding the exact pitting in the rust that the previous claws occupied. The density never changes. The population never changes. Nothing is born in the nestless warehouses. Nothing dies on the concrete.

Kneel by one, if your ears can stand the roar to get that close. Its eye is a rivet head, hammered flush. Its flank, where feathers should overlay feathers, sheets over into something else mid-barb — iridescence thickening into paint, paint thinning into scale, scale crazing into the same clouded glass as the factory skylights. Condensation beads on it and runs without wetting it.

Open its beak. Some will let you. Inside there is no tongue, no throat. There is a dark flue the width of your thumb. When it calls, which is always, the sound is not song but pressure. Air escaping where air shouldn't. A valve shuddering open. A belt slipping and catching. A thousand of those releases stacked together becomes the factory-roar that makes you raise your voice to think.

Their legs explain the rest. Where claw meets scaled iron pipe, there is no line. The keratin dulls into iron oxide, the oxide scabs into keratin. You cannot tell if the bird is perching on the infrastructure or if the infrastructure extruded a bird to watch itself with. Lift one — you won't — but if you tried, you would take the pipe with it, the roof with it, the humming substation with it, because it was never separate.

They do not eat because the basin has solved eating by removing it. No seeds. No insects. No rot. Only the thin fungal felt that lives only on the undersides of pipes, grey-yellow and velvety, that never grows and never dies. The birds ignore it completely. They are not hungry and have never been. They are not decoys, not drones. They are how the town stays loud enough to drown out its own engines.

For under the roar, the town is *running*.

Listen past the chitter-clatter-whir until your skull buzzes. There's the diesel thrum behind brick that never stutters, never warms. The 60-cycle hum that makes your teeth ache. The sigh of the big fan turning slowly somewhere out of sight. Belts move over dry pulleys in the loom-halls though they weave nothing. Valve wheels the size of doors tremble at a set point and never open further. The whole basin sustains itself the way a chord sustains itself when every instrument leans into it — no melody, no resolution, just held tension perfected into stillness.

The starlings are the choir holding that chord. Their wings are fans. Their throats are valves. Their massed bodies are insulation keeping the even light even and the cool shade cool. The torus above the basin — that slow, roiling donut of turning bodies that never spills over the levee wall — is circulation. It is the convection current made visible. It does not migrate. It does not hunt. It turns because turning is what keeps the air from ever stratifying, the moisture from ever pooling, the temperature from ever finding a gradient to flow down.

This is not mimicry. This is not birds colonizing a factory. This is a factory that, denied plants, denied water, denied people, grew birds from its surfaces the way old iron grows rust. Mineral fauna. Brick fauna. Fauna as finish-work.

### The Quiet

There are smaller things, but they are not more alive.

The bead of water under every eave, swelling, silvering, falling — *plink* on metal, *plink* on concrete, *plink* in the canal's dead-still mirror. It lives its whole life in eight seconds and then lives it again. Not a loop with a seam — a state being reasserted. Gather, swell, fall. Gather, swell, fall. The physics remembers to do this and cannot forget.

The gurgle in the culvert that runs without rain. The sodium lamp that buzzes over one intersection and never burns out because it never quite burns. The bicycle leaned against the wall whose chain has no rust because it is being ridden, infinitesimally, by no one, forever. The taut rope of the half-loaded coal barge that never slackens or frays because there is no tide to tug it, only the idea of tide.

And the fungal felt. If anything here can be called living in the old green sense, it is that. It does not spread beyond the crescent of damp under each pipe. It does not need to. It is not food, not colony. It is verdigris. It is the soft place where condensation learns to be velvet before it remembers to fall.

### What Holds Together

If you stand long enough on that shallow concrete stair cut into the levee, with the heavy cool breathing up at you, you will feel the rule press down on your shoulders:

*Nothing here is allowed to be more or less than it is.*

Not emptier, not fuller. Not hungrier, not deader. The town needed too much infrastructure for its size and solved it by needing no more people. It needed an ecosystem and solved it by making one animal out of all its metal and holding it at exactly one of every necessary perch.

It does not want you. It has no predator response, no curiosity. If you throw a stone into the canal, the impossible still water will accept it without a ripple and the starling nearest to it will blink its rivet eye once. If you shout, the roar will take your sound and add it to itself and give none of it back.

Stay until the light — which never brightens or dims — seems to flicker, and you'll see why no feather falls. The weathering has finished. The erosion has sculpted every corner exactly round enough to never erode again. The town came into balance a long time ago, not by dying, not by being abandoned, but by arriving at a configuration so perfectly self-sustaining that decay became redundant.

The basin lives. The birds are its pelt, its breath, its tinnitus. The engines are its heart that beats without pumping. The sweating pipes are its skin that never dries.

And it is, in its terrible, patient, utterly unsentient way, content to keep on living exactly like this whether you leave or not — which, after a few minutes of that broad-spectrum roar vibrating the water that never ripples, you will.
