They do not live *on* the Roof.

They live *in* it.

### The Mat

First, what lives there is the Roof itself. Not stone, not anymore. The stone is a floor far below, a memory. What lives is the thatch.

Call it *Aeonbroma* if you need a name, though there are a hundred species braided into one habit. It is not a colony and not a single plant; it is a continent-scale clutch.

Each rosette is born in air. Its seed - feathered, cork-light - lodges in the damp axil of its mother and germinates without ever touching mineral. It sends out fleshy leaves the size of shields, then doors, then tables, each one guttered down its center with microscopic hairs that pull water uphill by capillary action. The leaf tip curls into a spout. The base swells into a watertight cistern. An adult house-sized rosette holds forty tons of water in a good fog-season, and will not spill it. Its leaves are waxy on top to bead, velvet underneath to breathe. They are pale sage-green, dusted with rust-red trichomes that taste of bitter tannin and keep everything except a few specialists from chewing them.

It has no taproot. It has stilts.

From its underside it throws down prop roots as thick as a man's thigh, mottled white and green, segmented like bamboo, hollow in the center to carry air. They do not seek soil. They seek each other. The root of one bromeliad fuses with the root of another on contact, grafting, anastomosing, until the whole Roof is one knotted raft. Old leaves die but do not fall; they lignify, turning to a hard, fibrous wood that creaks when the wind moves the mat. Layer after layer of living felt, dead lattice, and humus - composted bird guano, shed insect wings, fallen feathers - forms a floating peat fifty feet deep that you can walk *through* more easily than walk over.

It breathes. On warm afternoons the stomata on a billion leaves open at once, and the entire plain exhales that upward rain you saw. At night it closes and sweats fog. If you cut it - and the Roof people almost never do - it bleeds clear, slightly sweet water, then seals the wound with resin in minutes.

It is not intelligent. But it is attentive the way a scaffold is attentive: it makes rooms, and rooms invite tenants.

### The Cistern Seas

Every funnel is a world. There are more lakes on the Roof than in any lowland country, only none of them touch each other and none are larger than a bathtub. Some are a thimbleful held in a fist-sized bromeliad. Some are deep, tea-black pools in the great basal rosettes, with their own leaf-litter bottom.

And everything that needs standing water lives there.

If you lift a leaf and look, the surface is never empty. Mosquito wrigglers the size of rice grains hang under the meniscus. Above them, diving beetles the size of coins, black and glossed like oil, row in slow circles. Flatworms glide on the underside of the surface film. And then the true owners: the frogs.

They are small, and there are thousands of kinds. Most never leave the cistern they were born in. A brilliant vermilion frog no bigger than your thumbnail spends its entire life in a single hand-bromeliad, laying eggs on the damp leaf above the waterline so the tadpoles can drop in when it rains upward. A larger one - mottled stone-and-lichen grey, with a back as warty as the humus - sits half-submerged for hours, throat pulsing, waiting for a moth to drown. At night they all call. Not a croak but a glassy *plip, plip, plip* like water dripping into water, multiplied by ten thousand pools until the cathedral under the mat thrums with it.

Above them are hunters. There is a damselfly that never flies in open air. Its nymph lives like a freshwater shark in the big cisterns, jetting, translucent, six inches long, snatching tadpoles. Its adult is a needle of metallic blue, wings too delicate for wind, that hunts by walking upside-down across the inner face of a funnel. There are crabs. Pale, blind, the size of a walnut, climbing with hooked legs from cistern to cistern along the wet gutters, carrying water in the hair on their bellies like canteens. There are even small fish - or things like fish: translucent, lunged, evolved from killifish washed up here during some impossible storm a million years ago and never left, now passed beak-to-beak by birds as eggs stuck to feathers.

Cut open any large cistern and it smells of tannin and life, a pocket of lowland swamp held aloft in the desert sky.

### The Air

What truly owns the Roof is not aquatic. The air under the mat is too vast and too loud to belong to anything that swims.

It belongs to the birds that stopped being birds.

There are no trees, no cliffs in the interior, no ground predators. So the swifts came and stayed. And stayed. Over hundreds of thousands of years of warm, bug-thick, windless halls, they radiated until “bird” is a poor word.

The most numerous is the Veil Swift. The size of a sparrow, ash-grey, wings that have grown so long they look like ribbons. It never lands. Not truly. It snatches moths in flight, drinks by skimming a cistern surface at full speed, mates in a free-fall of two, and sleeps by locking one hemisphere of its brain and gliding in great slow circles in the high air above the mat, thousands of them turning together in a silent wheel you only see as specks against the sun. Their nests are not nests but felted pouches of down and spider silk glued to the underside of a leaf-dome, hanging in rows like lanterns in a street. When the larvae hatch, the parents paste them to the wall with saliva and stuff them with white moth flesh until they swell.

Bigger: the Kestrel-Finch. Hawk-sized, yes, finch-beaked, brutal. Yellow-breasted, with a short hooked bill meant to crack the waxy bromeliad beetles that no other bird can open. It perches - the only bird that truly perches - on the stilt-roots, tilting its head to listen for the crab-scrabble inside leaves. It has learned to follow people, because people dipping nets disturb the cisterns.

Biggest: the Mantle. You hear it before you see it, a deep *whoom-whoom* under the high shriek. Wingspan two meters, feathers the pale sage of the mat itself, face owl-flat but beak swift-long. It does not hunt insects. It hunts other birds. It flies *through* the mat, not over it, barging through hanging roots and leaf-walls, folding its wings at the last second to slip through gaps narrower than its body. It takes Veil Swifts mid-air under the canopy where they thought they were safe. When it calls, every other sound stops for a heartbeat.

And under them, the insects that feed them. At dusk the mothfall happens. White moths, hand-sized, snow-sized, each with feathery antennae to catch the fog, rise from their daytime roosts under dead leaves in a single synchronized pulse. The air under the mat becomes a blizzard. The birds rise to meet them. The sound becomes physical - that deafening metallic wall, sawing, clicking, screaming - until your ribs buzz with it.

There are other fliers you mistake for birds: wasps as long as your finger, banded black and amber, that have abandoned stinging to hunt spiders inside rolled leaves. Gliding lizards, flat as leaves themselves, with skin flaps between ribs, that launch from root-arch to root-arch and never touch humus, eating flies off the waxy surfaces.

Everything under the mat flies or climbs. Nothing walks far. To walk is to be lost in air.

### The Root-Mimics

On the humus floor - that deep, spongy, cool peat where light comes only as green shafts - something else lives. Something quieter, because the birds cannot hunt well in the clutter of ground-level arches.

The Ribbon Snakes. Not snakes. Legless lizards, green-brown, with a faint keel along their back that exactly matches the ribbing of a prop root. They move so slowly you think they are roots until a line of beetles disappears down their throat. They lay eggs in the warm compost and leave them to hatch alone.

The Thatch Hares? There are no hares. The nearest thing is a marsupial the size of a rabbit, with backwards-facing feet to climb down roots headfirst, a pouch lined with waterproof hair, and a nose like a shrew. It is solitary, nocturnal, deafened by day, and it lives by raiding cisterns: it hoists itself onto a leaf rim, dips its snout, and drinks, lapping frogs and beetle larvae as bycatch. Its droppings - dry, fibrous pellets - are the only solid food the blind crabs will travel far to find.

And everywhere, the beetles. Polished shield-backs, horned, clicking. They are the decomposers, the cleanup crew. A dead swift falling into the humus is covered in them in minutes, a moving black coat that hisses. They do not fly unless startled; they prefer to bulldoze through peat, chewing dead leaf-wood into humus that feeds the mat that shades them.

Predator and prey here is not a chase. It is patience. A funnel snaps shut over hours. A snake waits for a day. A Mantle hangs motionless on air.

### The Roof People

And then, in clearings too round to be natural, they live - few, scattered, 1.4/3 if you count hearths per valley.

They are short because the tunnels are low - five foot at the crown, often three - and broad because the humus is soft; you need wide feet, like snowshoes, to not sink. Their skin is weathered a deep, even umber, even the children, from a lifetime of high, mild sun that comes through a filter of leaf. Their hair is kept cropped short or bound with fiber, otherwise moths lay in it at night. They speak little above a murmur. The old ones are half-deaf from the birds.

You will not find a village. You find a family. Three leaves bent and thorn-pinned, a hearth of imported stone carried up generations ago from the cliff-rims where rivers still exist. A grinding stone - always that same smooth imported basalt, because there is nothing on the Roof to make one from - for crushing moth larvae and beetle meal into a nutty paste. A net of root-fiber, weighted with bone, for scooping cisterns without falling in. A hollow gourd, grown not gathered, tended for years on a favored rosette until its shell hardens, for carrying fog-water to an elder who cannot climb.

They are not hunter-gatherers so much as gardeners of rooms. A woman knows sixty cisterns within a half-hour walk: this one is bitter with tadpole toxin in summer, good in winter; this one always holds fat white larvae; this one belongs to a snake and is left alone. A man knows how to read which root-arches will hold his weight - tap, listen for the hollow drum - and which will creak and betray a Mantle above. Children learn to move sideways, never pushing through a leaf wall, always slipping into the gap beside it where the birds go.

They burn nothing. Smoke would bring birds, and there is nothing to burn that would not ruin a water source. They eat raw or stone-warmed: larvae sliced like mango, frogs skinned and swallowed whole, the starchy heart of a young bromeliad shoot roasted on a sun-hot stone until it sighs. They keep no livestock; anything penned would die of damp or be taken.

At dusk, when the mothfall rises and the sound becomes unbearable, they go inside. They pull a leaf-flap shut and listen to the thrumming through fiber. They tell stories in that low tone, about the cliffs where the mat ends and the world drops away in waterfalls of root and stone, about lowlanders who came once with metal and shouting and left because they could not learn to be quiet.

They do not think the Roof is strange. To them, every world is thatched, every ground is hollow, every water lives in leaves, and the sky beyond the cliffs is the strange thing - an emptiness without rooms.

If you stay still, as they do, the birds forget you. Condensation beads on your shoulders. A tiny vermilion frog climbs onto your boot, thinking you are a root, and blinks.

Below your feet, fifty feet down through peat and root and humming air, the tired old mountain holds you up without noticing, as it has for a hundred million years.

Above you, a leaf fills, drop by drop, with fog.
