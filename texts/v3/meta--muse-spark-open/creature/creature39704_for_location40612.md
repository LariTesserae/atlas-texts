At first you think nothing does.

That is its first defense.

From standing height, with the sun directly overhead and the heat pressing up through your soles, this place is too loud to live in. No shade, no cover, no movement but heat-shimmer. No birds, no hum, no wingbeat. The silence is complete. If something lived here, you would see it. Nothing can hide when the plain is this flat.

Then you crouch down, because the heat forces you low eventually, and the plain changes scale.

You have to get low — knee-high, ankle-high, cheek-almost-to-the-clay low — to see who made this.

### The builders are still here.

They were never gone. They are just not on your clock.

During the day you will not see them. The surface is 60 degrees Celsius at noon. It will cook anything soft. That is why everything you see looks tended and empty at the same time, like a workshop at midday with the tools set down. The fresh daub is drying. The little slump where a wall gave way last night has already been patched with a thumbprint of darker mud that hasn't yet cracked.

Come closer. Put your eye at the lip of a basin.

That crisp, hand-smoothed crest: it is not smooth. Under magnification it is pebbled with tiny balls, each the size of a mustard seed, each carried in a mouth and pressed into place. You can see the seam lines where one pellet was butted against the last. Fingertip-wide drags? No. Mandible-wide. The crescent scrapes along the inside of the pan where wet silt was scooped — those are lower jaws.

An inch beneath your face: a hole you thought was an air bubble pop. It's a doorway. Perfectly round, about as wide as your smallest finger. Cool air breathes out of it.

If you wait — and you have to wait without casting a shadow, without breathing on it — something moves.

Not one thing. Many somethings. So pale they are almost the color of the clay, so slow in the cold open they look like clots of wet earth detaching themselves. Then they resolve.

They are small. Worker-small. The length of a grain of rice, soft-bodied, blind, wingless. Amber-white and translucent enough that you can see the dark line of gut through the abdomen. No armor, no sting you can see, no sound. They emerge only on the shady side of the wall, only where your body blocks the sun for a second, and when the light touches them they freeze, then reverse, backing into the hole with a jerk.

This is not one animal. This is a continent-animal.

There is no hill to point to and say *they live there*. The whole plain *is* the nest. The knee-high walls are not fences. They are lungs and hands. Under every pan is a gallery. Under every gallery is another pan and another gallery, down deeper than your arm can reach, where the earth finally stays damp all day. If you could peel the baked crust off like skin, you would see it honeycombed: chambers the size of walnuts, pillared vaults, narrow throats stuffed with damp, chewed cellulose, black fungus gardens breathing out humid air. Billions of mouths moving earth grain by grain.

What you took for erosion *is* them. They do not fight water. They invite it. They are farming the rain before the rain knows where it wants to go.

That is why the watershed is unfinished. It is intentionally unfinished. One basin spills into the next through a notch exactly one builder wide. The spillway ends in a blunt wall because tonight they will decide whether to open it or not. If the rain marches east yesterday, they will extend to the east tomorrow. The entire plain, Texas-large, Brazil-large, is a slow, collective computation for where water should be allowed to go. It is architecture without a blueprint, because every builder is the blueprint.

They work at night and at the edge of morning. In the hour when every underside beads with condensation, the whole plain sweats awake. The workers come out in sheets, not marching — flowing — each carrying a bead of wet clay rolled against its chest. They lay it, pat it, lick the salt to cement it. You would hear them if you had ears tuned for it: not a whine, not a buzz, but a faint, dry rustling like a million eyelashes brushing dust. The only sound small enough to hide in a silence this big.

They have no kings, no tools, no metal. Just numbers and patience.

### And they are not alone, but they set the table for everyone else.

That solitary shrub every thirty meters that looks like it was planted precisely in the corner where water lingers? It was. Not by them intentionally, but by necessity. Its seeds only germinate where the builders have deepened the corner, where night-dew runs down both sides of a wall and collects. Its roots — when they die, they bore a hole. The builders widen that hole. The plant widens the builders' cistern. Neither leads. They have grown into each other.

Lift the edge of that dark green-black crust in the basin that held water last week. Underneath it is still wet, still cool, smelling faintly of sulfur and iron. The crust itself is alive: cyanobacteria, thousands of filaments woven into a skin that holds the pan together when the water leaves. When it rains, it turns to jelly in seconds and photosynthesizes violently for an hour. When it dries, it flakes and waits, dust that is not dead, just paused. The wind lifts it and seeds the next basin.

Between the shrubs, between the crusts, there are other punctuations, each in its own made place:

In the deepest, house-sized brine pan that never quite dries, a film of pink halophiles thickens the water until it looks like diluted blood. Salt blooms at its edge in cubes you could pick up.

In the long, shadowed trough that stays cool to the touch, pale springtails the size of poppy seeds flicker and vanish when your shadow moves. They drink the air itself.

In the cracks that were carefully widened — not weathered, *widened* — fungal threads white as nerve lace run down to find the deep damp, breaking the lithified mud back into soil so the builders can carry it again.

Nothing crowds. Nothing can. There is too little water to share a basin. Each life has its cistern, its pan, its share of dew, and a respectful desert of baked clay between it and the next. The density is low on purpose. This is a city that learned to be a desert, or a desert that learned to be a city, until the difference disappeared.

If you come back at midnight and put your ear to the ground, you will finally hear the plain.

Not silence. Industry.

A faint, almost subsonic continuous ticking and patting, the walls being softened with saliva and rebuilt a little lower, a little wider, the spillways being nibbled open. The watershed learning, basin by basin, how to be a river.

You will not see them all at once. You cannot. But you will feel, with your palm flat on the warm clay, the continent breathing under you: drawing dew in through its million open mouths, holding it, letting it go as steam at noon, beginning again.
