No one lives there the way living is usually meant.

Not at first. That is the confusion of the place. You climb for hours or days up red talus and basalt steps to get to it, and with every switchback you are rehearsing some greener world that must be waiting on top — because water means green, altitude means meadow, wet means soft. You expect the table to be a bog, a cushion.

And then you come over the lip and the light hits you like a sheet of tin and there is no green. No soil. No softness at all.

So at first you think: *nothing lives here.*

The report would say that. The report is almost true.

### There is no one to count.

If you mean houses. If you mean fires, fields, herds, roads, names on maps. Nothing has been built here because the place is already built to completion. There is no room to add. Every horizontal inch was taken a million years ago by stone crowding against stone, shoulder to shoulder, knuckle to knuckle. You could no more found a village here than you could build a house on the back of a beetle already covered in other beetles.

If you mean birds, big animals, anything that needs a nest or a burrow or a pause — no. The air is too loud to hunt in. The stone never warms enough to incubate. There is nowhere soft to land, and so what flies passes over high and fast with its shadow hardly kissing the flicker, as if embarrassed to be seen.

If you mean men — only as a visitation. Only as a trespass that the place corrects within hours by soaking to the bone, by deafening, by blinding with white sun off black water. You walk a little way with your boots finding stone and immediately the water finding your boots, that ankle-deep braid that will not be dammed or drunk dry, and you feel how quickly you would be simplified here. Skin would go pale and pitted like the travertine. Ears would fill with roar until you stopped listening for anything else. No one stays to live. A few have tried — the kind of anchorite or geologist or penitent who thinks emptiness is a diet — and the plateau makes them into its own kind of precipitate: a white-orange stain in a cleft, soon mineralized, soon forgotten.

But that is not the same as saying it is unlived in.

Stand still long enough for your eyes to stop watering and the roar to stop being a wall and become a place, and you begin to see who lives there has been there all along.

#### First, the pillars themselves.

Do not be too quick to call them inanimate. Here an equilibrium has lasted so long it has become a kind of behavior.

There are tens of thousands within sight, millions across the bowl. Some as tall as houses, some no higher than knuckles near the drowned horizon where the sheet-wash turns sky into stone and stone into sky. Columns, fins, teeth, hexagonal prisms split and re-sealed, plates stood on edge, shards fallen crosswise and wedged forever. Each exactly as wide as it needs to be to keep the water moving without giving way further. Each polished, fluted, lipped, throated. Nothing falling. Nothing advancing.

They are not strewn. They are assembled. They *fit.* They are a crowd.

On any other table you could walk between inhabitants. Here the inhabitants *are* the ground. You cannot see ground because the crowd leaves no gap larger than a body, no gap that is not already a throat for water. That density is not accident. It is tenure. The stone has learned to live crowded and to pass the only food there is — water and light — hand to hand without ceasing.

They live slowly, as stone does, by remembering. In every V-cleft, in every chute no wider than your wrist, there is the pale frill of silica like frozen foam, the band of travertine white as bone, the iron varnish orange as old blood where a seep has kept to the same line for centuries. That is how they grow. Not upward, but as a record of the water they've been given. Stone remembering water, water remembering stone. Without that, there would be no distinction from dead rock. With it, each pillar has a face, a beard, a scar.

If you crouch and put your hand to one, the cold goes straight through your glove even in full white sun. It is holding night. It will hold night all day. That constancy is its work.

The whole crowd does one thing together, which is why you thought at first the place was empty of purpose. They take the thin air, cold-condensed on their tops before noon, and sheet it, thread it, ribbon it, spill it two feet, three feet, six feet over lip after lip. No single waterfall worth naming. Ten thousand equal roads. The roar you hear pressing on your chest is their breathing. The flicker of each trickle flashing white on black is their speaking at once. You have to shout to think because they never stop talking among themselves.

This is the permanent population. The lithic people. The standing ones.

#### Then, the almost-not-living.

Because equilibrium is never quite perfect, there are renters in the seams.

At midday, if you hold still and look into a joint where a pothole the size of a cup fills and spills, fills and spills every seven seconds, never stagnating, never warming, you will see a translucence jerk and vanish — a midge born in that cup and drowned in that spill in the same minute, a life the length of a single filling. A few feet over, a mite the color of the basalt itself picks its way with infinite care along a seep-line no wider than a hair, grazing nothing you can see; there is nothing to graze but the faintest slick of silica, the ghost of a chemistry. Later an ash-white moth lifts like a flake of chert caught in wind, startled from a slot, finds no reason to be alive, and is gone.

They are not populations. They are typos. Windblown apostrophes that the crowd permits and immediately erases. They live on transit — on water that never pools, on light that never warms — so they themselves never pool, never warm, never gather. You could watch for a year and never see two mites meet.

This, too, is a form of habitation, just not the kind that leaves bodies. The plateau keeps life at the thinnest possible film, one atom thick, lest it get any ideas about soil.

#### And then, very rarely, the listener.

That is you. Or the one who came before you whose boot-prints have already been rinsed from the film between pillars. The plateau does not want a listener but it makes one for as long as you can stand being soaked to the knees though it never rained, deafened in brilliant sun.

You learn the manners quickly. Do not try to dam. Do not try to divert. Do not look for a lake. Do not look for shade. The water wants to keep moving and will find ten thousand other ways around your ankle. The light wants to strike everything without shadow and will throw back glare from every wet face until you squint and see better with your ears. The roar wants not to be listened *to* but to be stood *inside of*, like weather.

You understand, standing in the middle of more presences than you can count — all wet, all alike, all exactly in place — why the old stories call such places holy or forsaken, which are the same thing. They are places that were once peaks and learned to be tables. That were once fire and learned to be pavement. That were offered soil and refused it.

Nothing will ever be built here because someone already filled it with quiet, patient, sun-scoured, water-passing inhabitants who do not need to eat or sleep or move, only to continue. Who do not die because they never had to be born.

You leave no sooner than you came, and behind you the flicker and the thunder do not diminish by one voice.
