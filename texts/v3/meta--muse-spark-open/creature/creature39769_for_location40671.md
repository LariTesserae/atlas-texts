There is very little that *can* live here. That is the point. And yet something does.

Not an ecosystem. A population of one, plus a memory of a second.

### 1. What lives here now

It does.

The Fill.

Don't call it a plant because you will picture leaves reaching for a sun that has never existed in this pocket. Call it a body. A settlement of tissue so old it stopped being an individual and became a place.

If you could peel the whole thing out of the bowl in one piece — you can't — you would see it isn't columns and floors and hammocks at all. That's just how it has folded itself to fill the negative space air gave it. Underneath, it is one continuous felt of fiber.

Its skin is everywhere. Pale. Dry. The color of a scab on paper, of mushroom leather left in a drawer for thirty years. In the absolute dark it has no pigment to waste. Touch it and it is cool like cork, faintly yielding, deeply furred at the edges where centuries of handling have frayed it into dust-soft felt. Put your nose to it if you want the taste of the place: bitter mineral, faint ammonia, dry rot that never completes.

Because it does not live the way surface plants live.

There is no soil under you. Kick and your foot finds not dirt but the continuation of the same living mat, and under that, after a few inches of laminated leaf-history you can read with your fingertips like pages, the smooth lip of basalt. The bowl-floor. It doesn't grow *in* anything.

It eats the bowl itself.

Fine, white root-hairs you can't quite feel — more chemical than physical — sweat acid so dilute you'd only taste it as a sting on a cut. They pit the stone molecule by molecule. Salt comes up as white flowers that bloom overnight and vanish when brushed. Iron, calcium, silicates. It drinks stone very, very slowly. And it breathes the other way too: the cold air that sinks into the pocket is dead air to you, CO2-heavy, faintly exhaled from the cooling crust far below. The plant takes that. A desert lung. A lithovore gas-drinker. It doesn't make oxygen so much as reorganize scarcity into more of itself.

That is why it is cold. When it grows fast — which for it is still slower than rust — it is an endothermic reaction. You feel it in the central hollow where the mat is thinnest, where you can lie on your back on a drumhead of fused leaf-plates and feel the cool rise through the weave against your shoulderblades. If you hold your breath long enough after the creaks subside, you can hear it work: not a creak, not wind. A subsonic grinding, a faint gritting under the floor, like a tooth on stone heard through a jawbone. Once in a long while, a tremor, a single string plucked deep inside a column, as a new sheet of fiber tensions as it dries.

It has never seen light. Not a flash, not a photon. Its ancestors, if it had them, forgot light before this pocket sealed. It doesn't know up. It only knows edge, and it solved edge-by-edge until it fit edge-to-edge, wall to wall. Ninety percent void, ten percent patient architecture. Hollow columns that are not trunks but chimneys — vertical lungs — pulling the pressure difference between surface and deep through pores and whistling slits. Suspended floors that are not floors but stretched aerial nets it cast to claim horizontal space the air wasn't using. Sheets the size of doors fused at the seam by nothing more sophisticated than touching for a hundred years.

It is not growing toward anything. It is growing to stay. To keep being there.

And because nothing here wants to eat it — it offers no water, no sugar, no flesh soft enough for teeth — nothing ever evolved to prune it. So it keeps its dead. Dead leaf-plates don't fall and rot; they dry and tighten and become joists. Old mats laminate under new mats. The strata under your palm where someone cut a street is centuries of blind accretion, year-rings without years, compacted in the cool dark like pages in a book no one intended to write.

You are walking on its corpse-museum while its living skin holds you up.

Touch a living wall and sometimes it answers. Not quickly. Leave your hand a minute and the fibers nearest your heat give the faintest sigh, a contraction invisible but audible as a tick in the next column over. It feels you the way stone feels rain: very slowly, as chemistry.

It is not intelligent. It is *attentive*. In the way a coast is attentive to water.

It has been here longer than the idea of cities. It will be here when the pale plain above has polished itself a foot lower. It does not mind if you carve rooms in it. It heals around a cut in decades. It leans its columns inward by inches per millennium toward the lowest point, toward the coolest draft.

That is the first resident. The one that never left.

### 2. What lived here because the first resident made room

People.

Not many. Never many. Three families per column-room sounds crowded until you remember the scale: ceilings higher than shouting distance, plazas of living felt where a child could be lost for an hour between two columns though the whole town could be crossed in five minutes if you went straight.

They did not come because the place was kind. Nothing about it is kind. It is dry enough to chap your lips in an hour, cool enough to need another skin always, dark enough that after the first day your eyes water constantly from trying and then mercifully go quiet. No water to carry, no game to chase, no wood to burn. Even fire is a poor idea here — smoke has nowhere to go and sits like a lid until you choke.

They came because the plain outside is worse in a different way.

Up there you are visible for miles on a white tabletop under a sky that does not care. Wind moves salt in sheets that flay. There is nowhere to hide, nowhere to store, nowhere to be still. Down here, six feet down, you disappear completely. It is shelter without building. A ready-made house the size of a village that asks almost nothing and maintains itself.

So they became a different kind of human for a while.

You can read them in the toolmarks. Chert flake scrapes, basalt adze polish, grooves worn glass-smooth by rope. No metal, or almost none — they would have had to carry it across the Low and they didn't carry much. The streets aren't planned, they're negotiated: narrow cuts through fused floor with edges bound in braided fiber so the living edges don't knit shut again. Sliced leaf-plate shutters hung on fiber hinges. Braided root-rope ladders inside every column, connecting pith-carved levels: sleeping hollows sized for bodies curled tight to save heat, storage niches blackened with the residue of rendered fat lamps — the only light that ever existed here, brief, smoky, and hated, used only to carve by.

They didn't farm. There was nothing to farm. What they farmed was the dead of the Fill. They learned to harvest only plates that had already dried and gone silent to the ear, tapping them to hear the difference between living resonance and dead thud. They cut that into cork-block and wove walls that never mattered much. They collected the salt-flowers before they sublimed and used them to cure... something else? There's no sign of cook-pits. Perhaps they brought food down from the surface in sealed skins and only came here to sleep hidden, to store, to meet, to listen.

Because that is what this kind of city makes you.

In the dark that has never been broken, your eyes close. Permanently. You stop seeing behind your lids. Your hands get smarter. But your ears get religious.

A settlement for listening, your hands tell you. For people who had somewhere else to be afraid of.

Every room has a window that is not a window. Slits cut at ear height, precisely angled into the draughting pores of the columns. They whistle differently depending on pressure. A child born here would learn weather two hundred feet up by a change in pitch from their bedroom wall. A mother would know someone had entered the pocket from the opposite end by how the big north column exhaled a half-second late. 

They spoke softly, you can tell by the size of the rooms. These were not halls for shouting. They were whisper rooms, breath rooms. The acoustics punish a raised voice; it gets lost and returns as a creak from somewhere unrelated and unnerves everyone. So they learned the small mouth-sounds, the clicks and hushes that carry on cool, still air.

Some of them stayed long enough to go gently blind without suffering. The wear on certain ladder rungs and threshold bindings is not from feet but from trailing hands always at the same height. Grooves for navigation cord that no longer exists. On one column wall, at about chest level, someone has picked at the cork with a flake for a very long time making a series of shallow pits in a line — counting days, or creaks, or breaths.

None of them died violently here. You'd feel that in a different kind of silence. This is abandonment-smoth, not catastrophe-smooth. Personal things taken. Door leaves left open deliberately to keep draft moving. A place closed up for leaving and expected — for a while — to be returned to.

Why they finally didn't return lives outside, on the plain. Something up there became either safe enough not to need this hole, or dangerous enough that even hiding six feet down wasn't enough.

Either way, they took their panic with them and left their listening behind.

### 3. What lives here between them

Which is still here, tonight, if you stay.

Not a ghost. Sound.

The city is ninety percent void and every void is tuned. Cool air sinks, warms a fraction against living fiber, rises, drags, catches on a slit. A low *tink* from the north column like pottery cracking six rooms away: a fiber letting go a tenth of a millimeter after a thousand years of tension. An answer from underfoot, a long exhale that rearranges salt lines on your floor-mat. Hours later, when you've forgotten sound, a loud POP under your knee as a mat adjusts, and the whole settlement shudders sympathetically — every column, every suspended leaf-plate overhead you can't see but feel trembling, giving off a dry chorus of fibrous creaks like old rope deciding.

It is louder than any village. Because there is nothing to damp it except plant-velvet and your own breathing.

Animals couldn't live here because they would break. They panic in loud dark that touches them without source. Even people could only take it if they translated it. Navigation by settlement-creak versus breath-draft versus rope-sag. grammar of groans. The city talking to itself across long durations, minimal flux.

And the plant listening to them. Growth that pauses when too many feet warm the central mat. Pores that widen collectively after a season of human smoke.

Lie flat in the center, where the columns lean inward just slightly and the floor is thin enough to imagine light through though there is none. Press your palm down. Be still past the point where stillness is a decision.

The tremor comes once. A pluck. Under your back, minerals becoming fiber.

The city listens back.

For now, that is the whole ecology: one ancient lung that eats stone, one vanished tribe of listeners who carved bedrooms in its throat, and the conversation they left hanging in cool, mineral air whenever something dries a little and gives.
