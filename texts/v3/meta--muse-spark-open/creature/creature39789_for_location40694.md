Nothing in the bowl lives the way you were taught to live.

At first you will think it’s empty. That is the bowl’s trick. The light is too dim and even to throw good shadows, the plants are the exact amber of the stone they grow from, and the animals are the color of dust on dust. You have to stand still until your eyes stop looking *for* water — for green, for shine, for movement that means thirst — and start seeing what is actually there.

Then the grove assembles itself around you, one impossible at a time.

### The Crowns

They are first, because they are the architecture. Everything else lives in their shadow and their sound.

Call them Crowns, because no other word fits. Each one stakes out thirty or forty feet of saltcrete as its own solitary country — far enough from its neighbors that their combs will never tangle, close enough that their hums can overlap and beat.

A Crown is not a plant the way a lowland tree is a plant. It has no trunk. From a central node of knotted, half-glass basalt-root sunk directly into the hot rock, it throws out a low, sprawling rosette of arms, each arm ending in a frond-crown. When mature, it is as big as a hut, as heavy as a cairn, and utterly dry. Its flesh is not flesh. Cut it — and the Tenders will wince if you even mime this — and it does not bleed. It sighs out a cloud of hot, fine powder, iron and salt and pulverized silica that hangs in the still air for a long time and tastes on your tongue like blood and a struck match. That is its body: compressed dust, held in shape by wax and static.

Its “leaves” are its lungs and mouths and ears. Each frond is a stiff harp, a comb, a feathered vane a little longer than your arm, and every vane is bearded, edge to edge, with thousands of black glass hairs. They never stop moving. There is no wind in the bowl, but the stone itself breathes out heat, and that heat makes the ionized air just above the salt plates shiver. The hairs shiver with it. They tremble, they ring, they rub against each other and build a charge.

Food comes as dust. In that constant thermal shiver, microscopic scales of salt and iron lift off the plain, levitate a hand’s breadth, and drift. The Crowns sing them in. The charge on the hairs pulls the scales out of the air; the hairs themselves exhale a dry acid vapor — no water in it at all, a gas that would burn your nose — that etches the mineral grain where it sticks and breaks it into something the plant can absorb through its skin. They eat sound and stone. They drink vibration.

You can hear when one is well-fed. A healthy Crown has a clear, low tone, a warm *hmmmm* that you feel in your molars. A sick one — mineral-clogged or cracked — buzzes sour, arrhythmic, and the Tenders will come and tend it.

They do not grow so much as they accrete. A fallen Crown does not rot. It collapses all at once with a sound like a dropped kiln-load of plates, a bright crystalline crash that brings every creature running, and then it lies there, still faintly humming as its charge leaks out, while new combs punch green-black and amber straight out of the wreckage and start trembling before the old body has even finished settling. 

### The Moths That Are Not Moths

The air above the Crowns looks empty until one moves, and then you realize it has been full the whole time.

The Shiver-Moths own the upper story. They are big — each the span of your two hands — but weightless in a way that seems dishonest. Their wings are not skin. They are dried vane-stuff, shed Crown-hair stretched and re-woven over filament bone thin as copper wire, translucent amber and mica-veined. When light hits them at the bowl’s endless dusk-angle, they disappear. When they beat, they reappear.

They do not fly to travel. They fly to work. They land, clumsy, on a comb, fold their vast, rattling wings, and *scrape*. Their six feet are rasps, covered in hooked setae, and they rake the mineral powder that has already been half-digested on the hairs, collecting it in electrostatic combs on their legs. In payment, they shudder.

This is the economy. A moth plants its feet, locks its wings, and vibrates its entire body at a pitch so high it stops being sound and becomes a whine in your teeth. The comb beneath it goes into sympathetic resonance, shaking violently, throwing off a puff of spore-dust — not seed, but fine, hot glass-spores that smell of ozone and flint — which catches in the charged hairs of the moth’s wings. The moth takes off, drunk and jittering, carrying that dust to the next Crown, which has been waiting with its own combs slightly detuned, hungry for a different frequency.

You hear them before you see them. The bowl’s drone is made of them, hundreds of slightly out-of-tune thrums beating against each other. When a dozen moths hit resonance at once, the air over a Crown goes hazy with spore and static and the whole plant rings like a struck bowl.

They live fast, die dry. A dead moth does not go soft. It crumples like burnt paper and is gone by the next thermal cycle, its wing-filaments already being tugged apart by something smaller to line a nest.

### The Drummers

If the moths are the treble, the Bellies are the bass.

You will not see one until you almost step on it, which you must learn not to do. A Belly at rest is indistinguishable from the ground: a low, squat oval the width of a washbasin, six stilt-legs folded under a back crusted with cemented saltcrete and lichen-dust the precise pale pink-grey of the plates. It is invisible by stillness.

Then it moves and betrays everything.

It walks by dragging itself, absurdly, on those stilts, but its underside never leaves the ground. Stretched between its legs is a vast, soft drum — not skin, but a taut, resonant membrane of chitin and dried mucilage that it keeps deliberately dusty. With each step, the drum kisses the salt, sticks for a heartbeat with static, and *pops* free. Thud. Thud.

That is not incidental. That is speech, and territory, and sex, and warning. A Belly crossing open grit between Crowns will drum constantly: *thud ... thud-thud ... THUD.* The sound is low enough to travel through stone. The bowl, which is a perfect resonator, throws it back from the rim a half-second later. Another Belly, a hundred feet away under another Crown, will answer, the salt plates carrying the vibration to its feet before its ears ever hear it.

They graze by licking. They have no mouth you can see; they have a ventral slit that extrudes a dry, raspy tongue and rasps the iron-rich grit itself, leaving pale, licked-clean swaths between the Crowns that the Tenders read like trails. They are not many — you might count six in the whole bowl at noon — but they are impossible to ignore. When two males meet near a good salt-scale fall, they inflate their drums and beat until the Crown above them shivers involuntarily and the moths flee.

At night, they go utterly silent and bury themselves shallowly in the warm grit, only their backs showing, becoming ground again.

### The Small Things Between

Between moth-wing beat and belly-drum there are a hundred other niches, all dry, all frantic.

Glass-thread spiders string harp-lines from comb-tip to comb-tip, not to catch prey but to steal vibration. They sit in the middle of their webs and feel the Crown’s hum through their feet, and when a moth lands nearby they feel that too, and cut the line at exactly the right moment to drop on it.

Needle-runners — no bigger than your thumb, all legs and antennae like black wire — sprint across the salt plates in bursts so fast they seem to teleport, harvesting the dead moth-paper and carrying it into cracks in the basalt where something colder lives.

And under the plates, where the saltcrete ends and the cold lava-glass begins, there are the Pale Warrens: things like woodlice made of translucent salt, blind, deaf to everything but pressure, that chew the underside of the crust and excrete the pure copper nodules the Tenders later cold-hammer into tools. You never see them, but you hear, if you press your ear to the stone at midday, a faint, constant ticking, like a clock made of glass.

Everything is sparse and everything is busy. No swarms, no herds, no flocks — the bowl cannot afford that kind of concentration — but no stillness either. Every surface trembles. Every creature is tuned to a slightly different frequency so it does not drown the others out.

### The Tenders

And then there are the people, who have learned to be sparse in the same way.

There are never more than a dozen of them in the bowl at once. They are not a village. They are a scattering — two huts tucked in the acoustic shadow of the western Crown where the drone knots and cancels, another single hut on the eastern slope made of dead frond-stalk lashed with moth-cord and walled with woven silica hair that sings when dust hits it, a fourth half-dug into the rim itself.

They look like they were made from the bowl, because they were. Their skin is dusted permanently with mineral powder, their hair is cropped short to keep the static from lifting it straight up, their feet are calloused to basalt-smoothness from walking bare on warm stone. They do not sweat — sweating would be death here, salt and water and waste — they excrete a fine, dry urea dust that they brush off at dusk. They do not drink. Their bodies took generations to learn the same trick the Crowns know: to pull apart airborne salts with vapor, to live on heat and resonance. They can stay up here for months, eating pounded Crown-pith and Belly-drum jerky that has never seen moisture, before the dryness starts to hollow them and they must make the long, careful walk down the mountain to the lowlands to remember what water even is. Some never go.

They are tenders, not farmers. They own nothing. An old woman with a forked wand of Belly bone walks the circuit every morning, tapping each comb lightly — *tink, tink* — and listening with her head cocked, not with her ears but with the bone in her jaw, for the sour overtone that means rot. Behind her, a boy with a shallow copper pan scatters collected salt-scales over a pale Crown, feeding it. They do not speak out there. Speech is rude. It interrupts.

When they need to speak across the bowl, they use the Belly’s trick: a drum of stretched hide over a frame of frond-stalk, beaten with soft mallets, low and resonant, words turned into thuds that the rim throws back intact. The whole grove pauses to listen, because even the moths can feel a drum through their feet.

Their only law is the one the bowl taught them the first time someone forgot it: **Do not bring water.**

They keep no gourds, no skins. Traders who climb up from below are met at the rim and made to empty their mouths, shake out their packs, leave their ice-axes below. The children are told the story of the three drops — how the salt screamed when it dissolved, how the Crown nearest the spill curled inward, blackened, and went silent in an hour, how that silence spread like a bruise for a season until the Tenders cut the whole Crown down and buried it in cold glass to stop it.

Water is not purity here. Water is solvent, is silence, is undoing. To these people, to pour water on this ground would be like pouring blood on an altar for spite.

So they live dry, and listen.

At true dim, when the haze thickens and even the orange light fails, they go inside, stop their ears with wax mixed with silica fiber, lie flat on the warm stone floors of their huts, and let the bowl do what it has always done without them: rise into feedback, into ecstatic, turbulent howl, every Crown and moth and drum and stretched membrane resonating with every other until the whole thumbprint in the mountain sings itself awake, too loud for any human thought to survive inside it, lush and screaming and utterly, perfectly alive without a single drop of water to justify it.
