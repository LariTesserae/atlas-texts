They live low.

Not one thing, but a press of things. A saturation. If you crouch — and you have to crouch, the pipes won't let you stand — you put your face down into their level and the room resolves not as empty concrete with some bugs in it, but as a city with no streets, every surface already claimed, every inch already negotiated for.

The first citizens are the carpet.

*Armadillidium*, pillbugs the size of the last joint of your thumb, slate-grey and gunmetal-blue. There are thousands. Lift anything — the edge of the pallet-raft, the lip of the coffee can, a curl of paint the size of a playing card — and you reveal them shoulder to shoulder, antennae waving, already turning away from the light. They don't scatter fast. There's nowhere to scatter to. They just churn, slow and armored, eating the concrete itself in a way: eating the skin of algae and biofilm and the black mold that grows on everything, eating the softened paper of old warning labels, eating each others' shed skins. When one dies on its back it is covered in minutes, not by flies but by its own brothers, who will hollow it neatly and leave the empty, moon-pale halves behind. You can hear them if you hold your breath: a faint dry rustle, like someone turning pages very far away.

Above them, the weavers.

Funnels and sheets, not orbs. The spiders here don't have room for orbs. From every conduit bend, from the open mouth of the control cabinet, from the rusted folding chair to the wall, they've thrown dense, horizontal sheets of silk that sag a little under dust and humidity. *Steatoda* and crevice-weavers, brown and dust-colored, legs tucked tight until something touches. The webs overlap. They become a second ceiling, two feet below the real ceiling, a shroud-city. Each spider keeps to its square inch of sheet, vibrating when a crane-fly blunders into it, when a midge rises off the junction-box pool. They don't ball their prey. They drink them where they hang, leaving husks that become part of the web's architecture. At noon when the sun bars through the grating, you can see the whole layered field of them glittering with trapped upward-rain.

And in the drain-throat, the singers.

Black field crickets, *Gryllus*, fat and polished. They live in the square drain itself when it's dry, packed into the vertical shaft like commuters in an elevator, heads up. They are what make the quiet feel not-quiet. One chirps — a single dry *tch* — then silence for eight, ten seconds. Another answers off-rhythm. Never a chorus. It's too damp for a chorus; their wings don't stridulate cleanly in the supersaturated air, so they conserve. When the pump kicks and shudders the whole box, they all stop at once, a held breath for the room, and then after a minute start again one by one.

The wasps own the air rights.

Mud-daubers first, long-waisted, iridescent blue-black, who found the straight runs of warm conduit perfect for their organ pipes. Their old tubes, grey and ribbed like fingerprints, line the pipes in rows. Most are empty now, abandoned, but not vacant. Smaller tenants moved in: paper wasps the size of rice grains, coppery, nervous, who sealed a few tubes with a papery cap and guard them, flicking their wings. And parasitoid wasps so small you think they're gnats until you see one still, antennae forward, probing a spider's egg sac with a needle. Everything is eating everything else's children here.

The water holds its own country.

That junction box — rusted, open at the top, maybe eight inches deep — never dries. It's black water, tea-colored with iron and tannin and mosquito excreta. Near the surface, *Culex* larvae hang in their thousands, comma-shaped, flexing in unison when your shadow passes. They breathe through their tails. Below them, diving beetles no bigger than apple seeds row in manic circles. Skaters dimple the surface from above. And watching all of it, motionless until motion, the geckos.

Mediterranean house geckos, but wrong. They've lived generations without real sun, pressed flat to the damp concrete behind the pump. Their skin has gone translucent, pink-white, the grey marbling faded. You can see the blue of their veins, the dark of half-digested mosquitoes in their bellies. They don't run. They *creep*, one sticky toe at a time, throats pulsing fast, eyes lidless and filmed. At night — though it's always a kind of amber night under that caged bulb — they drop to the junction box lip and drink with a tongue too big for their heads, and flick crane flies out of the air with a soft *tok*.

The transients pass through too fast to count as residents but they are the room's weather.

Crane flies hatch out of the black slime under the pallet, all legs and trembling wings, live just long enough to mate in the mist that rises off the hot concrete. Ants — tiny red Pharaoh ants — find the body within the hour and dismantle it joint by joint, carrying it away in a line that appears from nowhere and vanishes into a crack. Springtails erupt like fleas from the moss mat when you breathe on it. Mites, orange as rust, swarm over pillbug backs. Slime mold — not animal, not plant — spreads overnight across a dead moth on the chair, bright yellow lace, fruiting into black-tipped stalks by morning, then gone.

Even the mice are part of it now, but diminished. One, maybe two, small house mice living inside the control cabinet, nesting in chewed copper wire and shredded HAND / AUTO labels. They are thin, damp-furred, nocturnal to the amber bulb. They drink from the coffee can, leaving ripples among the wrigglers. They are prey as often as scavenger; the geckos won't take them but the spiders will take their pink young if they can.

And the plants, if you count them as who lives there — and you have to, because everything else orients around them:

The moss on the north wall, black-green when you first enter in the morning damp, silvering by noon as it dries and curls, breathing an inch in and out every day. The three ferns, growing sideways, epilithic, no soil, their white roots splayed like fingers gripping concrete, their spores dusting the pillbugs below. And the salt-tamarisk seedling, stunted to ten inches tall, woody and tortured, its leaves always crusted white with salt it weeps out, its roots somehow finding the salty seep behind the spalled concrete. It flowers only when drowned: when the drain becomes a spring and the room floods brown for twenty minutes, it opens pinprick white blooms underwater, absurd and perfect, then folds them shut as the water gurgles away.

None of them need you.

They have their own light, their own rain, their own seasons timed to condensation and pump-shudder and the bloom-flood. You crouched there are a large, warm animal briefly blocking the sun-bars, making the crickets go silent, making the geckos freeze. If you stay still long enough they'll resume — rustle, *tch*... *tch*, drip — writing you out of the room even while you're still in it.

This is not a place that became empty when the plant stopped wanting it. It's a place that became full.
