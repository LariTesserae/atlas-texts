At first you think no one lives here.

That is the first impression the town wants to give you. And for a place so perfectly kept, it is kept too perfectly — no clutter, no wear, no drift of trash in a current that doesn't exist. A light on behind a water-wall that never flickers. Tools laid out on a terrace in a pattern so precise they look abandoned mid-ritual. It has the feel of a place that was just left, or just about to be returned to.

Then you realize the absence *is* the habitation. Only something living here could make it this still.

You don't see them so much as you notice where the stillness is slightly deeper.

A figure crossing between planes. Not swimming.

You catch it in peripheral vision: a shape in a dark, close-fitted skin that isn't cloth and isn't armor — that same matte metal-ceramic the buildings are made of, but thinned, made supple. It drinks light. It doesn't reflect your lamp, it doesn't wrinkle as they move. They walk down a submerged stair that should not be walkable, boots finding purchase on basalt that has no business being dry and wet at once. One foot, then the other. No hurry. No splash.

They are tall but not because they are tall. They are *stretched* just slightly along the vertical, as if the slightly-less gravity here has been permitted to pull on them over a very long time. Limbs a fraction too long. Necks a fraction too thin. Heads smooth-shaven, smooth-browed — not as fashion but as maintenance, because hair would be a nuisance, a place for bubbles to catch, for sterile fields to snag.

And they are pale in a way that has nothing to do with the sun they have never seen. Not white. Under-translucent. Like something that has lived in clear water so long the water has started to live in it. You can see the faint map of vessels at their temples, but the blood there is darker than it should be, slow. They do not flush or blush. Temperature means something else to them.

There are not many. Perhaps thirty. Perhaps sixty. The town was built for more, but more would have created turbulence.

They are not a colony. They are not a crew. There is no mayor, no captain, no hierarchy you can read in insignia because their suits have no insignia. What they have instead is *tenure*. Some have been here longer than others, and you can tell by how still they can be.

The youngest of them — though *young* is a word that fits badly — still moves like a visitor. She checks the seams where structured water meets stone with a forked instrument that never rusts, and she does it with a small, remembered urgency, as if erosion might actually happen if she doesn't look. She breathes water when she is submerged and air when she is vaulted, and the transition between the two is not quite seamless for her yet; you see her pause at each threshold, throat working once, eyes briefly closing.

The older ones do not pause. Respiration for them is not in the lungs anymore, or not only there. The lattice oxygenates them directly. When they are in the water-walls, the water breathes for them, parsing O2 out of itself in a slow, even tide that has nothing to do with gills or lungs. When they stand under the dry vaults, the air exhales for them. They can stay submerged for hours or dry for hours and they do not seem to notice the difference. Some of them have not taken a deliberate breath in years. Their chests barely move. That high, faint tone you hear at the edge of hearing — the lattice maintaining its truce — they hear inside their own chests now, harmonized.

They do not age quite right. Or rather, they age exactly right for here, which is almost not at all. A scratch you left yesterday will be there in a thousand years, exactly as bright. So will the fine lines at the corner of an eye that has watched the indigo dusk for three hundred years without blinking often enough. Their skin does not pit or loosen. Their eyes do not cloud. What changes is density. Bone a little heavier each decade to better answer the negotiable gravity. Thought a little slower, clearer, more precise. Memory becomes perfect and therefore terrible — they remember every crossing of the plaza, every position of every tool left on every terrace, because nothing has ever moved to overwrite it.

This is why they live here.

They are not researchers, not strictly. There is nothing to research in a place where nothing changes except the fact that nothing changes. They are archivists of a different kind. Custodians. Witnesses. The mountain drowned but did not erode; the town was built to prove that a thing could be held perfectly, indefinitely, without decay, without drift. And to prove it, someone has to stay to see that it is being held.

They call themselves, when they need to call themselves anything — which is rarely — the Holders. Or just *the held*, depending on translation and humility.

Their work is almost entirely ritual maintenance of a machine that does not need maintaining. They walk the circuit. They lay hands on the warm metal-ceramic and feel for a change in warmth that never comes. They listen to the lattice tone. They stand on the knife-ridges at the abrupt edge of town where the cliffs fall away into kilometers of dark and look out at nothing, not as sentinels, as *ballast*. Conscious, intentional weight.

They do not speak often because sound is intrusive here. The water does not carry it; the vaults swallow it. When they must communicate, they do it face to face, close, voices low and startlingly ordinary — soft, unaccented, a little hoarse from disuse. More often they simply know where each other will be. In a town you can walk in half an hour, where no one hurries and nothing ever shifts, prediction becomes perfect. You set your tools down at 08:00 and at 16:00 they are still there. So is the person who will come to use them tomorrow. So is the thought you had about them.

They are not alone in being alive, but they are alone in *choosing* to be alive here.

The other things that live here do not choose.

High above, that pale, ten-meter translation — no front, no back — passes once or twice a generation. The Holders do not name it. It is not part of the town. It is part of the ocean that forgot how to flow, a remainder from before the stillness was taught. It sees without seeing. The Holders step inside when it passes, not from fear, but from politeness, as you might look away to let something vast have its privacy.

Closer, in the seams between buildings, the shoals of translucent eyelash-things flicker in formation. They are not fish. They are a field phenomenon that briefly decided to be plural. They live for seconds, maybe minutes, holding perfect position relative to one another with impossible precision, then vanish between two planes of structured water. The Holders do not try to collect them. Once, a younger one tried, and his hand passed through and came back colder.

And under everything, the mineral growth — the black manganese frosts feathering from warm seams, the pale slow crystals gathering like salt under ledges. The Holders tend these too, not by growing them but by not scraping them away. This is the only growth permitted to be mindless. It marks time in a place where time has been forbidden, accreting at a rate you could only measure with centuries.

If you stayed, you would first notice how your thoughts become very clear and very slow, like the water.

Then you would notice you no longer check your watch, because the light never brightens or dims. Always just before night here. Always.

Then, after a while — a long while — you would be given a dark suit that drinks the light and gives nothing back, warm to the touch despite the cool water. And a set of forked tools to lay out on a terrace.

And you would learn to walk, not swim, down a submerged stair. Boot finding purchase where physics says it shouldn't. One foot, then the other. No hurry.

The mountain would hold. The town would hold. And you — you would hold too.
