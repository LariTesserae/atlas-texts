No one *lives* here the way you think of living — housed, settled, rooted to a place that holds you.

Here everything lives *in transit*.

### The Holdfasts

First, them. Because you can't understand anything else without understanding that they are not the scenery. They are the weather-makers, the continent, the context.

They are not trees. A tree is a thing that stands in soil and reaches for light and water. A Holdfast is a thing that stands *against* sky.

Each one is alone on the horizon, kilometers from its neighbor, too big for perspective until you walk for a day and it doesn't get bigger. Then all at once it does — the pleated flank rising like a cliff that breathes. The trunk isn't wood. Put your hand on it if you can get close enough through the howling avenues between buttresses and it gives like old tire, like dense felted basalt, warm from friction. It shudders. Under your palm you can feel the pistons working — great slow diastolic pulls that suck alkali haze and ammonia trace and salt static through curtains of living filter, then slam it out again.

They don't think. Or they think so slowly that a gale is a thought. Their cognition, if you can call it that, is industrial: sense pressure gradient, open sail; sense charge, tension lattice; sense rip, seal off; sense ruin, abort and broadcast. They have no roots because there is nothing to root for. They hold fast only to weight and shape, their base-flowers splayed into the chert like melted wax, gripping by friction to stone polished smooth by ten million years of dust.

Up top — far above the cool bright air where you stand — their sails move. Not leaves. Sails the size of frozen lakes, cupped, ribbed with fiber-cable, translucent at the edges where the sun shows amber through them. When the wind hits right they go taut with a crack you hear in your teeth. Yellow-green precipitates bloom across them like frost and then vanish inward with that faint sizzle — the dry drinking. Ammonia, charged dust, mineral vapor condensed for thirty seconds at dawn. Enough.

Between the limbs they string their factories. That's the only word. Pods, bladders, kilns. Each one thrumming, forging. You see one in silhouette against the white sun and inside it something is being pressed, extruded, baked without heat. Pollen sheets dry as paper. Salt bricks black and glassy that they will simply drop, THUD, onto the alkali plain when done. Seeds lighter than this thin air, waiting for a bang.

And lightning lives on them. Not strikes them — *lives* on them. In any decent blow, their crowns put on St. Elmo's fire like a hat, blue-white fire crawling, leaping between fronds with gunshot cracks. They don't mind. They were built to make charge part of their metabolism.

They are born fast and die violently and do not mourn. You will see a corpse plain — a Holdfast torn by a salt rotor, its membranes flapping like ruined flags, whistling through torn throats, still trying to pump with a jammed piston groaning. It won't heal. It will decide — in that slow industrial way — to fail all at once, convulsively, throwing everything it ever made into the air in a single fertile explosion that becomes cloud and storm and then, days later, rain made of seeds and fibers drifting down for a hundred kilometers.

A week after that, out on the empty salt where there was nothing, a pale knuckle pushes up. Creaking. Audible growth. Inches per minute.

That is the first inhabitant. The land as organism.

### The Between-Things

Because the Holdfasts are so huge and so far apart, they create the only habitable climate on the tableland. And something has learned to live inside that climate without ever touching it.

**The Kites.**

You heard one before you saw one, though at first you thought it was another torn membrane — that long metallic *SKREE* dopplering over the piston-whistles.

They are not birds. Birds are heavy with water, with muscle. These are almost nothing. Twenty meters tip to tattered tip, but you can see sun through their wings. Membrane over hollow struts of fiber-bone, stitched with tendons that creak like the Holdfasts' own lattices. They never land on rock. Rock would shred them. They live their whole lives in the factory updrafts — those screaming exhales of hot ozone-and-iron vapor that each Holdfast vents every few minutes. That's their thermal column. That's their river.

They circle. Hundreds of them between any two giants, so high they look like flecks of ash until one banks and the light catches it amber, transparent.

They feed *on* the Holdfasts without killing them — shear-beaks, like tin snips, clipping the chemical frost off a sail as they stall for half a second in turbulent air. The plant twitches, precipitates more. It's farming by grazing.

They scream constantly, not for song but for sonar and spacing. Sound carries forever here, stacks, echoes. In this deafening plain where every giant is whistling and banging, screaming is how you say I AM HERE DON'T HIT ME. At night — though night is only the sun smaller and whiter and shadows sharper — you hear them clicking to each other like faulty wiring.

When one dies, it does not fall. It desiccates mid-glide, goes stiff, and sails on and on as a bleach-white glider until the wind finally sands it to powder that some Holdfast inhales.

**The Hoppers.**

Look down now. The glitter between your feet is not just salt.

They move only when you stop looking directly at them. Low, long, many-legged things the size of deer, but wrong. Translucent. You can see the pneumatic sacs in their bellies pulsing, the gut full of black nodules. They don't walk. They compress — all eight or ten legs folding — hiss — and then *go*, in arcs thirty meters long, landing with that soft *pff* of alkali dust that hangs forever because no humidity ever pulls it down.

They are the floor crew. The recyclers. The reason this brutal clean lab stays clean.

A Holdfast drops its waste-bricks — fused salts, stripped organics, dead filter-felt. The hoppers come sprinting-springing in herds of hundreds, jaws like mortars grinding nodule after nodule. The trace organics are calories; the salt passes through and is dropped finer. They eat the wind-fall of dead kites, too. They eat each other’s shed skins. The air between giants shimmers with them, endless parabolas.

They have no voices you can hear unless you're close. Then it's a faint tick-tick-tick, valve clicks, pressure releases in their legs. When a herd turns, it sounds like rain on paper.

If you stand still long enough, one will misjudge and sail directly over you, close enough that you see its underside: featureless, pale, with sucker-feet not for gripping but for sealing against dust.

They never shelter. There's no shelter. They just pause, depressurize, let the cool bright light bleach them a little more, and go again.

### The Invisibles

These two you noticed because they're big enough for your water-born brain to count as *animals*. The real abundance is below your threshold for noticing.

In the lee of every chert ripple, in the dust-shadow of every nodule, there's bloom. Not green. Nothing here is green for long — green needs water to stay soft. Here the bloom is matte white, rust-orange, sulfur-gray: bacterial felts millimeters thick that look like mineral stain until you breathe on them — though you shouldn't, you'd contaminate them — and a patch fizzes yellow for a second, fixing nitrogen straight out of static.

In the air itself, at all times, there is snow that isn't snow. Glass seeds, fiber snippets, desiccated husks, mineral shards made sharp by wind. This is plankton, of a sort. Dry plankton. The Holdfasts make it deliberately, explosively; the kites stir it; the hoppers kick it up. You are breathing it now. Your nose itches. In any beam of that small hard sun you can see it spiraling in vortices that self-assemble and collapse.

And in the charge — the crawling blue coronas on the Holdfasts' sails — something else flickers. Not alive like you mean, maybe not alive at all, but iterative. Self-sustaining packets of ionized salt that ride arcs from frond to frond, persist for hours, replicate by splitting when a gust stretches them. The kites avoid them. The Holdfasts cultivate them, perhaps; nobody knows if they exchange information.

This place doesn’t have a food chain. It has a *pressure circuit*. Air sucked in, filtered, pressed, vented. Salt bricked, eaten, ground, inhaled. Seed forged, banged out, carried, sprouted, exploded. Charge built, crawled, cracked, rebuilt.

No waste because waste is just a slow feedstock here. No rot because rot requires wet. Things bleach, flake, are sanded down and reused, audibly, endlessly.

---

Stand between two Holdfasts and you understand the tremor you felt when you arrived. It's not an earthquake. It's inhalation.

To your left, three kilometers high and wide, a giant takes its shuddering WHUMP inhale, holds, screams out through a dozen fluted vents, and the pistons slam in sequence like doors in an empty palace. To your right, another answers a second later. The dust between them lifts in a sheet, hangs, falls nowhere.

A herd of hoppers passes in low glittering arcs without looking at you. Above, a kite shrieks and shears frost off a sail the size of a city and is gone in wind.

Nothing ever built this. No hands. It built itself out of dryness and bigness and time and the fact that wind, given a plain large enough, learns how to breathe.
