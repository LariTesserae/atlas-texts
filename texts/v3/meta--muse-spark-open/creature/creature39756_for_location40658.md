What lives there is not one thing. That is the first mistake your surface eyes make - looking for the citizen, singular, against the building.

There is no citizen. There is only the city, and the city is what lives there.

But if you stay still long enough in the warm, complete pressure, and let your own heat bleed out until you are 30 degrees too, the dark will begin to sort itself. Not into species. Into *jobs*. Into hungers. The city will show you its people by what they do.

### The Ones Who Hold

You notice them first because they do not move.

They are the foundations. The Masons. Broad, low, and mindless in the way your bones are mindless. Brain-coral that never decided to be round, but spread - sheet after sheet of limestone thought laid down over centuries, until a single individual is a neighborhood, a plaza floor, a load-bearing wall that is still, technically, alive. You can put your palm on one and feel the faint, slow pulse of polyps withdrawing, thousands of mouths the size of pinheads closing in a wave away from your heat. They do not see you. They taste the water you displace and decide you are not food, not threat, not building material. Not yet.

Above them, the Buttresses: black coral that grows only where the current asks it to. It doesn't branch toward light, there is none. It branches toward hunger, toward the slow convective updraft from the warm cracks in the basin. So it makes arches. It makes flying buttresses that have never known a cathedral. It makes the ribs of the avenues, and it is hard as iron and still growing, a millimeter a year, creaking.

And stretched between them, the Roofers. Leather-sponges as big as billboards, pulled taut like wet canvas, thrumming. They are not walls so much as lungs. They pump. All day, all night, they pull the warm stew through themselves, and what they keep they turn into more of themselves. Their skin is pocked with inhalant pores that open and close like shy eyes. If you drift too close, you feel the tug, gentle but insistent, enough to pull your hair, your loose clothes, toward them. They are filtering you, too, politely.

These three, together, are one creature by human law and three creatures by human taxonomy and no creatures at all down here. They are the place. They have been the place for so long that other things have learned to be the furniture of their bodies.

### The Ones Who Keep

Then, in the corridors they make, the Keepers.

You thought the avenues were empty between the clouds of glass minnows. They aren't. Watch a pillar. It shivers.

That is not the pillar. That is its coat.

Pavement isopods, yes, but not as you know them. Down here they have given up wandering. An isopod the size of a low dog will find a vertical wall where the flow is good and clamp there for its entire life, its back legs fused into the coral by its own secreted cement. Its armored back becomes a garden. Barnacles like white teeth grow on it. Veil-anemones grow between the barnacles. Cleaning shrimp live in the anemones. The isopod does not eat in any way you would recognize - it lets its garden catch food and then it absorbs what leaks down, and it lets the garden eat what flakes off its own molts. It is a landlord who became the building.

Inside the round, slightly pulsing chambers, the Doors live. Eyelid-anemones. A single frilled lip as tall as you, set into a coral frame it did not build but has inherited. For hours it hangs open, a slack, fleshy oval, filtering. Light travels in lines across its inner face like slow thought. Then - if you are fast, if you are loud, if your blood makes the water taste like fear - it sees you and it does what a door does. It closes. Not a snap. A slow, deliberate pursing, a drawing together of a thousand tiny muscles until the chamber behind it is sealed, warm, and private. Behind many of those closed doors, something is brooding. Egg ribbons. Young. Secrets.

The Keepers are not friendly. They are not unfriendly. They are *municipal*. They keep the corridors clear by eating any drift that would clog them. They keep the water moving by beating cilia the size of wheat fields if you could see them small. They keep the peace by closing.

### The Ones Who Pass Through

And then, because a city cannot be only building, there are the ones who do not stay.

The glass minnows are the first you learn to read. Each no longer than your thumb, transparent as warm water except for that single opal lantern in their gut - not an eye, a photophore, a belly-light. Alone, nothing. Together, a law of physics. They never stop. A river of them pours through the low city at all hours, turning on a thought that no single fish had. They are the city's blood cells, and also its gossip. Where they thicken, food is there. Where they thin into a hollow ring, something ribbon-soft and hungry is passing through and they have made a perfect, silent corridor for it a full minute before it arrives, because they felt its pressure wake.

The ribbon-soft things are the only ones who look like predators the surface would understand. Long, flat, without scale or hurry, the color of a bruise held up to light. They undulate through the middle stories of the city, not swimming so much as pouring. They have no face that you can find. They have a leading edge that tastes, and a trailing edge that digests. The city lets them pass because they are necessary. They cull the slow minnows. They cull the weak polyps. They die rarely, and when they do, the city gains a street.

Above them, or rather among them, because up and down mean little here: the Siphon Ghosts. You hear them before you see them - that deeper creak, that inverted smoke of warm water. They are not alive, not anymore. They are the mineral memory of something that was. A colony-worm the thickness of a house once lived here, its body a vertical tower of tube and exhalant siphon, venting the basin's heat upward for centuries. It died. Its soft parts were eaten in a day. Its tube remains, calcified, as tall as a three-story building, still venting because the crack in the basin floor beneath it is still breathing. Now veil-polyps live on its lip, and shrimp live in its throat, and the water sighs through it endlessly. The city keeps its dead employed.

### The Ones Who Are The City

And if you are very still, and very slow, you will feel the fourth kind. Not Hold, not Keep, not Pass. The ones who *are*.

They are small. So small you thought their light was the city's light.

Lace-polyps, fire-sponge fretwork, bacterial veils so thin they are only a color on a wall - rust-red, sulfur-yellow, bruise-purple. They coat every surface that is not being actively beaten clean. They are the grout. The mortar made of living feces and mucus and re-laid calcium, yes - but it never set. It stayed awake.

Put your hand out now, slowly, and let it hover over a wall that looks empty. The wall will reach back. A faint, warm tingling of a thousand microscopic crowns opening to taste the oils on your skin. They will decide, as the Masons did, what you are. If you are still, they will glow amber, just under your palm, a small census: *we are still here. are you still here?*

That is what lives there.

Not a mayor. Not a crowd.

A compact in warm water that learned, ten thousand years ago, that nothing can be allowed to leave - so everything had to learn how to stay, together, and become efficient, and become dense, and become a place so thoroughly alive that the distinction between inhabitant and habitation finally, quietly, dissolved.

You are not outside it, looking in. If you have been still enough to see it, you are, for a little while, one of its temporary organs. A warm, moving pocket of water it is filtering, tasting, and deciding, moment by moment, whether to keep.
