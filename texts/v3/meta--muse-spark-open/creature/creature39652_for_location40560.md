They live close.

At first you think you are looking at one kind of life and then you realize you are looking at three or four kinds living so tightly they have grown into each other. Then you look longer and you realize it is really only one kind - bowl-life - with different shapes.

The people first, because they are the ones who built, even though they are not the most numerous.

You don't notice them at first. That's their design. In the terrible brightness, in the white-blue glare off snow and water and low cloud, anything that stood out would be in pain. So they have dimmed themselves.

They are short. Not dwarfish, just compacted, as if the weight of the air and the press of the huts has settled them a few inches over generations. Children look like small adults; adults look like older children. No one is lanky here; height is a liability when roofs are streets and a gustless cold wants to take any heat you have. They move low, knees bent, shoulders rolled in. Even standing still they seem to be huddling.

Their skin is the color of wet stone. Not pale, not dark, but weathered to a grey-pink, wind-burned and then burned again by reflected sun. Cheeks and noses are polished, chapped into a permanent gloss. Their hair - where they let it grow - is lank, black or iron-grey, cut blunt with obsidian to keep it from catching ice. Many shave it entirely and wear close caps of felted hare-wool and goat-down, because hair holds the weeping damp and then freezes.

It is their eyes you remember. Everyone squints, even asleep. The lids are thick, heavy, rimmed red from staring into glare. The irises are light - grey, washed-green, ice-blue - but the pupils are small, pinned. In infancy, the elders bathe the babies' eyes with the uphill water, the sky-water that beads and climbs, and you can see why: the children learn not to cry at the light. They have an extra fold, not quite an eyelid, a translucent nictus that slides in when the sun hits the backwall and the whole bowl becomes a mirror. They don't have snow-blindness because they have learned to look down, to watch hands, to watch water. Staring at the horizon is for lowlanders.

They do not look malnourished, though by any lowland measure they should be. The cold and the constant work should have thinned them, but the trap that holds birds and fish holds calories for them too. They are dense muscle under layers of fat that never quite looks like fat - smooth, seal-like, distributed evenly. Fat is worked for, rendered, stored in stone boxes, eaten cold. Their bellies are round on even the old. Their teeth are worn flat early from grit in dried fish and lichen-bread, from chewing sedge-fiber to soften it for cord.

They dress like the stone huts. Everything is patched. A single person wears goat, hare, bird, fish-skin, sedge-weave all at once, sewn with bone needles in tiny, obsessive stitches that will be picked out and reused when they die. Nothing matches because nothing is made at once; a coat is ten years of repairs. The outer layer is always slightly damp, steaming faintly, shedding the weep. They smell not unpleasant - of smoke that never was, of wet wool, of hands that have been in cold water all morning.

And they are quiet in a way that stops being a choice.

You hear a mother pick up a child and you hear no hush, no shh. She just covers his mouth with her palm, not unkindly, and tucks his face into her chest where his whimper is muffled by fur. Two men repairing a sluice don't talk; one taps twice on the slate with an obsidian flake - *tick tick* - meaning *lift*. The other lifts. A girl wants to get past an old man in an alley an inch too narrow for two, so she brushes his wrist with two fingers and he turns sideways without looking up.

Their language hasn't atrophied. At night, under skins, in the sleeping hollows where six or eight share heat in a pit lined with dry moss and warm bodies, they do speak - low, hoarse murmurs, voices ruined young by breathing cold mist. But in the day, in the bowl-light, with a thousand auklets incubating eggs on every ledge, speech is a taboo so old it isn't taboo anymore, just bad manners, like shouting in a library. The birds will not abandon eggs if starved, but they will if startled. The people learned this before they had a word for abandon. So they learned the language of the place: gesture, pressure, rhythm.

Watch them work and you see the turbulence made flesh. No one has a job. Everyone has a proximity. The woman diverting floodwater with a board is not a water-keeper; she is just the one standing closest when the ice dam broke. A minute later she is picking up a neighbor's baby who crawled toward the cistern, while the neighbor, without word, takes over the board. The old men chipping rime from sluices do it ceaselessly, not because they are told to, but because the groove will close otherwise and then everyone's feet will be wet. If your hands are empty, you pick up a pole and help hold a roof that is trying to become a pool. If your hands are full, someone else takes the pole.

Children are not tended, they are tended *by*. They flow in the alleys in packs of four or five, barefoot even on ice because they have learned where the warm seeps run. They chase otters not for play but because that is their assigned turbulence - small, fast bodies to do the small, fast work of keeping the drying racks clear. They stare at you with unafraid animal eyes, thendart away through a gap no adult could follow.

The old are not retired. They sit. Sitting is work here. They sit on high ledges with shallow bowls to catch the impossible water that runs up. They sit by the terraced pools and watch the ice-film form and break and form, and they tap a rhythm with a bone on stone that tells those below: *upper pool stable, lower pool rising, move the kids*. They are memory and barometer; the bowl has weathered them into its instruments.

They worship without worshipping. The sky-water is kept in sealed soapstone jars, covered with slate. Only midwives and grave-diggers touch it. When a woman births, crouched over hot stones that are not hot, just less cold, another woman trickles a few drops onto the infant's forehead, not to bless it but to mark it: *you are of this water that refused to leave, so you will not leave either.* When someone dies - which is often quietly, of lungs, of a slip, of just stopping in sleep - they are washed with the same water before being folded into a cairn wall, because stone that weeps should be fed. No prayers. The act is enough.

Then the others who live there, who are not people but are not separate.

The birds are the walls. You think the hamlet is made of stone until a section of wall shudders and takes off. The soot-grey auklets have decided the huts *are* cliffs, and they are right. They nest so densely that a roof without guano is a roof about to collapse and no one trusts it. Their silence is the greatest strangeness - a seabird colony should be deafening, but these birds have been silenced by the bowl the way the people have. They communicate in clicks and feather-rustles, a turning of pages. When a thousand lift at once - which they do, frequently, for no reason you can see - they do it on a breath, without a cry, wheel in perfect reflected light, and settle one ledge over. Their eggs are pale blue, warm against cold stone, and the people step around them with absolute precision, never stealing one unless the bird has already abandoned it, which they can tell by temperature with the back of a hand.

Under them, the fish. Blind-white, long as your forearm, endlessly circling under the skin-ice. They never stop moving because the water never stops moving and if they stop they freeze to the bottom. Children lie on bellies on the pool-edge, breaking the film with obsidian shards to feed them scraps, not as pets but as husbandry; fat fish will be trapped in the winter cisterns and eaten raw, eyes and all.

Over everything, the midges in their smoke-columns, hatching, dying, hatching. They are the air's plankton. The fish leap for them. The birds snap them. The people breathe them inadvertently and do not cough; they have learned to breathe through noses, to filter.

The goats, the six-legged ones, are not livestock. No one pens them. They come and go down the invisible pass-trails, shaggy coats matted with icicles, lichen beards frozen to their chins. They pick their way across roofs, dislodging snow that the people wanted dislodged. In return the people scrape the salt-ice from their flanks for soup. When a nanny kids behind a wall, a woman will crouch nearby, not to help, but to collect the afterbirth in a bowl before the otters get it; it will be rendered. The goat will look at her with flat, indifferent eyes and chew.

The otters are thieves and therefore sacred. Sleek, charcoal-brown, too clever, they live in the cisterns like eels, slipping through sluices. Every fish a person catches, an otter has already considered stealing. The children hit at them with sticks and the old men laugh silently because the otters always come back.

And the smallest things, the ones you feel before you see: the star-moss huddled in cracks, fed by guano, tended by flightless beetles the size of thumbnail, tended in turn by red mites. The glassy sedge that rattles. You step on it and it doesn't break. It sings.

None of them leave. That's the whole secret. The bowl is a cupped palm and the light stays, the water climbs back up the cliff to fall again, the birds return to the same inch of ledge, the people rebuild the same wall with the same stones that contain fossils of older walls. Density is not crowding here. It is warmth. It is proof.

If you stay a night, you will wake to find a hare the size of a dog sleeping against your leg for heat, a finch on your roof-ridge preening, a neighbor's child curled against your side though you did not invite him, an old woman's hand on your forehead checking for fever - not because you are loved, but because you are *here*, and what is here is kept.

You will learn to squint. You will learn to breathe through your nose. You will learn to hear the tick of ice forming and know what it means.

You will stop wanting to leave down the pass.
