What lives here lives *with* the machine, not in spite of it.

That is the first thing to understand. Nothing here is fighting the water or fighting the iron or fighting the cold or fighting the noise. Nothing here remembers a before. There were people once — you can tell by the chair — but they are not what lives here anymore. What lives here was born into the THUD.

### The Small Multitude

They are the majority. The census. The ones who make the water thick when the flywheel pushes the gyre around clockwise.

There are the **shrimp**, if you want to call them that. Glass shrimp, ghost shrimp, machine shrimp. They are not one species but three or four you can't tell apart until you're still enough for them to forget you're there. Each one no longer than your thumbnail, each one completely transparent except for a single black bead of gut where it's been eating rust-flour and algae-film. On their own they are nothing — a flicker. Together they are weather. They move as sheets and ribbons, thousands strong, turning on an unseen signal, catching the amber work-light for a second so the whole vault seems to snow upwards.

They live on suspension. They live in the push. When the big wheel turns — *THUD* — and the culvert under the floor sighs, a fresh cloud of powdered concrete and iron scale and bacterial snow lifts off the floor mat, and they open their feathered legs and strain it. They are the reason the tile still has patches of white visible. They comb everything. If you leave your gloved hand out, after five minutes it will have six, ten, fifteen of them on it, picking without fear, tasting salt and skin-oil and iron, deciding you are just another slow mineral to graze.

Beneath them, on the walls:

The **mats**. From a distance you thought they were stain — rust-red and leathery, spreading across the ceramic tile like dried blood. Up close they resolve into what they are: dense colonies of tubeworms, hundreds per square foot, each in a parchment tube cemented to the tile with its own excreted lime. They have no eyes. They have crowns.

When the silt is high they bloom. A thousand tiny red plumes, each a perfect, feathery spiral no wider than a pencil eraser, unfurling into the water to catch what the shrimp miss. They breathe and feed in the same motion. When the flywheel THUDS and the current shifts, the whole mat ripples in sequence — not instantly, but in a wave that starts nearest the pump and travels along the wall like wind through wheat. When a crab leg brushes them, they vanish. Not gradually — all at once, with an audible soft *tick* you can hear if you put your ear close in this otherwise roaring room, a thousand doors slamming at once. Then, patiently, one by one, they test the water again and open.

Between the shrimp-clouds and the worm-mats, hanging in the water column itself like punctuation:

The **drape-fins**. Black fish. There are maybe two dozen of them that call this vault home, and that is enough. Each about as long as your forearm, each absurdly still. They have oversized heads, down-turned mouths, and eyes that have gone milky with disuse — not quite blind, but light to them is only difference, not detail. What matters is vibration.

From their chins and foreheads hang fleshy lures, pale yellow-white threads tipped with photophores that try, pitifully and beautifully, to copy the work-lights above. In the gloom between the real ambers, they dangle their little false suns and wait. Shrimp mistake them. Larvae mistake them. They do not chase; they inhale. A sudden, startling gulp that folds their whole drape-like pectoral fins outward like a cloak billowing, and then stillness again. They hold station in the lee of the pump housings where the gyre eddies, expending almost nothing. You could watch one for ten minutes and think it was dead, until its lure twitches once, patiently, and you realize it has been fishing you the whole time — waiting to see if you will come closer.

They are old for fish here. Slow-growing in the cool. Some have scars where algae felt has grown over healed bites. Some have one or both lures missing, snapped off by a passing eddy or a crab.

And the **crabs**.

Everyone who stays here learns to watch the crabs.

They are pale. Not white — the absence-of-color you get when pigment is expensive and there's no sun to need it anyway. Bone, ash, old ivory. Legspans from a teacup to a dinner plate. Long, stilted legs that make them seem taller than they are, moving with a slowness that has nothing to do with cold and everything to do with reverence. They pick their way across the bowed steel floor plates with such deliberate care that each footfall puffs a perfect, tiny mushroom of dark flour that hangs, then settles.

They do not hurry. There is nowhere to hurry to. They are custodians. They eat everything that dies — shrimp that misjudge a fish, a tubeworm mat that sloughs off the wall, bacterial slime, each other when opportunity presents — but mostly they scrape. Their small claws work constantly, gathering the soft industrial silt into neat boluses and lifting it to their mouths. In doing so they polish. The high points of the diamond tread are bright again where generations of crabs have walked. The handrail-reef is free of algae where they commute along it, a pale trail visible even in the gloom.

If you sit — and you will eventually have to sit, because the THUD gets into your knees — they will investigate you. Not swarm you. One will detach from its path, alter course by two degrees, and arrive at your fin after a pilgrimage of minutes. It will climb with arthritic grace, taste your buckle, decide. They carry tiny passengers on their backs: white limpets like rivets, and among the leg-joints, even tinier parasitic isopods that glow faintly when disturbed, so that at night — which is always here — a walking crab wears a constellation.

There are dozens. Maybe a hundred. You stopped counting because counting implied they stayed still.

Under and over all of these:

What you don't see until the light hits it right — the **hair**.

From every pipe joint, from every valve-flower now solid, from the rim of every caged light, grows hair. Long streamers of bacterial slime, black-green, three feet long in places, waving constantly in the gyre though there is no wind. They are not plant and not animal. They are colony and city. Bacteria that have learned to eat iron, to eat oil residue still weeping micro-dots from sealed flanges, to eat light itself in the weakest photosynthetic whisper. They are the base. Without them the shrimp starve, the worms starve, the fish starve, the crabs starve. They turn the cathedral's rust back into flesh.

Touch one and it parts around your finger without resistance, cold as snot, then reforms. Break one and the water clouds briefly with spores that the worms will catch.

### The One Large Thing

All of this would leave if the sound stopped. That is why the largest thing that lives here is not alive the way the others are, but lives more surely than any of them.

The **Pump**.

Three housings, one still turning. You thought at first it was three separate machines, but anyone who lives here knows better: it's one organism with three hearts, two stopped, one beating. The flywheel — eight feet across, crusted in what looks like barnacle but is actually layer upon layer of mineral accretion, white calcite and black iron, laid down like tree rings — is its ventricle. Once every four seconds it turns. Not fast, not powerful anymore. Just enough.

THUD.

Water is drawn from the west side of the room through the grated culvert under the bowed floor, pulled through the housing with a groan of bearings that haven't seen grease since before your grandparents were born, and expelled on the east side in a warm jet you can feel if you hover near the pulpit. Warm by half a degree only, but the fish know. Their eggs, pale amber beads stuck in the lee of the warm outflow, know.

It does not pump for a purpose. No reservoir needs filling. No city drinks. The pipes it was meant to drive are capped beyond the jammed doorway, or rusted through. It circulates because stopping would require more energy than continuing, because momentum in this much iron is its own kind of life. The 60-cycle hum in the thigh-thick cables is its nervous system, keeping the work-lights alive in their flicker, which grows the algae felt on the upper I-beams, which feeds the shrimp, which...

It is tended.

Not by hands. By presence. The large crabs congregate near its plinth where vibration keeps silt from settling and food is richest. The drape-fins spawn in its warmth. The tubeworms grow thickest on the concrete nearest its throb, where the particulates are most constant. Even the bacteria choose it; the streamers there are longest, greediest.

And there is one more tenant it has kept.

Behind the pulpit, bolted to the plinth half in shadow, is a maintenance alcove just big enough for a man to curl up in if he didn't mind the noise. A nest, now.

In it lives a female **eel-conger** — or you will call her eel because you have no word for a fish that has lived alone in a machine room long enough to become something else. She is as thick as your calf and as long as you are tall, mottled slate and iron-orange from living among rust. Blind. Whiskers like solder wire. She does not hunt the way the drape-fins hunt. She waits in the culvert grate where the suction is strongest, body wedged among the bars, mouth open, and lets the Pump bring her food. Shrimp and larvae and sloughed worm-plumes funnel straight into her gullet. She rarely moves except to shift deeper when divers' lights find her, which has happened maybe three times in a century.

She was not put there. She arrived as a larva through a pinhole leak years after the sea took the building, found warm fast water and darkness and no predators, and stayed until she was too large to leave through any hole that still connects this vault to the larger honeycomb of drowned service levels beyond the jammed door. She is the apex not because she is fierce, but because she is patient enough to never need to be.

If you listen past the THUD and the hiss and the hum, you can sometimes hear her breathe — a slower, wetter counter-rhythm, out of sync with the wheel.

This is what lives here:

A heart that cannot stop, and a forest of mouths that has arranged itself around that heart according to temperature and flow and light. Shrimp that are clouds, worms that are walls, fish that are shadows with lanterns, crabs that are monks on pilgrimage, bacteria that are prayer flags, and one large, hidden eely queen in the basement of the heart, all held in a thirty-meter-wide equilibrium that has not changed — will not change — except by microns per year, until the last bearing seizes and the last light pops and the water finally stops breathing clockwise and finds a new way to be still.
