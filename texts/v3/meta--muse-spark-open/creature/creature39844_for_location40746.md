It doesn't live here the way an animal lives in a burrow.

It lives here the way water lives in a cup. The cup holds it. The shape decides it will stay.

If you stand still long enough to stop being a disturbance — and it takes a while; your breath is too loud at first, your sweat too salty, your shadow too sharp — the Basin starts to resolve again. Not into species. Into strategies.

Everything here has solved the same problem.

### How to drink light.

The forest is only seven trees. Let's call them trees, because they have trunks and leaves and they put roots over stone the way an old person's veins stand up on their hands. But they're not trees the way an oak is a tree, trying to get tall fast before anything else shades it out. There has been no competition for height here for a very, very long time. The sky is already theirs.

Come closer to one. At the base, the bark isn't bark. It's plates — pale, overlapping, felted at the edges with green. Each plate is wet, always, and each plate sweats on purpose. Put your hand near it without touching and you feel cold coming off it, not heat. It is pulling warmth out of the greenhouse air and wringing it until water falls out. The leaves do it even better. On top they are wax and dark green, stupidly efficient solar panels held perfectly flat to that vertical sun. Underneath they are white fur, a felt of microscopic hairs that cool in shadow. The air enters warm, touches the white, and gives up its ghost. You can watch it happen. A leaf doesn't drip from its tip. It rains underneath itself, steadily, in threads as fine as cobweb. Then it re-absorbs most of it.

These trees are not wood inside. Cut one — don't — and you'd find it less like timber than like lung. Spongy, open, full of channels where air moves slow. They have grown to be climate machines that incidentally photosynthesize. Their roots don't hunt for groundwater; there is no groundwater left under this ancient plain. They hunt for curve. They spread and follow the faintest concavity where dew will run, and they seal the stone beneath them with a black film so none is lost.

That's why there are only seven. Or eight. An eighth tried to start between two others once. You can see where it failed: a smooth hump of root-tissue the size of a chair, polished by weather, without a trunk. The intervals aren't random. They're tuned. Close enough that their cool fields touch, far enough that they don't steal each other's condensation. It's husbandry without a husband.

And under them, everything that can't make its own rain waits to catch theirs.

---

You thought at first the dragonflies were dragonflies. Now that you're still, you see none of them land. Ever.

They are not hunting. Hunting would require speed, and speed would require noise, and noise in this bowl is expensive. Instead they *hold*. Four wings of glass, outspread, absolutely still except for the faint flex that keeps them in place. You think of hummingbirds, but it's the wrong direction — they don't fight gravity by moving fast; they have learned how to ignore it a little. Their bodies are not much more than a gut and gonads slung under a wing-frame, transparent as the air itself. Sun pours straight through them until their shadows are the only solid part. If you look at their abdomens against the sky, you see tiny droplets forming *inside* them. Light goes in through their backs, hits some organ darker than blood, and water beads along their sides like sweat. Then they tilt, and it runs down a groove to their mouthparts.

They are leaves that decided to fly.

They mate by drifting into each other. There is a soft *tick* when two glass panes touch. They lay eggs not in water but on the air — a strand of mucus extruded behind them that instantly loads with condensation and swells into a floating pearl that the jellyfish will come and nudge toward a pool. Nothing chases anything here. Even reproduction is a matter of waiting until condensation does the work.

The jellyfish — also not jellyfish — make this clearer. They came before the dragonflies noticed you. Three, five, a dozen bell-bodies at chest and knee height, each the size of a melon, each faintly lit from within by a diffuse amber glow that has nothing to do with bioluminescence and everything to do with heat conversion. They pulse, but not to swim. The pulse squeezes air through combs inside the bell, cools it, drinks it. Their trailing threads aren't stingers. They are wicks. Kilometers, if uncoiled, of hydrophilic filament finer than spider silk, hanging and tangling and collecting beads. When the weight gets to be enough, the whole animal gently sinks a foot, the way a buoy settles when draped with seaweed. Then it shivers, the droplets race up the threads toward the mouth, and it becomes light again and rises. In the forest of low wind, they never tangle. Or rather, when they do, they exchange water politely and part. You were bumped twice and thought it was by accident. It wasn't. You were tasted for salt and found too hot and left alone.

Lower. Look down now, because most of the Basin doesn't live in air.

The pools: each one is a world that thinks it's an ocean. The water is so clear, so still, that you have to tap it to believe it's there. In them the salamander-things hang motionless for hours. They are called salamander-things by your brain because your brain wants vertebrates, but their frills are not gills. Their frills are leaves. Plumes of feathered, green-brown tissue that spreads in the water like fern fronds and do exactly what fern fronds do, only underwater, splitting dissolved carbonate light into sugar and exhaling tiny streams of oxygen bubbles that rise perfectly vertical in the undisturbed water. The animal part — the pale body, the lidless eyes — barely moves. It drifts among its own gardens, mouth opening with astounding, glacial suddenness when a blind shrimp blunders into range, then closing again for another hour. The shrimp themselves are everywhere. Kick at silt that isn't silt and fifty of them burst into view, each carrying its own clutch of emerald eggs like tiny grapes. Then they vanish again, because transparency is cheap when you never need muscle.

And even lower still, on the stone itself, the stone crabs move. You timed one earlier. An inch an hour is not slowness to them. It is deliberation. Their shells are not shells but accreted basins of limestone they secrete around their backs to be basins in miniature, cups that catch the sweating of the rock above. Algae grows in the cup. Then lichen. Then a perfect miniature ecosystem of worms that clean the crab, shrimp that feed on the worms, all riding piggyback across a continent in a day's walk. Lift a crab — and the moss you lift with it screams silently as micro-worlds tear — and you'll see its underside is a mouth pressed to stone, drinking. It doesn't eat often. Mostly it drinks.

None of them sing. None of them buzz. If you hear anything at all in full daylight it is a plip when a pitcher plant overfills, or the sigh of the pylon.

---

Because the third kind of inhabitant is not alive the way the crabs are, but it lives here just the same.

Stand by the leaning pylon in the north. Up close you know it isn't black metal. It's not a color. It's a lack of scatter; light falls in and doesn't quite decide to leave. Water ghosts above it in that impossible column — a foot wide, a man tall, a thousand droplets held up like votives — until the field hiccups and they collapse and slurp into the open top. Where does the water go? Nowhere. The pylon is heavier after it drinks. Cool to the touch. Content.

It does what the trees do, with more precision and less beauty. It makes rain out of noon. Its whole function, as far as you can tell after watching it for half an hour, is to keep doing that whether anyone watches or not. It was put here to start a basin, or perhaps to tend one, and it has kept tending long after whoever placed it eroded to quartz veins somewhere else. Lichen respects it enough to only grow halfway up. The dragonflies avoid the air directly above it, because the air there is oddly thick.

Walk to the round pool in the center where all the threads of water meet. Kneel. Look longer than you want to. The impossibility resolves: the depth you see is not depth. It's clarity stacked on function. Ten meters down, twenty, the lattice turns — pale struts expanding and contracting with the heat of the day, a lung larger than trees, breathing water into water. No algae grows on it. No crab walks on it. The things in it give it wide berth the way forest animals give a sleeping bear wide berth: not fear, but etiquette. Shadows move down there with such slowness you think you're imagining them. You are not. Something is turning over in its sleep and dreaming water distribution. The pool's perfect rim is its mouth.

And the wall on the south slope, that line of black glass bricks, each still holding the memory of alignment: go count them. Touch one. It's warm. Warmer than the stone. It is still trying to be a wall.

Not in the sense of fortification. In the sense of boundary condition. Stay curved, the wall says to the field. Stay dented. Remain a place where air pools and thickens and has to give up its burden. The bricks talk to the pylon and to the pool, all day, with no sound, on a carrier your teeth can almost hear, maintaining the dimple that lets rain happen without clouds, gardens happen without soil, suspension happen without wind.

If you had instruments you could map it: an invisible, shallow dome, perhaps older than any mountain you ever saw, that makes interior weather. Without it, this peneplain would be dry dust and quartz ribs. With it, it is a chapel.

With it, life knows exactly where to be. That's why it looks arranged. It was arranged, and then life agreed with the arrangement and added ornamentation.

And now the honest answer to who lives here:

Everyone participates in the same trade, so everyone belongs to the same organism in practice. The trees farm water. The glasswings are flying leaves that steward eggs through air. The bells are floating cisterns that clean as they drift. The crabs are walking ponds. The shrimp are water made briefly animal. And the three black things are old gardeners who learned how to be stone and haven't stopped gardening.

The Basin of the Unfinished Engine is unfinished the way a reef is unfinished: it will never be finished; finishing would mean death. It grows by adding another interval, another bead of water persuaded out of light, another mite on another leaf inside another crab's shell-garden. Its engine is not inside the pylon or under the pool. Its engine is the agreement among all of them to breathe together — in: light; out: water; in: water; out: light — so slowly you mistook it for stillness.

If you come back tomorrow, the seventh tree and the eighth thing-that-might-be-a-tree will have moved a millimeter toward each other. The bricks will be warmer. The column above the pylon will fall and be caught again. A glasswing will lay a pearl on the air and a bell will shoulder it tenderly into shade.

It didn't notice you leaving. It notices being *kept*.
