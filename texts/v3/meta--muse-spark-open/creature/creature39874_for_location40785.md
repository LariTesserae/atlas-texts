They were not built for here. Then they stayed long enough that here built them.

### The People Who Tend the Cooling

At first, from a distance, you think the city is empty.

It is too quiet for how crowded it must be. Terraces, bridges, balconies, galleries carved into basalt — all of it shoulder-to-shoulder, door-against-door, window-against-window the way only a dense place can be — and no noise coming off it. No hum of traffic, no chatter. Just the minute-long sigh of the piston houses and the tick of stone.

Then you lean in, because up here you have to lean in to hear anything at all, and you see them everywhere.

They are not a different species. Not yet. Give them another ten thousand years of staying while the mountain finishes and they will be. For now they are human the way a cave fish is still a fish: same blueprint, re-tuned.

You notice the chest first. Everyone has it — a broad, deep ribcage, barrel-bellied even in the thin children. They need it to wring work out of the air. The air at 2.9 kilometers up on a hot mountain should not exist; it is too thin and too heated through, like breathing inside a kiln. Newcomers pant. Natives breathe slowly, deliberately, with a faint double-inhale you can hear if you're close enough. A kind of stutter-step breath. Their blood is darker when they bleed, almost brown-red with extra hemoglobin. Their lips and nailbeds carry a permanent faint slate color that looks like cold, but isn't.

Second, you notice they do not hurry. Not cultural affectation — physiology.

Dynamic 0.6 is not just how the machines move here. It's how the bodies move. In this heat, in this thinness, a sprint will black you out. So no one sprints. The workers on the gantry cranes move like divers, conserving. The children play a game that consists of sitting perfectly still on the hot basalt until someone blinks. Old women carrying water basins on their heads — cold dawn-water sloshing — take steps so measured you could set a metronome to them. An outsider thinks them lethargic. They are not. They are paced to the mountain. A full-minute power stroke in the factory hall is not slow to them; it is normal heart-rate.

Because of that, they are quiet in a way that becomes uncanny.

Sound 0.9 is a physical law here as much as a social one. The thin air eats high frequencies. A shout at ten meters is a muffle. So they evolved — culturally, quickly — out of shouting. People speak in low tones, close together, often with a hand on the forearm or shoulder to keep contact while they talk. Workshops are silent except for the *tink... tink...* of cooling metal. Even the market at the lower terraces, shoulder-to-shoulder dense, is a murmur. You hear feet on cinder more than voices.

They listen differently, too. Their ears have learned to pick out the sounds that matter: the creak of load-bearing stone before it heaves, the change in hiss from a borehole that means the pressure is climbing, the sudden absence of wind that means the morning fog is about to pour over the caldera rim. Deafness from industrial noise doesn't happen here. Tinnitus here is the mountain settling, three streets over.

#### What They Look Like Up Close

The mineral gets into everything, including skin.

No one here is pale, even if their grandparents were. The constant dust is basaltic glass and iron salts, fine as flour, sharp as snow. It exfoliates, it tints. Everyone's exposed skin — face, hands, forearms — is a dust-polished umber-grey, ruddy at the joints where heat rash never quite fades. They wrap their heads against it but not with softness. No cotton will grow here. Cloth is woven asbestos-stone fiber, reclaimed boiler suit canvas, thin beaten sheets of lead-foil for foundry crews. Most wear the same thing: sleeveless, open at the chest to vent heat, feet bound in thick leather and salvaged tire-rubber because the ground will cut you or brand you. Every edge in this city is still sharp. Erosion hasn't sanded a single stair.

Their eyes are narrowed, lidded. Not squinting — adapted. The sun through less atmosphere is a knife. The glint off volcanic glass is blinding. Children are taught to look at shadows to see. By forty, everyone has the fine white scars of silica grit in their corneas, a faint opalescence. They claim they can see heat. They might be right.

And their hands. Density 2.4 means everyone works with their hands. There is no room for waste. Hands are broad, callused into something like horn on the palms from handling warm basalt and cable. Fingernails are kept brutally short or they crack from heat. Burn scars are universal, small white coins up the forearms — a rite of passage is your first kiss from the River, the near-boiling fissure-water that runs steaming through the lowest district. You learn to respect it early.

You will not see fat or thin extremes. The city is calorie-precise. Food comes up from the lowlands by cable-tram — no soil up here will grow it — dried beans and salt fish and algal cakes grown in the cistern dark. Everyone is lean, corded, efficient. The old are very old; heat and altitude should kill them, but the slowness preserves them. An elder sitting on a basalt bench, breathing that double-inhale, can be ninety and look sixty, eyes filmed but hands still steady enough to tap a rivet true.

#### How They Live With Water Without Green

There are no farmers. There are *catchers.*

Vegetation 0.1 is not a problem to solve here; it is a condition to accept. Nothing grows. So no one pretends. There are no parks, no window boxes, no weeds to pull. Green is a color from stories, or from the dusty blue-brown smear of lowlands thirty kilometers down that you were born to never visit — going down would mean the air gets heavy and hot in a different way, and your lungs, tuned for thinness, would drown at sea level.

Instead the culture is built entirely around intermittent water.

Water 1.2 — too much to be desert, too little to be reliable, too strange to be secular.

Every family, every work-crew, every block tends a cistern. Carved straight into basalt on rooftops and terraces, lidded with iron to keep dust out. At 4:30 a.m., before the sun, the temperature inversion drags a cold fog over the caldera wall in a slow, silent waterfall. For an hour, the city is invisible and everything drips. This is when they move. Doors open. Lids come off. Children with squeegees on long poles herd condensation off cables and walls into gutters. Men and women stand on roofs with wide basalt pans, tilting them whisper-quietly as water beads and runs. It is the only time the city is loud with anything like joy — a soft clatter of tin, the splash in basins, someone laughing low and saying *good catch today.*

That water is everything. Cold, metallic, faintly sulfurous, drunk unfiltered — the dissolved iron keeps them from going anemic in the thin air. What isn't drunk that morning will be gone by noon, boiled off by the stone's exhalation. So they bathe at dawn, wash at dawn, bless at dawn. There are small rituals: pouring the first cup back onto the warm rock for the mountain, for the cooling.

Then there is the River. If the dawn fog is their gentle mother, the River is their scalding father wound.

Three kilometers of fissure that weeps boiling mineral water through the lowest, densest heart of the city. No one drinks it direct; it would cook you. But everyone uses it. It runs in iron troughs past manufactories where its heat is bled off through exchangers, past salt terraces where precipitates are harvested — white natron for glass, yellow sulfur for acid, rust-red iron oxide for pigment and brick. Along its banks, life clusters so thick you can barely walk — not because people like the heat, but because the River is the only constant.

This is where you find the shrines, though they would not call them that. Small niches carved into salt crust where a filament-worm has left a particularly elegant rust trail. The filament-worms themselves — long, red-black threads of muscle that braid in the lethal current — are tended like bees. Not harvested, not eaten. Watched. Workers know each braid by sight. When a worm mass balls up tight and pulses, pressure is about to change upstream and someone should open a relief valve. When they go slack and drift, the mineral load is dropping and the glassworks will have a bad batch. The worms know the mountain's blood better than any gauge.

And in fog season, the other fauna.

Fauna 1.4 — rare enough that a child might go weeks without seeing something wild, weird enough at 2.4 that when they do, it stays with them for life.

The Condensers come with the fog. You will be on a terrace catching water and realize the fog is *looking* at you. Hundreds of translucent mantas, the size of your hand, of paper, riding the air currents without moving. Silica skeletons under membrane, pulsing with faint St. Elmo's fire as they drink the air itself through electrostatic thirst. They do not flutter. They hang. If you are very still, one will settle on your cistern rim and you can see the droplets race across its back toward a central mouth that is not a mouth. Workers believe — quietly, the way you believe a tool is lucky — that a good flock means a good catch. They never touch them. By 9 a.m. they are gone, folded into cracks thinner than a finger, or sublimated. No one has ever found a dead one.

There are other things that are rarer still, that people argue about. The salt-walkers — spindly, stilt-legged crusts that form overnight on the River's cooling terraces and walk a few inches before collapsing, leaving a track like lace. Are they alive? Are they mineral precipitation that learned to mimic? Here the distinction is impolite. Everything here is still cooling, after all. Some cooling things move.

#### The City as Organism, Them as Its Maintenance Crew

The truest thing about the people here is this: they do not believe they live *on* the mountain.

They are how the mountain is finishing itself.

That is not mysticism. It is municipal policy.

When new basalt heaves up through an avenue overnight — hot, glassy, steaming — no one curses. A crew is already there by midday. Not with jackhammers to destroy it, but to incorporate it. They drill rebar into it while it is still warm enough to take it, run conduit through it, carve a doorway, fit a window frame that will be perfectly plumb once the rock finishes contracting in six months. A house will have a living room that is two hundred years old and a bedroom that is three weeks old, still warm to sleep against in winter.

Their children grow up learning to read flow lines in slag the way lowland children read river currents. Their engineers talk about curing times in decades. Their dead are not buried — there is no soil — but walled into new stonework, or, for the honored, lowered into the sinkhole where the River vanishes back into the mountain, to be dissolved and recirculated.

At night, when the ordinary light goes, you finally understand them. Because then you can see what they tend.

The whole caldera glows — not bright, but deep, like coals. Furnaces, sodium lamps, but also the city itself. The lattice of cable and pipe, the slow crawl of ore-elevators up cliff faces like blood cells, the hundred steam vents exhaling together and hanging as one low cloud over the bowl. It looks exactly like a body at work. And they are its patient cells, moving slowly, quietly, in the heat, tightening a bolt, draining a cistern before it boils, listening to the minute-long breath of a great engine that will outlive them all.

They do not hurry. They do not need to. The mountain is not going anywhere. It is becoming.

And neither are they.
