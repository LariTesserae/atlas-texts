Nothing lives here.

Not in the way that sentence is supposed to work.

Nobody *inhabits* this basin. No one arrived, no one adapted, no one made a niche and defended it. What lives here *is* the basin — differentiated only as much as a body differentiates into thumb and eyelid and gut flora, and no more.

If you try to count species you will fail within the first square meter. Not because there are so many — although there are — but because the idea of species hasn't finished inventing itself yet.

Here is what lives here, under the unmoving amber light, under the law that **everything must be wet, everything must be close, everything must continue:**

### The Chorus — which is also the Air

You heard them before you saw anything. You thought it was insects.

Closer — put your ear to a leaf the size of a table, the one weeping constantly from its tip — and you understand.

Every leaf holds its choir. Frogs no larger than the pad of your thumb, skin so thin you can see the blue-green liver working underneath. There are hundreds of morphs but they are not separate species; they are expressions. One hatches cobalt with yellow knuckles if it gestates in a pool shaded by black-vine; the same egg hatches translucent orange if it gestates two inches to the left where a fungal bloom warms the water half a degree. They don't croak. They don't call and answer.

They *hold*. One note each, lifelong.

One holds a high F that makes your teeth ache. Another holds a low thrumming G below human hearing that you feel in your molars. Another — the turmeric ones with the red eyes — holds a middle C so pure it's painful. Together, layer on layer on layer, millions upon millions of them, they make the single sustained chord that has been playing since this place exhaled itself into being.

They do not stop to breathe; they breathe through their song. They do not stop to eat; gnats blunder into their open, singing mouths and are swallowed without interruption to the tone. If one dies — slipped, eaten, dissolved by a fungus that was already growing in its throat while it sang — its neighbor shifts its pitch by a fraction to fill the gap. The chord never changes.

They are not animals *in* the swamp. They are how the swamp listens to itself.

### The Mat — which is also the Ground

Kneel. Press your palm down. That give, that spring, is not soil.

It is a felt, meters thick, of root and rhizome and mycelium and half-rotted leaf and living leaf and egg mass and the peaty bodies of things that died an hour ago and are already being unbraided. White fungal threads — not hyphae, ropes, cords as thick as your wrist — run through it like nerves, like sutures. They are stitching constantly. If you tear the mat, you can watch it close. Not grow closed. *Stitch* closed, fibrils reaching, knotting, pulling leaf to root, root to shell.

In this Mat live the things that have forgotten where plant stops.

The ferns that birth canoes of frond only to have those fronds become gardens: each frond carries its own soil on its back, its own tiny pools in the axils where salamander larvae flicker, its own flowering vine that roots in the fern's own living tissue without killing it. The vine's waxy black leaves are warm to the touch; they are heating themselves, composting their own fallen leaves in tiny ovens to stay above the ambient 38 degrees.

The flowers are not reproductive ornaments. They are watering cans, dehumidifiers, lungs. The great white trumpets that open with a *pop* do so to dump a cupful of distilled water they have condensed all morning onto the fungus below that begged for it chemically. The maroon bells never open; they stay closed and full of rain, and things live *inside* them — entire generations of beetles are born, mate, and die in the drowned darkness of a single bell, never seeing the amber sky.

Nothing here waits to fall to earth to germinate. Seeds germinate in mid-air where two leaves almost touch and the humidity is 100%. They germinate on the backs of beetles, on the damp black feathers of the Drinkers, on the film over your eye if you stand still long enough.

This is why you cannot clear ground. Ground is not a place. It is a committee, and it votes instantly to be covered.

### The Weavers, The Rollers, The Tasters — which are also the Blood

Since nothing is prey and nothing is predator, what moves must be understood differently.

**The dragonflies.** Hand-sized, amber-winged, so numerous the air looks flecked. But they don't hunt. They hover — absolutely motionless except for that slow, visible beat, *one... two... one... two...* — and their abdomens do not glow, they *radiate*. Each one is slightly hotter than the air, a tiny furnace. They are thermoregulators. When a patch of peat gets too cool from condensation, half a dozen congregate over it and warm it with their bodies until the bacteria resume fermenting. When a pool gets too hot, they leave. The swamp uses them to stir its own soup.

**The rollers.** Beetles like wet stones, obsidian and iridescent green, pushing not dung but balls of fermented fruit, fungus, shed frog skins, all rolled and re-rolled and inoculated. They are not feeding themselves; they are feeding the Mat. Mites ride their backs — bright orange, leggy — and mites ride those mites that are too small to see, and protozoa ride those. Each ball is a moving city, a compost starter culture being delivered door to door. If you follow one beetle for an hour it will walk the same ten-meter circle forever, distributing.

**The tasters.** Leeches, but you have to unlearn leech. Translucent, ribbed, with external gills like red ferns that flutter as they inch. They do not want blood. They crawl vine to vine, leaf to leaf, tasting condensation. Each bead of water holds a different chemistry — the weeping basalt's mineral sweat, a fern's exudate, a frog's skin secretion — and they read it. Where the taste is wrong — too acidic, too still — they bite the leaf, not to feed, but to bleed it a little, to let fresh water mix. They are the white blood cells, lancing.

**The strainers.** In every ankle-deep, tea-black pool that is not a pool but a dimple in the Mat, the lace-salamanders hang. Six inches long, pale pink-white, with huge feathery external gills that pulse like lungs outside the body. Mouths permanently agape. They do not swim. They drift, filtering spore, algae, shed skin, the endless rain of microscopic life that snows down through the still air. They excrete a fine silt that the fungal ropes immediately bind into new terrace.

And above them all, moving through it at the speed of growing, are

**The Drinkers.** You thought they were birds. They are taller than you, stilt-legged, with necks that bend like reeds and beaks like folded leaves. Soot-black feathers so dense they stay dry underneath while the outside is soaked and grows its own moss — so each one walks as a small, mobile hill, with beetles and frogs living in its back. They never call. They unfold their beaks in the air and the fog condenses inside, runs down grooves straight to the throat. They drink the air because there is no need to drink the water. They step, wait an hour, step. One footfall in peat will stay water-filled for a year, and become a new pool, with its own salamanders, by evening.

None of them flee. None chase. If a roller blunders into a taster's path, the taster tastes it and moves on. If a Drinker accidentally steps on a patch of frogs, the frogs resume singing under its foot. Death here is not an event. It is a thinning, a softening at the edges, and then incorporation. A thing stops moving and within minutes it is veined with white thread and already warm with new growth.

### If a Human Lives Here

You could, for a while.

You would never dry. Your clothes would rot off you in three days and be replaced by a green film that is not clothing but not *not* you. Your footprints would become ponds behind you. If you tried to sleep, a vine — thin, pale, blind, with leaves like wet paper — would find your ankle, not to strangle but to hold, because *everything must be close*. Condensation would bead on your eyelashes until you blinked constantly. A small blue frog, no larger than a bead of sweat, would climb into the warm, wet cave of your open mouth while you breathed through it in your sleep, and it would sing from there, trusting your throat to amplify it.

If you stayed long enough to be honest, you would stop trying to be separate. You would learn to stand very still and regulate your breathing so your exhale fed the white trumpets. You would become a place where water hangs a little longer before it falls.

And when you finally lay down — because you would, from the heat and the weight of the air and the pressure of the chord on your chest — the Mat would not cover you.

It would *include* you. White threads would find the salt on your skin and stitch inward. Beetles would roll their balls a little closer. Dragonflies would hover over you to keep you exactly at 38 degrees. The frogs would adjust their chord, by a fraction of a semitone, to account for the new shape of you.

And the light would not change. And the cloud would not move. And everything would continue, wet and close, exactly as it was, with you now perfectly, indistinguishably, part of the sound.
