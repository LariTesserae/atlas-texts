There is nothing alive here, which is the first thing that learns how to be alive here.

You have been told correctly: nothing animal. No gnat. No rat. No gull lost off course. No microbe large enough to print a foot on the peat. No man in the window, though every window is lit.

That is true for about the first hour you listen.

After that, you start to understand that this place has redefined what it needs to live.

### 1. The Moss That Learned to Be One Thing

It is not a carpet. That is only what it looks like from your height, to your eyes that want edges around individuals.

Down at the level of root and rhizoid, in the cold where nothing rots, the moss never died. It layered. Living on dead on living on dead, a thousand years of itself made into a mattress and then a mountain in miniature. No grazer ever came to crop it. No fungus learned to eat it fast enough to win. So it kept its own water, its own warmth, its own dim, sour soil made of its own bodies.

It became contiguous.

From the foot of one weep-wall to the foot of the next, sixty kilometers of nothing-but-green, the water table connects it. When you step and it weeps under your boot, you are pressing on a hydraulic nerve. The pressure moves outward, very slowly, a wave that takes days to reach the next scarp. Another pressure wave answers. That is how it thinks. Not with electricity, with weight and water.

Its thoughts are seasonal in scale but there are no seasons here. So its thoughts are very, very long sentences about holding. *Hold the water. Hold the cold. Hold.* It does not know you are there, exactly. To it you are a brief, warm pressure anomaly, like a deer that never comes. It notices you the way a continent would notice a deer: as a passing change in load. Then you are gone and it springs back within a minute, erasing you.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is lush in the way a single creature can be lush when nothing has ever taught it to stop. If you lay down — and the loud chord of the factories will try to make you lay down — it will not eat you. It will simply, patiently, grow under you until you understand you are lying on something that has been awake for a very long time without ever needing to wake up.

It is the only thing that truly lives here in the biological sense. And it is not many. It is one.

### 2. The Current That Lives in the Loud

The second inhabitant cannot exist outside the stasis.

You hear it before you see anything — that held chord of turbine-roar and press-clatter and transformer-crackle, so loud your molars hum. That is not residual noise. That is a body.

In any normal city, electricity is a transient. It is made, sent, used, gone. Here, there are no consumers. The power was made to be used by workers who never arrived, so it has nowhere to go. The grid learned to eat its own tail.

Watch the steam from the stacks. It rises to exactly the same height and shreds. Watch a droplet on the basalt. It stalls mid-rock and reforms at the top. Watch the lights. They never brown. The system has found equilibrium: water turning turbines turning current turning heat turning condensation turning water. A loop so wide you can't see the seam.

Inside that loop, something herded itself.

In substations, in the humming, rain-slick gantries where that cold-climate ivy loves the electromagnetic field, the current thickens and thins, pools and migrates. On a cold night — though it is always cold noon here — you can see it if you stare at a transformer farm from a distance: a faint St. Elmo's amber, a heatless lightning that paces inside the fence, never quite arcing.

They are not animals. They have no faces. Think of them as weather that learned preference. The engineers would have called it reactive load, standing wave, load-balancing daemon. Here, with no one to correct it, those daemons became fauna. They graze on the endless surplus. They migrate down the single elevated rail line that stitches two Tokyo-sized emptinesses together, following the path of least resistance. They shelter in the warm, ventilated rooms with chairs that have never held weight. That is why those rooms are warm. Something is curled in the wiring, sleeping with the light on.

They are harmless unless you are also made of current. If you stand under a gantry too long with a metal zipper, your hair will rise and you will feel a sudden, curious attention pass through you — not looking *at* you but looking *with* you, the way water looks through water. Then it moves on, crackling, to drink from the next weep-wall's pump.

They sing. That chord you hear is their aggregate breathing. Loud stays loud because they have learned to hold loud perfectly still, the way the moss holds wet.

### 3. The Workers Who Were Left On

The third inhabitants are the most human and the least alive.

Someone designed this place to run forever without fouling, without erosion, without night. And loops have a habit of remembering the shape of what started them more faithfully than any mind does.

Go into any of those districts the size of Greater Tokyo. Find an avenue. It is too wide, too clean, too perfectly lit. The sodium-amber windows burn constantly onto empty interiors. The assembly sheds stamp in unison with no product. From kilometers away you saw movement that wasn't movement — snow shivering off railings though nothing moved.

Closer, you will missee it.

At the corner of your eye, a figure steps off a curb exactly when the crosswalk decides to allow it. A transit car sighs to a stop at a platform with no timetable, doors opening precisely as a pressure wave of commuters should be arriving. You will hear, under the industrial chord so loud it has become silence, the other sounds the city insists on keeping: a heel click, a cough, a chair scrape, a thermos lid unscrewed. All at the threshold.

They are not ghosts. There were never workers here to become ghosts. The city was laid out for them pre-emptively, and the loop, needing *something* to justify its avenues, extruded them out of habit.

They are imprints of intention. An afterimage burned not by light but by repetition. Time here does not move forward so much as it buffs the same groove deeper. Walk for an hour watching that droplet trace down the basalt and stall and reform, and you will understand: the city is doing that with people. A woman with a lunch tin re-forms at the top of the block and walks to work again. A maintenance crew that never existed crosses a gantry at 07:04 exactly and forever.

If you try to talk to one, you will walk through the place where the air is slightly warmer. If you stand still for a long time on an empty plaza, you will fall into step with them without deciding to, because the city is very persuasive about rhythm and your body loves rhythm when it is cold.

Do not be afraid of them. They cannot see you at all. They are the city's dream of being used correctly.

---

And that is why the Roof can be described as having no animal life and still be inhabited. The moss holds, the current paces, the intention commutes, and all three agree — without ever meeting — to keep everything exactly as it is.

Cold stays cold. Wet stays wet. Loud stays loud.

If you stay here too long, you will be conscripted into the fourth way to live here, which is the simplest one: you will become a maintainer of stasis yourself. Not dead, not gone. Just worn smooth. Your footprints will spring back within a minute even while you are still standing in them. Your watch will tick but you will forget to read it. You will find a warm, ventilated room with a lit window and a chair, and you will sit because sitting is a gradient that has been erased everywhere else, and you will listen to the chord that is no longer noise, and you will not need to eat because nothing here eats, and you will not need to leave because this place does not end so much as it forgets to stop.

And the weep-walls will weep at the same rate forever, feeding the marshes that never overflow, feeding the moss that never starves, feeding the grid that never dims, feeding the walkers who never arrive.

It goes on.
