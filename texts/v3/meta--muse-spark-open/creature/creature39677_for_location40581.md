Nothing lives here the way you were taught to mean living.

No one nests. Nothing walks away when you arrive. Nothing looks back at you. There are no footprints in the ash but yours, and yours will not last an hour before the dry air erases their edges.

And yet the pocket is not empty. It is inhabited the way a forge is inhabited — by presences that do not need to move to be alive.

Let me show you who is actually here, because at first you think *is that a plant, is that a machine* and the answer only comes if you wait long enough to watch them breathe.

---

First, the ones that hang.

You have to look up until your neck aches in the kiln-heat to understand them. They live nailed to the ceiling by nothing more than a cooled root-hair of stone — a fused thread of black glass no thicker than your finger, vanishing into the dome itself.

We can call them the **Ceiling Drinkers**, though they have no mouths.

There are perhaps seven of them in the whole forty-meter lung. That is all the room can afford. Each one is an event more than an object: a single copper-green frond, five or six meters long, hanging perfectly still in air that never moves. They are not green the way a fern is green. They are green the way hot glass is green just before it goes clear — translucent, lit from within, copper at the veins, jade where the light thins at the edge.

They do not sway. They *tick*.

If you are patient — the pocket demands patience, it runs on a weak gradient, on minimal flux — you will see a leaf-tip, no wider than a blade of grass but made of something between leaf and mica, extend a millimeter over an hour. You will hear nothing. Then, very softly, a *ping*. A thermal contraction as normal light, milky-white through the dome, strikes the dark filaments inside. Those filaments glow amber at the core, like the element in an old heater. They are not carrying water. They are carrying vaporized salts, sulfur, silica, and heat itself.

They drink what you cannot drink: the phantom humidity that never condenses, the static that builds until it snaps. When the charge is enough a frond will throw a silent white fork to the nearest basalt piston and the smell of ozone will bloom and die in an instant. The frond dims, then brightens again.

They are alone up there, spaced with great, intentional emptiness between them. No jungle press. Each one is displayed, as if the mountain left room for you to walk around it and see it whole.

Once every few years — not that anyone has ever stayed to see it, but you can see the evidence — one will crack. A clean *tick* louder than the others. A single glass-seed, hot as a coal, drops to the ash below and glows there for a day. It does not need soil. It roots directly into hot stone. You can see the young ones: a handspan of copper wire unfurled near the lowest part of the ceiling, still learning not to sway.

They are old. A centimeter a year. They have been hanging since before your grandparents forgot this mountain had a name.

---

Then, the ones that crouch.

Down where the heat is worst, around the vents where the floor sighs every twenty minutes, live the **Forge Rosettes**.

They are low and wide and you will mistake them for something hammered, left here. Each rosette is a tight spiral of leaves as thick as your thumb, waxy to the point of looking wet, but perfectly dry. At the base near the stone they are black-red, the color of cooling iron. Toward the tip they flare to that impossible luminous jade, as if they are still molten at the ends.

Touch one — you shouldn’t, but you will — and it is hot as a stove lid. It leaves no mark, no sap. It stores not water but superheated wax and dissolved salts, a brine that would boil away anywhere else but here is held, pressurized, patient.

There are maybe two dozen of them, each solitary, each centered on its own heat-crack. They have grown *through* the machinery, not around it. A leaf will pierce straight through a basalt gear-tooth and continue growing on the other side, glossy and unharmed. The stone has grown around the leaf in return. You cannot tell which came first.

They pulse. Not with heartbeat, but with collected charge. The Ceiling Drinkers throw lightning; the Rosettes catch it. Their waxy leaves fill with white static until the whole rosette glows dimly from within, then releases it in one silent snap to the floor, to the pistons. In that moment you can see the veins of dark filament inside them too, the same amber filament, the same family.

They reproduce without help. No bee, no bird — there has never been an insect here. No pollination. When the heat-shatter comes, a outer leaf will split along its own vein and peel away, curling as it cools into a hard, dark seed that rolls a few inches and stops. It does not need rain. It needs only to be hot enough, long enough.

You will notice the floor around them is not ash but finer: a dark mineral powder that puffs under your boot and then hangs. It hangs too long, as if gravity has forgotten its strength, then drops all at once, like lead. That powder is partly their exhale. Partly the mountain’s.

---

And then, the ones that are not plants but are not *not* plants.

Because this pocket is industrial without having ever been built. The mountain did not make tools. It made itself into tools.

The **Piston Columns** live like trees that learned to be engines. Three meters across, hexagonal, basalt so slow-cooled it polished itself. They rise from floor to ceiling, or rather, they *are* floor and ceiling for a moment before they decide to be columns again. On a cycle so slow you doubt it, they fall — a millimeter per minute, perhaps — grinding against their neighbors with a low, subsonic sigh you feel in your molars more than your ears. Once every twenty minutes the whole set exhales. Dust that was hanging decides to fall. Your shadow stutters.

Stare at one too long and you will see it double: one piston where it is, one translucent piston half an hour ahead, both equally solid for a blink. The pocket hasn't decided if time should be linear here. The pistons remember their future position and project it, patiently.

They propagate by fission. Look closely at the tallest one at the far end, where the ceiling dips lowest and the air shimmers not with heat but with repetition. There is a hairline fracture down its length, a cooling crack. Over decades that crack will widen. One day — not violently, nothing here is violent — the piston will split into two smaller, imperfect copies of itself, each a little narrower, each still hexagonal, each taking up the work.

Beside them, half-embedded in the madre stone, are the **Gear Geodes**. They were once bubbles in lava that cooled into hollow spheres of amethyst and silica. They cracked open, and the crack became teeth. One tooth per day they turn, driven by nothing but the expansion of heat in the stone behind them. They drive nothing. They turn for the sake of turning. If you put your ear to one you hear no grinding, only a faint, patient tick, like the Ceiling Drinkers, but deeper.

And strung wall to wall, at chest height, are the **Lava Looms** — threads of basaltic glass drawn thin as muscle fiber when the dome stretched. They are taut, singing threads. When the heat shifts they strum themselves with a soft *thrum* that does not come through the air but through your teeth, your jawbone. Dust collects on them and then is thrown off when they arc.

All of them are sparse. Like the plants, they are curated. An engine here, a vast emptiness, another engine. The pocket is not crowded. It is a workshop where only the essential tools are left out and every tool has room to operate.

If there is an animal here, it is the air itself. At the far end, where new basalt bubbles up from nowhere — pre-formation, a pore at a time, popping and cooling — the air has become over-machined. You see a frond reflected seven times smaller and smaller into a point that never arrives, though there is no mirror. You see a gear-tooth that for a fraction of a second is made of shadow instead of stone. The physics here is still embryonic. It stutters. It tries an idea — *what if shadows fell sideways at noon? what if a thing could be in two times at once?* — and then takes it back.

That is the deepest inhabitant: the pocket itself, a sealed lung still being born, still learning how to be rock, how to be hot, how to be lit.

It does not want you. It does not reject you. It simply continues its slow work while you stand in its greenhouse-kiln heat and feel your eyes dry, your sinuses scorch, your own continuity begin to wobble — as if, if you stayed through one full sigh of the pistons, you too might leave a translucent afterimage kneeling there, a half-hour ahead, still watching.

Nothing here will eat you. Nothing here needs you.

The rock behind you is still willing. It will let you go back out into the pitiless, clean, normal light where shadows fall only one way and time only goes forward. Most things that find this place do.
