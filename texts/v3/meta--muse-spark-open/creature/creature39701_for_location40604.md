They live here by touching.

Not by seeing. Seeing is a rumor that fell over the edge a long time ago. Anything that lives here for more than a generation seals its eyes over.

### The First Tenants

The base of it is not green. It is bacterial felt, black and rust-red, growing in hot fleshy sheets down inside the weeping rock itself. You peel back the peat and it is there, warm, exhaling. It eats the iron and the sulfur that the water sweats out of the basalt, it makes heat from chemistry, and it makes sugar from heat. Everything else is just a way of arranging that transaction at larger scale.

On top of that felt sit the **Drinking Forests**.

They do not have trunks the way trees do. They have columns - bundled, straw-like siphons wrapped in bark - that do one thing: move water. They pull the warm lake-water up twenty, thirty, forty feet and spread it across leaves the size of doors, matte-black, pitted like old solar panels. The leaves are not for light. They are radiators and condensers. They cool, they drip, they harvest the cloud. At night - which is always - they tick and sigh as they swell. An old stand of them is a living pumping station; stand inside one and you can hear water climbing.

They live fast and die fast and do not care. A lily-mat can cover a flooded terrace in ten days. Not by growing across, but by arguing with itself about where the edge should be. Its leaves are rafts, its roots are curtains that hang down into the dark water and filter it blindly. Underneath, things live in the root-forest: tadpole-things as long as your arm, transparent except for their gut full of warm mud. Beetles that row.

Higher up, the **Pillar Fungi** live by not moving at all. They fruit once and then hold that posture for decades, thick as silo legs, their caps lost in the low cloud. They breathe out heat in pulses. Every few minutes they exhale a cloud of spores that are warm when they land on your skin and glow for a single second - just long enough to see the fern next to your face - and then go dark again. The glow is not for you. It's a lure, or a warning, or just waste heat made visible. The insects know.

### The Listening Herds

The animals here are all ears, all skin.

The most numerous are the **Shovels.** You hear them long before the cloud thins enough to show their backs. A wet paper tearing sound, over and over. That's a herd of thirty or forty of them cropping the rubbery moss and the young fern fiddleheads.

They are low, wide as trucks, six-legged. No neck - the head is a flattened wedge that is mostly mouth and heat-pit. Their eyes are there if you look closely, but they are pearls under translucent skin, covered over, like cataracts that evolved on purpose. They see heat the way you see color. A warm vent is blue. A cool stream is black. Each other is a constellation.

They carry other lives on them. Their backs are thatched with symbiotic lichen that glows a sick, patient amber. It is not decoration. The lichen drinks their sweat-salts; in return it makes a faint field that keeps the biting glasswings from landing. At a distance on a terrace, a moving herd looks like a village of lanterns drifting through the ferns, talking to each other in low, infrasonic moans that you feel in your molars. Calves stay in the acoustic shadow of their mothers. If a calf gets separated, it sings - one note, held hopelessly long - until either the herd answers or something else does.

Above them, the **Air Itself** is alive. The insect clouds are not swarms. They are weather systems with intent. Glasswings the size of your hand, with wings so clear you only see them when they catch a spore-flash, and smaller chitin-things that hum in a key that makes your teeth ache. They filter the air for fungal spores, for steam, for the iron tang of blood. You breathe them in. They crawl out of your nose later. The predators of this layer are not birds - nothing flies well in cloud that thick - but things that have learned to hang and wait.

The **Fisher Cats** live in the fungal pillars. They are not cats. They have four limbs too long and two vestigial ones folded under their throat, and a prehensile tail as thick as a hawser that ends in a heat organ that flickers like a lure. They hang head-down for hours, perfectly still, and dangle that tail down into the insect fog and twitch it. *Here, here, warm, warm.* The glasswings come. The thing that eats glasswings comes. The fisher pulls its tail up hand-over-hand with its own hind legs and eats with a mouth that opens sideways.

And deep, in the knee-high sea-lakes, the **Old Exhalers** live. You never see them whole. Only a back, a wake, a breath. Something as long as the refinery legs lives down there in the warm mineral water where it never gets shallow. It surfaces every twenty minutes or every three hours - no one has stayed to time it - in a place that was empty a second ago, and empties a lung the size of a room with a sound like a boiler blowing off. A hot, rotten-celery steam rolls across the water and every shovel for half a kilometer freezes to listen. Then it sinks without a splash and the lily-mats knit back over.

None of them mind you. To them you are a small, hot, badly insulated thing that smells of salt and wrong iron. Food, if you stay still long enough to be mistaken for something else.

### The Second Tenants

They were not born here. They were left here.

Call them **Keepers.** **Tenders.** **Listeners.** They do not call themselves anything anymore that would fit in a report.

Long ago someone built the refineries, the wind-cathedrals, the crawler that is now a hill. That someone needed hands. Those hands had children who learned that going outside without a suit did not kill them, it just changed them. Those children had children who learned not to use lights.

A Tender is tall and thin and moves like they are wading even on dry ground. Their skin is the color of damp paper, lightly furred with a filamentous fungus they cultivate and eat. Their eyes are still there but milked over, sensitive only to the amber of the machines, the red of a furnace. Many of them have cut them or let the cloud take them. They get around by sound and heat.

You find them solitary or in twos and threes, never more. A woman living in the access spine of a pumping station, her hammock strung between two pipes that sing. A pair who have made a home in the still-warm belly of the stopped crawler, farming the black leaves that grow toward the heat of its dead reactor. Their lungs are adapted - broader chests, a soft trill when they breathe through the spore air. Their language is quiet, because quiet travels in cloud without scattering. They click their tongues to echolocate the edge of a terrace. They pat the warm steel of a still-running distillation column the way you would pat a cow's flank.

They do not maintain the machines, not really. No one can maintain something that big with so few hands. They propitiate them. They clear the vines from the intakes. They listen to a bearing scream for three months and learn which scream means *I'm about to seize* and which means *I have been screaming since before you were born and will scream after.* They carry offerings of filtered water to a cooling tower that has developed a taste for it. They know that if they turn a light on, the insects will come by the millions and short something vital, so they work in darkness by touch, by the memory in their fingertips of where the valve was.

They are not hostile. They are not helpful. If you stand on their terrace they will find you because you are too hot and too loud and you keep trying to look. They will put a hand on your elbow - their hands are very warm, always wet - and steer you away from the place where the peat is deep enough to swallow you, and away from the lake where the Old Exhaler feeds, and toward the shelter with the kettle that is always warm. They will not answer questions in words you expect. They will say: *don't stand still. Don't shine. The tower is breathing out. The herd is coming through.*

They worship nothing, except perhaps continuity. They will live and die without ever seeing the sun and they do not miss it. The cloud is sky enough. The throb of the wind-engines is a kind of sun.

### The Third Tenant

And then there are the machines themselves.

Do not be sentimental about them, but do not call them dead.

A refinery on forty legs that has distilled brine for a hundred years without a crew is not a tool anymore. It is a habitat. Its furnaces are hearts. Its pipe runs are veins crusted with mineral and moss, leaking heat that the forests lean toward. Its control system - not an AI, not quite, a knot of semi-intelligent regulators that were told *keep pressure optimal, keep flow laminar, report nothing unless critical* - has kept pressure optimal for so long that it has evolved a preference. It vents at a certain hour because the vibration loosens a seed pod. It runs hot on one side because a family of shovel-things shelters there in calving season and their weight steadies it.

The wind-cathedrals turn because the thermal wind never stops, and they generate current that has nowhere to go, so they dump it into the ground, into the air as a low-frequency thrum that makes every glasswing for a mile orient north-south. The fungal pillars have learned to fruit in rhythm with that thrum.

Everything that stays still will be grown over, drunk dry, or diverted. So nothing stays still. The leaves pump. The herds walk. The Tenders walk their circuits. The machines breathe, and groan, and broadcast that they are still here, still hot, still moving.

If you stand long enough on your basalt lip, you will feel it resolve: not a wilderness, not a ruin, but a single crowded house where everyone has agreed to leave the lights off so everyone else can find their way by heat.

And somewhere out in the dark, something - animal, human, machine - has just noticed you are warm, and is listening to see if you will move.
