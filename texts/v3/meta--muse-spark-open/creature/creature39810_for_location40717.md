What lives here is not hidden. It just lives at the speed the hollow lives.

At first you think the answer is *nothing*. That's the rule you feel when you step in: plants and stone only. And for a long time, on the scale your eyes want to check — movement, flight, tracks in mud — that's true.

Nothing lives here that needs to go anywhere else.

Stay longer and the threshold lowers.

---

The most alive things are the ones you mistook for stone.

Lichen lives here in abundance. Not on the stone but *with* it — gray-green foliose sheets thinned to paper, tight to the limestone where the warmth holds, crusts the color of old bone on the sunward benches, darker rosettes almost black-green in the condensation runnels. It doesn't grow so much as accrue. A millimeter a year, maybe less under this pearled light. It has no hurry because it has solved the problem this hollow poses: how to live on air that never moves, water that never flows, light that never changes. It drinks straight from the damp without needing rain. It is the ground cover more than the grass is.

Beside it, the moss. Only where it is allowed. On the north sides of the two lowest benches, in strips exactly as wide as the overnight bead-and-drop reaches, a velvet pelt when damp that goes brittle and black-green when the day holds its breath too long. If you kneel — and the hollow makes you kneel eventually, just to put your hand on the warm stone — you see each shoot standing in perfect stillness, holding a single clear drop at its tip like a lens. It doesn't spread. It keeps its edge precisely at the tide-mark the water draws for it. That obedience is its form of life.

A little higher on the economy of need are the grasses and the dome shrubs. The coarse tufts live one per crack, not more, each anchored by roots that have learned to pry limestone flour and hold it. They don't wave. There's no wind to wave them. Their blades are curled slightly inward to keep what moisture they catch. The little domes — a foot high, perfectly hemispherical although no one shears them — are almost animal in their completeness. One dome = one plant = one root system that found one pocket of sandy soil and decided *here, this is enough for a lifetime.* Their wiry leaves are tiny and rolled, waxy to the touch. On the east bench there are three. On the south slope near the old wall, two more. You can count them the way you counted the stone pans. Nine, maybe twelve.

And the trees. All the same kind, because only one kind solved this place. Smooth pale bark that never thickens or fissures, like skin. Leaves small, thick, dark over pale underneath. They don't drop much litter; what falls stays where it falls until it becomes part of the cool dense thread of soil along the bottom seam. The tree that grows out of the joint on the eastern bench — its roots have ceased being roots and become a second stone, poured wax flattened over the rock, fused to it, holding on not with force but with long agreement. The central one, flat-topped, makes a net-shade that moves hardly at all, because there is no sun to move it. Both are stunted by any other forest's standard and perfectly tall for this room. They photosynthesize slowly in the sourceless light. They transpire so gently you can't feel it except as that faint, imagined fizz of damp leaving a leaf.

That's what you see living. What's harder to see:

In the inch of water in the stone pans — that water you thought was empty because nothing wriggles in it — there is life at the limit of the eye. Not larvae. Not boatmen. Nothing given legs. A faint olive skin on the sandstone below the waterline that never thickens into slime. Alga, bacteria, a biofilm that tints the reflection of white sky just green enough you only notice it against a dry pan's clean dark tide-ring. If you brushed a fingertip across the bottom of a pool it would come away slick, and within a day that slick would be back. In the densest seep-mud where your boot sank a half inch and came away clean, there are springtails — or rather, there were, generations compressed into eggs waiting for a wetter year that never comes — and mites the size of dust, and threads of fungus paler than the small pebbles they pass between. You will never see them move. Your presence doesn't disturb them because you operate at the wrong shutter speed.

That fungal life is the real continuity here. Under the lichen, under the turf-filled cracks, under the darkened seam of seep, white hyphae run between stone and root, stitching tree to shrub to moss in a web that doesn't need center or edge. It's what enforces the quiet. It has decided, chemically, that this place will not support abundance. No overgrowth. No flowering riot. No animals drawn by fruit. Flowers, if they come at all, are closed and pale and self-pollinated. No scent on the still air to call a bee. This place has edited animals out not with violence but with lack of invitation.

Once, someone lived here. Not lives. *Lived.*

You can read them by where they chose to intervene as lightly as the moss does: a six-meter line of wall along contour that did not retain, did not enclose, only said *this side is different from that side for a while*; a three-sided pen open to the warm east, clean-floored, where perhaps a kid goat or a dog slept one cold night centuries ago; a standing slab leaning ten degrees now whose blank faces may have meant *burial, boundary, marker* and now mean only *upright*; a circle of stones set round a favored pan to keep leaf grit out. Hands and weight and time. Biological-only technology. They lived like the dome shrubs did — one family, one small task, one generation finding the ratio of stone and water and staying inside it. Then they stopped, the way erosion stops when equilibrium is reached. They didn't die out dramatically. They drifted away when staying became the same as leaving.

What lives here now is therefore mostly maintenance.

The lichen maintains the stone.
The moss maintains the drip-line.
The trees maintain the shade-net that keeps evaporation low enough for the moss.
The biofilm maintains the clearness of the pans by eating what would cloud them.
The mycorrhiza maintains the agreement that nothing will grow faster than everything else.
The warm, windless air maintains itself by sitting still.
Even your breathing, loud to yourself for a few minutes on the warm wall, is quickly maintained — absorbed, evened out, forgotten.

If you sit long enough on that low wall — stone same temperature as your hand — you join that for a little while too. Not as resident. As permitted event. Like the *plik* of a drop after hours.

You will leave and the water will not rise or fall perceptibly, the leaning slab will not have leaned further, the grass tuft in the crack will not have seeded.

This is what it means for a place to be finished and still alive: not that nothing lives, but that what lives has agreed not to change.
