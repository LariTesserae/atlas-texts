It lives here without ever having arrived.

Nothing *colonized* this place. Nothing washed in after the drowning and made a home in the ruins. The things that live here are the fourth event, after was-built and was-grown and was-drowned — they are the staying.

There is no single dominant species. There is a set of *strategies* for continuing, and everything here is one of them.

### The Ones Who Hold

You notice them first because you mistake them for part of the building.

On the vertical faces, where the hanging root-mats drape like black curtains, they are anchored. At a distance in the indigo gloom they look like knots in the weave, like darker thickenings where many vines converge. Closer, you see they are bodies.

They are crustaceans only if you use the word very loosely. Their backs are broad, barnacled domes two or three meters across, flat on the bottom where they seal against the worked stone with a ring of muscle so perfect it leaves no shadow. Their carapaces are not chitin as you know it - it's accreted. Grown. Layer after layer of mineral they pull from the cold water and lay down until it has the same fused, river-stone smoothness as the ziggurat blocks beneath them. They have become the same texture as their wall.

From the front edge they extend arms. Not many. Four, sometimes six. Each joint is as thick as a ship's mast, each limb longer than you are tall, but they move with a care that looks like reverence. They are grazing. Picking. Tending. The hanging gardens would choke themselves without them — root-felt overgrows root-felt until it sloughs off in dead slabs — so these holders comb it. You watch one reach, impossibly slowly, take a mouthful of dripping moss and liverwort and old kelp holdfast, and bring it to a mouth you cannot see, hidden under the dome. The motion creaks. The water carries that creak for a kilometer. *Creak... creak... creak...* It is not a hunting sound. It is a maintenance sound.

They do not walk. They have not walked in a hundred thousand years. Some have grown directly into the seams between the house-sized blocks, their back-domes now continuous with the stone. Some have other things growing on *them* — pale kelp stipes sprouting from their backs like trees on a hill, anemones opening and closing on their shoulders. If you touched one — and you won't, because the cold has your hands tucked close — you wouldn't know where building stopped and animal began, which is the point.

They are old. They do not reproduce quickly. Every few decades, maybe every few centuries, the water between two terraces will cloud faintly with larvae like dust motes, most of which will never find an empty palm-sized patch of true stone to seal to. There isn't any. So they wait.

They remember nothing. They tend.

### The Ones Who Sing

They own the emptiness.

In the long boulevards between kelp towers, in the plazas that are now meadows of waving frond-grass, there is nothing for a long time — just clear, cold, loud water and the sway of distant trunks. And then a shape eclipses the faint indigo.

They are flat. That's the wrong word - they are *broad*. Whales would be right in size, wrong in shape. Think of a manta made from dusk and muscle, forty meters from wingtip to wingtip, but as thick through the middle as a house, trailing behind them not a tail but a skirt of fleshy fronds that are exactly the same black-green as the kelp they glide over. When they are still, they are invisible. When they move, they do not flap. They undulate the whole breadth of themselves at once, a single slow wave that pushes them forward without hurry. They never hurry.

They sing the whole time.

It is not song like a humpback's - brief, melodic. It is a continuous organ tone, low enough you feel it in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. It comes from a cavity in their vast heads that opens and closes like a barnacle the size of a drum: *thunk... OOM... thunk... OOM...* The water itself vibrates. The kelp shivers without current. And it never stops, because the singing is not communication. Or not *only* communication. It is feeding. The tone shakes loose marine snow, bacterial clots, spores, dead skin from the forests - makes the water itself precipitate food, which they then swim through with their wide mouths open. They are filterers who farm with sound.

There are many of them, but you never see two together. One holds a territory that is a whole tier of the ziggurat — a province of kelp and avenue and plaza — and it circuits it endlessly, counter-clockwise, always counter-clockwise, for years. When its path crosses that infinitely distant indigo where another's territory begins, the two tones interfere for a while, making a beat frequency so low it makes you nauseous and dizzy, and then they pass and the loop reasserts. The chorus you thought was a loop *is* a loop. It is just very, very large.

When one dies — rarely — it does not sink. Its gases hold it mid-water for months while it is taken apart by smaller mouths, hanging in its own boulevard like a second, paler ceiling, still trailing its kelp-skirt. By the time the bones, if they are bones, finally settle into the frond-grass, another singer has already taken the circuit, on the same heading, at the same speed.

You think at first they are blind like the other fish. Then one passes within thirty meters of you and you see that the fronds on its back are not fronds. They are eyes. Hundreds of them, stalked, pale, unfocused, all looking in different directions without looking at anything. It does not deviate. It does not know you are there. You are too brief.

### The Ones Who Are The Place

And then there is the substrate itself, which is not one thing and is the only thing.

You thought the kelp and the moss and the root-mat and the bacterial sheets were plants. They are, but not like surface plants. Nothing here photosynthesizes in any meaningful way. That last bruised indigo from kilometers above is a rumor. These forests eat the mountain.

They eat stone. Not quickly — nothing here does anything quickly — but irrevocably. You run your hand over that hanging tapestry on the wall and feel how warm it is, a fraction of a degree warmer than the 4-degree water, because inside that felt, acid is working. Hair-roots as fine as nerves have sought the seams between the house-sized blocks and widened them. They have learned the shape of corners and kept it. An arch made of living root has the exact dimensions of the arch of worked stone that dissolved inside it a million years ago. A colonnade of kelp stipes stands exactly where a colonnade of columns stood, each trunk thicker than a redwood because it had to be, to hold the same load of water.

The forest remembers the city by imitating it.

In the avenues, the kelp grows tallest where the avenue was widest, as if it remembers traffic. In the plazas, the frond-grass grows low and even, trimmed by grazers but also by its own agreement, as if it remembers being walked on. Doorways remain doorways not because anything uses them as doors, but because the root-mat on either side has agreed to leave a negative space in the shape of passage. The city is a habit the plants have not broken.

And they are conscious, in the way a forest is conscious. No thought, no self. A preference. A spacing. That eerie solitude of one colossal kelp holding its territory alone for a hundred meters — that is not accident. They signal through the water with bitter compounds and through the stone with slow electric pulses, saying *here is enough, here is not yours.* They have kept this balance since before human language. The ziggurat gives them form; they give the ziggurat persistence. Without them it would have finally silted flat. Without it they would have nothing to hold to in the abyssal mud desert. They need each other.

If you cut one — you won't — it would bleed cold salt and then seal. If you listened long enough, you would hear beneath the creaks and booms and organ drones a fainter sound: the almost inaudible crackle of growth, of root-hairs splitting mineral one cell at a time. The mountain getting, by fractions of a millimeter per century, larger.

---

There are smaller things too, of course. The pale blind fish the size of canoes that shuttle endlessly between kelp trunks, mouths agape, never seeing. The shrimp as long as your forearm that live in clouds inside the hanging mats and clean them. The barnacles grown to the size of drums that open and close with that resonant *thunk* that is the metronome for everything else. They are all part of the same economy: filter, graze, sing, hold, grow-over.

None of them built. None of them remember building. None of them will drown, because drowning is already done. Finished.

You press your back against the living wall that was once a temple or a granary or a house — the root-felt gives slightly, warm — and you feel through the water the whole tier thrumming. Creak of the Holders. Thunk of barnacles. Low OOM of a Singer far below, coming up the next terrace. Rustle of kelp without wind.

You are the only thing here that knows it was built.
