Who lives there is not something you count. At first.

From the trail, from outside the rim, you would say nothing lives there. That's the honest report. A closed tin can on a dead ridge. Inside, when the gate lets you in and the roar gets into your teeth, you would still say nothing — for a few seconds — because the proportion is wrong.

There is too much air for what there is.

A cathedral proportion. A desert proportion. You could stretch your arms in any direction and touch only vibrating heat and afternoon light. The first thing that lives there is that emptiness. An emptiness that has been kept.

And then your eyes adjust to the scale it was built for.

---

On the lowest level, the floor-level, what lives there is rust becoming soil.

It shouldn't. Steel doesn't turn to dirt quickly, but give it a century of sweating and weeping and being dripped on by its own weather, pit it microscopically, let wind drop a spoonful of granite dust a year, let one vine-leaf fall and rot, and another, and a thousand, and you have a thin black-brown skin over everything that is mostly iron oxide and mostly alive. Batter-thin in most places, a foot thick in the gutters. If you scraped it with a fingernail you'd smell iron and mushrooms.

Rooted in that skin, or rather holding that skin together — because which came first is hard to say now — is the dominant.

The Vine. It's not one species, but there it acts like one animal. Thick as a forearm at the base, then branching, thinning, throwing ropes up over boilers, across catwalks, hanging down in sheets that move a centimeter a day. Leaves like lacquered plates, dark to almost black-green on top, pale and sweaty underneath where they trap condensation against a pipe. They do not flower from tips. They flower from wherever a seam of heat tells them to. A knot swells on a stem pressed to a hot-water line, splits, and opens a heavy white-yellow trumpet you could put your head in. It will stay open eight or nine days, breathing perfume timed to the machine's sigh, and then close.

Nothing planted them. Nothing waters them. They found the gradients — here cold enough to wring water from air, here warm enough not to freeze, here a trough to follow — and they wrote themselves along those lines until the entire skeleton of the machine is veined.

Between the vines, where the drip is constantest, the smaller green lives crowd in.

Ferns in fist-sized rosettes that shiver with each piston stroke. Moss so saturated it squelches when the low thrumming shakes it. In the concrete cuts that lead everything back to the pump-house, hair-thin algae paint the flow-lines emerald. And in that one corner where someone, a long time ago, thought to lay river stones for no reason that is now remembered as decoration but was probably care — there the water is allowed to pause and be a river for thirty feet before it must go be coolant again.

In that ring-rivulet, which is only ever a foot deep and goes in a perfect, lazy circle, the watercress has gone wild. It chokes the channel. Under it, copepods flicker. On the surface, coin-sized lily pads hold perfectly circular rain caught from the beams above. If you kneel, the roar finally backs off enough to hear water ticking, and you see that the lilies all turn slightly toward the west gap in the rim where real sun comes in. They still know which way is sky.

That is the plant-life: opportunistic, waxy, patient. Not alpine. Lowland humid things that have learned to live on air that is technically too thin by living only inside the pocket the machine makes. They do not root in the mountain. They root in the machine's old age.

---

What moves through that green is, at first look, also not right for five thousand meters.

A beetle — or the form that has settled into being beetle-shaped — clacks along a handrail, slow as the flywheel above it. It is not pretty except up close: its carapace is oil-green and pitted, exactly the color of copper that's weathered by seawater. It stops every few steps, lowers its head, and scrapes. It's eating the fine algal felt directly off the metal. Behind it, in its grazing line, the steel looks briefly brighter. There are hundreds of them. You didn't notice at first because they move at machine-speed. A week's grazing by beetles will clean a pipe.

Above them, in columns of light that haven't been broken by dust in a hundred years, hang the gnats. Or what you call gnats because you have no other word. Midges the size of rice grains that do not swarm anxiously the way lowland gnats do. They hang absolutely still, suspended in warm rising air, motes in amber, only shifting when a dragonfly passes.

The dragonflies are why you know the place isn't dead.

They are impossibly large. You see one coming from the far side of the cylinder and it takes you a moment to understand scale: wing tip to wing tip wider than you are tall, four gauze wings edged in rust-red veins, body long as a canoe paddle. They should not be able to fly here — thin, cold outside air — but inside the held damp, the heated bowl makes a denser sea. And they fly differently than any dragonfly you've ever seen: one beat per second, two at a chase. A papery, percussive *whup, whup* that you can just hear under the basso roar, out of phase with it. They patrol. Their territory is volumetric. This one owns the empty cube between boiler three and the east wall. Its neighbor owns the space over the river-circle. They turn without banking hard, drifting, looking down with heads that are mostly eye. They never hit the slow flywheel. They have timed it the way gulls time surf.

Then the moths. As broad as your open hand, flat, ash-grey with undermarkings of sulfur yellow. When they land with wings folded they vanish into rust-scale and weld-seams; when they open, you see the false eyes that mimic rivet heads. They are sluggish in the early morning of the cylinder, when condenser drip is heaviest. By mid-afternoon, when the whole precinct has warmed three degrees, they blunder heavily from flower to flower of the vines, carrying pollen on furry legs that is the same grey as grinding dust. They live too long for moths. Four weeks, five. Everything here lives longer because it moves slower and gets eaten less.

And the pollen needs them, because the other thing that lives here is even less expected:

The hummers. Not birds. Not insects. Something between. You hear one before you see it — a true hum, higher than the machine, lower than a mosquito. It hangs in front of a vine-trumpet, round-bodied, the size of a thumb, fur that is not fur but dense, branched setae the color of old pollen, two pairs of wings blurred out. It inserts a tongue longer than itself and shivers. Heat from the adjacent pipe radiates visibly. The flower yields. These are the tenders. Without them, the vines would clone themselves stem-wise forever and slowly choke. With them, new vines seed in new cracks. They are as abundant as they need to be — maybe twenty — each staking a cluster of blooms, each sleeping tucked under a leaf with its hum stopped, which is how you finally find them when you look.

There are others you don't see but that live there just as surely: the fungus threading the moss mattresses, the pale mites in the river silt, the bacterial gloss that makes the handrails slick. The smell of iron-and-greenhouse is theirs.

All of them share two rules.

The first is *slowness*. A bloom that stays open a week. A beetle that steps once every ten seconds. A dragonfly patrol that takes eight minutes to loop the yard. Even the water in the circular runnel moves as if it is thinking about it, taking more than an hour to come back to where it started, brown and unhurried. It is the pace of pistons that need a full minute to breathe. Anything that tried to live fast here would be deafened out, or outrun its food, or burn up in air that only just sustains it. So the selection years ago was brutal and simple: what could slow down stayed.

The second is *staying*. None of what lives there needs to leave to complete itself. The vine drops its leaves into its own soil. The hummers feed only on those vines' flowers. The dragonflies eat moths and midges that were born in that same air. The beetles scrape algae that grows only on that metal, which sweats only because the heaters under them run, which stay unclogged because roots wedge their joints shut against frost-heave. It is a circle inside a cylinder placed like a cup on a barren table.

---

There used to be a third thing that lived here.

You can still find its molt.

The control cabin is high on the north curve, reached by a grated stair the vines have politely left half-clear. Its windows are gone, frames grown in. Inside, two seats face a panel of bakelite switches and dials whose needles still twitch faintly with vibration. Levers wear smooth wooden knobs. Each knob is gripped by a living bracelet of vine-stem, thickened exactly where fingers once squeezed daily.

Under plastic weighted by a stone, the logbook. Last page you can read through condensation spotting:

> *Exch. 3 retarded 0.04 rad. Condensate +4%. Growth NOMINAL.*

No distress. No goodbye. Someone came down off this roof one day because the shift ended, or because the team that tended the tending-machine was told to tend somewhere else, and the vines kept their shape-learned grip.

Humans lived here the way lighthouse keepers live in a light. Briefly, for function. The place did not need them to be beautiful, only to be adjusted. When they left, the adjustment continued by other means: root pushing seal shut instead of rubber replaced, beetle scraping pipe instead of brush.

What lives there now is therefore not *wild*. And not *tame*. It is *maintained*. Even without intention.

If you sit long enough by that ridiculous little river that circles to nowhere — the only place where the bulk of the machine blocks the direct roar and lets you hear ticks — you see the real occupant.

It is not the vines, not the dragonfly hovering like a kite with infinite patience. It is the whole arrangement pretending to be separate pieces.

Rim-keeping-wind-out. Stone-keeping-rim-up. Heart-machine-keeping-air-warm-and-wet. Leaf-mat-keeping-heart-from-cracking. Beetle-keeping-leaf-from-rotting-too-quick. Dragonfly-keeping-beetle-and-moth-from-over-taking. Hummer-keeping-vine-open-to-new-cracks.

Each needs so much empty space around it to exist that they looked lonely until you realized loneliness was the design. Low density is not failure at this altitude; it is how you keep from fouling the very narrow condition that lets you live at all. A sparse, roaring, steaming cathedral-garden built for big, quiet bodies that beat their wings once per second and listen to steel breathe.

Outside, the ancient, weathered, truly empty mountains continue with nothing to prove.

Inside the small, absolutely made, absolutely invaded, warm room nailed into the place where a mountain once stood and decided to keep only its outline — it is always, stubbornly, deafeningly spring.
