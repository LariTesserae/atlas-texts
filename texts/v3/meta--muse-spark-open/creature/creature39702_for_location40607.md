What lives here lives in water that hangs in the air.

That is the first thing to understand. On the Mesa, you do not live *on* the land, you live *in* the wet. The air is a habitat the way a reef sea is a habitat elsewhere - thick, warm, slow-moving, full. Everything that persists here has learned to drink without bending down.

### The Law of Unafraid

It starts with the small and the slow.

Isolation made them fearless, and the machines made them endless.

There is food everywhere, water everywhere, warmth everywhere, and no winter to punish waste. So nothing here is lean. Nothing is fast in the way a hunted thing is fast. Even the predators amble.

You see it first in the things that should flee and don't.

**The Mope Deer — or whatever they were before deer:**

Knee-high, no taller. Their legs too short for their barrel bodies, their coats not fur at all but a felted mat of hair and living moss and liverwort growing directly from the skin. When one stands still in the fern sea you lose it entirely - just another mossy hummock until it blinks. They move in loose singles or in mother-and-one patterns, never herds; there isn't territorial pressure to herd. They feed by wading, not grazing. Their muzzles are broad and soft-lipped, made for scooping mats of aquatic ferns and water lettuce off the tarns. Their feet are your second clue: splayed, almost amphibious, with fleshy pads that spread on peat and do not sink.

They have no musk of fear. You can walk to within three meters before one looks up, chews, looks through you, decides you are a new kind of warm rock, and goes back to drinking.

Their young do not hide. They lie in the open beside the water in perfect, damp stillness while dragonflies land on their noses.

**The Tarn Pigs:**

Not pigs. Not capybaras, though that is the shape your brain reaches for. Forty, fifty kilos of dense, hairless, slate-grey skin that is always wet and always cool to the touch. Their backs are perpetually colonized - lilypads germinate there, small frogs live their whole lives in the divot between shoulder blades.

There are three or four to every large tarn, because every tarn can only feed three or four. They are the engineers of open water. All day they wallow in slow circuits, churning the warm mineral broth that collects around the pylons, cropping the lilies that would otherwise seal the surface, keeping the water breathing. Their droppings are warm bricks that fall apart into clouds of red worms, which feed the fish that feed the birds. Without them the tarns would skin over and die.

They grunt rarely, a low *huff* that vibrates across the water. When you enter a tarn to your knees they surface nearby, nostrils flaring, eyes small and intelligent and entirely unbothered, to see if you are a new kind of tarn pig.

### Those Who Live Above Ground

Ground is an afterthought here. The true country is vertical, between six and fifteen meters up, where the trees flatten their crowns into platforms and everything else grows on everything else.

**The Green-Backs:**

The canopy rustle that never stops. At first you think monkeys, because you see hands - long, delicate, four-fingered hands with opposable thumbs on both front and hind limbs. Then one hangs and looks at you and the illusion breaks.

They are almost naked. Their skin is pink-grey and loose, like a baby's, except across the shoulders and spine where a short, velvety pelt grows that is not hair at all but filamentous algae, cultivated. It greens with age. The oldest males are saddle-backed in a deep, healthy emerald. They harvest it, you realize - grooming each other not to clean but to eat, scraping the algae with wide incisors, farming their own backs.

Their faces are flat and solemn, eyes huge for dusk-foraging, ears tuned to the humid air. They whistle, not chatter - true chords, two notes at once from a divided syrinx, that carry for a kilometer in the still. They build nothing. They weave nothing. They sleep in the bathtub bromeliads, curled inside a living pond with only their noses out, babies clinging like burrs.

They are neotenic, slow-breeding, long-lived. A troop of eight may range the same square kilometer for fifty years, mapping every fruiting orchid, every vine that will bear that season. They know the pylons as warm places where sleeping is good. You will wake from your nap by the wall to find one sitting two meters away, watching you learn how to be slow, and then, satisfied, moving on at exactly the speed the mist moves.

**The Air Itself:**

The birds are too many for a mountain. That is how you know the Mesa feeds them from outside.

Flocks of small things - finch-shaped, thrush-shaped, but glossier, wetter, colors too saturated, as if painted while still damp - rise and settle in cloth-like sheets. They nest not in trees but in the hanging fern drifts, where their nests are perpetually wet cups that never dry. Their eggs are speckled turquoise to match algae.

Above them are the hunters: white hawk-forms with wingspans twice what they should be, riding the warm lift the machines make even when there is no wind. They don't stoop fast. They sink, slow and inexorable, onto the canopy, and the green-backs go still but do not scream.

And the insects are not insects the way lowland insects are. They are the size insects become when air is thick and predators are lazy. Butterflies with wings like handkerchiefs, moving so slowly you can see dust fall off them, drinking not nectar but condensation off bromeliad rims. Dragonflies as long as your forearm, transparent wings ticking, hovering perfectly still for minutes to watch the tarn pigs. At night beetles the size of plums, black-green and varnished, blunder into the warm pylon fields and sing with their wing-cases - a low, electrical buzz that becomes part of the water-sound.

### Those Who Live Without Moving

Most of the Mesa's inhabitants do not move at all, and they are the ones that truly own it.

Every surface that is not open water is covered in what first looks like moss and then resolves into ten different strategies for the same problem: there is too much water and too much light.

The bromeliads are animals in the way coral are animals - whole colonies. Each one is a watertight rosette, a tree-dwelling lake. Inside each: mosquito larvae, tadpoles of frogs that never leave, tiny pale shrimp blind from evolution in a hundred thousand separate ponds inches apart, orchids that grow only on the inner wall of this one species, and bacteria that fix nitrogen out of the mist. Lift one and you spill an ecosystem.

The peat is not ground but a living thing half a meter deep - roots and fungal threads and dead leaves suspended in a sponge that has never dried. Step and it exhales. Cut it and it bleeds cold brown water for an hour. Things live entirely within it - white, eyeless millipedes long as pencils, worms as thick as your wrist that you never see but hear at night turning over.

And the lichen. The black sheet that comes first on any bare stone. It is not one organism but a pact between fungus and algae so old on the Mesa that it has become a third thing, a skin that can taste basalt and sweat acid and wait a hundred years for a crack to widen.

All of this grows on, and into, and politely around --

### The Ones That Still Work

They are not alive the way the deer are alive. But they live here more completely than anything else, because the place is an expression of them.

The pylons do not have inhabitants. They have attendees.

The pale material sheds water because it vibrates at a pitch that water cannot hold. Touch it - warm, faintly giving, like dense cartilage - and your fingerprints disappear off it in a perfect, beading recoil. Put your ear to it and you hear no engine, but you feel in your teeth a low, contented thrum, rising and falling over hours.

Around their bases life clusters, the way life clusters around a vent on the seafloor. The lilies there are twice as wide with faint light under their pads because they are photosynthesizing a little of the pylon's leakage. The tarn pigs prefer those tarns. Algae grows thicker. The air above them shimmers even on cool mornings. They are gently warm to ten meters out, and in that ten meters orchids bloom out of season and never stop.

The grid lines - those faint, kilometers-long geometries softened under peat - are not inhabited either, but they *decide* where inhabitation happens. Follow one and you notice water runs parallel to it, not over it. Trees align. Even the monkeys travel along them, as if the slight kindness in gravity at the rim, the slight thickness of air, is easiest there.

They are still holding the table up. Still wringing moisture out of cloud sea below and exhaling it through stone. Still keeping high air hot and wet and heavy when it should be thin and killing. They have been doing it so long that evolution here has forgotten any other climate.

What lives here, then?

Everything that can live in a greenhouse that has been left sealed and tended for ten thousand years after the gardener left.

The deer that is a hill. The rodent that is a pond. The primate that farms its own back. The frog that never touches ground. The fern that lives on air. The insect that is too big and too slow to be afraid. And underneath and through all of it, patient, humming, shedding water forever, the old machines - not dead, not awake, just working - who made this high, impossible summer and keep making it, breath after breath, while the forest drips.
