They don't live here the way animals live in a forest.

There is no chorus. No canopy to hide in. Nothing here can afford to be numerous, or noisy, or quick. What lives here lives *thinly* — spread across such a distance that abundance and emptiness are the same measurement.

If you stand still long enough for your ears to adjust past your own blood, you start to sort them by heat before you sort them by shape.

First heat, because on the plateau that is what size is:

### The Furnace Phylum

Everything here is warm-blooded, but not the way you were taught. They don't make heat as a byproduct; they make it as their business. Their metabolisms are combustion processes, slow oxidation furnaces behind ribs that never quite became ribs.

**The Forgebacks** you have already met at a distance. Up close they resolve wrong.

They aren't black. In the near-dark you thought they were black plates — but the plates are a temper color, oil-slick blues and purples over iron oxide, flaking at the edges like old boilers. Six legs, but not in pairs. A tripod stance at any moment, the other three resetting with a hydraulic sigh that puffs alkaline dust off the ground. Their heads are almost nothing — a low, blunt cowcatcher of mineralized horn housing the intake grinder, two deep-set eyes the size of dinner plates but milk-white and nearly blind. They don't need to see. They navigate by barometric pressure and the infrasound of other vents.

Watch one feed:

It does not bend to eat. It walks *over* a hemisphere shrub, and the shrub disappears under its chest where a ventral maw opens — not jaws, but three counter-rotating radulae of basalt-tooth. You hear glass cracking. Sparks spit sideways and die in the damp air. The whole animal shudders and then quickens. The lateral stacks along its flanks — six, eight, twelve depending on age — dilate. Cherry light leaks from between them. Then: *huhh-SSH.*

A venting. Too loud to be breath, too rhythmic to be voluntary. Superheated steam carrying fine soot jets out horizontally for a meter before it cools into a cloud that smells like wet iron and hot batteries. Inside, through the seams where the back plates overlap like roof slates, the gut-kiln briefly goes incandescent. You feel it on your face from fifteen meters away.

What comes out behind is not waste in the way dung is waste. It is slag — a segmented loaf of black glass still pliant and steaming, dropped without ceremony while the animal keeps walking. It will crack as it cools into hexagonal columns, identical to the salt flats but smaller. For the next hour it ticks.

Nothing else this big moves like this. They never run. When two herds pass each other a kilometer apart, they synchronize venting for a few minutes — a rolling, plain-wide respiration — and then fall out of phase again. Not communication. Thermodynamics. If they don't dump heat now, they'll cook themselves later when they climb toward the thermal lane.

Young are different. Calves are born fully plated but thin, their vents undersized. They whistle constantly, unable to hold pressure. They run — actually trot, clumsy — alongside the mother and overheat every twenty minutes, forcing the whole group to stop while the calf sprawls, splaying its belly plates flat to the cooler dust to bleed heat into the earth. The mothers wait with infinite industrial patience. You can see the heat mirage shimmer above them like idling trucks.

And predators have learned the schedule.

---

**The Stilt-Cutters.** If the Forgebacks are locomotives, these are transmission towers that broke loose and learned to hunt.

Three meters at the shoulder, but most of that is leg — four legs, each joint reversed twice, tipped not with hooves but with wide ceramic pads that distribute weight so well they leave only faint crescents on the dust. Pads that also sense vibration. They can feel a Forgeback's piston-heart from two kilometers out, transmitted straight through bedrock.

They are bone-white where Forgebacks are black, not pigment but actual exposed high-alumina ceramic, grown like teeth. The skull is a narrow vise, no snout to speak of, just two interlocking shearing blades that meet with a tolerance measured in microns. They don't growl. Their mandibles, when excited, vibrate at ultrasonic frequencies and produce that lathe-whine — a clean, rising *weeeeeeENK* that makes your molars ache before you realize you've heard anything.

How they kill is rude:

They don't chase. They intercept the thermal lane the herd wants and plant themselves downwind, folding until they look like dead radio masts. They wait hours. Days. When a Forgeback passes within striking distance — usually a calf, usually the one still whistling — the Stilt-Cutter unfolds with a speed that betrays how much stored elastic energy was waiting in those legs. It doesn't bite the throat. It drives its head to the softest seam it can find, under the flank where a vent opens, and clamps the lathe-jaws around the lip of the valve.

Then it drinks.

Not blood. Coolant. A superheated slurry of ammonia-water and dissolved iron, pressurized to nearly critical, which is what carries metabolic heat to the Forgeback's stacks. To the Cutter, it is both food and torment — scalding fuel that lights up its own internal ceramics orange-hot for minutes after feeding. You see this at night: a long silhouette shuddering while its throat glows through like a forge-tong snatched from a fire. The Forgeback rarely dies immediately. It stumbles off, one stack crippled, hissing continuously on one side until bacteria finish what the wound started.

Adults Cutters are solitary; juveniles sometimes hunt in twos, learning how to clamp opposite vents so the victim can't roll. That's the only teaching they do.

---

But neither of those are the commonest life. The largest animals are simply the ones you notice first.

If you crouch, if you get your face near the warm, damp dust, the density corrects itself.

**The Precipitators** don't count as herds or hunters. They are furniture, until they move.

From far off you might take them for more black brush — low, domed, inert. Then one twitches in a wind that isn't there.

When fed and unfolded, a mature one is two meters across. The body is a squat, heatstone cylinder half-buried in the salt pan, ringed with cilia that look like rust-black wheat. From its crown it raises two to four sails — not membrane but branching vanes of carbon whiskers, electrostatic arrays ten microns thick, humming faintly as they charge. In the day you can't see them work. At dusk, if you put your hand near one you feel your hairs lift. Dust streams sideways toward it in lines.

It is drinking air. Not just water from the condensation sweats — though it does, and you can see droplets beading on the downwind edge of each sail and trickling down to a gutter-mouth — but ions, ash, aerosolized sulfur exhaled by the planet itself. The crackle you hear is particulate discharge. Every few minutes the whole organism convulses softly and swallows what it's gathered, the sails flicking clean. Its underside glows faintly green through translucent rootlets where chemosynthetic symbionts burn the harvest into sugar.

They cannot walk in any meaningful sense. When a pan dries permanently, they retract the sails into a hard black bud and pull their cilia underneath, extruding acidic salts until they slip free of the stone like an old tooth, and then roll. Wind pushes them. Sometimes for weeks. They bounce off Forgeback legs, collect against canyon walls, get stuck in scree. Most die rolled on their sides. One in four finds another shallow alkaline mirror, re-seats itself with a wet sucking sound, and unfurls again within hours.

You can tell age by how pitted the base cylinder is. The oldest ones have grown so heavy with precipitated trace metals that the salt around them forms a raised metallic crust, coppery and slick. Smaller things live exclusively on that crust.

**The Spinners.**

Size-of-dogs was not quite right. Size-of-elongate-dogs-with-no-front-legs would be better. One muscular fore-body dragging a soft, overgrown abdomen like a foundry sack. The head is a blunt dome of fused plate housing a spinneret the temperature of a stove burner.

They don't lay webs. The "hot wire" is literal — a filament of silica-iron glass drawn molten from their own gut, thumb-thick at the extrusion and cooling rapidly as they back away, stretching it thinner and thinner until it is hair-fine and brittle. They string it in long, purposeless lines across open flats: fifty, a hundred meters, anchored on brush hemispheres or cooling slag or each other’s older lines. No trap pattern. Just lines.

Why? Two reasons, both industrial:

First, once cooled, the filaments are hygroscopic. Dawn condensation beads on them like dew on a fence. The Spinner returns at first light and reels the wire back through a buccal comb, stripping water and trapped ash into its mouth, swallowing the glass itself to be re-melted and reused. It is farming dew across an acre with an acre of intestine.

Second, sound. Each line sings when the plateau's endless, tired wind crosses it. A different pitch based on tension and length. The Spinners park themselves in the middle of a little harp field and feel strumming through their abdominal skin. Footfalls of a Stilt-Cutter two dunes away register as a dissonant twang. An approaching Forgeback herd is a chord change from kilometers off. For an animal with no eyes bigger than pinheads, hearing the world through its own cold-laid telephone wires is perfect.

Their dung is coils of broken, cold wire that gather in windrows and eventually sinter into mats of native glass. In certain basins the ground crunches underfoot from centuries of it.

---

Underneath all of this — literally beneath — are the ones who do the quietest, largest work:

**Cinder-Lice.** Not lice. Fist-sized, slate-grey pillbugs with magnetite scales, each foot a tiny ion-exchanger. They swarm fresh Forgeback slag within hours of it dropping, while it is still too hot for you to touch. Hundreds to a single loaf. They don't eat the glass. They rasp its surface with manganese-rich radulae, aerosolizing the black exterior so the wind-bacteria can oxidize it faster. Their bellies pulse with stored charge. At night they detach and mill aimlessly, glowing faintly violet where abrasion charges their backs. When you disturb a pile, they don't scatter like insects; they clatter and curl into armored marbles that roll downhill and keep going until friction stops them.

In the riverine threads, where water pretends to flow, there are **Reed-Mouths** — amphibious things the size of house cats, all hinged mouth fringed with baleen made of hollow keratin tubes. They anchor facing upstream (though there is no stream) and pump canyon air through the tubes, condensing moisture internally even without standing water. When true water does appear, they bloat grotesquely and release clouds of tadpole-fry that are transparent except for their kiln-stomachs, visible as red dots.

On the lichen-fields, mites graze that are themselves bioreactors — their guts contain consortia that fix nitrogen at temperatures that would denature any Earth enzyme, and their feces is raw nitrate fertilizer that allows the next generation of lichen bloom to creep a centimeter farther across the cliff.

And always, everywhere, the invisible: thermophilic films slicking every rock underside, oxidizing iron, sulfur, hydrogen seeping from the mantle vents a kilometer down. That warmth you feel on your skin not from sun but from stone — *they* are why it's warm. Planet-scale metabolism.

None of them look *made*. If you find a Forgeback skull after years of exposure — plates weathered thin, tusks of iron-silicate fallen free — it looks like a natural fossil of something that just happened to grow a boiler inside it. Which is exactly what it is. There was no leap from animal to machine. There was only a plateau where being able to store heat, vent steam, draw wire, precipitate ash was rewarded every generation for fifty million generations until anatomy forgot how to do anything else.

If you lie down out here — and you shouldn't near anything large when it vents — and press your ear to the cracked salt, you won't hear industry. You will hear one Forgeback's distant piston, one Spinner's wire humming D-flat in the wind, the faint, oceanic crackle of a Precipitator field discharging the morning's dew, and underneath all of it the patient, almost subliminal tick of a thousand Cinder-Lice turning yesterday's slag into tomorrow's soil.

Not silent. Not loud. A sparse, thrumming equilibrium that has been holding itself in tune longer than any tool has ever existed.
