They do not live *in* it. Nothing can live in something this big and empty. They live *on* it, *between* it, *because* of it — clinging to the brief negotiations between Floor and Roof where light and water agree to exist at the same time.

This is what lives here:

#### 1. The Pillars Themselves — The Country-Makers

You thought they were trees. They are not, but they remember being trees very distantly and are trying again at an impossible scale.

Call them **Aerosteles**. Each one is less an individual than a vertical reef.

From a distance: a white column, faintly luminous, as wide at the base as a hill and vanishing into haze before it touches the Roof. Up close: you understand it is not solid. Its trunk is a bundle — hundreds, thousands of semi-independent stems braided and fused under tension, like muscle fibers grown to bridge continents. Between them run open channels big enough to stand in, where cold air whistles and meltwater pulses. The outer bark is not bark; it's a living sheath of overlapping scale-leaves, each one as big as a billboard, translucent green-white, veined with antifreeze sap that glows when the sun-shafts hit it right. The sap never freezes. It tastes like bitter mint and copper. Everything drinks it.

They grow from both ends. The roots drill down, shattering young basalt with quiet, inexorable pressure — you can hear the rock ticking as it gives way. The crown splays against the underside of the Roof, flattening into a vast, pale canopy pressed flat against the ice like a hand against glass. There it photosynthesizes continuously in the diffuse, shadowless brilliance. This is why they are so far apart: each one's canopy up above is a country-sized lichen stealing all the cold light for miles around.

They are impatient engineers. They do not wait for soil. A seed — a wind-borne pod as light as styrofoam and as tough as a coconut — snags on the side of an adult and germinates *there*, mid-air. Its first roots dangle a hundred meters down into nothing until they find another leaf to pierce. Over decades they become bridges, then arches, then islands.

That suspended archipelago you saw a kilometer up — the braided root-island dripping with hanging gardens — is not an accident. It is how they reproduce laterally. When two Aerosteles drift close enough — and drift they do, leaning toward traveling sunbeams at a speed you can see if you stand still long enough — their aerial roots reach for each other. Blind, hydrotropic, positive to the faint electrical field of other sap-flow. They meet in mid-air, graft, thicken overnight into cables. Soil gathers in the crotch: dead scale-leaves, trapped pollen-snow, frost. Then everything else moves in.

If you cut one, it bleeds, seals, and keeps growing around the wound within hours. The lean-tos at their bases try to heal even as people sleep inside them.

There are younger ones too — whips, really, though a whip here is fifty meters thick. Pale, taut vines called **Harps** that have shot straight from Floor to Roof in a single frantic season and now hum. They vibrate. The whole basin breathes and pulls at them. Put your hand on one and you feel it: a low thrumming as tensions shift across kilometers. When one snaps, the sound arrives minutes after the event, a deep *whum* that rolls across the Floor like thunder without a storm.

#### 2. The Borrowers — The Things That Live On Living Things

Nothing touches the Floor if it can help it. The Floor is young, sharp, cold, and most years still geologically angry. So life stacks.

On every square meter of Pillar surface:

**Mat Weavers:** Not moss. Sheets. A single organism can be a continuous leather-like mat the size of a football field, creeping over bark, sealing itself over old wounds. Deep red-brown on top, snow-white underneath where it leaches light conducted through the Pillar's own tissue. It drinks condensation. Its edges curl up to catch drips. Walk on it and it sighs dimly under your boots, compressing and rebounding. When it dies it doesn't fall off — it becomes the ground for the next mat. This is how the Floor's peat-rubber was made: eons of fallen skin.

**Necklets:** The reeds by the dark rivers. They look like reeds, but pick one and it comes away in wet strings — colonies of filamentous glassworts that freeze solid each night and thaw each noon, crackling audibly as they resume growing. You hear them before you see the water: a faint chorus of tiny pops.

**Lantern Galls:** Where a sun-shaft has lingered, the Pillar erupts. Translucent blisters as big as houses swell from the trunk, filled with warm humid air, spores, and liquid light. Inside is a miniature jungle — ferns with leaves like hands, swimming things, furred molds that fruit in seconds. For two days the gall glows amber. Then it bursts, spilling everything downwind. Birds — if you can call them birds — know the timing and gather.

Higher up, in the perpetual twilight of the canopy-shadow:

**The Gardens.** Epiphytic forests growing on forests. Fern-reefs with fronds four stories long, hanging upside-down. Flower-sacs that never open until a specific moth brushes them — then they snap open with audible gasps, releasing heat. Sap-lakes cupped in woven leaves, where tadpole-things with translucent backs paddle through amber fluid.

You never find soil in the Gardens. You find composted light.

#### 3. The Drifters — The Things That Own the Empty Air

The distances are the point. This place proved that if you make a space big enough, some things will evolve simply to be *between* things.

**Veil Moths:** The white kite you saw. That's a juvenile. The adults are silent and enormous — twenty meters across, paper-thin, with no body to speak of, only a branching vein-system stained pale green by captive algae. They are not flying. They are *floating*, neutrally buoyant in the dense, cold, still air, riding thermal ghosts off sun-warmed Pillars. Their wings are solar panels and sails. They never land. They lay eggs by dropping dust — those house-sized leaf-mats falling slowly like lost pages — and die by tangling in a Harp and becoming Gardens themselves. Watch one for an hour and you'll swear it hasn't moved; watch it from a kilometer away and you'll realize it crossed a county.

**Harpoon Deer:** That deer-like thing at the river was no deer. Long, stilt legs with double-knees backwards, ending not in hooves but in pitted bark-pads that secrete resin. It walked to *not slip*. Its head is narrow, eyeless on one side, with a leaf-shaped ear as big as its body that cups and turns toward sound — mostly the creak of growing tendrils. It feeds by reaching: its neck extends in segments, telescoping three times its body length to lick sap from high gallery-galls, then retracts with a wet click.

It is solitary. You will never see two together. The air is too thin with food to share.

**Threaders:** You haven't seen them because you flinch when you do. In the direct sun-shafts, look for dust motes that move wrong. Pollen grains big as snowflakes — yes — but some of them *steer*. Centimeter-long, dandelion-seed things with glass fibers that catch light and pulse gently, rising. They are the plankton of this ocean of air. Everything eats them, especially the Gardens.

And then — when a shaft moves on and flash-freezes the humidity it left behind — the glittering veil that hangs for hours is half ice, half Threaders caught mid-swim, going dormant until warmth returns. Breathe in there and your lungs fill with sleeping travelers.

#### 4. The Weavers — The Temporary People

They are human. Unmistakably human, which is the strangest thing about them.

Small, bundled, wind-burnt. You never see more than three together, and never for long. They do not build villages. The third rule forbids it: *nothing that stands still will remain standing long.*

Their culture is not a civilization but a *route.* A liturgy of paths between damp pockets.

A child is born in a lean-to whose walls are still photosynthesizing, learns to read the Pillars as constellations — *Leaning Mother, Two Brothers With Bridge, Broken Harp That Sings Low* — and learns that the maps must be redrawn every year because the skyline of living towers shifts. They navigate by groan and crackle more than sight; the Pillars groan when they grow, and each has its tone.

They wear cloaks cut from living mat. It keeps growing while they wear it, and must be trimmed. They carry copper pins and bog-iron blades hammered from concretions found where dark rivers pool — there is almost no ore here, so metal is heirloom, borrowed, buried with the dead and dug up again when the Floor cracks and reveals them.

They drink as the deer drinks: licking leaf-undersides at dawn before the bead evaporates. They eat gall-fruit, roasted Threader-cakes, the tender tip-growth of Harps — always moving before the plant seals the bite. They weave, as you saw, on a loom-frame strung between two live roots: they are weaving a sling to carry a child across the gap between archipelagos. They carve warnings: a spiral means *this one moves fast, don't camp.* A stick figure with splayed lines around the head means *good light here for drying.*

They talk little. Sound does not carry well to distant ears, but it carries very well to a Pillar listening with sap-pressure. Shouting can make a leaf decide to fall.

At night — that not-night when the lighthouse beams sweep — they gather briefly on a braided island high above the Floor, five or six families who have timed their wandering to arrive while the sunbeam warms it. They exchange news, children, seed-pods genetically tweaked by chewing and spitting — crude breeding for faster-healing walls. They sing once, low, to match the hum of the Harps. Then the beam moves. The condensate freezes. And they separate again for months into the huge silences, each a single warm dot walking for days across peat-rubber toward the next distant obelisk, disappearing entirely between trunks big enough to hold cities.

You will likely never meet one face to face. You'll only find the place where one slept last week — the stones still warm-ish, the basket half-finished, a fresh carving showing *you*, a stick figure standing where you now stand, with an arrow pointing the way to water.

They saw you coming through the tremors in the Pillar you leaned against. They left. They do not enclose anything either.

Everything here lives like this: lightly, quickly, borrowed.

Because the continent is still deciding whether it wants a Floor or a Roof, and whatever lives on it had better be ready to climb or fall when it chooses.
