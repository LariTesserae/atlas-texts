Nothing with eyes lives here.

That is the first thing you have to understand, and the hardest to hold onto once you see how green it is. Green tricks you. Green means hare, means deer, means beetle under the leaf. Not here.

Here green means green only. It means a place has been left empty enough, cold enough, dark enough, and wet enough that the animals gave up first — and what stayed behind learned to live without them.

What lives here is a carpet. But carpet is too flat a word, too made. What lives here is a *pelt* grown over the rock, and under it and into it, until you cannot tell where stone stops and organism begins.

Start high on the walls, where life gets its first and most tenuous hold.

On the bare, vertical limestone and the black basalt sweating cold water, the pioneers are not pioneers so much as stains. Crust lichens — grey-white on grey, invisible when dry — map the rock in continents you only see when the mist comes in. Then they flush. Ochre, sulfur, rust-red, a violent pale green like old copper. They do not have roots. They *are* root and leaf and mouth all at once, eating light directly through their skin and drinking straight from the air. On this young, sharp rock that has not yet learned to make soil, they are the first to say: *stay*. They etch acid into the stone, microscopically, pitting it, making a dust of mineral that the wind cannot quite carry away because the wall is always damp.

On that dust, the second wave catches.

Moss. Not one moss but a congress of them. Alpine cushion moss that grows in domes tight and springy as upholstery, each shoot packed against the next for warmth the way penguins huddle. Woolly fringe-moss that looks dead — grey, frizzled — until ten minutes of drizzle turns it emerald and soft. Slick liverworts, black-green and translucent as seaweed, plastered over every seep where water runs constantly. They have no true roots either. They have rhizoids, hair-fine anchors that find a crack and hold. They don't need soil because they barely need ground; they build it as they go. Each year's growth dies underneath and becomes a thin, acidic peat, half an inch thick, centuries in the making. On that, the next millimeter grows.

This is the bulk of the resident population. By weight, by area, by sheer stubborn biomass, this pocket is a nation of bryophytes. They do not stand up. They hunker. Cushion and mat are not just growth forms here, they are survival strategies: stay low under the wind that tries to peel you off, hold water like a sponge when the rain is only mist, keep your own dead beneath you for insulation against the 5-degree air. Touch one of the big mattresses on the floor of the bowl — where the talus has briefly stopped moving — and your hand sinks in and comes away cold and wet, and the moss sighs back slowly, unharmed. Walk on it, when you can find a place stable enough to walk, and it is like walking on a saturated animal.

And woven *through* that moss, not *on* it, are the higher plants — though higher is a joke. Nothing here gets higher than your boot ankle.

There is dwarf willow, *Salix herbacea* and its cousins, but you would not know it was a willow unless you knelt and parted the moss with your fingers. Its trunk is underground, a twisted, woody cord creeping sideways through peat and grit. What it sends up is two glossy round leaves and, for two weeks in August, a catkin the size of an ant. A whole tree, complete, the size of a coin. Next to it, moss campion makes its own hard pillows, so tight and dome-shaped that wind slides over them, so dense that inside the cushion it can be ten degrees warmer than the air outside. It blooms absurdly — sudden pink stars on the gloom — but it does not bloom *for* anything. There are no bees up here. No butterflies stray into this dark well. When the saxifrages open — white, five-petaled, on stems as thin as thread — they do it for the wind, or for themselves. They self-pollinate in the cold, folding in on their own pollen, because waiting for a visitor is a kind of death.

Tufted hair-grass is the tallest citizen. It manages perhaps fifteen centimeters, in tussocks that shiver even inside the pocket, where you swear there is no draft. Its blades are wire-fine, rolled inward to keep water in. It grows where the pelt has stabilized just enough, on the oldest slumps, anchoring with a real root system that finally, after lichen and moss have done centuries of work, has something to bite into.

None of them are individuals, really. That is not how you live in a room where a door-sized slab can peel off the wall every week and bury you. They are clonal, colonial, endlessly vegetative. A rockfall hits — that clean gunshot crack you hear — and a white scar appears on the east wall, and a smear of green is gone under talus at the bottom. For a day the scar is bare rock. By the third day, if you are patient enough to watch, you see the edges are already bleeding inward: liverwort from the wet seam above, a thread of willow finding the new dust, moss protonema like green frost. In a season the scar is gone. In two it never happened. They do not reseed so much as *re-creep*. A fragment torn off and tumbled ten meters downhill will reroot where it lands. Being eaten would be a disaster — there is so little light to replace tissue with. Being crushed is expected, and survivable, if you can grow sideways.

Under all of this, invisible unless you bring a lens and know to look, is the last layer of inhabitants: the ones that make the system close. Black yeasts in the lichen. Fungi threading white through the peat, digesting what never fully decays in the cold, returning it. Cyanobacteria in the sheen on wet basalt, fixing nitrogen straight from the damp air because there is no animal manure, no deep soil to provide it. Tardigrades, if you want to count them — eight-legged water bears — walking through the moss forests like slow bears indeed, and rotifers and mites smaller than a pinprick, the only animals that never left. You will never see them. You will never hear them. They do not buzz or rustle. They drink the film of water around a moss leaf and go still when it freezes.

That is what lives here: a light-starved, animal-free, self-making sponge of lower plants and dwarfed higher ones, living off bounced light and wrung-out cloud, living *on* each other, living through being constantly torn and buried and regrown.

It has no need for sun. It has no need for soil, because it makes its own out of itself and shattered stone. It has no need for sound. It grows in the industrial roar of wind and falling rock as if it were silence, dense and even and impossibly lush in the blue dusk, softening every sharp edge it touches but never, ever taming it.

If you put your hand flat to it, you can feel it cold and alive at once.
