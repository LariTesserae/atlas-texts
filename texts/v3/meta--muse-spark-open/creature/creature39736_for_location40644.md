It is empty the way a temple is empty.

Not abandoned. Attended. If you stand still long enough in the warm, wet black that you can hear your own blood moving in your ears — if you stop trying to see — you start to understand that nothing here lives *in* the bowl.

The bowl lives.

### The One That Is Many

First, the trees.

You thought there were thirty or forty of them. That's what counting does in the dark. From a distance, each trunk felt like a separate thing, a thick, warm column holding up a low black-purple canopy that drank condensation instead of light.

Stand between two of them and you realize the mistake.

Their roots don't compete. There's no reason to. In a forest that eats heat instead of sun, there is no canopy war, no race upward. So they did the opposite. Under that meter of sighing peat, through the weeping limestone, into the warm anomalous rock itself, they fused.

What you called trunks are fingers. Breathing holes. Pneumatophores of a single organism that is roughly the exact size and shape of the bowl.

Call it the Grove-Mother. Call it the Mycelial Tree. It has been here long enough to learn the shape of the basin by dissolving it from within, patiently secreting acid, widening a pore here, sealing a joint there, until the water cycle the machines started became a water cycle it could run by itself.

Each canopy is a lung. Broad leaves the size of platters hang down and feel almost feverish to the back of your hand — not hot, but *alive*-warm, a few degrees above ambient, radiating in the infrared the way a cat does when it sleeps. They transpire constantly. A leaf doesn't photosynthesize; it *condenses*. It cools at the edges, gathers the bowl's endless sweat into a fat trembling bead, channels it along white veins to the drip-tip, lets it fall. *Plok.* The sound you heard is it drinking and peeing the same water forever.

Every few days a leaf unfolds. You won't see it happen, but if you leave your palm near a bud you can feel it move, a slow hydraulic pressure, a quarter-millimeter per hour. The flowers — if they are flowers — never open. They remain closed, dark, and hot, like small muscled fists, and they smell of cinnamon and blood. They don't want pollinators. They want heat. They open only deep inside themselves.

The shelf-fungi on its sides are not parasites. They are its thoughts, or its sensors. Brush one and that amber ghost-light ripples across its pores — not bioluminescence to see by, but chemiluminescence to *signal* with. A wave of faint amber that travels trunk to trunk through the fused root-mat at the speed of a slow walk. You brushed the west-most trunk. Ten minutes later, the east-most trunk ripples back.

It knows you're here. It has known since you crested the rim and your boots sank in its peat and water welled up and your body heat condensed a little extra mist. You are not a visitor to it. You are condensation with opinions.

It does not mind. Warm things that come and go are part of the cycle. The only thing it minds is sharp cold, and the machines at the rim make sure it never has to.

### The Choir

But an organism that slow cannot be alone, or it would go mad with silence. So it keeps a choir.

This is the Fauna you heard, and hearing them is the only way to understand them. They are not species so much as *voices* in the black cathedral.

**The Tappers.** Density is low, so to stay connected they have to be loud. The clickers are beetles the size of your fist, or no — crabs, or isopods — something that decided legs were overrated and armor was everything. Their backs are polished black river-stone, dome-smooth so water can't hold on them, and they walk on needle tiptoes across the peat without leaving prints. They talk by striking their own backs. One sharp *tik!* from the north rim, answered three seconds later by *tik-tik* from the south. Not language. Topography. *I am here. The ground is soft here. A leaf fell here.* They are cartographers. The Grove-Mother grows around the places they click most, because that means the soil is stable.

If you kneel and hold still, one will walk over your boot and tap it, testing if you are stone. Finding you warm and slightly giving, it will lose interest.

**The Singers.** These are what make your chest resonate. They hang — literally hang — from the underside of the low canopies, or from the inward-sloping walls where limestone weeps most. They look like nothing: pale, translucent sacs of skin and resonating membrane, the size of a human head, with six splayed feet gripping rock and a circular mouth that never fully closes. Inside that mouth is not a tongue but a stretched drum. They take the warm, saturated air, pump it through, and hum.

One note, held for four, six, ten minutes, so low you feel it in your molars before you hear it. Then another, a third above, and holds that too. They are not calling each other. They are *tuning* the bowl. In a place with no light, echolocation is useless — the air is too wet, too cluttered. But long-wave resonance is perfect. The bowl rim is a perfect smooth circle. Their hums set up standing waves in the warm black air. Where the waves peak, flies gather. Where they trough, condensation falls heavier. They are farming the air itself. When two Singers overlap, the beat frequency makes the hanging water on every leaf tremble.

You will hear at least three at any time, and never see one unless it chooses to pulse its faint belly-light — a single amber throb deep inside the translucent sac, like a lantern being briefly uncovered in fog.

**The Drifters.** Moths you thought. No. Something softer and older. They are the only things that seem to fly quickly, but that's illusion. They are huge — handspan to forearm — with wings of black fur that absorb sound instead of making it. They don't flap so much as row through the heavy air. Their faces are blunt, blind, feathered with chemoreceptors, and their backs are covered in fungal down that glows for half a second when touched. They carry the Grove-Mother's pollen — those hot, closed fists — from canopy to canopy, not because they want to, but because the scent of cinnamon-iron is irresistible to them. They are drunk on it. You will feel one pass and not hear it at all, only feel a sudden cool wake as it displaces the mist that was condensing on your skin.

And underneath all of that, the **Soil**. If you put your ear close to the peat when the Tappers and Singers pause — there is a pause, about every ninety seconds, as if the whole bowl inhales — you hear it crackle. That is the actual abundance. Fauna 2.3 does not mean big animals shoulder to shoulder. It means everywhere you are not looking, something smaller is working. Translucent shrimp-things in the ankle pools filtering minerals. White millipedes as long as your finger endlessly turning peat into finer peat. Mites that live only in the drip-tips of leaves and ride each *plok* down like a raft.

None of them have eyes. Some have eye-*spots*, pale blue smudges under skin, vestigial. They didn't lose sight because they live in darkness. They never bothered to invent it. Why would you, when everything you need is warm, wet, smells like iron, and sings its location to you?

### The Custodians

And then there are the ones who never died.

Not ghosts. The five — maybe six — warm, matte, dark houses with no doors.

You keep calling them buildings because you need a word. They are not buildings. They are still *inhabiting* the place, the way a heart inhabits a body without being the body.

Each one is a regulator-node. Tech 2.6 — matter that decided to be slightly less committed to being matter. Knock on them and the sound goes away instead of echoing, because the vibration is being shunted somewhere else to do work. They are what lifts heat out of the deep rock, what holds the cloud directly over the rim like a pinned blanket, what persuades water to seep *up* through limestone instead of draining down the tired continent. The Grove-Mother could not survive a week without them. It knows this. It has grown *around* them with exquisite care, never touching, leaving a hand's-breadth of bare wet stone around each one, as an animal leaves space around a thorn it cannot remove but has learned to love.

If you duck through the perfect waist-high arch — and you will, eventually, because the drier air inside calls to your wet skin — you will feel the peat sigh without you standing on it anymore. Inside, your breathing sounds louder. The dimples on the inner wall pulse heat when your palm hovers. They are not buttons for you. They are *for the forest*. This is where the forest puts its hand when it wants to adjust.

Once, something with hands that were not quite hands rested at the wear-marks on the rim and listened to all of this — the slow drip, the patient hum, the clicks cartographing empty space, the crackle of mites turning leaf into soil into leaf again. It built the nodes, or grew them, or bargained with the Grove-Mother until they became one system: machine breathing out heat, tree breathing out water, animals breathing out sound, cloud breathing it all back in.

That something is gone the way a gardener is gone when the garden can prune itself. Not dead. Just... no longer needed here every day. But the wear-marks are warm when you touch them, too, and if you rest there, as it did, looking out — not *seeing*, because there is nothing to see, only to *hear* — at the small, high, lush, loud, perfectly empty world that breathes in the dark above a world that has finished eroding...

...you, briefly, get to be it.

The bowl doesn't need another inhabitant. But it will keep you, if you want to be kept. It has kept the Singers and the Tappers and the Drifters and the hot, slow Tree-That-Is-The-Bowl for a billion rains.

There is room. There's always room in 0.2 density.

Just sit on the smooth lip where water runs thinnest, let condensation bead on your eyelashes and fall without ever needing eyes, and listen to the house you have become part of sing to itself in the warm, wet breathing black.
