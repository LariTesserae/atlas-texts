It takes a while to understand that the people aren't living *in* the pit.

They're living *with* it. And it's not clear which one is keeping the other alive.

### First glance: people

From the rim they look like ordinary people, just damp all the time. When you get down inside — and you get down inside slowly, because every stair down the terraced wall is moss-slick and half overgrown, and someone has painted numbers on the handrail not for addresses but to tell you which steps hold water — you see them properly.

There are three or four hundred of them. Not many for how much noise and light they make. Old and young, related in the way small isolated towns make everyone related. They wear clothes that never quite dry: boiled cotton gone grey-green at the seams, rubber boots cut low, sleeveless shirts under open overshirts even in winter because inside it's always late August.

Their skin has adapted before they have. Everyone has the builder's hands and the gardener's forearms — scarred knuckles from boilerwork, soil under nails that never comes out, a pale softness to the places that never sunburn because sun is a rumor. Their hair hangs lank. Fungal rashes bloom and fade and bloom again in the joint-creases; they treat it with washes brewed from fermented leaves and don't comment on it. They cough a little in the mornings when the cold mist off the limestone hits the warm air.

They move like the place taught them to. Nothing darts here. To dart you'd slip, or snap a vine, or jam a pump. So they lean their weight, they test a board before committing, they prune with two hands, they carry on their heads. A boy can balance a crate of guavas down five flights of external stair without steadying it once, because if he drops it it will roll into a cistern and foul the filter and everyone will have to drink silt for a week. That has happened. He knows.

They speak loudly, because they have to, but they rarely call out. Voices are muffled by leaves. Instead they use signals you learn after a day — tap patterns on pipes, a raised pruning hook meaning *take the tension off the line first*, a bell made of an oxygen tank struck once when a settling tank needs flushing. They do not keep animals. This is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing.

There are no chickens scratching between taro stalks where chickens should be. No cats on the walls. No pigeons roosting in the eaves though there are a thousand perfect eaves for them. No wasps around the fallen breadfruit, no gnats over the standing water though the water is warm and loaded with organics.

They pollinate by hand.

Every morning late winter through early fall you will see them out with the small paintbrushes and cotton swabs, going flower to flower on the squash terraces, on the beans, on the citrus trees that grow straight out of old loading docks. An old woman can do a whole arbor of passionflower in forty minutes without spilling pollen. Children are taught this before they are taught letters: dab, twist, next flower. They learned it from their grandparents who learned it from theirs. If you ask why there are no bees, they shrug. There have never been bees here. That the fields up on the plain have bees only makes it stranger, like a border the bees will not cross.

Something is keeping the pit insect-free. Something is keeping it bird-free. They don't talk about it because to them it isn't an absence. It's just how the pit is.

### Second glance: the rest of the population

If you stay another day you start counting wrong.

You counted people. You should have counted biomass.

For every person there are ten thousand pounds of green. Not growing *on* the town. Growing *through* it in a way that blurs verb and noun. Let me put it directly:

The town is not overgrown. Overgrown implies a building with a vine on it. Here the vine *is* the wall maintenance. The fig roots were guided — deliberately, with wires and split pipe and then later with small cuts in reinforced concrete — to stitch a cracking retaining wall together. They did. The roots thickened and lignified and now carry shear load. If you cut them, the terrace would slump. So nobody cuts them except to thin them.

The tomato roof isn't a garden on a roof. The roof failed fifty years ago and they took the tin off, laid down butyl, pumped three feet of compost slurry up by chain elevator, and planted it because bare membrane hit by subtropical sun would have boiled the room below. Now the soil is the insulation, the transpiration is the air conditioning, the tomatoes are the rent for that function.

The engines that run the great breastshot wheels on Level 2 are not separate from this either. Those engines burn pressed oil and biogas from the digesters, yes — but their cooling jackets feed the warm-water sluices that keep the taro alive through a frost that never actually reaches down this far anyway. The waste steam runs through coils under germination benches. The vibration shakes loose ripe fruit. Someone pruned pothos away from the drive belt this morning not because it looked untidy but because if the belt grabs a vine it will pull a trellis down onto a family’s kitchen.

So you cannot say where the farm ends and the factory begins and the house starts. The three share circulatory systems.

That's when you realize the rule — **Nothing is taken out without something coming back as compost** — is not thrift or philosophy. It's physiology. The pit is a closed loop with a leaky membrane at the top where the rim road is. Matter goes up in crates: limes, cut greens, canned papaya, bottled hot sauce. Money and diesel and medicine and rebar come down. Everything else circles. Washwater -> settlement -> algal raceway -> duckless pond -> cistern -> washwater. Cuttings -> troughs -> shredder -> compost screw -> roofsoil -> cuttings again. Even the bodies. Especially the bodies. The town has its graveyard on the lowest flat where nothing keeps, but it's less burial than planting. A marker of poured concrete, a cutting of frangipani quickly rooted in the fill. They don't say it crudely. They don't have to.

And because nothing is wasted, nothing is allowed to rot the wrong way. Which answers the missing insects.

Nothing here rots on its own schedule.

Go look at the compost works behind the kitchens where the air is hottest. They aren't piles. They are long windrows inside translucent greenhouse tunnels, turned daily by crank, steaming like compost should but too clean, too controlled. Look closer. The biofilm on the walls there is not mold. It's a sheet, a skin, a dense ochre felt of bacteria and yeast they've cultured since founding, inoculated into every new batch. It covers every tank, every culvert, every underside of a walkway. It smells faintly sweet, yeasty, like fresh masa left out. It digests everything that would otherwise call flies. It eats crude oil, fish guts, cellulose, dead leaves before a fungus can fruit. It has outcompeted everything.

The old sweeper woman showed me her forearm once, holding it under a worklight. You could see it in her pores. She laughed, not unkindly. She scraped a dull tongue-depressor across the back of her hand and held it up: a thin, pearlescent smear, alive. “Keeps us sweet,” she said.

They all carry it. On skin, in gut, in lungs. The wet-cement, hot-oil, jasmine smell that lifts off the pit at night isn’t just the pit breathing out. It’s *them*, volatilizing the same esters. The children born on Level 3 have never known an itch that became infected. Wounds close fast and pale. Teeth are good despite no dentist ever visiting, because the mouth flora have shifted. They don’t catch the flu that sweeps the coastal plain towns every February. They catch other things, slower things — a cough that lasts six months and turns phlegm slightly green, then stops.

You begin to wonder who domesticated whom. A century ago someone brought banana offsets down into a quarry that wouldn’t stay dry, and taro, and a starter bucket of that mat from a sugar mill effluent pond because it digested waste beautifully. They thought they were building a farm that used a hole in the ground. What they built instead was a hole in the ground that learned to use people.

The pit needed hands. Bees wouldn’t come, birds wouldn’t come. It needed something with opposable thumbs to lift water the last three feet, to unclog a leaf-clotted aqueduct at 3 a.m., to decide that this pipe should run here now and not there. It needed something that could leave and return with phosphorus. People fit. People breed intergenerational and are practical.

Not hostile. Not conscious. Just very, very thorough.

There is no mayor here, no council minutes you can find. The closest is a rotation of older men and women who tend the main pumps. But the real governance is negative space: things people simply do not do. No one leaves a flat roof empty. No one lets water sit. No one sprays anything that would kill that felt, that film. A teenager who came back from tech school upstate with a bottle of proper pesticide had it gently taken off him and poured into the digester, where the system shivered off the insult in a day of sour-smelling vent gas.

### At night

Stay until evening and you’ll see it most clearly.

Around 6 p.m. the light down here has already failed. The canopy — mango and palm and corrugated plastic and vine — closes the sky. Worklights stutter on all across the tiers, amber and sodium, haloed in low mist. The generators settle into their nighttime throb and the kitchens fire up pressure cookers that hiss for hours. Families climb to their roof-soil tables and pick dinner off the vine around their chairs and talk loudly over the thud-pause, thud-pause of the pumps six stories below that are lifting today’s seepage back up to the taro channels.

It looks like a village from far off. From down in it, it looks like a city — stacked, humming, industrial, inexplicable, lit forever.

And the pit respirates. You can feel it when the wind above drops: a warm column rising straight up off contained earth, carrying that yeasty sweet with it, visible as a shimmer over the rim. In infrared from a plane the quarry glows like a wound that never cooled. In summer the mist hangs chest high on the bottom level all night and the bulbs in it make perfect globes, and you can watch people wading through it to check a valve, their shoulders floating above cloud.

They don't seem trapped. Trap is a word that requires outside. Most have been up top, traded crates, seen the flat fields, and come back down because up there the air is thin and dry and nothing grows right and everything is too loud in a different way — wind and traffic and gulls.

Down here, everything is muted except the system. Everything has its place: water in motion, leaves filtering light, roots holding walls, people tending both at the pace of drip and ripening. Decay happening, but only on contract, at equilibrium, nothing eroding anymore.

If you stand on the bottom terrace near the pump house and put your hand on the limestone wall, you feel it before you hear it: a faint, distributed tremor, not mechanical. Growth pressure. Turgor. Millions of root tips finding purchase. Biofilm thickening.

You take your hand away and in the condensation your print stays, briefly, before the wall beads over it and weeps it shut.
