What lives there has never needed to be anything else.

Not a colony. Not an invasion. A congregation that arrived by accident and stayed because staying became possible.

### The Holders

All of them are plant. Or plant-adjacent. Or what plant becomes when you subtract sun and season and competition-by-eating and give it a million patient years to renegotiate.

They don't live *in* the chamber. They *are* the chamber's second skin. If you peeled them off the walls, the rock underneath would be raw, pink-grey and shocked by exposure. With them on, the room has lungs.

**The Carpet - the mosses:**

They are the dominant citizens. Not one species, but a treaty of three or four that have learned to grow *through* each other instead of over.

* **Black-green mat moss** - dense as felt, saturated to saturation, each shoot no longer than your thumbnail. It holds 20x its weight in cold water and never lets it go. If you press it, it doesn't spring back quickly. It remembers your hand for an hour, then forgets you slowly.

* **Rust-green cushion moss** - grows in domed hummocks along the lower walls where drips land most often. Its tips are always amber with new growth that never browns. It fruits constantly but its spores are abortive — fat, dark capsules that never open, just swell and reabsorb. Reproduction by fragmentation only now. A piece breaks off, lands an inch away, becomes.

* **White-green ghost moss** - the one that only ever knew this darkness. Pallid, almost translucent, its leaves are too thin for pigment. It grows deepest, under the other two, and where you peel them back you find it glowing faintly — not with bioluminescence but with the lattice-light it has stored, conducted cell to cell like a rumor.

They layered themselves three and four deep and stopped competing. Upper moss drinks condensation and shields lower moss from desiccation; lower moss anchors upper moss to stone. No one wins. That is how they all live.

**The Paint - the lichens:**

Where moss can't grip — too smooth, too vertical, too close to the warm rings of tech — the lichens have taken the patent.

Flat rosettes, coin-sized to hand-sized, slate-grey and sulfur-yellow and a black so matte it eats the 0.1 dark and makes it 0.0. They are the oldest. You can tell by how precisely they fit the pits in the mineral-skinned concrete, growing not *on* it but *flush* with it, as if poured together.

They don't grow. They *accrete*. A millimeter a decade. Their borders with the mosses are razor-sharp, negotiated chemical lines: *you exude this acid, I exude that antibiotic, we agree.* No overgrowth. No war. A map redrawn every few years by committee.

They photosynthesize nothing. They chemosynthesize the faint ionization the lattice exhales — the after-lightning taste in the air. Their algal half and fungal half have stopped being able to live apart even in theory.

**The Supplicants - the ferns:**

Stunted, glassy, reverent. They grow only from cracks where concrete meets basalt, only where an anchor point or a seam in the console bleeds the warm field.

Their fronds are small — no longer than your hand — and they never unfurl fully. They stay in a permanent fiddlehead crook, translucent, veins traced with living ice that never quite melts, never quite grows. Translucent because chlorophyll is vestigial now; their green is diluted with a new pigment, near-black, that drinks not photons but the lattice's permission.

All of them lean. Not dramatically. Two or three degrees off true vertical, toward the center of the room. If you stood in the exact center where the lattice hangs, you would be in the focus of a hundred slight bows. Not worship. Tropism. The only sun they've ever known is that knot of impossible geometry at chest height, so they bow to it the way sunflowers bow, quietly, without choice.

They are the only ones that attempt sex. Once or twice a year — if year means anything here — a frond will release a dust of spores so fine you only see them when a drip disturbs them. They hang in the still air, they slow near the lattice, they almost stop. Most resettle on the moss below and do nothing.

**The Curtains - the algae and liverworts:**

The lowest citizens, and the water-bearers.

Filamentous algae hang where the ceiling dips, curtains of green-brown hair each bead strung with condensation. They don't photosynthesize; they *condense*. Their whole metabolism is a wick. Water forms on them, beads, runs by capillary down into the liverwort mats below.

The liverworts mat the floor in a continuous, leathery sheet. Dark olive, rubbery, pocked with tiny cups that catch the 15-minute drip and hold it like offerings. They are the gut of the pocket. They filter, they store, they evaporate just enough to feed the algae again. Riverine with no river — just one bead's endless journey from ceiling to floor to air to lattice to ceiling.

No rot. That's the uncanny part. In a bog this dense you should smell peat, tannin, anaerobic decay. You don't. You smell cold green and clean stone. Because nothing dies fast enough to rot, and nothing eats the dead. A frond that fails doesn't get consumed; it bleaches to white, flattens, and becomes substrate. Moss grows over it within months, incorporating its ghost. There are no animals to make decay matter — no springtails, no mites, no fungi in the animal sense. Only growth upon growth, patiently preserving even its failures.

### The Other Resident

And then there is the lattice.

It is not alive the way the moss is alive, but it lives in the chamber longer and more completely than anything green.

It doesn't grow. It holds. It hums in your molars. Frost refuses it. Dust respects it. Water climbs it.

The plants lean toward it. The condensation braids toward it on the console. The whole damp cycle orbits it like a slow, cold heart that never beats.

You could call it tech. You could call it a gardener that forgot it was built and became weather. It is post-material, which here means it has achieved what moss achieved: it stopped needing to be new.

It does not notice you. It permits you, the way it permits the fern — a little extra warmth to distribute, a bootprint to eventually erase.

If anything *rules* this pocket, it is that negotiation between green that learned to live without light and a thing that learned to give light without shining.

Hold cold without freezing.
Hold dark without extinction.
Hold damp without flood.
Hold old without decay.

That is what lives there: a lush, crowded, silent agreement in the dark, still growing because nothing has told it to stop.
