Who lives there lives *with* it, not on it.

Because nothing can live *on* something like that for long without becoming part of the weave.

### The Tenders

They are still people, in the way that people remain people when you take away everything people think they need.

You won't see them first. You'll walk a mile-wide plateau and think it is empty — fern meadow, two motherpillars holding up the sky, mist ticking down from leaf tips — and only later realize the meadow *is* tended. The ferns are all one height. The vines that make the far wall hang plumb, unknotted. The pool in the axil-leaf is clear, its lip pruned clean so it doesn't rot.

They appear out of that maintenance.

Small-boned. Not stunted, but thinned and lengthened by vertical life. Men and women both around your chest high, narrow-hipped, long-armed. In dim amber light your eyes are almost useless; theirs are huge, not nocturnal huge, but dusk-adapted — amber irises flecked with green, a faint tapetum that catches when spores glow at night and makes their eyes seam briefly lit from within. Skin very pale under a thin olive wash, because sun never strikes hard here. It is always damp, so their hair never quite dries; they cut it short with stone flakes or let it mat into felted ropes where orchids take root. Many older ones have seedlings sprouting from those ropes and don't pick them out.

Feet tell you the most. Always bare. Soles broad, blackened, callused into something closer to bark than skin, toes splayed and prehensile enough to grip a wet root-column. Their ankles click softly when they walk — a thickening of ligament from a lifetime of standing on slopes that breathe. Their lungs are loud when you get close. Everyone here breathes like they are breathing through a wet cloth, because they are. The air is water. Children learn not to cough at it. Old tenders develop a deep rattle and say it means the mountain likes them.

They wear almost nothing woven. Why weave when leaves will volunteer? A single great leaf, cured over low smoke until pliable, folded and pierced and worn like a poncho. A skirt of living moss that must be watered. Ornaments are practical: ear plugs of polished beetle chitin to dull the drip, necklaces of bone hooks for splicing shoots.

They don't have chiefs, or cities, or history in the way you mean it. A village is a knot — 8, 20, maybe 40 people — where several root-roads happen to stay stable enough for a generation. Their huts are not built, they're *convinced*. You bend four sapling-pillars together and tie them with live vine, tuck a flowering bromeliad on top for a roof and tell it where not to grow for a few years. Fire pits smolder continuously in shallow bowls of clayish humus, not for heat — it's never cold — but to make smoke to keep the mimic vines confused, to cure leaves, to keep midges off sleeping infants.

Their whole technology is tending. Stone adze, bone awl, fire, patience.

Every morning someone walks the section. Not patrol — pruning. She cuts here so an aerial root does not find purchase and seal a path shut by evening. He redirects a shoot-tip with a splint of thorn so in ten years it will become a new handrail. A child sits for an hour and picks the white mouth-fungus off the underside of a beam before it can fruit and drop spores that rot lung. If they stop, the mountain heals over them. Paths close. Huts are swallowed. People disappear not killed but *grown over*, found months later sleeping peacefully inside a new wall, breathing slowly through green.

Predators for them aren't fanged. The mountain doesn't need teeth. 

It sends the Handrail Vine, which learns the exact brown-green of treated wicker and waits to look like support until your weight is on it — then cinches. It sends the Kin Flower, a door-sized bloom that opens nowhere near any colony and exhales precisely the warm milk-smell of a specific dead grandmother to coax you off a tier edge. It sends the soft rot that takes up residence in your boots, then your toenails, then the webbing of your toes, until you stop wanting to move.

The prey is balance. They eat what the columns offer — the structural fruits that swell straight from pillars, starchy and faintly sweet, always in season somewhere; the shelf-fungi that can be peeled in sheets; the pools of axil-water strained through moss; occasional grubs split from boring-beetles the size of hounds. Nothing dies quickly here. Everything is slow, wet, fermentative. Even grief is tendsed rather than performed.

They don't believe they built this. Ask where the mountain came from and they'll look at you puzzled and say "We grew it," the same way you'd say you grew your fingernails. Grown and ungrown is the only distinction they make. A wild strangler fig that throws a buttress across a chasm is ungrown — meaning no one has spoken to it lately, trimmed it. A hut-wall that flowers when you brush it is well-grown. For them, natural and artificial are the same word, because both imply something other than attention, and nothing out here survives without attention.

Children are taught early about the fold.

"Don't take the short path back," an elder will say, pressing a thumb into a boy's palm. "The short path back is longer going the other way now."

Because that is normal to them — that a drop-line falls slower the further it falls, that a spore cloud can make a perfect right angle around empty air, that if you shout down a root-chasm your echo comes back from above. The oldest among them, who sit still longest, can feel the terraces shift — not movement, but *re-spacing*, as if the mountain inhales and distances stretch like sap. They navigate by smell and drip-pattern, not map. They know places where parallel walls meet if you stare long enough, where a lower terrace hangs inverted in haze above you, because the mountain still remembers being a seed the size of your fist. Inside itself, it is still small. It folds to stay small.

They revere that folding without worshipping it. They leave offerings not to gods but to quiet points — those places where spores orbit nothing — little knots of braided root and hair, to mark: *here, space is thin, step carefully.*

### The Others Who Live Between

The tenders are sparse. That is the rule of this lung-world: immense surface, mostly void. But the void is not empty.

High between terraces, riding the perpetual warm updraft forced up the stair by the white cloud crown, are the Kites. From below you think they are leaves. When one passes low you see it's an animal — or a plant-animal — a yard, sometimes two yards across, translucent as jellyfish skin stretched over a frame of carbonized veins, rippling without flapping. No head that you can find. Just a ventral mouth that sifts pollen and spore and steam. They never land. They are born aloft, die aloft, snagging finally on a curtain-vine to dissolve and feed it. Silent. The tenders call them *the laundry*, because to see a fleet of them is to know weather is changing higher up.

On the verticals live the Grazers. Soft-footed, sloth-slow, six-limbed things the size of cats with sucker-pads and algae-combs for faces. They spend entire lives on a single pillar the width of a house, licking the green film that grows on bark, never needing to drink, never touching horizontal ground. Their droppings are little dry pellets that smolder if they fall into a fire pit, prized as incense. If you watch one long enough you realize it has perfectly straight horizontal stripes — not camouflage, but tide-marks of how high it has climbed over years, darker where the seep is richer.

Inside the wood, in the half-living, half-dead laminate beams that make up the cliffs, are the Borers. Beetles like lacquered dogs, black and iridescent green, mandibles slow as vices. You hear them before you see them, a low *creek-creek* inside a terrace wall. They chew galleries big enough to crawl in, and their backs are ecosystems — miniature forests of lichen, mites, flowering epiphytes that bloom and die in days. The tenders leave them alone unless a load-bearing pillar is threatened, then they smoke them out gently, harvest the grubs — blind, fat, sweet — and patch the hole with mud and prayer. To kill a borer wantonly is ungrown behavior.

And the leapers. Every visitor loves and fears them. Gliders — lemur, maybe monkey, maybe something that converged on human from a different branch entirely. About the size of a child, gray-pelted, pink-nosed, with flat, solemn faces that look uncannily like a tender infant's. Membranes stretch wrist-to-ankle, furred, veined. They live on the lip of the world. They climb five hundred feet up a dangling air-root, test the wind with whiskers, and throw themselves outward into nothing. For a long count there is only falling — a beautiful, terrible slow fall where gravity seems to forget itself halfway — then the membrane catches and they become kites themselves, surfing sideways for a half-mile down to the next curtain of vines where they snag and cling, panting. Rarely do they vocalize. When they do, a single fluted note, pure as quartz, that hangs in the wet air for seconds after they are gone. The tenders say that's how they say *I am still here*.

Deeper, where horizontal becomes theoretical, in the nation-sized hollows under your path where roots hang like stalactites and never touch bottom and the wind comes out changed, there are things that may not be animals at all. The tenders do not name them. At night, when cooled air exhales spores in bioluminescent clouds, you see it plainly: some spores orbit an absence. Some drip sideways. Some reverse and climb into a crack that wasn't there yesterday. There is fauna that lives in the fold itself — kink-birds, the old women call them, not birds — distortions that catch light at an angle and look for a blink like an eye made of distance. They do not eat or breed as you understand eating. They browse on geometry. You leave them a wider berth than you leave a loose vine.

All of it is quiet.

That is the final trait that binds every resident, human and not. In a true jungle, sound competes. Here, moss and mist and endless wet timber swallow it. The dripping is constant, arrhythmic, so omnipresent your brain edits it out until it stops — which feels like deafness. Movement is reverent, slow. Even the waterfalls that feather from tier to tier over moss do not crash.

So you learn to listen for what is missing. A gap in drip means a body just passed. A glider's note means a terrace edge is near. The creak of living wood adjusting under its own impossible weight means the distance to the far pillar tomorrow will be a few feet longer than today.

If you stay long enough — if your boots rot and your pack sprouts white rootlets from the seams and the condensation on your eyelashes beads and stays beading without falling — you stop hearing silence as emptiness. You start to hear it as occupation. As the sound of room being held open by green, warm, dim, patient attention.

And you understand that the mountain did not make dwellers to inhabit it.

It made inhabitants to *keep it inhabited* — to keep pruning, redirecting, remembering, so the hollow cathedral doesn't close, so it can keep trying, terrace by impossible terrace, to weave itself tall enough to finally touch, and become, the cloud that never leaves its crown.
