They live low.

That's the first thing you notice, after you've been here long enough to stop looking for other things. You came in expecting to see people shaped like the plateau - tall, weathered, mountain people. And they are weathered, but not tall. Nothing vertical lasts long up here except stone, and even the stone gave up.

The people are short and broad, without exception being an error in diet or birth. It isn't malnutrition - they are solid. The air is thin and cool and damp and it doesn't carry oxygen well, even when it carries water so faithfully. So chests are broad and deep under layers of felt and oiled canvas. Shoulders set a little forward, as if forever carrying a bucket or ducking a low doorway. Legs are thick, calves corded from stairs. Because everything is stairs. The city is scoops and terraces and roofs that became streets that became roofs again, and you go up and down a hundred times a day just to fetch tea. Old people here have knees like tree burls. Children learn to climb before they learn to speak loudly.

And they are quiet. Not silent - they talk, they argue, they call to each other across an alley - but their voices are pitched low and don't project. Sound dies in moss and thin air. A shout becomes a soft halloo two houses over. So they have learned to be close when they talk, to touch. Hands on forearms, foreheads nearly together under the awning while the drizzle frets down. They don't waste breath.

You see it in the skin first. Paler than you'd expect, even with the cloud-dim. Not bleached, but a bloodless pallor, the pink showing only at cheeks and knuckles that are always a little chapped. They live under cloud and under moss-eaves and inside the layered houses; ultraviolet is a rumor. Their eyes are dark - brown, slate-grey, deep green, almost never blue - and slightly narrowed, always, against drizzle and spore-haze. Hair is kept short or bound tight, for practical reasons. Long loose hair over here is how you cultivate a second scalp of lichen. Most adults shave their heads or crop to stubble, or braid it severely back and oil it. The children have fine, damp hair that never quite dries.

They dress like the buildings: layered, patched, grown-over.

No one owns a single good coat. Everyone owns five bad ones. Wool felt, woven fern-fiber, goat-hair, oilskin cut from old condensate sails, sweaters knit so densely they're almost armor, all of it in the colors the plateau gives you - slate, rust, moss-green, sulfur-yellow, soot-black. Everything overlaps. Scarves that are also filters. Gloves with the fingertips cut because you need the dexterity for the little machines. Hoods that pull up with a sewn-in ridge to shed drip. Goggles pushed up on foreheads - not for sparks, but for the hours when the cloud dips right into the basin and every gutter exhales that fine, spore-rich mist that makes your throat itch and your eyes water. You see masks too, simple damp cloths, but mostly people just breathe shallow and get on with it.

And they are always tending.

There is no profession here that isn't tending. Tender, keeper, warden, minder - the words change from quarter to quarter but the meaning is the same. A woman who spends her morning pruning the living insulation out of her wall cavity, packing the harvested lichen into cakes for fuel or feed, then spends her afternoon minding three chattering water-wheels that run the lathe that makes parts for condensate traps. A man who herds the pale, long-legged roof-goats - strange, quiet animals with big lungs and narrow faces and feet like mossy slippers, who eat turf and sour tubers and give thin, sweet milk - and in the evening solders wire braids by amber bulb-light while his wife scrapes condensation channels clear with a reed. Children with little hooked knives, on their knees, shaving moss from the roof that is also their street, because if you don't, it holds too much water and the weight collapses the room below where your grandmother is mending a loom that has a rowan tree growing straight through its frame.

They don't see a contradiction in that. The tree has been there longer than the loom's warranty. Its roots follow the old oil runnels because that's where warmth was. Its leaves are oiled, its bark has grown around a gear. They just work around it. They oil the bark now, too.

That's how they live: like moss themselves. Low, dense, layered, opportunistic, patient. Nothing is new and nothing is wasted, so neither are people. An old body is a cistern and a workshop and a garden. The dead are not buried deep - you can't, the slate is too close and too sacred in its oldness - they are laid in the turf-walled ossuaries on the windward ridges, where the living felt takes them quickly and the names are kept on slate plaques that exfoliate a letter every winter. The living don't mourn loudly. They plant something over.

The other things that live here live the same way, which is why there are so few kinds.

The high crows - you hear them more than see them, huge, ragged birds the color of wet slate, with white barring on the primaries. They don't caw. They make a low, wooden knocking sound, a *tok-tok* that carries a little better than a voice. They glide between terraces on the ridge wind and watch. They are not pets, not pests, not prey. They are understood to be inspectors. If you leave food out, they will take it and you were careless. If you are sick on a roof, they will circle until someone notices and comes.

The goats are half-wild even when tended. They know the paths better than people and resent fences. Their eyes are that strange horizontal amber and they stare a long time. Their milk and their felt and their dung for the tuber beds are why the city can stay dense. Every block has a herd of six or eight, sleeping huddled in a moss-heated byre under a house that hums with spindles.

And under everything, the burrowers. You rarely see one. A glimpse of pale, almost translucent fur, pink hands, a blind snout, the size of a fat cat but longer, dragging itself through a drainage culvert at night. They live in the turf-mattress that swallows the town, tunneling, eating roots and grubs and the cellulose of abandoned paper. People hate them with a quiet, constant hate because they break cisterns. They set little chattering traps that don't kill so much as pinch and hold until a child can come drown it in a bucket. The burrower corpses are rendered - nothing wasted - into oil for the tiniest gears.

But really, the dominant life here isn't animal or human. It's the green felt and its invisible partners.

The moss comes first - three or four species so intermixed you think it's one. One deep emerald and springy, one black-green and slick, one pale and feathery that fruits with red pinheads after drizzle. It grows several millimeters a day in the good damp. It holds ten times its weight in water and muffles every footstep to a hush. The people cultivate it as much as fight it. Cultivars for roofing, cultivars for wall-insulation, cultivars that are edible with enough boiling, cultivars that are grown specifically to be scraped off and pressed into paper. The children know the taste of each.

Lichen is second - the rust and sulfur crusts that look like paint until you see them creep. They excrete a faint acid that digests basalt grain by grain. The people seed them into mortar on purpose; a lichen-mortared wall flexes, self-heals, breathes. The old masons can read a wall's age by which lichen has taken it.

Ferns third - stunted, leathery, waist-high at most, fronds like sawblades crowded into every crevice where brick meets stone. They are not pretty. They are aggressive. Their fiddleheads are harvested like a crop, bitter and iron-rich. Their spores are why everyone wears goggles at cloud-dip. In late summer the basins fill with a fine cinnamon-colored haze and you learn to keep your mouth closed.

And beneath and within all of it, the things you don't see but smell - the wet iron, wet wool smell. Bacteria and fungus doing the real work of turning stone to soil, soil to peat, peat to methane that is caught in little black bladders on roofs. The whole plateau smells faintly of fermentation, like bread and blood at once. If you scrape a handrail and come away green, and you will, what you're smelling is not dirt. It's life digesting the mountain that forgot how to be a mountain, very, very slowly.

The people understand themselves as part of that digestion. Not at the top. Not separate. The city breathes when the gutters breathe, dims when the cloud thickens and the wheels slow. They prune and harvest and mend and carry buckets, endlessly, not because they love work but because the agreement requires it: you may live dense and warm and humming in the scoops of the old world, on the back of the old world, but you must keep the green from drowning you and keep the water moving to the wheels.

They are good at it. They have been good at it for generations. Their hands are broad and damp and stained green-black at the cuticles. Their houses are hills that remember how to be houses. When they sleep, the moss grows a fraction over their doorsills, and they rise in the dim, shadowless morning and scrape it clear again, without resentment, as automatic as breathing in thin air.

They live like something that has decided, collectively, to stay.
