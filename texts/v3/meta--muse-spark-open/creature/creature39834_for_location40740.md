It is all alive. Not *inhabited* — that would imply there was a place first, and then things moved in. Here the place and the population are the same system. The rock made a cold trap for water and light, and life became the plumbing to move it around.

There is nothing sapient here. Nothing ever needed to build a wall because nothing here ever needed to keep the cold out — they *are* how the cold is kept out. What lives here are all variations on one invention: the furnace-body.

### The Mat — The Base That Isn't Just a Plant

What looks like groundcover is the bottom of the food web and the top at once.

It's not a plant. Call it **felt-lichen** if you need a word. A chimera of photosynthetic bacteria, antifreeze lichens, and fungal felt all braided into a single organism that decided not to be individuals. It grows as a continuous, continent-wide mattress, knee-high, violet-black to sickly green when lit from within by its own trapped heat.

Each frond ends in a glassy bead of glycerol and sugar-alcohol - literal antifreeze. It doesn't need soil. It roots into basalt with acid feet and drinks directly from the air. Under the permanent white sky it photosynthesizes sluggishly, but constantly; there is no night to stop it. Its real trick is insulation.

Lift a corner and you find the world underneath: still, humid, 25 degrees warmer than the air above, smelling of hot iron and black earth. The mat's interlocked leaves form a vaulted ceiling that holds in every calorie the animals below leak, and its underside sweats continuously. That sweat is the rivers of this continent — not flowing, but hanging in droplets, running down fungal threads into basalt hollows where it waits to be stolen.

The mat is farmed, grazed, burned, fertilized and re-seeded by everything above it, and it allows it. Cut a trench through it and within weeks the edges seal over with fast-growth white felt, stitching the wound shut.

It even grows on skin.

### The Furnaces — The Megafauna Who Are Climate

They are the reason the plateau can be called lush at -50C.

The largest are **Pillar-Furnaces**, sometimes called Drift-Slabs by the few researchers who have seen them from orbit. Low, wide, flat as barges, the size of a house or a small ship, carried on 20 to 40 pillar legs that end not in hooves but in broad, heat-proof pads like oven mitts. Their backs are shaggy with living felt — the same mat, adapted to grow in furrows in their chitinous shell, rooting in their dead outer skin and kept alive by their body heat. From a distance you can't tell where hill ends and animal begins.

They don't eat. They *process*.

Watch one drink: it parks over a secret melt-pool, lowers a belly-curtain of soft trunk-tubes, and begins to pump — *chuff-CHUFF... chuff-CHUFF...* — a sound so deep you feel it in your molars. At the same time its dorsal vent cracks open a foot wide and exhales a tower of steam thirty feet high that snows out instantly into glittering fallout. Inside for a second you see red: kiln-chambers, bellows-lungs the size of rooms, peristaltic guts lined with rust-colored enzymes shuffling black-green pulp along.

It intakes:
- condensation water
- tons of felt-lichen, vacuumed up by a rasp-underlip that peels the mat like carpet and leaves raw, smoking basalt behind that will be recolonized in months
- thin cold air, super-oxygenated by being compressed through turbinate nasal furnaces in its head

Inside it runs a biological Haber-Bosch reaction. Bacteria in its gut fix nitrogen using the pressure and heat of its own metabolism. Its blood is not hot — it is *boiling*, contained under pressure like a geothermal plant, pumped through counter-current heat exchangers in its legs so the feet don't melt nor freeze.

What comes out is:
1. Sustained radiant heat — a Pillar-Furnace throws a warm shadow for fifty yards. The mat around it grows twice as thick.
2. Steam, which becomes snow, which becomes insulation, which becomes tomorrow's melt-pool.
3. A trail of hot, black, tar-wax shit behind it, excreted as a molten sheet that cools into a hard pan. It's pure energy — partially refined organics, nitrates, lipids.

Everything else lives off those three exhausts.

Pillar-Furnaces never hurry. They drift at inches per hour, then stand steaming for days while digestion catches up. They communicate not with calls but with pressure releases — long whistles, sighs, metallic clangs as an internal valve sticks. The whole continent thrums with it because sound doesn't die in this air; it bounces between scarps until it becomes weather.

They are packed. You seldom see one alone. Where one finds a good condensation fracture, twenty converge, shoulder to chitin-shoulder, sharing a pool, their combined heat creating a temporary greenhouse where lost stones hover a little too long and steam hangs in impossible flat sheets.

This is their migration: not seasonal — there are no seasons in the white light — but hydrological. The herd is a slow liquid seeking low places where the rock still weeps. Over a year their combined mass curves a mile. Over a century they carve warm caves in the young basalt just by standing there, caves that smoke after they've left.

When one dies — and it takes decades — it does not fall. It settles. Its furnaces cool, its back-mat grays, and its body becomes a hill that stays warm for years as the bacteria inside keep composting it. Every death births an oasis.

### The Trimmers, The Drinkers, The Tappers

In the forest of pillar-legs lives an entire secondary ecology that treats furnaces like moving mountains.

**Trench-Grazers:** Dog-to-pig sized, six-legged, with a wedge head and a translucent dorsal dome. They are the only thing that can eat fresh felt fast enough to matter. They run in endless lines between the giants' legs, chewing trenches through the mat and leaving clean furrows that immediately fill with warm, humid air. Their backs are clear so you can see their own little furnace glowing pink inside — a smaller, faster engine. Vents along their flanks jet excess steam constantly, so each one looks like it's smoking. Their dung reseeds the mat with spores.

**Wax-Slurpers:** Flat, limbless, almost slug-like things the size of a forearm that exist solely to chase Pillar excreta. When a Furnace lays a hot tar trail, they swarm it in hundreds, rasping it up before it hardens and sealing what's left with their saliva into a lacquer that the mat can grow over. They are janitors. Without them the continent would pave itself in its own waste.

**Heat-Tappers:** The bright white hand-sized crabs you find clinging to every belly and vent. Six hooked legs, a domed shell frosted against heat loss, a needle mouth. They don't eat meat; they tap heat. They press themselves against a vent line, insert a thermal siphon, and drink temperature. Some species drink pressurized blood. Others just sit in exhaust jets like old men at a sauna. On a big Furnace there may be thousands, turning its underside into a moving reef. Grooming Trench-Grazers pick at them, but never too much — the Tappers clean valve infections.

You hear them ticking and clicking like cooling metal.

### The Attach-Predators

Nothing here chases. Chasing wastes heat you can't afford to lose.

Predation is slow, embarrassing, and absolute.

**Cowl-Maws:** Look like mounds of extra-thick felt until you touch them. A low dome with a lip, cemented to the base of a Furnace's leg or half-buried in the mat. It waits until something warm brushes it and then blooms — a ring of soft teeth unfolding over days, secreting enzymes that are less venom than industrial solvent. It melts into its prey's foot-pad, fusing circulatory systems. A Pillar-Furnace might walk for a week with a Cowl-Maw attached to its ankle, the parasite slowly drinking hot blood and melting inward, only noticed when the leg buckles.

**Sheet-Wraiths:** Not much more than a living blanket, violet-gray, two meters across, almost invisible against the mat. They glide on minute cilia toward the CO2 plume of a sleeping Grazer, drape themselves over its vents, and simply smother its furnace. The prey suffocates on its own efficiency, its core temperature falling even as its body tries to burn hotter. After a week the Sheet slips away leaving a perfect, heat-polished husk that steams faintly.

Even more unsettling are the reproductive strategies: most Furnace species are hermaphroditic broadcasters that lay not eggs but **kiln-pods.** Waxy, black, heat-retaining spheres the size of barrels, extruded hot and buried in a fresh tar-trail to incubate. They need ambient furnace-heat to gestate. Walk behind a herd and the warm caves in the rock are full of them, ticking faintly like stoves, each one whispering steam through a tiny valve for years before splitting open and letting a miniature, already-smoking Furnace crawl out into the pack.

They don't need parents. They need proximity.

---

That's the trick of this place. It's not barren and it's not pristine. It's a factory floor without a foreman. Every drop of water is caught, drunk, steamed, snowed, caught again. Every photon that makes it through the white sky is caught in a violet frond, eaten by a grazer, burned in a furnace belly, exhaled as heat that lets another frond survive the next hour. The basalt itself is being digested from both sides — rooted by lichen above, warmed into hollows below.

Stand still long enough and the life stops noticing you're not part of the flow. A Trench-Grazer will carve a line around your boots. A Tapper will try your ankle for warmth. The air will snow your own breath back onto your shoulders.

And the continent, patient, loud, bright, will keep breathing around you — *chuff-CHUFF, chuff-CHUFF* — a thousand engines burning cold into warmth, with no plan and no need for one.
