They live crowded.

Not in families. Not in swarms. Crowded is different — crowded is what happens when something is pressed into a space that was never meant to hold that much life, and then kept wet so it can't die back. When you first step in, you think the walls are moving with water. Then you think the water is moving with animals. Then you realize the animals *are* the water, in the way a net is still mostly holes but you still cannot put your hand through it.

They are one thing. You have to understand that first, even though it looks like many.

If you tried to sort them in the field notes you aren't carrying — because paper would pulp here in minutes — you would write: decapods, amphipods, isopods, branchiopods, larvae, nymphs, none of the above. And you'd be wrong each time, because you settled too fast. Look longer.

They are small at the edges and larger toward the center, not because they grow inward but because there is nowhere else to get bigger. The ones on the highest walls are thumbnail-sized, flat as lice, translucent-white with a faint rose blush where their gut runs. Their backs are not backs. They are shell-windows, soft chitin still mineralizing, through which you can see the sulfur they have eaten glowing dull yellow in a kind of liver. Each has too many legs — eight, ten, twelve — tipped not with claws but with pads, damp suckers that make that sound you hear: *tik... tak...* wet fingers finding purchase on glass.

A little lower, where the wall sweats faster, they are thumb-to-fist sized, and this is where the shape starts to commit. Shrimp bent over backwards. Crabs that forgot how to harden. Fetuses curled around nothing. All of them have gills — that is the unifying fact — great feathery external gills like ostrich plumes, like coxcombs, veined red-black and constantly fanning, constantly needing the film of water. If a rivulet dries for twenty seconds they shudder and crowd toward the next. Stay wet is not a preference. It is the law that holds them together.

And the hands.

You notice them fifth or sixth, after the legs, after the gills, after the eyes. Every other variation has them. Not forelimbs modified into hands — *hands*. Tiny, startlingly primate hands with six joints per finger, skin still translucent, nails like chips of obsidian, already holding something. A flake of volcanic glass. A splinter of basalt column. A perfect hexagonal chip pried from that fallen honeycomb slab.

This is their industry. It is quiet and endless and almost silent.

One peels.

Just beside your face, where you hadn't dared breathe, a creature the size of your palm stretches up on four stilts and uses both its hand-pairs to pull at the rust-red blush on the rock. That blush — which you thought was mineral stain — comes away as a sheet, paper-thin, leaving a pale scar of fresh basalt beneath. The creature folds it with care, like phyllo, and tucks it into a ventral pouch that opens sideways. Its mouth is not a mouth; it's a purse that purses. Next to it, a slightly larger cousin ignores the mineral and leans forward to rasp directly at that same scar, its face a smooth plate with two pits that secrete acid.

Another pries.

In the knee-deep central basin, where the water never stops turning over from heat below, bodies are stacked three deep. You see movement under movement. A cat-sized one — the biggest you've seen, the color of skim milk — wedges a shard of black glass under the lip of a sessile, barnacle-like form that is also, somehow, one of them. The sessile one has closed itself into a dome of faintly iron-stained plates. The big one taps, adjusts angle, *taps* again. Not mindless. Testing fulcrum. On the third tap the dome pops with a soft sigh and the meat inside, all gill and jelly, is scooped out in handfuls and passed not to the pryer's own mouth but to two smaller ones waiting on its back, who take it with their own smaller hands.

Another hones.

Half-in, half-out of a ceiling drip, a shrimp-thing sits alone, which is rare enough to mark it. It holds its glass flake in two hands and draws it across the basalt edge over and over. *Tap-tap-tap.* Not eating. Making sharper. When the edge glints to its satisfaction it reaches down — impossibly far, its arm elongating a little more than an arm should — and slices a membrane-skin that has formed over a still pool. The membrane tightens and splits like a nostril cut open. Nutrient broth wells up. Half a dozen heads dip at once to drink.

They do not build. That is the most human thing to look for, and its absence is instructive.

There is no architecture here because architecture requires dry places to store a past. Everything here is temporary usage of very new stone. To reach a higher seep, three will brace and two will climb, forming a living stepstool of interlocked legs, hold for ninety seconds while the topmost scrapes, then dissolve apart and become individuals again. In the corner where your exhaled breath went to pool, someone has diverted a rivulet by lying down in it sideways and letting five others build a dam of backs behind him. You watch the water braid around them. In five minutes they will be somewhere else and the dam will be gone and no trace will remain except a slightly cleaner patch of basalt.

What are they eating? Everything that is here.

The membranes — those taut fungal skins on every puddle — grow back in hours, fed by heat and mineral weep. They eat those. They eat the bacterial blush, rust-red and sulfur-yellow and iron-black, which is not growing *on* the rock but precipitating *as* rock as fast as they eat it, a living rust forming and being cleared. They eat the obsidian dust they make while honing. They eat each other's shed gills, which float like red feathers. They eat the hopeless overcrowded young that fail to find a wet line — you see a mother-shape extrude a string of pearl-beads from her belly, press them to the wall, and then without pause two neighbors sweep over and gather most of them up in those terrible delicate hands. No malice in it. She does it again immediately.

They are not hostile. Hostility requires territory, and there is no territory when everything touches everything.

When they notice you, it is not together but as a wave of attention that moves through water on skin. Eyes — or eye-spots, opalescent, lidless, set on stalks or flush or in clusters of six along a spine — turn. Not all of them. Enough. What looks back at you is not hunger, because you are too big to be food and too dry to be understood as an animal. Dry things do not happen here.

A few of the nearer ones reach toward you, not to grab but to test. A hand no bigger than a child's fingernail brushes the soaked cuff of your pants and comes away beading water. Another extends its glass shard toward the sweat on your wrist, perhaps wondering if it is a seep worth mining. Their touch is soft and obscenely warm, the temperature of blood-heat water that has been inside a body.

They will cover you if you stay. Not attack — incorporate. You can see how it would happen: legs finding purchase on your boots, then your knees, weighing you slowly into the pool until your breathing joins the breathing of the room, inhale-condensation, exhale-mist, until your knuckles bend a few degrees wrong behind your wrists and the light makes your shadow fall just left of you and time smears enough that you see yourself consider staying just after you have already decided to go.

That density wants to keep everything wet, including you.

That is why you leave through the crack while you are still large enough, still dry enough inside your own skin, to remember that high cold wind exists and that some mountains are supposed to be empty and sharp and lifeless on the outside.

Behind you, the quiet resumes — ticks on glass, water braiding, *tap tap tap* of stone being taught how to be a tool by things that were never quite shrimp and never quite children, patiently shaving another micron off a room that was born yesterday and will be gone tomorrow, and is, for now, completely full.
