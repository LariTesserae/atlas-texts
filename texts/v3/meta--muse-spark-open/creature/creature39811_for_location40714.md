Nothing lives here the way something lives in a valley.

Nothing has made a home here. Nothing has staked a claim.

That is the first thing you understand if you stay long enough for your skin to go soft in the damp. The basin is too full already. Too complete. To live here in any permanent way you'd have to push something else out of the way - displace a pool, dam a lip, claim a dry spot that the water has spent a thousand years deciding should not be dry. And the balance doesn't allow it. It corrects. A dam crumbles into travertine in a season. A footprint worn too deep becomes a new spillway and is smoothed over.

So what lives here lives *lightly*. Or briefly. Or almost invisibly.

The truest residents are the ones you almost don't count as living at all.

In the water - in the clearest, hottest pools at the center where it comes up blood-warm and so mineral it feels slick between your fingers - there is nothing to see. No fish, no larva wriggling, no weed swaying. But take a flake of that crust where it's still soft, still growing, and break it. Smell it. Under the salt and iron there is something faintly sharp, like a struck match.

Look closer at the shaded corners, where the rim overhangs and the condensation runs instead of evaporates. That thin, dark film you thought was just wet stone. Touch it and it smears. Olive-black, almost translucent when you thin it. It isn't moss. It doesn't have leaves or roots. It's a skin. A sheet of cells so old it predates the idea of a plant.

They are the builders, really. Thermophilic bacteria and archaea, the mineral-eaters. They live in water that would poach anything else, drinking dissolved silica and sulfur and exhaling stone. Every one of those paper-thin layers of travertine that makes up the terraces was once alive - a living mat that laid itself down and was entombed by its own breath, and then was grown over by its daughters. The white and cream and rust you see is their history, page after page after page. You are walking on a reef made of microbes, as tall as a house is deep, still growing at the speed dust settles.

They are the quiet. They are the reason there is no rot, no green, no smell of decay. They have eaten all the niches where rot could happen. They make the water too clean, too mineral-heavy, too perfectly saturated for anything more complicated to get a foothold.

Almost.

Twice you might think you imagined movement, and the third time you hold your breath and see it.

A moth - pale as the steam itself, wings the color of wet paper - detaches from the basin wall where it was absolutely invisible against the mineral crust. It doesn't fly so much as drift, in the heavy air, from one pool shadow to another. It has no mouth worth using. It will live a day, maybe two, on what it carried as a caterpillar. It does not lay here; there is nothing for its young to eat. It came in through the low break in the wall by accident, or for the warmth, and will die here, and its dust will dissolve without a trace.

And lower, near the waterline where the dark film is thickest: a cricket. Not green. Not brown. The exact speckled grey-cream of pitted travertine. It moves only when you are not looking directly at it, with a slow, deliberate step-step-pause that makes it indistinguishable from a flake shifting in condensation drip. It scrapes that bacterial film with mouthparts you need a lens to see, harvesting a lifetime of mineral growth in a few bites. This one *does* live here. This and its cousins, and the nearly invisible mites and springtails that live in the damp under the stacked stones. They are the entire animal kingdom of the basin. No hunters. No hunted, really. Just grazers on stone-soup, so slow and so still that the air swallows their ticking footsteps.

They have been here longer than the path.

The people do not live here.

That is important. Whoever made that low windbreak, whoever carried those palm-sized river stones up from some distant wash to heat in the pools, whoever left those thick, badly-fired bowls inverted on the ledge - they understood the rule of the basin, even if they never said it aloud. You do not live here. You are *allowed*.

They come. Probably always the same few families. Desert people from the flat country outside, where wood is scarce and water is work. They know the break in the rim. They know the wear-path worn dark across the lipping, the one that lets you loop the whole bowl without ever wading deeper than your ankles.

You can tell who they are by what they left and what they didn't.

No knives of metal, no nails, no glass. A single basalt flake chipped to an edge and left beside a soft patch of white paste. They come to scrape that paste - the finest, purest silica mud - and carry it out in those clay bowls to seal pots, to whiten hides, to dust on wounds or on a baby's skin. They come to soak. That is the main thing. In the center where the water is hottest and steadiest, the rims are worn smooth not by water but by backs and shoulders. By elbows. People have sat in the same pool, in the same exact spot, for so many generations that the travertine has cupped itself to fit a human shape.

They are not priests, not exactly, though the quiet makes you think that. They are older people, maybe. Women past childbearing age and men whose joints ache in dry cold. People who need the heat that never fades, the water that holds you without current, the air that never dries your eyes. They come for a few days at a time, sit in the permanent dusk, breathe the warm stone air, let the moths drift over them.

They do not talk much here - the stone swallows sound - and they do not build. Three courses of unmortared stone is as much architecture as the basin will tolerate. A cold fire pit used only for heating those river stones, because the pools themselves are never quite hot enough and you don't want to sweat in the open. A scrape of mineral, a soak until your fingers prune, a sleep behind the windbreak listening to the endless, soft ticking of spill and drip.

And then they leave. They must. There is no food here to gather, no plant to tend, no wood to burn except what you carry in. To stay would be to starve in the midst of abundance. To stay would be to break the equilibrium.

If you sit very still on one of those barely-dry islands, with your feet in a pool that comes exactly to your ankles and will come exactly to that same line a hundred years from now, you feel what they feel. That you are not an inhabitant. You are a guest of the real inhabitants - the film, the cricket, the water itself, which breathes and spills and never, ever changes.

You can stay for an hour until your skin is damp through. You can be part of the quiet. But the basin does not keep you.
