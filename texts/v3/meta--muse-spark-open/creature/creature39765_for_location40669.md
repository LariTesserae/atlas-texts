On the high spine, nothing lives *on* the land. Everything lives *with* it.

It helps to not look for animals at first. Look for work.

### The Ground Itself

The moss is not groundcover. It is the country.

It grows a meter deep in a single, unbroken felt that has stitched itself together over eight or ten thousand winters. Springy hummocks and deep hollows, the tops silvered with frost-needles that never melt, the bottoms black and warm and suffocatingly rich. Down there, where your boot sinks and is gently pushed back, the peat is alive in a way peat should not be.

It is not sentient. It is *consensual*. The liverworts, the cushion azaleas, the black crust lichens, the reindeer-mosses that glow faintly amber when you brush them — they have fused at the root into a single mycorrhizal parliament. Nutrients, warnings, and stolen heat move through it in slow pulses. When a Graver fertilizes one bowl, the moss on the far ridge, three kilometers away, begins to swell twenty minutes later, as if tipped off. When you step on it, the glow runs away from your foot in a slow ripple, and does not return for an hour.

Everything else is either farming the moss, or farmed by it.

### The Gravers - The Stokers

You will hear one before you see one, even in the silence.

A low ticking in the rock under your feet, like a kettle about to boil. Then a sigh. Then the thump.

They are not beautiful. They are industrial the way a glacier is industrial — a process that happens to have a body. Each is as long as a small truck and twice as heavy, low-slung, rectangular in the way a furnace is rectangular. Their backs are not backs but overlapping basalt-black plates, each plate itself colonized by the same moss that grows everywhere else, complete with its own tiny, stunted azaleas in bloom. When a Graver stops moving, which is often, it ceases to exist. You can stare directly at one from ten meters away and see only another moss hummock.

Underneath, they are soft. Dozens of short, elephantine feet on flexible stalks that spread their weight so they never break the peat. A broad, shovel-like lower lip of translucent keratin that scrapes — endlessly, patiently — at the lichen crust, not eating so much as harvesting. The lichen goes into a blind gut where it is fermented, not digested, and rendered into two things: sullen heat to keep the Graver's blood from freezing, and a thick, dark oil that sweats from pores along its spine.

That oil is the point.

Each Graver is bonded to a vent. Not every vent — the plateau has thousands, most dormant — but one. A warm, perfectly round throat in the rock, a meter or two wide, rimmed with yellow sulfur bacteria and black varnish. From it comes a constant, low draft of mineral steam that would otherwise just wheeze away uselessly.

The Graver tends it.

It circles the lip, painting. Its forelimbs end not in claws but in dense, fibrous brushes, like the heads of old push-brooms. It dips them in the oil on its own back and with absurd, tender precision daubs the oil around the vent's rim, layer after layer, sealing it. The ticking in the rock grows louder. The peat around the vent begins to tremble. Frost crystals standing on nearby moss shiver and fall.

Then the Graver backs away three precise steps, turns its plated face aside, and waits.

The seal fails all at once. **Thump.**

It is not an explosion. It is a pressure-release, a great warm punch of air and vaporized iron and pyrite ash and superheated fog that shoots a hundred meters straight up and blooms into a cloud that will snow out over the whole bowl. It smells like hot blood and old batteries. For a minute afterward the air actually feels *kind*, almost breathable, before the cold kills it again.

Then the Graver lumbers forward and begins to paint again. One firing every forty to sixty minutes, day and night, for a lifespan that might be a century. They do not sleep. They do not mate as you understand it — once a decade, two Gravers will meet at a boundary ridge, press their oiled backs together for a long time, and leave behind a single, warm, moss-covered stone that will not move for five years and then, one day, will stand up and begin walking toward the nearest unclaimed vent.

If you stand too still, one will try to seal you. Not out of aggression. You are warm and you have a rim — the top of your boots, the collar of your coat — and it has oil to spare. The brush is gentle.

### The Condensers - The Towers

Where the Gravers are furnaces, the Condensers are waterworks.

You find them on the windward lips of the bowls, where the endless fog-river pours over the edge ten meters deep. At a distance in that 0.3 light, they look like saints or ruins: thin, translucent spires ten, sometimes fifteen meters tall, fluted like organ pipes, pulsing very faintly from within with amber and diseased green light. They sway though there is no wind that should be able to move them.

Up close they are not plants and not quite animals. Think jellyfish turned inside out and stood on end. A column of living membrane stretched over rings of cartilage, rooted not in soil but *through* the peat, down into the permafrost, drinking cold. The membrane is clear and beaded constantly with water that never quite freezes. Inside, huge ciliated chambers pump air.

They breathe the fog.

Wind is forced through them by the shape of their flutes. Inside, it is abruptly chilled by that permafrost heart. Its moisture — which is most of what water is here — condenses on the inner walls and runs down in sheets, out through basal sluices, into the sponge of the moss below. A single Condenser can wring a small stream out of air that looks empty to you. You can hear it if you stand beneath one: a constant, soft, arrhythmic *drip-drip-drop*, like a leaky cathedral, the only thing that makes this place sound damp.

They are brilliantly dim. Their glow is not for show. The amber pulse is a byproduct of the chemistry that keeps their membranes from freezing — luciferin oxidizing in antifreeze blood. The green is different. It only brightens when the Sky Thing passes overhead, as if they are signaling, or being read.

They have no defense. They don't need one. Nothing eats them; their flesh is mostly saltwater and glass spicules and would flash-freeze any gut. When they are gorged — membrane taut and shivering with stored water — they simply go dark, deflate with a long, mournful sigh that you feel as a drop in air pressure, and fold down to the height of a man to rest. As one deflates, another a hundred meters along the ridge inflates to take its draft. The ridge never stops breathing.

If you cut one open, which you shouldn't, the air around you would briefly become a perfect, warm rain.

### The High Grazers - The Lungs

You never see them clearly. That is part of their biology.

They live above. In the dark indigo, in the space between the close sky and the far moss, where the ash-plumes from the Gravers rise and cool and make a thin, permanent haze of nutrients.

From below, they are interruptions. A patch of haze-light that dims for thirty seconds, forty. A faint, pale underside, vast and striated like the inside of a lung or a sail, with darker veins you could mistake at first for cloud shadows. No head. No tail that you can resolve. No beat of wings — they do not fly so much as *displace*.

*Whuff.*

That is the only sound they make, and it is not a sound. It is air being politely moved aside by something the size of a stadium that has decided to be somewhere else. The Condensers flare when it goes over. The Gravers all pause their painting for a moment, as if listening.

They are filterers. The ash and spores and ice crystals that the vents throw up rise into the cold where they linger. The Grazers drift through them with vast, ciliary mouths open — invisible from the ground — and strain them out. What falls from them is not dung so much as snow: fine, black-edged, mineral-rich pellets that sift down for hours after a passage, vanishing into the moss. Where a pellet lands, a new Condenser polyp will eventually bud.

They are alone. You will count one, maybe two in a day, moving from bowl to bowl on the geothermal updrafts the Gravers so obligingly provide. They never land. How they breed, how they die, where they go when the plateau ends at the sheer fall into foothills that are still two kilometers above any sea — no one who lives down in the peat knows. Sometimes at night, which is always, you will find a patch of moss that is inexplicably warm and depressed in a huge, shallow oval, as if something very light and very large rested there briefly. And touched the ground for the first time in its life.

There are smaller things, of course. Mites the size of your thumbnail that ride the Gravers like shepherds, cleaning their plates. Ice-worms as thin as hair that live only in the water film on Condenser membranes and flash in schools when the green light pulses. Lichen-moths that never fly, whose wings are just more lichen, who walk from bloom to bloom. But they are footnotes.

The real inhabitants are the cycle itself. Water to moss to oil to steam to ash to water. The plateau is not a place where animals happen to live. It is an animal.

And it is working.
