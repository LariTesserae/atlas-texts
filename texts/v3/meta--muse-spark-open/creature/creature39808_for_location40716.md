They are still there when you look again.

Not *on* the floor. They *are* the texture of the floor, until your eyes learn the difference between pitted concrete and a back that is pretending very hard to be pitted concrete.

### The Grazers

They are the first to resolve, because there are so many of them that your brain has no choice but to stop calling them dust.

Each one is a flat oval, from the size of your thumbnail up to the span of your palm. Slate-gray, the gray of cold iron left out in rain and then dried. Their backs are not shells exactly but overlapping mineral plates - seven, nine, eleven to an animal - pressed and dull, the same color and pitting as the scale that sifts down from the press-bed above. When the amber work-lamps catch them at the right angle you can see the growth lines.

They have no hurry because they have nowhere to go. They move in the slow, aimless, perfect coverage of a Roomba that never needs to dock. A faint, soft mouth underneath - not teeth, a kind of damp pad fringed with bristles - laps and rasps at the lime-pan. You can hear it if you put your ear close enough to the floor, under the chittering: a million tiny *scrit, scrit, scrits* like someone trying to erase pencil.

They are eating the room itself.

Iron oxide for blood, salt for nerves, the varnish-skin of old oil for calories, your sloughed skin cells if you stay long enough to leave any. Behind each one is a faint lightening, twin trails where the crust has been polished a half-shade paler. Give them an hour and the patch of floor you thought was bare concrete is re-etched with a lace of trails, overlapping, erasing each other. Give them fifty years and they are why the floor is smooth.

Put your palm down. You will touch three. Four. They do not bite, they do not flee. A large one, palm-sized, will pause, antennae - short, blunt, almost invisible - tasting the sweat on your skin, and then decide you are a warm boulder. It goes over your knuckle like a slow coin, cool and dry, its underside briefly soft and gripping. Your weight doesn't crush them; their plates flex and settle. They flow around the pressure of your boot, re-knitting behind you seconds after you lift it. You cannot make a space among them that stays empty.

### The Middle Kingdom

On top of them - literally, walking on them, using their backs as ground - is the second city.

These are faster and you hear them first. The constant dry rustle you thought was the grazers shifting is not the grazers at all. It is these.

They are the length of your finger, most of them, or your whole hand if you count antennae. Wingless crickets, but wrong: their legs are too long, jointed backwards one too many times, their bodies translucent amber at the joints where you can see the oil they've eaten moving inside. They don't chirp. They click, with their mandibles, with their knees against their own sides. A dense, arrhythmic ticking like an old factory full of typewriters, all typing different letters.

With them, the hissing roaches - fat, mahogany, their bellies pale and almost clear, dragging egg-cases like little purses. They barge through the grazers, who part and close. The roaches eat the same varnish, but faster, and they will eat the grazers too if one is turned over and soft.

Along the edges, where the low ceiling slab meets the buckled concrete and the wall makes a crease that never quite dries even though it is dry, run the pale ones. Centipede is the closest word. Twice as long as your finger, bone-white except for the gray dust in the fur between its segments, with a head that is all antenna and feeler and venom-hook. They do not live in the open. They flicker - that is the only word - from shadow to shadow, from under one lumpy iron ridge to under another, and where they pass the crickets go very quiet for three seconds too long.

None of them drink. If you watch one of the crickets, you will see its abdomen pulse, but never the dip of a head to water. The air is 50 degrees and still and smells faintly of hot pennies and ozone because that is what they breathe out: metabolized rust, metabolized hydrocarbon, the ghost of water made briefly during the chemistry and then spent again. Touch one and it is dry as paper. Crush one - and you will crush some by accident, shifting your stooped weight - and it doesn't bleed. It splits and leaks a clear, slightly oily fluid that beads and is gone in a minute, already lapped up by the nearest grazer who has turned and arrived with that same unhurried purpose.

They eat each other constantly, without violence. A cricket stumbles, a roach is quicker. A roach molts and is soft for ten minutes, and four crickets are on it. No chase, no scream. Just the ticking getting briefly louder over a spot.

### The Ceiling

You don't see them until one moves, because your eyes don't want to look up. The ceiling is low - you have to keep your neck bent - and it is industrial, scarred, humming. You think the dark patches up there are oil stains, or folded rags left by a worker who never came back.

They are not stains.

They hang in rows, spaced almost too evenly, like hams in a cure-room. Each is the size of a fat rabbit, or a terrier curled nose-to-tail. They are covered not in fur but in a tight, matted felt that was maybe fur once, now caked with fifty winters of concrete dust and iron scale until it is the same color as the ceiling. It makes them silent and insulated.

Their faces are the shock. Turn your lamp - though you don't have a lamp, the two caged amber lamps do it for you - and see: no eyes. Smooth, blind planes, with two wide, shallow pits on either side of a flat muzzle, open to the air, twitching. Heat pits. They feel you instantly as a bloom of warmth, 98.6 against 52, and they orient, all of them, a degree toward you. You feel it rather than see it.

Most of the time they do nothing. They breathe, shallow, cool air pooling under them and spilling down onto your neck. A faint, sweet-sour smell, like old batteries.

Then - a drop.

No warning. A bulk detaches and falls three feet straight down with a soft *whumpf* you feel in your knees. It lands splayed on the seething carpet, and for two seconds you see what it really is: thick, pale limbs with black hooked claws, a mouth that opens impossibly wide, a tongue. It scoops. It doesn't hunt one cricket; it shovels a double-handful of whatever is there - grazers, crickets, roach, dust, a concrete chip - and folds itself around it, making that low, satisfied grunt you heard earlier and thought was the machine.

And then it climbs back up. Its claws find holds in the worn-down bolt-nubs and the lip of the press-bed, and it hauls itself hand over hand, belly full, to its spot. Above you, in the flat amber light, you can see its guts work. A minute later a dry, felted pellet detaches and falls, almost odorless. Before it hits the floor it has been intercepted. The grazers converge on it as they converge on everything. Nothing is wasted because there is nothing to waste. The machine above leaks light and scale; the big ones leak pellets; the middle ones leak bodies; the grazers polish it all back into lime-pan.

If you lie still and breathe shallow - you are already breathing shallow so you don't inhale the permanent haze - they decide you are not a predator and not prey but terrain. A cricket walks up the side of your boot and tastes the laces with its antennae for a long time, deliberating. A pale centipede pauses at the edge of your shadow and then decides your shadow is just another crease. On the ceiling, the felted ones resettle, pits still aimed at you, waiting for the next sixteen-hour day to tick over. The lamps hum. The press throbs. *Whump ... whump ... whump.* A little more snow falls.

The room remembers, without ever being taught, how to stay full.
