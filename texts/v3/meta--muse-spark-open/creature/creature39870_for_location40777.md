Who lives in the Basin are the people who learned how to stop drying off.

Not amphibians, not at first. They came down as workers. Machinists and electricians and concrete-men from the district above, then their wives and their kids, then the ones who found the rent upstairs too dry and too expensive. They stayed long enough that the place tuned them.

You spot them first by their boots.

Everyone wears them, even indoors. Black rubber to the knee, or patched green waders cut down to clogs. The leather shoe never stood a chance here; it curled and spotted and died. The rubber boots clop and squeak on the flagstones, and the people in them have learned to walk without lifting their feet quite - a shuffling glide that doesn't splash the runnel eels on the tram line. Their jackets are the same: waxed canvas, oilskin, layers that never fully dry. On a line outside a terrace house you'll see three identical work shirts hanging for days, never crisp, just moving from wetter to damp. No one brings them in. Why would you?

They are pale the way cave fish are pale, but not sickly. It's a soft pallor, like stone kept out of the sun. Their hair is always a little damp at the temples. Their hands are what give them away - not webbed or strange, just constantly pruned at the fingertips, cuticles ragged from picking at rust, nails short and clean because long nails hold water. Everyone's hands look about ten years older than their faces. They oil things the way other people check the mail. At every doorway there's a rag tin, a wire brush. You will see a woman on her stoop at 6 a.m., not sweeping, but wiping down her own front door hinges just so, methodically, because if you don't the orange lace comes overnight.

They are not numerous. That's the point. Maybe three hundred now in a place built for seven. The sparseness isn't sadness to them. It's manners. They keep the intervals the Basin wants. A family could easily take over the empty lot next door - god knows they need the storage - but they don't. They leave the yard as wet flagstone and a single rusting bench, because their father tried roofing over half of one and woke to a spring in his kitchen that wouldn't stop muttering until the roof came off. They understand that the space between houses is not empty. It's where the water breathes.

So they live thinly, deliberately. The houses are all facade and one deep room, with big windows left on all day - amber sodium, warm incandescent - not for light exactly, but as a signal: *I'm still here, I'm occupied, don't fill me in with water.* Inside, every kitchen has a kettle always warm, a towel rack that does nothing, and a frog.

The frogs are citizens. You can't separate the human residents from the animal ones because no one here does.

The people live layered inside a fauna that has worked out that humans are not predators, just large, slow, warm rocks that drip less than limestone. The white pigeons know every rafter and every scale in the butcher's shop. They don't startle. The swifts that cut the central air - winter and summer both, impossibly, never landing - are watched the way other towns watch traffic lights; their patterns tell the tram drivers when the pressure is going to drop.

Below, the salamanders are everywhere. Orange as hazard paint, blindly confident. They crawl over boots, over sleeping children's foreheads, over the pages of the half-finished mural on the retaining wall where the artist just paints around them. Children are taught very early not to pick them up with dry hands. Not because it will hurt the salamander, but because it will be rude.

In the open aqueducts and the silver-lipped stair, the eels. Fat, mottled, escapees from Terrace Four's farm tanks three generations ago, now wild and as long as your arm, living under gratings. You hear people say "excuse me" to a grating before they step, and they mean it. In the flooded basements you can look down through the little pavement-glass windows and see the carp, huge, unhurried, turning slowly in what used to be someone's pantry. The chore given to the smallest kids is to scoop the fry out with a dustpan and tip them back into the drain before the foreman comes to check the levels.

And the sound of them together is the sound of the town's human life, too. At night, when the big screw pumps kick on - *whum... whum...* once an hour, never loud enough to shout over - the frogs answer. Thousands of small brown throats using the bowl of limestone as a drum. The people talk underneath that chorus without raising their voices, pausing when a particularly deep voice hits resonance and makes the tea in their mugs shiver. They have learned not to compete. They take turns.

Because the other thing that lives here is not an animal at all, or not quite.

Call it the interval. Call it the Basin itself.

It lives in the empty lots, in the too-thick shadows under the aqueducts where drips hesitate and then, for half a second, fall up. It lives in the lag between dropping a wrench and hearing it clang, a lag a fraction too long, as if the sound had to ask permission to return. It doesn't haunt. It keeps house.

You feel it most in the people who insist on dryness. The ones who bring a new dehumidifier down, who seal their windows with foam and plastic, who want a crisp loaf of bread. They get a headache first - a cold pressure behind the ears, as if they'd gone too deep underwater. Then their sealed room starts to sweat anyway, but wrongly, in beads that don't run. The walls listen too closely. Most of them unseal the room within a week, open a window to the plink and trickle, and the pressure eases. They learn.

The long-term residents have that pressure all the time, lightly, and they've come to like it. It makes them slow in the deep-water sense of slow. Not lethargic. Unhurried. They leave projects half-done for a decade because time here isn't measured in completion but in maintenance. The factory making parts for itself is not a failure to them; it's the most honest work there is. The foreman for the pumps doesn't give orders, he keeps a schedule. Disputes are settled over tea with frogs on the sill because raising your voice is pointless when the bowl will echo you back late and make you a liar.

If you stay a few days you will notice they all cock their head a little the same way when the pumps stop, listening for the drip that continues underneath. If you stay a few weeks you will start doing it too. If you leave - climbing the forty, sixty, impossibly many steps back up to where the air is sharp and the road kinks - you will find yourself oiling your boots for no reason, leaving your porch light on at noon, and missing the particular weight of cool, wet air that was never cold enough to be cruel.

They aren't trapped. The stairs are right there. They're acclimated.

They are the people who agreed with water about how to share a room.
