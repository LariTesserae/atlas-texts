What lives here lives aslant.

Nothing in the Drowned Heights gets to live upright for long, or still. Everything that endures has learned to lean into surge, to cling, to flex.

### The People on Top

Starting from the top down, because that's where we put ourselves.

You live on rafts. Not boats - rafts in the truest sense, lashed-together industrial pontoons and oil-drum floats and flexing steel truss, all of it breathing with the swell. On the highest tables, where the drowned prairie comes within fifteen meters of air, they've driven legs down. Four-legged, six-legged platforms on hydraulic knees that jack up and down with the storm cap. The rust is endemic. You never get it off your hands. Above, diesel smokestacks and kelp-rendering chimneys cough black into that thin ten-meter ceiling of air. Below, everything drips.

The crews are small and loud. You have to be loud here. Helmets off on deck, you shout through wind ripped off a cold skin of ocean. In the water, you shout through the suit radio until static swallows you.

There are:

**The Tablehands.** Young men and women, mostly, because your knees and eardrums go first. They work the summit meadows in tethered suits - thick yellow vulcanized rubber over drysuit wool, brass helmets or cheap plastic domes fogged with breath. Their job is to ride the harvester combs. House-sized rakes of chain and bar that the gantries drag through eelgrass and feather-weed prairie, shaving it like wheat. The hands walk alongside in the brutal shallow light, just a few meters down, where surge turns the world to washing machine. They cut snags with long hooks, kick off grazing chitons the size of dinner plates that won't let go of a blade, yank the steel cable when it fouls. Their hands ache perpetually in that five-degree water. Their skin never quite dries. At night they huddle in the winch house with steam hoses blasting crabs off their boots and drink tea so hot it scalds through chapped lips.

They are adapted without being adapted. Noses flattened from equalizing four, five times a day. Sinuses blown out. Ears scarred. Shoulders overdeveloped from fighting current in a suit that wants to float you. They have a gait - found nowhere else - half swagger half stumble, learned on a deck that never stops moving.

**The Valley Divers.** Older. Scarred in other ways. They go deep. Off the edge of the mesa, you clip off and drop - a hundred meters, two hundred, three - down the broad, whale-backed ridge through forest into dusk. Where the light columns fail and the dredge lights take over. Down there are the ore crews and net crews. The old glacial valleys hold silt black with organics and cold-water minerals. The dredges crawl along the U-shaped floors on tracks, vacuuming. The divers swim alongside guiding the heads around house-sized erratics, around the perfectly preserved stone walls of villages no one remembers, around forests of giant kelp with stipes thicker than your waist.

They don't use yellow suits down there. They use black, heavy suits with lead boots and tether lines as thick as your wrist, and helium in their voices. They go down in pairs and come up slow, hanging under the raft for hours while their blood fizzes. They talk the least. They have seen what pressure does to a bolt that wasn't torqued right, to a lung that burped. Many of them have that thousand-yard stare that deep divers get, because down there in the krill-snow and coral light, it is very quiet inside your helmet compared to the factory roar above.

**The Children and The Old.** Because this is not a temporary rig. This is a town scattered over a mountain range turned to sea. On the larger, triple-rafted refinery platforms - the ones stable enough to have railings - there are shacks bolted to shacks, tarpaulins, galley kitchens where someone is always frying charr. Kids in orange life vests who have never stood on ground that didn't move. They learn to swim before they walk well. They fish with handlines off the pontoons for shrimp as long as your forearm, pulling them up screaming and kicking. The old sit in oil drums cut into chairs, mending nets with bare, blue-knuckled hands, watching the weather build. When someone dies - fall, bend, stroke, drowning - they wrap them in net-weight and drop them off the table. There is no burial in stone here. The crustaceans take them in days. The old know this and keep mending.

None of them are still. Even when asleep, they rock.

### The Herds

Below the people, and vastly outnumbering them, are the grazers. For it is a pasture now.

On the high tables, in that sun-blasted eelgrass prairie in twenty meters of violent light:

**The Slow Livestock.** Herds of chitons and urchins. Not the little tidepool things you know. These are the cattle of the Heights. Leather chitons - *Cryptochiton drownae*, the crews call them "slatebacks" - a meter long, eight-plated backs mottled green-black to match the grass. They move in herds of hundreds, scraping the prairie down with radulae you can hear grinding from ten meters away. You have to hose them off the pylon legs or they'll rasp the anti-fouling right off the steel.

Among them, the **Black Urchins** - spheres of spines as long as your forearm, rolling slowly across the mesa on hydraulic tube feet. Purple-black, with luminous green gonads inside that the divers prize. They graze the moss and the short coral. When a storm rips the top off the water, they lock down - spines interlaced with neighbors, forming a living reef pavement that the surge can't move. From the surface they look like the mesa itself bristling.

And the **Vermicular Snails** - not snails as you think, but colonies of worms in calcium spirals as thick as drainpipe, building reefs of white whorls on every abandoned cable. They filter the snow.

### The Forest Itself, Which Is Also An Animal

The kelp is not passive. You learn this when you try to work it.

**The Giant Feather-Weed and Black Kelp.** They root in old alpine soil that never washed away - thin, feral earth held down by granite. Their holdfasts are root-balls ten meters across, gripping glacial polish, gripping monastery walls, gripping each other. The stipes rise - 100, 200 meters - as thick as old larch trunks, creaking, knocking together in surge like a forest of masts. Their bladders - pneumatocysts the size of oil drums - pop and sigh as pressure changes. To cut them is to fight them. A felled trunk doesn't fall, it *flies* sideways in current, dragging cable, tangling divers. Left alone, they regrow at insane speed in that cold, bright shallows - a cleared meadow is head-high again in three weeks.

Clinging to every stipe: a vertical city. Leggy **Spider Crabs** the color of rust, with leg spans wider than a man is tall, picking endlessly. **White Tunicate Flags** streaming like prayer replacements. **Anemone Fields** lower down - bone-white, pink, arterial red - each polyp as wide as a bucket, waving in perfect sync, eating whatever drifts too close. You do not brush an anemone field in a thin glove.

Higher in the canopy, where light fractures green-gold:

**The Shoals.** This is the true biomass. The **Pale Mountain Charr** and **Silver Trout** - descendants of glacial lake fish who found themselves, over generations of flood, with an ocean overhead. They never got small again. A single charr is as long as your leg, silver-sided with a blush of red at breeding time, mouth still faintly pouting like its freshwater ancestor. They move as a *body* - not a school, a body - millions strong, turning on a thought. When they pass over a mesa table they eclipse you. The light goes out for a minute, two minutes, as the shoal pours overhead, flank scales flashing, water thrumming with a million tails. Behind them the water is cloudy with eggs and shit and shed scales, and the filter-feeders below go wild.

The crews time their work to them. When the charr come, nothing else hunts. When they leave, the hunters come.

### The Hunters

Because a pasture needs wolves.

**The Long Seals.** They are not seals anymore, not quite. The old alpine river otters and snow-leopards have no place here; what filled the niche was something like a seal with a second chance. Two to three meters, sleek, dappled granite-grey, with whiskered muzzles and hind flippers that can still awkwardly haul them onto a pontoon in a storm to bark and cough and stare at you with offended eyes. In water they are torpedoes. They hunt by sound, whistling and booming through the kelp cathedral, herding charr against the mesa cliffs. You hear them before you see them - a deep *whoop-whoop* that carries through your hull and your bones. They are curious and dangerous in equal measure; a young bull will mock-charge a diver for fun, veering at the last second, blasting you with cold bubble.

**The Cold Squid.** Deeper, in the valley dusk. Not giants, but big enough. Arms as thick as a man's thigh in mottled, iridescent cold colors - blues and dead-leaf browns that vanish against kelp shadow. They hover, utterly still - the only still thing in the Heights - and then unfold. They take charr, they take crabs, they take a careless glove if you dangle it. The divers respect them. Their eyes are as big as saucers and reflect your dredge light back at you like you are being studied.

**The Crustaceans, Endless.** Below everything, the cleaners and opportunists. **Armor Crabs** - Dungeness descendants grown massive in the cold, carapaces a meter across, claws that can snip a cable. **Shrimp clouds** - not krill, true shrimp, snapping so constantly the hydrophones hear only static. They are over every surface that stops moving for an hour - over a dropped wrench, over a stupa, over a drowned corpse, over your faceplate if you hover long enough. The platforms have to run steam hoses twice a day just to keep walkways passable. They are loud, mindless, industrious as the humans. The crews fry them by the bucketful. They taste like cold iron and iodine.

And everywhere, the **Krill Snow** - the base note. Clouds so dense they turn a clear valley morning into blizzard. Each one a red eye, a flicker. The charr eat them, the anemones eat them, the whole forest breathes them.

### The Holdovers

Up in that razor-thin layer of air, a few things still try to live as if the mountains were still mountains.

**The Birds.** Gull-like, but tougher, high-altitude things - choughs and terns with salt on their wings. They nest on the rust-red caissons, streaking them white, screaming against diesel smoke. They have learned to dive the top ten meters for fingerling charr, to ride storm surge like dust devils. On calm days you see them by the hundreds, wheeling over a mesa meadow visible through clear water below them, their shadows racing over grazing chitons. They are confused, stubborn holdovers, and the most lonely sound in the Heights is their crying at night over water that has no shore.

**The Prayer Remnants.** Not alive, but inhabited. The old stone walls, the terraces, the half-buried stupa now wearing white tunicates. Small **Blennies** and **Sculpins** - fist-sized, mottled to match granite - live in every crack of that cut stone, peering out with territorial eyes. Octopi make dens in old hearths. Anemones grow from Buddha's laps. The monks left, or drowned, a long time ago. Their walls remain because the cold preserves and the crabs clean, and the fish cannot eat stone.

That is what lives in the Drowned Heights: men and women who rock even in sleep, herds of armored grazers mowing sunlit prairie a hundred meters under angry sky, a forest that creaks like a fleet, silver rivers of fish that turn day to night, barking almost-seals and watching squid and an endless, snapping carpet of crabs, and above it all, bewildered birds who still think rust is rock.

All of it leaning. All of it filtering snow. All of it listening to diesel throb through water, and answering with click and whistle and creak, while the valley current starts to run like a river again and buries your morning's work by noon.
