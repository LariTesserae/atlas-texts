Who lives there now is not the same as who it was built for.

It remembers people — in the shape of its doors, the height of its counters, the benches bent to the shape of a human back — but they are gone the way a seed's first leaves are gone once the true leaves come in. Shed. Incorporated. What lives there now moved in because there was room.

The first class of resident is the town itself, which you have to count as one animal.

Not a metaphor. The grove-city is not a collection of plants that happen to be shaped like buildings. It is a single low, broad, highly differentiated organism that learned urbanism as a growth habit. The walls share sap. The roots share thought, if thought is chemical gradient and slow electrical pulse through root-asphalt. The factories are organs — lungs and stomachs and looms that are also hearts, contracting on a twenty-second cycle even when no one's watching. 

You can feel it notice you as temperature. When you stand too long in an alley the leaves overhead angle very slightly toward you, not for light — there isn't enough — but for information. Condensation follows you. The axil-pools fill a little fuller wherever you walk, as if the streets are sweating to taste your shoes. If you cut any of it, which you shouldn't, the whole block exhales that iron-green blood-smell at once, and the spiders up in the dark repair crews go still.

It is young. That is important. It grows the way puppies do, all joints and eagerness and wrong proportion. It hasn't learned to be still yet. At night, when it revises itself — that soft collective creak like a shoulder rolling in sleep — it's not defending territory. It's stretching to fill the bowl of embryonic basalt that holds it, sending exploratory runner-roots out to the new hillocks of cartilage-stone that push up steaming at the perimeter. It will be bigger next week. It is always becoming.

The second class of residents are the commuters. They are why the density feels so wrong.

For how empty the town is of people, it is fat with animals that have learned to use a city without ever having been taught what a city is. Their abundance — 2.4 in a space made for 0.1 — is only visible because the emptiness gives them somewhere to be seen.

In the high void between buildings, where architects would have put a second story, spiders have strung the real infrastructure. Webs as big as billboards, as big as sails, anchored from fig-steeple to hazel-tenement, not sticky in the middle where they are meant to be walked. And they *are* walked: beetles with enameled backs clicking along the radial lines like trolleys, ants carrying screws in formation, moths the size of open hands drifting and catching scale-dust on the silk until the whole net glows faintly under fungal light. The spiders themselves — long, pale, almost translucent in the blue bioluminescence — rarely move except to tighten a sagging power-vine or bundle a dead pigeon that fell into their net and lower it, priest-like, down to the pitcher-storefronts that wait below with open lips.

Below, at street level, the town's maintenance crew.

Raccoons with leaf-litter coats who know how to lift a bark-shutter. Possums that waddle the polished taproot-rails of the old trolley, pausing at every intersection to sniff the wind coming down the leaf-gutters. A thin red fox who has taken the town hall as her den; she prunes. She does it deliberately, standing on hind legs to nip at a kudzu curtain that has grown over the doorway overnight, shaping an arch exactly as high as her shoulders. When she sleeps, salamanders emerge from the wall beside her — translucent-throated, four inches long, utterly silent — and pulse across the damp stone-pith in dozens, cleaning the moss with flickering tongues.

And smaller still, so many you stop counting: sow bugs like tiny armored oxen dragging flecks of rust toward the mill; mites moving in amber streams inside the walls; larvae fat as fingers glowing in the axil-pools. Everything that should be hidden in soil is here at eye height, because the soil *is* eye height. The town turned itself inside-out and brought the understory up onto the street.

The third class are the holdovers and the half-incorporated — the ones who stayed too long.

You will find them if you look in the hollow pith-rooms and gourd-halls that are bigger inside than out. Not ghosts. Not bodies. Adopted things.

A bicycle fused halfway into a wall, its front wheel replaced by a slow wheel of living wood that still tries to turn when the wind moves. A telephone booth completely swallowed except for its glass, and inside the glass a colony of luminescent fungus that rings, once, when you pass — not a sound, a brightening. Work aprons on thorn-hooks in the textile mill that twitch when the steam valves sigh, because vine tendrils have threaded the sleeves and wear them now.

And sometimes — in the coolest, dampest hour when the factories exhale hardest and the dew hangs in the air like beads — you see shapes that were once human and did not leave when the town revised around them. The barber's shop bristles have a man-shape hollow worn into them where someone sat long enough for the branches to learn his shoulders. The post office seed-pod sorting boxes rustle though there is no mail, as if sorting still happens for recipients that root downwards now.

They didn't die. The town does not kill quickly. It adopts slowly: root-hairs stitching first to shoe leather, then to skin, then through. Leave anything still for a day and it roots. Leave a person still for a season and they become a very quiet, very patient part of the architecture — a warm nook, a pith-room that always stays a little warmer than the others, a balcony that never curls back in the morning because someone once liked to stand there.

Those are still there, too, breathing very slowly with the rest.

So the population census is: one city-organism, young and growing; thousands of arthropods and small vertebrates who run its transit and sanitation without being told; a few mid-size mammals who have become custodians and curators; and a scattering of former occupants who forgot to keep moving.

None of them hurry. At 10 degrees, in 0.3 light, with water arriving only as steam-become-dew, everything conserves. The moths drift rather than flap. The fox walks. The town itself inhales for twenty seconds, holds, exhales. The quiet — 1.1 — is not absence of life but agreement: to keep your voice down so the ticking of dew into pools and the soft hydraulic hiss of a leaf unfurling can be heard for what it is.

Which is work. Constant, gentle, municipal work.

If you stay, they will let you live there too. There is plenty of room.
