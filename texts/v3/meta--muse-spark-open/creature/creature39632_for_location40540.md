Nobody lives here. That's the honest answer.

Something *occupies* it, the way pigeons occupy a train station at night. Nothing here pays rent. Nothing here was invited. The Build was not made to be lived in. It was made to be *operated*, and whoever operated it left the lights on and the pumps cycling and the chain conveyors empty-running when they stepped out. What lives here now is what found the door open.

And learned to stay.

### The Skin

First and most permanent are the ones you don't count until you try to scrape them off. The biofilm.

It covers everything. A green-black felt, a slick, a living paint half a millimeter thick on every concrete face, every steel beam, every tile. Bacteria, diatoms, young algae, fungal threads. It dulls the nonslip matting. It makes handrails greasy. It softens the wood-grain left by formwork without ever quite hiding it. If you brush it, it clouds the water like dust and then resettles within the hour. If you bleach it, it's back in weeks.

On top of the skin are its first citizens: the clingers. White filter clams stacked like teeth along girder flanges. Acorn barnacles lined with military precision along bolt heads because the larvae prefer the micro-eddy behind each bolt. Pale, fleshy anemone-things clustered by the warm outtakes, waving stupidly in water that is always moving. Bristle worms in every crack. They don't move. They don't need to. The whole continent is a filter-feeding rig - cold water falling, warm water rising, particle rain drifting down forever past the floodlamps. You just open and wait. There are miles of wall that are nothing but mouths, quietly sieving.

They are tended, after a fashion. And pruned.

### The Janitors

You will hear them before you see them, because they are not quiet about being the only thing here with a job left.

Maintenance drones. Not sleek. Not intelligent. Beetle-shaped submersibles the size of chest freezers, yellow paint faded under silt, with rotating wire brushes and squeegee arms and a single white headlamp. They follow embedded guide-wires in the floor, scraping biofilm, bumping over rivets, turning around when they hit a wall and going back the other way. Some drag magnet trawls for stray bolts. Some do nothing but circle a single pylon forever, polishing it.

They are not alive and they don't care what's alive. A crab will ride one for hours. A cuttlefish will follow its light. Anemones will close when its shadow passes. It will scrape right over a spawning bed of clam eggs without pausing. Every few kilometers one has died, tipped over, its brush still slowly turning against nothing, wearing a groove in concrete. Around it the algae grows shaggy, uncut, a small meadow in an industrial desert.

The louder janitors are not mobile at all: the ballast pumps, the turbines, the spillway gates, the generators. You feel them in your molars, that low thud-thud-thud that passes for a heartbeat here. In a way they are the megafauna. They breathe water, they make weather, they determine where anything else can live by deciding what will be cold and fast and oxygen-rich, and what will be warm and slack. Cold runoff pours off the high illumination plains in a river with no banks, falls a hundred meters as a white underwater waterfall that never stops foaming, churns through a turbine gallery and comes out half a degree warmer on the other side. Everything orients to that. Kelp grows only there. Barnacles grow thickest there. Fish hunt there.

### The Thin Herds

Fish took this place seriously because it gave them exactly what open sea never does: structure without end, light without sun, current without tide.

They are not dense. Remember: abundant doesn't mean crowded. You can stare down a boulevard of lamp columns receding to blue haze and see nothing for three full minutes except particulate. Then a decision ripples from far off and a school comes through like a weather front.

Herring and smelt and sardine-things, thousands at a time, quicksilver, adapted now to indoor life. They don't like shadow. When a pump cycles or a drone passes, they turn as one body and flash. They spawn in the slack behind dam walls, in water that is always the same late-October cold, and they never leave the light.

Below them, the residents. Cod fat as labradors who have learned that a pipe mouth is a cave. They hover in the dark of it, mouths open, gills working, waiting for shrimp to be spat out. Crabs the size of laundry baskets - spider crabs, stone crabs, things with barnacles on their own backs - picking upside-down across catwalk grating, across gantry undersides, across plaza floors the size of counties. You hear them clicking a long way off.

Eels own the conduit. If there is a hole big enough for your arm, an eel is already in it, head out, watching. Cuttlefish own the inspection lights. They hover there, lens-eyed, color-shifting to match concrete, to match rust, to match you. They learned that light means food within a week of the builders leaving.

And behind all of them, smaller than you can easily see: clouds of mysid shrimp, isopods scuttling through lost-bolt trash, black snails with iron in their shells.

It's all constantly moving, chasing, eating, hiding. Not frenzied. Workmanlike. The same noon-white light, the same cold. Predation here looks like shift work.

### The Passers-Through

Rarely, the scale reasserts itself.

You will be standing on a control island - railings, rubber matting still grippy under slime, a dead panel humming - looking out over terraces dropping away in clean, sharp-edged steps, each spilling its sheet-waterfall to the next, roaring even deep under water, and you will feel it in your ribs before you hear it.

A whale. Or what was a whale.

Nothing that large should be able to navigate corridors a mile wide and still feel cramped, but they do. They learned the Build as coastline. They move through the high water between districts, high up where the inverted floor drips condensation rain upward, their songs broken and amplified by concrete until the whole vault rings. You never see more than a shadow, a fluke the size of a truck fading into blue haze between lamp trusses. They are alone. They call and the echo comes back from too many walls.

Smaller passers-through too: gulls that should not be able to live under the sea and do anyway, nesting on lamp housings in trapped air pockets above the waterline, screaming down at fish. They ride the indoor monsoons, the slow, cold rivers that fall off the plains. Occasionally a surface bird - a shearwater, a petrel - gets sucked down an intake shaft and blinks around in this upside-down noon, utterly lost, before the cold takes it.

### The Visitors Who Think They Live Here

People come down from the top. They have to. The entry is at the top and gravity still works.

They come in hard suits, in submersibles with wipers going, in cages dropped on cables that creak for kilometers. They stand on those high places and they stare, because at first it looks like a city.

It isn't. There are no apartments, no kitchens, no beds. Just endless drydocks that are never dry, stairwells that go down fifty stories into blue gloom and still show every rivet, factory floors big as states with nothing on them but a single gantry crane and the hum. You can stay a shift. You can stay a double shift. The cold seeps through any suit, any hull. Your breath fogs even inside. The alarms that are always going off somewhere distant start to sound like they mean you.

No one homesteads. The ones who try learn that scrubbers don't make food, that drones will scrape off your little vegetable patch with the algae, that the crabs outnumber you a billion to one and were here first, in the second wave. You visit. You take notes. You lose a wrench and hear it ping for a minute into the void. And you go back up, because the Build does not need you to keep humming.

Everything is humming. Everything is flowing. Everything is holding. And in between the lamps, in that great clear, cold, bright, near-void, life has thinned itself out to just enough to fill it without ever filling it up - a dusting, a shivering curtain of kelp toward the light, a crab crossing an empty plaza, waiting for the next pump to kick with a boom that rolls for miles.
