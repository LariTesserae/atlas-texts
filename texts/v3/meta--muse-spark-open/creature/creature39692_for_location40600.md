They live pressed flat.

That is the first law of the mesa. Anything that stood up here was taken by the wind a million years ago. So what remains has learned to be thickness without height.

They are called **kites** only because you have no other word. In truth they are not one animal but a *mat* — a single distributed animal that has learned to live as many.

### The Pavement

Individually, each one is about as big as your hand would be if you could open it — a kite, a diamond, a flattened teardrop with the point always downslope by a fraction of a degree. The dorsal side is exposed to the sky. It is leathery, matte, the exact color of the basalt when it is cooling from 600 degrees: a deep iron maroon that is black until it moves. It has no eyes, no face. The dorsal skin is not skin at all but a thermal sail — a dense plate of sclerotized protein impregnated with iron and vanadium salts that drinks infrared the way a leaf drinks light. By day this would burn them. By night, it is everything.

The ventral side you only see when they flip — which is to die. It is pale, translucent, the color of the salt pans, wet with non-water. Not mucus. Oil. A heavy, amber, hydrophobic fluid that never evaporates because the air has nothing to pull from it. That oil is their blood and their solvent. They are not water-based life. Water left this mesa ten thousand years ago and they stayed anyway, rewriting their chemistry around sulfuric-acid salts and hydrocarbon films. The white crusts in the potholes — the evaporite hexagons — are not scenery to them. They are wells. Under magnification the crust is pitted with microscopic etch-marks, acid-kisses where a kite has pressed its belly down and leached out the last bound water of crystallization, the trace metals, the chemical gradient.

They have no legs. Around the entire margin of the diamond is a fringe, a ruff of cilia each as fine as an eyelash hair, but stiff, chitinous. On their own they do nothing. In concert they are a grip, a zipper, a loom. They interlock with the fringe of the neighbor, and the neighbor's neighbor, so that the whole basin becomes a shingled roof three, four, five deep. The bottom layer is etched to the rock. You could not pry one loose without tearing it. The top layer is dry, cracked, leathery — the old ones, the sun-shields. The middle layers are supple, engorged, gently pulsing with stored heat.

And it *breathes*. Not like lungs. Every three to four minutes — hold your breath on the rim and you will feel it — the entire carpet beats its cilia once, in a wave that starts at the center of the mesa and runs to the edges. A pressure drop. A sigh. Hot, mineral air only a few degrees warmer than ambient is pulled down into the interstices of the mat, scrubbed for the last iron vapor, for the sulfur exhaled by the basalt itself, and then expelled. The waste heat ripples above them on cold nights as a visible shimmer, although there is no water to make it.

They metabolize heat. Literally. During the day the basalt soaks until it is too hot to touch. At night the stone re-radiates in the infrared, a low maroon afterglow you feel on your shins. The kites press themselves to it and run that thermal differential through thermoelectric stacks in their dorsal plates — biological Seebeck generators, protein semiconductors. Heat *is* food. When the rock cools toward dawn, they go torpid, flat and still. When it warms again, they wake.

That is why they bleach the stone beneath them. It is not just mining. It is cooling. They are drinking the stored fire out of the rock.

### How They Are One

Watch for an hour and the horror and the beauty become the same.

There is no sound because there is no need to signal with sound. The mat signals with touch and with infrared.

A kite shifts — perhaps the stone beneath it has gone a fraction cold. It lifts its edge, a papery *flick*, exposing salt-pale belly for a heartbeat. The hundred around it all tilt a millimeter to close the gap, and the wave propagates outward, a ripple across a dark pond, until the whole basin has adjusted. No overlap. No panic.

Predation is not an interruption of this. It *is* this.

There are two other residents, and they are not separate.

**The first are the Needles.** You thought they were salt at first. They are long, thin, centipede-pale, almost transparent, lying flat in the white pans where the kites thin. They have no sail. They do not need heat from the rock; they steal it ready-made. They lie motionless for minutes, hours, until a kite at the edge of the pan lifts wrong. Then a proboscis — not a mouth, a harpoon — fires two body-lengths out on hydrostatic pressure, barbed glass. It punches through dorsal leather and anchors. It does not pull violently. It reels, slow, chemical, dissolving the oil out of the kite as it comes. The kites next to the victim do not flee. There is nowhere to flee to. They flow closed, sealing the wound. A new kite will bud there by morning, splitting off the margin of a neighbor.

**The second is the mat itself, eating itself.** Sometimes a kite in the middle of the thickest pavement will shudder, flip over — salt-pale to the dead stars — and go rigid. Senescence, or a fault in its thermopile. Instantly the half-dozen around it raise their fringes, not to touch, but to hover, and exude a clear bead of acid-oil from their ventral pores. No teeth. No chewing. The flipped kite softens at the edges, then liquefies from the outside in, its oils wicked directly into its neighbors. The pale shape gets smaller and smaller until there is only rock again, briefly damp with hydrocarbon, then dry. The pavement is seamless.

In both cases, observe the whole mesa: when a strike happens in the north pothole, the south basin goes utterly still. When the south basin ripples with self-digestion, the north is silent. Attention is finite. The mat is not a colony of thousands. It is one nervous system distributed across millions of flat bodies, passing a token of awareness around by pulses of low infrared — each dorsal sail flashing, invisibly to you, a brief dimming as it dumps heat — and by the traveling wave of cilia-tug. It cannot attend to everything at once. So it attends in turns.

It is not intelligent the way you are. It does not wonder. But it remembers heat. The places where stone stayed warm longest last night are where the mat will be thickest tonight. The places where a Needle struck yesterday are avoided tomorrow by a subtle thickening of the top leathery layer, a callus.

### Reproduction and Death

They do not mate. Why would they? At the margins of the pavement, where the carpet frays into single individuals at the very lip of the basalt drop, you can see a kite develop a second point at its rear. Over a night the point grows, darkens, forms its own fringe. For a day the two kites share one oil belly. Then with a soft tearing — that papery page-turn — they part. The parent stays. The daughter crawls, inch by inch, cilia rowing, to fill a gap.

Spores of a kind also exist — not airborne, the wind would kill them — but salt-encased cysts the size of peppercorns, dark and glassy, tucked into cracks under the cairns. If a whole basin were scoured — a rockfall, a rare rain that would actually murder them by introducing water — those cysts could wait decades for dry heat again.

Nothing rots here. There are no bacteria to rot them. When they truly die and are not recycled, they desiccate into a brittle flake identical to basalt scale and blow away.

### The Builders

They did not build the cairns.

The four or five circles of dry-stack basalt are too vertical, too full of angles the kites avoid. Inside, the wind eddies wrong and the mat will not grow. That is why the salt there is thick enough to write in.

Whoever stacked them came when the mesa was already covered, saw the living floor, and understood not to sleep on it. They stayed a night, or a season, breathing the thin dry air that sticks your nose shut, feeling the whole mesa inhale under them. They scratched lines in the salt — tallies of nights, a map of the rim, a constellation directly above the saddle where the wind polishes stone to glass — and left because there is no water and heat alone is not enough for your kind.

The salt is healing over their marks, hexagon by hexagon. In another year the circles will be empty again.

Above, the stars give no heat. Below, the rock gives all of it. And between, pressed flat against the cooling world, the pavement waits for the stone to exhale again, lifts its million fringes a fraction, and drinks the dark.
