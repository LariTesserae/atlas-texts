What lives here has never needed to be seen to be believed.

Nothing with eyes lives here. Nothing that ever learned to run.

That was the first thing the builders — without knowing it — decided for this place. By leveling the continent. By fitting it so perfectly with brick and basalt and limestone that no soil was left uncovered, no rock left rough enough to hide in. By making a Floor so vast and so regular that when the water came — slowly, or all at once, it no longer matters — there was nowhere for anything quick to hide from the cold. No tidepool. No reef edge. No warm shallows. Just flat, cold weight and a dusk that has no sun.

So what was left to live was what could live without moving.

### The Tenants

They are plants, but only if you use the word loosely. They are not green. Not really. Green is a luxury of strong light, and strong light never reaches the Floor.

Here they are black at a distance. Come closer — put your face where your breath would fog if breath fogged in water that cold — and they resolve:

The dark olive of the **Holdfasts**. They are the oldest things here. They do not grow *on* the Floor so much as *into* it. You will find one every few meters, wherever two bricks meet. At first it looks like a knot of tarred rope. Then you see it is alive; a low, flat rosette of leathery fingers gripping the stone, each finger swelling with stored dim light, each seam exuding a faint acid that softens mortar one molecule at a time. They do not hurry. A holdfast can spend a hundred years widening a crack by a millimeter. When it dies — if it dies — it does not rot so much as delaminate, peeling away in sheets that drift and re-root elsewhere. The bricks they have split are still holding them, like hands holding hands.

Above them, the **Canal Kelp**. In the long, straight avenues where the water is a fraction less still, they rise in rows. Not planted, but obedient. Each one is a single stalk thicker than your thigh, anchored deep in silt that has collected in the avenue's centerline, rising ten or twenty feet and then bending, because there is not enough light to justify standing straight. At the top, a crown of broad, brown-black blades, each blade pitted and heavy and slick with a cold oil that keeps it from freezing. They do not sway. They *lean*, all together, when a contour current passes — a current so slow you would not feel it as flow, only as a sudden slight cold on one cheek. Every few months, a bladder near the crown will ripen and pop — *pat*, soft as a knuckle — sending a single bubble of oxygen upward on a journey of minutes. That is the loudest thing the kelp ever does.

And everywhere else, the **Fur**.

It is not fur. It is sheet-algae, filament-algae, felt-algae, a hundred species that have given up being species and become a texture. An inch thick on vertical walls. A foot thick on plazas. Two feet thick in the sunken courtyards and dry cisterns where the spores settle like snow. If you brush it, it does not tear. It parts, cold and silken, and shows for a moment the pinkish terracotta underneath before closing again. If you watch a plaza for an hour, you will see it breathe: a ripple passing from one edge to the other as the whole mat contracts by a fraction, squeezing out water, then relaxing. No animal tells it to do this. It is simply exchanging what little warmth the stones held during the last turbidity bloom.

From every lintel that no longer holds a roof, from every colonnade stump worn to a dome, hang the **Curtains**. Filaments, fifteen feet long, translucent brown, fine as hair at the tip and thick as cord at the root. They never tangle. They hang perfectly vertical, spaced by the slow fall of silt, swaying only when you pass too close and your displaced water pushes them. They are cold to the touch — colder than the water, somehow — and if you hold one, you will feel it very faintly try to curl around you. Not to grab. To hold position. To taste the iron in your skin through a glove.

All of them live by the same unbreakable economy:

There is not enough light to grow fast, so they grow perfectly. There is not enough heat to rot fast, so they do not die fast. There are no grazers to prune them, so they prune each other by shading. A frond that shades its neighbor does not kill it; it simply forces it to lean a degree further toward the avenue. Over ten thousand years, this has produced a forest that looks tended. Hedges that follow property lines that no one remembers. Topiary filling rectangular pools to the brim. An entire city, clipped into a garden by physics.

### The Other Residents

Because plants alone could never be this lush, there are smaller things still.

In the silt itself — that grey-white flour that never quite settles — lives a bacterial snow. It is not visible as organisms. You see it only when you disturb the Floor: the silt puffs up and hangs, and in the bruised dusk it glints faintly, not with light of its own but with the way life slightly changes water. It smells, if you could smell here, like cold iron and wet stone. It is what breaks the dead fronds down, so slowly that a fallen sheet of algae can lie intact for years, softening, becoming transparent, until one day it is simply more silt.

And in the water column, forever falling and never landing, are the **Drift**. Spores, cuttings, leaf-bits, whole tiny rosettes that broke off a holdfast a mile away. They are too small to see unless a shaft of that directionless dusk catches them, and then they look like dust in a vaulted hall. They move so slowly you have to watch one against a brick seam to convince yourself it is moving at all. Every one of them is alive, and almost every one of them will never root. It doesn't matter. The forest makes millions for every one that finds a crack. It can afford to waste. Time is the one thing it has never lacked.

This is why the sound here is as it is. No clicks. No songs. Only the low groan of a million stones ticking as a tenth-of-a-degree current passes, the sigh of a mat sloughing under its own weight, the pat of a bladder. The forest listening to itself grow, at the speed stone rounds.

### What Does Not Live Here Anymore

There were hands here once. Primitive, patient hands that split basalt with wedges and pounded limestone with other limestone. You can still feel where they were, if you kneel and press your palm through the fur to the brick. The arris is gone, the face pitted, but the squareness remains — the stubborn, human insistence on a right angle, on a course that runs true for a hundred miles.

They do not live here. But something of them does: the decision to make the world flat, regular, endless, and therefore — when drowned — perfect for what does live here. They wanted a forever. They got one. Just not theirs.

If you stay long enough, the cold works itself not into your bones but into your sense of scale. You stop looking for a fish. You stop waiting for something to dart. You understand that what lives here succeeds precisely because it never needed to dart. It stayed. It spread. It learned the seams.

You are the only sudden thing this ocean has seen in ten thousand years. And if you leave — and you will, or you will become silt — the curtains will close behind your passage, the fur will smooth over where you knelt, and the Floor will continue, plaza after terrace after canal, black-green and rust-brown and utterly, attentively alive, in the patient, soaking cold.
