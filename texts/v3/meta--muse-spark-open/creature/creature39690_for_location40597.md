They live there.

Not many. Not few. Exactly as many as the pocket will hold without spilling.

At first, you think there are fewer than there are. Because they are so still, and so the color of the place, and because the light is so poor. You count houses — sixty, maybe seventy, all shoulder-to-shoulder, roof-become-floor — and you expect a town. Noise. Laughter. Dogs. Instead you notice one person at a time, the way you notice a stone that is wetter than the stones around it.

Then you learn to see them and you realize you have been looking directly at them for minutes.

### The Body That Fits

They are human. That is the first thing that has to be said clearly, because everything else makes you want to qualify it.

Human, but edited by a long, cool, wet patience.

Their skin has never been dry in living memory. It is pale the way things that have never seen the sun are pale, but not white — an undertone of grey-pink, like the biofilm in the cisterns. Like river stone held too long in a palm. Their fingertips never un-pruned. The pads are ridged and swollen, softened and then hardened again, over and over, until the whorls of their fingerprints are deep as flagstone grooves. Babies are born with them already puckered.

Their hair is dark and lank and almost always plastered. Not long, not short — cut raggedly with chipped basalt when it gets in the eyes. It never dries, so it never moves right. It clings to scalp and neck. The old have very little of it; their scalps shine like the belly-pillars.

Their eyes are the only startling thing. In that tea-colored 0.7 light, you need eyes that will take what little is given. So theirs do. The irises are washed out, hazel gone to amber gone to wet-slate grey, and the pupils are always large, even when they look at a lamp up close. They don't squint. They have learned not to. A child will stare straight into a fat-stone flame without flinching, just breathing that heavy air. In the dark between lamp-pools, their eyes catch the amber and throw it back for a half second, not like an animal, like wet stone.

They are not thin, not fat. Preserved. The cool that never quite becomes cold has slowed something in them. The flesh hangs close to the bone. There is little fat to lose when you live on scraped cistern-grease and blind shrimp. But nobody looks starved, either. Nobody looks like they are in the process of anything. Like the water level, they maintain.

They move slowly. Not from age, not from ritual — from economy. When you have to displace ankle-deep water with every step, when every sound you make has to compete with a hundred *tok-tok-toks* a second, when the ceiling is low enough to kiss your scalp if you stand too straight — you learn a different energy. They stoop by habit even where they could stand. They turn sideways to pass between houses because they have always turned sideways. They lift their feet clear of the floor-sheet and set them down flat, spreading the splash, so it doesn't slap. Children learn this before they learn to talk, because a child who splashes is a child who gets shushed by everyone within three houses.

And they are loud without knowing it. Their voices are hoarse, worn to a rasp by a lifetime of leaning in and speaking slightly too forcefully. When two of them talk, they stand very close, foreheads nearly touching beside a lamp-pool, and half-shout in voices wrecked by damp. An old woman will tell a story at what she thinks is a hush and you will hear every word across the pocket, bouncing off the wet ovoid walls.

They have stopped hearing the tok. You can tell by how they blink. A visitor flinches with every drop that lands near their face. They don't. Their ears are full of it, the way deep divers' ears are full of pressure. If you plug their ears they get dizzy.

### How They Are Together

There is no inside inside.

Doors are cured hide, dark and damp, never fully closed because they'd stick to the frame. Windows are just holes to share lamp light. Walls are shared. Roofs are floors. That one ladder you thought led to a roof actually leads to someone's kitchen on top of someone else's bedroom. You hear everything through the slate — not as sound so much as vibration through stone and water.

So they live as a single household that has decided to pretend it is sixty households.

Privacy is curtain-deep and everyone respects the curtain absolutely, because there is no other choice. You do not lift another's hide without calling. You do not look through another's hole without being invited by light. Inside, a family — two, three, four people, never many — tends its own things: its own pot set out to catch the sweet ceiling water, its own lamp that must never go out, its own runnel scraped in the flagstone to keep the floor-water moving past the door instead of through it.

The work is maintenance at the scale of a shrine.

One woman tends a line of tiny travertine terraces behind her house, empty white bone-dams no bigger than your palm, scooping silt that never accumulates with a flat stone, over and over. Not because they fill. Because to stop would be to admit they might fill.

A man sits for an hour chipping a bowl from oil shale that weeps oil as fast as he carves it. His hands make the tok sound when the hammer hits. A child watches, then takes over, same rhythm. The rubble at their feet never grows into a pile.

Another scrapes the grey-pink film from the inside of a cistern with a shell that is not from here — no one remembers where the shells came from — and eats it without expression, and offers you some. It tastes like cold stone and the faintest iron of blood.

Children are rare. You might count six, and then later realize two of those were small adults. They don't play the way outside children do; there is nowhere to run without squeezing, and splashing is rude, and there is nothing to chase. So they play at stillness. You see three of them standing under a weeping in the wall with their mouths open, seeing who can stand longest while water beads on their eyelashes and falls. You see two more sitting perfectly mirrored across a lamp, copying each other's blinks. They learn early that the most interesting thing to watch is another face in amber light, because there is nothing else that changes.

The old do not look old the way outside old do. Preserved, again. Their faces are smoothed rather than wrinkled, rubbed glossy like the pillars by shoulders brushing past for decades. Their stoop is more pronounced — they have been ducking the cool ceiling for longest. They speak the least, because hoarseness compounds with age until their voices are mostly breath. They are listened to not because of authority, but because it takes so much effort for them to speak that everyone leans in to make it worth it.

There is no chief, no council, no square. If you ask who decides where a new house goes, they look confused. No new house has gone anywhere since the second generation that built on top of the first roofs. If you ask who settles disputes, they say: we move our pots. Meaning: if your runnel sends floor-water into my doorway, I move my doorway. There is always another gap between walls to squeeze through.

They remember the rat. Everyone remembers the rat, even those born after it. How it came down the breathing fissure in the ceiling that no ladder reaches, how it was blind with terror and so loud in the water, how it took four of them to catch it and how they divided it and ate it raw because fire is too precious to waste on meat. They speak of it with a reverence that would be religious if they had religion. This place is too balanced for gods. They have the bubble's breath instead — that once-a-day thickening when the air shows your ghost back to you. At that hour, whoever is awake stops working and just watches their breath hang too long before dissolving, and nods. Inhale, they think. Exhale nothing. Equilibrium.

If you stay long enough, you notice the quietest thing about them:

They never look at the walls. Never marvel. Never touch the calcite fans or the manganese blooms unless they have to scrape them away. They never stare at the ceiling where all that water is coming from and not raising the level.

You do. You can't help it. Your mind snags on it — the thermodynamic impossibility made mundane — and you keep glancing up, waiting for the pocket to give itself away.

They have lived inside a moment that never passes for so long that they have become part of the moment. They don't watch the water fall. They are the thing the water falls around. The stone sweats, the lamp sweats oil, the air sweats, their skin sweats, and none of it ever rises.

If you lie on your back in the center and see every edge at once, you will feel unsettled by how comprehensible it all is. If they lie on their backs in the center — and sometimes at the breathing hour, a few of them do — they look comprehensible to themselves.

That is what lives here: a people weathered to perfection who have stopped weathering.
