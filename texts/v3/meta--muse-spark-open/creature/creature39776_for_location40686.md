They do not live *on* this country. They live *in its skin.*

Everything that survives here has made its peace with the same facts: there is no high ground, there is no shade, there is no green to eat, and the water will never leave you alone.

### The Sheet Itself

First — and most of them — is what you mistook for rock color.

Crouch until your eyes are at water level and the peneplain stops being stone at all. The black and amber slick on every slab is alive. Biofilm in colonies the size of countries, bacteria and fungus in pact, laying themselves down as leather, as varnish, as trembling jelly. In the pans it thickens into mats that give under your boot — pale translucence shot with blood-red filaments, retreating from your fingertip with a slow, muscular cringe. At night it breathes light. Not bright; a deep amber phosphor, like cooling iron, tracing every watershed and seep in glowing veins visible for a kilometer. It is the pasture, the forest, the field. Everything else eats it, directly or once-removed.

In the clear pools it builds itself houses. Stromatolitic domes, knee-high and perfectly round, breathe in the shallows — exhaling oxygen in visible strings of pearls. Tufa towers grow grain by grain where the old shield exhales cold spring water, dripped candle-wax of silica and lime. If you sit still you can hear them tick as they add a layer.

There is no photosynthesis as you know it here. No leaves ever learned the chemistry. Life runs on what falls from the dim sky, what degasses from deep gneiss, what the rain dissolves out of a billion years of polished flour.

And because that film is everywhere, everything else can be everywhere too.

### The Grazers and the Hiss

You hear them before you see them correctly. That endless *shhhhhhk, shhhhhhk.*

They are the lawnmowers. Smooth, dark ovals the size of dinner plates, then serving platters, then — in the deeper anastomosing runs — the size of shields. A single chitin-calcium shell, barnacle-black, seamless except for a soft, feathered fringe underneath where two dozen gills clap and flutter. They plow through biofilm with a radula you can hear scraping stone a hundred meters away, leaving pale grazing tracks that will re-darken by morning. When startled they flip, gills snapping shut with a wet *pop*, showing a blind, pale foot that can suction to basalt so hard you cannot pry it with a knife.

Where the grazers go, everything else follows. The stone between waters is covered in their shit — fine, mineral-rich paste that the next rain turns back into sheetflow.

### The Air Over The Water

It never clears. A low cloud of what you first called moths hangs a hand's breadth over every flooded pan — thousands, tens of thousands, a shivering, translucent smoke. They are each as big as your palm, with four wings so thin you see the rain through them, beating at a pitch that makes your jaw ache. They have no legs worth the name. They have a siphon, a soft proboscis they dip as they skim, filtering bacteria and spores out of the top millimeter of water the way baleen filters krill. Individually they are almost silent. Together their wingbeat is a high electrical whine that seems to come from inside your skull. The dragonflies hunt them.

The dragonflies are not dragonflies. Forearm-long, amber-black, with two pairs of glass wings that do not blur but *click* — audibly — at each stroke. Their heads are all eye and shears. They hawk through the moth-cloud and you hear the snap each time they take one, like someone breaking a dry stick.

And from the tufa towers, the pipers argue with both of them.

### The Pipers

They own the night. Six limbs — four squat legs to cling to tufa, two forelimbs too small for walking that they hold tucked like a mantis — bodies the color of wet jasper, throats like stretched drums. Each male inflates his throat to a grapefruit, to a melon, translucent and veined, and releases it: *PLOK.*

Not a croak. A clear, bell-like, almost metallic note that carries impossibly far under the cloud lid. One pool answers another half a kilometer away. Hundreds per pool, hundreds of pools. All day they are staggering, arrhythmic, deafening.

For five minutes after true dimming — when the pearlescent sky gives up trying to be day — they synchronize. Every piper on the plain, as far as you can hear and farther, locks into a single pulse. *PLOK... PLOK... PLOK...* so regular you feel it through the water in the soles of your boots, through the bedrock in your teeth. Then, as if embarrassed, they fall apart again into chaos. No one knows what they are counting to.

In the water under them, the eyeless knot. Eels of white muscle and cartilage, as thick as your wrist, with lateral frills for breathing air and no eyes at all, only heat-pits along a blunt snout. They surface to gulp with a despairing human gasp that echoes, then dive to hunt grazer eggs.

Between the pools struts the other nation.

### The Stilt-Walkers and The Cloak

On bare stone between sheetflows move herds of arthropods on legs so long and thin they look like compass-needles. Speckled armor exactly the mottled basalt and lichen beneath them — you will not see them until one moves and your brain edits it in. Twenty, forty together, picking their way with a delicate, constant *ticking* of joints, dipping needle-mouths to scrape the thinnest biofilm the grazers missed.

They are prey for something you will keep stepping on.

The cloak looks like a puddle. Wet leather, dark, fringed with cilia, with no front or back until it moves — and then it flings itself impossibly fast, smothering a stilt-walker, dissolving the joints with enzymes while the victim screams. The scream is stridulation, a tearing-metal screech that the other stilt-walkers ignore completely and keep walking around.

There are smaller hunters too — flat, segmented things under the jelly-mats that leave no sound — but the pattern is the same everywhere: graze, filter, drum, smother, snap. An entire ecology built without a single stem or trunk to hide behind. Everything happens in the open, in the wet, in the dim.

It is deafening, and it never stops, because the water never stops.

### The People Who Tend It

And they are here. Sparsely. So sparsely you could walk a day and think the plain was empty if you didn't know how to look.

They are not a lost colony. They are what this place makes when it makes people.

Short, broad, pale to the point of translucence — skin with almost no melanin left to waste under a sun that never shines, eyes light grey or amber with a nictitating lid to wipe the constant mizzle. Hair kept cropped or shaved, because everything that trails gets wet and stays wet. You find it first in the things they leave, not in them: the low C-shaped weirs of unmortared slate, mouth downstream, scraped clean of tufa to make a catchment pool for grazers; the cairns of balanced jasper with offerings tucked underneath — chert flakes knapped into perfect little grazer-shells, bundles of dried jelly-fungus, a river stone drilled clean through with sand and patience.

Their technology is hands and stone. Copper is legend — a single wire twisted around a staff, green with verdigris, carried for generations. Iron would rust before it could be kept. So they flake chert, they polish basalt, they stretch hides and rub them with mineral grease until they are translucent as their window panes. Their huts are not built so much as leaned: three greenstone slabs propped together and roofed with those greased hides, low enough you must crawl in. Inside, the floor is woven fungal cord — not reed, there are no reeds — dried and re-woven endlessly. The hearth is a star-shaped crack in the bedrock itself, in the same spot for a hundred years of fire.

They do not live in villages. The plain will not feed a village. Families or small bands of six to twelve move with the sheetflood, following the grazer migration which follows the biofilm bloom which follows the rain. They repair a weir, use it for a week until the pool is exhausted, then leave it for the plain to claim again. Their footprints fill with seep even as they make them.

When you do see them, in the dim clearing when the clouds thin, they are already looking at you. Standing ankle-deep a hundred meters off, perfectly still in their speckled, basalt-colored capes woven from fungal fiber and stilt-walker chitin. Not hiding. Not approaching. Watching with that pale, wet stare to see if you know how to be quiet enough to hear the plain over its own noise.

They do not farm. They *graze* as the grazers do — scraping biofilm and jelly-mats, roasting the big-shelled ones on the star-hearths, trapping eels with woven cord, collecting the moth-things in fine nets when they swarm low. Their children know not to drink from the pools that the rocks breathe into.

### The Ones That Breathe

Because there is one more inhabitant, and the people know to leave it alone.

Certain erratics — boulders that do not belong, granite and gneiss different from the pavement, left by ice that has been gone a million years — exhale. On cool days you see it: warm, mineral-smelling mist sighing from pitted holes in their sides, even when no sun could warm them. The biofilm around those vents grows in perfect concentric rings no current explains. Put your ear to one, and under the hiss of water and the PLOK and the whine, you hear it:

*click... click......... click.*

Slow. Irregular. Like a cooling clock. Like something counting down that has been counting since the mountains were mountains.

The water near those stones tastes of metal and numb. The stilt-walkers will not cross it. The pipers go silent around it. The human weirs are never built downstream of one.

At night the people will tell you — if they speak to you at all, in a low tongue that sounds like water over slate — not to pitch your hide near a breathing rock. Not because it will hurt you now. Because it remembers being a mountain, and it is still settling, and slow things do not like to be interrupted.

You are wet to the knee now. The amber light has come up in threads all around you, outlining the watershed you stand in. A grazer slides past your boot, scraping, ignoring you as you have learned to ignore the cold. Somewhere very far off, the pipers are starting to pull together. You understand why the people build so low, why they carve grazer-shapes out of chert and leave them for the water.

On a shield planed flat by a billion years of rain, to live at all is to learn how to be a film on stone — thin, widespread, and able to move whenever the world decides to weep.
