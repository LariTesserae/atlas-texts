What lives here doesn't live *on* the plain. It lives *in* the condition of it.

Nothing here is fast. Nothing here is warm. Nothing here wastes. To live at this depth is to have made a truce with pressure and cold and hunger so old it no longer feels like a truce. It feels like being part of the stone.

So you have to learn to see them by degrees. At first you think the plain is dead. Then you realize it is covered.

### The First Inhabitants: The Film

The true metropolis of this place is not animal at all. It is bacterial.

Every nodule — those fist-sized, head-sized, barrel-sized iron-manganese potatoes that tile the floor shoulder-to-shoulder for a hundred kilometers in any direction — wears a skin. A black-brown biofilm, thin as oil, slick to the touch if you had a bare hand. It grows a millimeter per thousand years. It does not photosynthesize; there has never been a photon here worth eating. It eats the nodule itself. It oxidizes manganese. It breathes iron and seawater.

It is the oldest living thing you will ever not-see. It coats everything. Between the nodules, where the pale ooze of dead diatoms and volcanic dust has settled into a cold porridge, other mats spread — tar-black, wrinkled like dried paint, cracking only when a current strong enough to roll a stone finally peels them up.

If you put your face close — close enough that your light makes a cone three feet wide in the black — you see it move, but not as animals move. It *creeps*. Over months. Over decades. A veil drawing itself one cell thicker toward a faint chemical seep from a hairline crack in the basalt below. This is vegetation here, if you can call it that. No leaves. No roots. Just hunger made flat and patient.

And on the film, the first animals graze.

They are smaller than your fingernail, and there are millions of them you will never count. Foraminifera like grains of sand that have grown legs, dragging their spiral shells through the ooze and leaving braided tracks that the next surge erases. Nematodes, transparent as glass noodles, thrashing between grains. Eyeless copepods the size of sesame seeds, flicking once an hour to reposition. They live their entire lives on a single nodule, born in a divot where two stones touch, dying in the silt a handspan away. The plain is, to them, not a plain at all. It is an archipelago. Each nodule is an island the size of a continent, separated by lethal deserts of ooze that take generations to cross.

They are the cattle. And like cattle, they are tended only by being eaten.

### The Hermits

The second kind of life here gave up on moving.

Where a nodule sits just a centimeter prouder than its neighbors — enough to catch a little more of the invisible river roaring endlessly overhead — something takes advantage of that useless height. It cannot afford to hunt. It cannot afford to swim. So it opens itself and waits.

A glass sponge, to start. Not a plant, though you will think it is. A vase of spun silica, white as bone, six inches tall, so fine you could read light through it if there were any. Its filaments are finer than hair. All day and all night, which are the same thing here, it sieves. The water that is the world passes through it — that cold, heavy, endlessly scouring thermohaline flow — and it takes what sticks: a flake of marine snow that has been falling for three weeks from a death two kilometers above, a bacterial clump, a scale from a fish that passed a month ago. Its body is mostly water and glass, because flesh is expensive.

Beside it, not quite beside it — ten meters away is *beside* here — a stalked crinoid stands on a single jointed stem like a flower that forgot how to be a flower. Its arms, feathered and pale pink, unfold into the current and do not close. They do not have to. Food comes to them, or it does not. They have been standing in that exact spot since before your grandparents were born. They will stand there until a turbidity surge finally topples the nodule they are cemented to, and then they will die standing up.

And lower, flatter, the tube worm you noticed: a white dot in the mud between two nodules that have leaned against each other for a million years. It owns a tube of mucus and iron it has secreted grain by grain. When the water is still — which is never, quite — it extends a crown of tentacles no wider than a pencil eraser, red with the only hemoglobin for miles. It filters. When the pressure wave comes, it vanishes. It can wait a year between meals. It has no eyes because an eye is a promise that light will return, and here that promise is a lie.

These are not a forest. They are not even a garden. Put twenty of them in a football field and you would call that field barren. Here, that density is a crowd. They are hermits whose hermitages happen to be visible to each other across the dark, but who never meet. The current is the only road, and it carries only crumbs.

### The Wanderers

Because crumbs are not enough for everyone.

So something has to walk.

Most of the time you see nothing, and you think the plain is empty again. Then the dark moves differently. Not a flicker — a displacement. A shape the size of a man, or a table, or a small car, that resolves only when it is almost too close.

First, the shrimp. Not the pink things you eat. These are *blind* and the color of old paper. Colonies of ten or twenty cling with hooked legs to the single glass sponge that can support them, their antennae beating constantly, not to swim but to hold place against the surge. They are not swimming. They are *hanging on*. When the sponge finally clogs or dies, they will let go all at once and tumble in the dark until another sponge, a hundred meters down-current, catches them or does not. Most do not. You will see empty sponges with the faintest white scars where legs once held.

Then the fish. Or what used to be fish before depth took most of what makes a fish a fish. A tripod fish, standing on three elongated fin rays like stilts, its body lifted just clear of the ooze so it does not sink. It faces into the current, mouth open, absolutely still. You could watch it for an hour and think it was dead, until its jaw snaps on a copepod you never saw, with a speed that betrays everything else about it.

A grenadier, long and rat-tailed, nosing between nodules like a pig in a field, stirring up clouds of silt that hang for hours. Its eyes are huge and useless — vestigial lenses built for a light that never comes — but its lateral line is a miracle, feeling the *knock* of two nodules clicking together a kilometer away.

And over them all, when it comes, the shark.

It is not like a surface shark. There is nothing fast or sleek about it. The Pacific sleeper, or its Atlantic cousin, comes pale as congealed fat, its skin pocked with parasites that glow faintly green, like algae on a stone. It is blind within a year of birth; its corneas are eaten by copepods that live nowhere else. It does not need eyes. It is six feet long, or ten, or fifteen — age has no meaning when you grow an inch per decade and live for four hundred years. It moves so slowly that the water does not part around it so much as *accommodate* it, closing behind it without a wake. It does not hunt. It patrols. It inhales. A dead whale falls somewhere far above, and six months later its scent finally reaches the plain, and the shark turns, one degree per minute, and begins to go, arriving a year later to a skeleton already cleaned by hagfish. To live this long, you have to have nowhere to be.

Every one of them is solitary. There are no schools here, because a school is a fire — it burns too much. There are no songs, because sound carries too far and brings only larger mouths. They pass each other in the black like trains with their lights off, feeling the pressure bow wake and nothing else, and continue.

They are perfect for this place: energy-conserving, unhurried, ancient individually and as lineages. They could have been here since before the nodules finished growing.

But they did not build the cairns.

### The Builders

You almost miss them, because you are looking for an animal.

The cairns are small. Embarrassingly small against the scale of the plain. Two or three flat nodules, broken from a larger one along a natural fracture, balanced on top of a round one. Or a nodule the size of your head that has been *moved* — dragged a foot to cap a hole between two others, where no current could have rolled it. A line, ten meters long, where someone brushed the ooze aside with something — a fin, a claw, a stick — exposing the paler basalt underneath, then let it settle again. Already half-erased. Fuzzy with that same black mat.

A fish does not do this. A shark does not do this. A shrimp cannot move a stone that heavy. And the currents, for all their violence, only knock down. They do not stack.

Who lives here that needs a marker on a floor that has no features?

You stay a second day — a third — your light lowered to save power, letting the occasional bioluminescent flash do your looking for you, and you finally see them move.

They are not big. That is the first surprise. In a place where bigness saves you, they are stubbornly small.

Think of a spider crab crossed with a squat lobster, but you have to throw away most of what you know. The body is perhaps a foot across, low and flattened like a living knuckle of nodule itself, its carapace the exact rust-black of manganese, pitted and bearded with bacterial film so it disappears against the stones even when you are looking straight at it. It has no eyes. Not small eyes, not degenerate eyes — *no* eyes. Where eyes should be there are only smooth, armored hemispheres, scarred over generations ago.

What it has instead are arms. Not two. Six, eight, jointed absurdly long for its body, ending not in claws but in flattened, shovel-like dactyls, broad and sensitive. And on its back, living on it the way lichen lives on rock, a colony of luminous bacteria that it farms — not bright, not pretty, a dull amber smear that it can cover or expose with a fold of skin, like shuttering a lantern.

It lives in the interstices. Not on top of the nodules, where the current would take it, but *between* them, in the narrow, ooze-filled canyons where two stones press together. That is its home. That is its world. To it, the plain is not a plain at all. It is a maze — a city of boulders seen from ant-height, where a gap a finger wide is a boulevard and a pothole is a plaza.

And it *tends* that maze.

Watch one — if you have the patience, and at this depth you have nothing but patience — for an hour. It emerges leg over leg, feeling with those shovel hands. It finds a nodule that has been rolled a centimeter by the last surge, blocking a gap where it likes to sieve. It braces its back against another nodule — you can hear the faint *tick* as carapace meets stone — and pushes. Slowly. Absurdly slowly. An hour to move a stone the size of an apple a hand's breadth. Then it stops. It pats the ooze flat. It places a flatter shard atop the pile. Not for shelter. The gap it just opened *is* its shelter. It places it to remember.

Another one, twenty meters away, answers. Not with sound. With light. It opens its back for two seconds, a dim amber glow that illuminates three nodules around it and nothing else, then closes. The first one, a minute later, answers with its own.

They are not building a city. They are not building art. They are doing what humans did before humans had a word for it: making the featureless place *legible*. This nodule means *food collects here when the northern current runs*. That stack means *the ground here is soft, dig*. That cleared line means *follow me, the elders go this way when the turbidity comes*.

It is tool use so early it barely qualifies. No carving. No fire — fire is impossible. Only rearrangement. Only memory made physical, because in total darkness and total sameness, memory cannot survive inside the head alone. It has to be set *out* into the world, or the world erases it.

They have been doing it for a very long time. The cairns you thought were recent are just the newest. Under the bacterial mat, if you brush the silt away, you find older ones, collapsed, then under those, faint rings where nodules were cleared and then grew back together. Generations. Thousands of generations. A culture with no sight, no sound beyond the knock of stone on stone, no light except the one it grows on its own back and shutters like an eyelid.

They know you are here. Or rather, they know the pressure has changed and a new heat source is nearby. They stop building. They pull themselves deeper between the stones and shutter their lights. If you were to leave your lamp off for a long time and wait, absolutely still while the cold burned through your suit, you might see one amber flicker at the edge of vision, then gone. Checking. Remembering where the strange new nodule — you — has been placed in their plain.

They will outlast you. The currents will topple your cairn, whatever you build. The nodules will keep growing, a millimeter per thousand years, around shark teeth that have not seen a shark in an eon. The worm will extend and retract. The shark will pass, centuries from now, over the spot where you floated.

And in the dark between two iron stones, a small, blind builder will feel its way out again, and with patient, shovel hands, begin to restack what the sea has undone.
