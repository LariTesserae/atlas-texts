In the Low City nothing lives alone. That is the first thing to understand.

Not because loneliness is forbidden — there is no lawgiver to forbid it — but because there is no *room* for it. Space is a shared fluid here. Bodies are packed so tight that they have become each other's environment. The air between people is not empty, it's a medium that has been breathed and re-breathed and spiced with skin-oil and ozone until it has weight. So what lives here has learned to live *in* other things.

### The People Who Are Still People

They are still mostly human, in the stubborn way lichen is still mostly algae. Shorter, you notice first. Not from hunger — there is no shortage of calories when every flat of warm water can be made to grow bacterial curd — but from generations under a sky that never opens fully. Children grow where the pressure is highest, in the low pans where the air sits heaviest, and their bones take the hint. Broad chests, thick wrists and ankles, necks built to carry sound. They have to be loud here, and they are.

You hear a Low City native before you see them: a voice that starts in the diaphragm and doesn't waste time on high frequencies that the water-air will eat. They speak low and fast, clipped consonants, vowels that hum in the teeth. Many are hoarse by thirty. Many are half-deaf by the same age and consider it winnowing, not loss; they learn to read the vibration in deck-plates, to watch mouths and hands.

Their skin tells you where they work. No one is pale in the way highlanders are pale. Even those who never see direct sun are stained. The mineral seep touches everyone eventually. Salt dries white at the hairline. Iron speckles forearms where splash from a retention pan has sat. The very young have soft feet, strangely soft, because they never quite dry. The very old have feet like coral, callused into plates by wading, toes splayed by clinging to slick flow-deck.

They wear little that can rot. Woven carbon, reclaimed membrane, sheets of that dark glass that the transit films are made of, cut and sewn while still half-alive so it heals around seams. Nothing with pockets that hold water. Nothing precious. Everything is tied to the body with cord because anything not tied will be taken by a draft or a child or the City itself. Beards and hair are kept short or braided tight against condensers; even so, everyone grows a faint mustache of limescale where their breath leaves them in sleep. You learn to scrape it off your eyebrows in the morning with a thumbnail.

And they move. God, they move. A Low City human at rest is either asleep, sick, or newly dead. Even standing still they shift weight, tap a foot to the pump-rhythm in the floor, fidget with wares, wave flies off. To stop is to invite the felt to claim you. So children are in constant, shrieking orbit around adults, adults in constant trade, repair, haul, gossip-orbit around each other. Work is not distinguished from walking. You carry, you sort, you shout a price, you sweep silt, all while en route to somewhere else, because somewhere else is always flooding or drying and needs hands.

Families are wide, flat structures. Not clans, not lineages — rafts. Ten, twenty, fifty people tied by blood and convenience and shared debt who claim a segment of deck-together. They sleep in shifts in the same niches, hammocks strung three high in the wormhole shafts, floors marked only by a change in tile. Their wealth is measured not in ownership — nothing stays owned here for long — but in *throughput*: how many liters can you filter before noon, how many kilos of mineral felt can you scrape and press into protein, how many passengers can you wave onto your stretch of flow-film before the next raft diverts them.

They are kind in the abrasive, elbow-to-elbow way of people who cannot afford grievance. They will yell at you for blocking a drain and then, still yelling, pull you out before the surge hits. They remember faces, because faces are navigation marks in a place without addresses. They worship small, portable gods that can be folded up when the market lane becomes a dormitory — a calcite chip from a stalactite, a smooth bolt that always falls sideways, a laminated picture of blue sky none of them have seen unframed.

> Some of them are not quite people anymore and nobody in the raft thinks this is strange.

### The Modified and the Maintained

In any crowd this dense, the human form becomes a suggestion.

You see them in flickers through the bodies: a woman whose calves have split into two extra backward joints so she can wade without wetting her trade-trays, her feet wide as shovels. A boy with nictitating membranes he blinks sideways when warm drizzle hits his eyes — cheap, clinic-grown, everyone gets them now. The dock-men who have had their lungs ridged with more surface, like gills that still breathe air but can drink the soup of the lower pans for an hour without coughing. The elders who have replaced their teeth entirely with basalt-enameled plates because real enamel dissolves in the constant sour of condensed reclaimed water.

Then the further ones. The filament-tenders: tall, too thin to be healthy, their scalps bald and laced with black carbon lace that interfaces directly with the maintenance shimmer — the dust-that-isn't-dust. They stand in the rain of indoor condensation and don't get wet; droplets sheer around their field. They rarely speak aloud. When they do, drones answer.

The carriers: not human, not drone, but somewhere. Human torsos on frames of articulated pipe-rack, or human minds in bodies that are mostly flow-deck, their skin that same dark glass, rippling as they walk. They ferry water, power, gossip; they are both person and infrastructure and they like it. If you ask one where it lives, it will point to the street beneath your feet.

And the ones who have gone sessile. This is not death. In the quieter weeps, where the turbulence slackens just enough — behind a heat vane, at the dead end of a service cut — you find them grown into the sponge. A person who chose, or was chosen by exhaustion, to stop moving. The City has done exactly what it promised: built a room around them. Their legs are gone, fused into the deck. Bacterial felt clothes them in black and rust. Cables find their wrists. They filter, they warm, they hum, they watch. People bring them offerings of clean water and news, not from pity but because a sessile ancestor is a useful anchor. You can navigate by Old Mara-in-the-Wall the way you navigate by a stalactite.

No one pities them. Everyone checks, absently, that they can still feel their own toes.

### The Uncountable Neighbors

For every human or post-human, there are ten thousand other things moving.

Lowest, the film itself is alive, or alive enough. Not a species but a parliament: engineered cyan and chemotroph strains that were seeded to eat oil, then to eat the polymers that ate the oil, then to eat each other, layering for centuries into a felt half a centimeter thick that quivers when you step near the edge of a pan. At noon it is black and flat, drinking heat. At the shift change it blooms rust-red with iron oxidation, then sulfur-yellow, then black again. It smells faintly of blood and hot springs. It is harvested with flat shovels, scraped off deck, squeezed, fried, and without it the City would starve. It asks nothing except to be left wet.

In the film live the grazers. Thumb-sized isopods with backs like poured stone, so well camouflaged you think the deck itself is crawling. Palm-sized roaches — true roaches, *Blattodea* so ancient they have seen cities rise before — their wing-cases polished concrete-grey, their antennae always testing flow. They are not pests; they are custodians. They eat scale, they prune felt, they are eaten in turn. Children roast them on waste-heat grates and sell them in paper cones, still ticking faintly.

Above them, the air churn.

You thought the chirring was cicadas. Up close it resolves into three overlapping swarms that have learned to mimic each other's song until predator and prey share a chorus.

First, the white-motes: biofabricated filter flies, each no larger than a grain of rice, translucent wings beating too fast to see, guts full of engineered zeolite. They were released to clear the perpetual mist and instead learned to farm it, herding droplets into beaded curtains that they milk. They move like dust with purpose, a shimmer that parts around your face and reforms behind you.

Second, the things that eat them: geckos flattened to paper-thinness to live on vertical weep-walls, their toes suctioned to stalactites; swifts and swallows that are almost all wing, screaming as they cut the dim light-shafts; pigeons gone feral and then feral again, their feathers stained green-black with mineral, nesting by the thousand in the beards of calcite where the warmest air rises.

Third, the bigger hunters that have followed the birds. Not wolves — there has never been room for wolves. But cats. The Low City cat is a specific creature now, after a thousand kilometers of selection for those who could swim, climb wet glass, and tolerate noise. Long legs, little webbing between toes, ears that fold flat against spray. They stalk the retention pans on stilt-legs, pausing to stare at their own reflections in the lane-marked water, then spearing blind cave-mollusks or filter-fly clusters with a paw. They are semi-owned by everyone and owned by no one; they consider the sessile ones their gods. The rats, similarly, are long and low and excellent swimmers, their tails flattened into rudders. They run the same transit films people do, in opposite lanes, carrying stolen felt-cakes.

And woven through it all, the other swarm: not animal at all. Maintenance dust. Repair motes. Each mote stupid alone, brilliant together. You see them only when they choose or when sun slats hit wrong — a glitter in the drizzle indoors, a heat-haze with an edge. They rebuild, unclog, knit bacterial mats, re-lay flow-film. Children chase them and try to catch them in cupped hands; the careful adults cup water for them instead. The filament-tenders pray to them by standing still and letting them use their skin as scaffold for a minute.

All of it loud. All of it overlapping. The roar of pumps is the bass note that the birds tune to without knowing. The bargaining of humans becomes another birdcall to the cats. The ticking of ten million insect wings and drone wings sets the tempo for feet.

### How They Stay

None of this should cohere. Ask a Low City native how they keep it from tipping into clog and rot — the mineral beards growing, the pans silting, the people breeding — and they will shrug and say the City breathes.

Inhale: evaporation off the pans, off skin, off the rain that forms inside. Condensers on the megastructure shoulders cool it, wring it, drop it.

Exhale: seep from every wall, the warm drizzle indoors, the overflow lips that never fully close.

Inhale: people, birds, motes crowded in.

Exhale: heat, shunted up the heat vanes in visible shimmers, dumped to the sky that never sees it, then borrowed again as work-lamp light carried down in fiber braids.

Everything eats everything else's waste. Filter flies drink condensate and are eaten by swifts; swifts guano feeds felt; felt is scraped and eaten by people; people's wash-water feeds felt; excess mineral becomes stalactite becomes harvestable building material becomes new deck for new felt. Even stone here is a crop.

And so the low condition that should be suffocating — sea-level, lung-low, no gradient, no edge, no exit — has produced not apathy but a furious, loving attention. Every hand learns to sweep silt before it settles. Every eye learns to see the first lap of black felt over a forgotten crate and kick it free. Every throat learns the pump-song well enough to hear when one note goes flat.

They live here because nowhere else is this *tended*. Because to be packed this dense and still breathing is a kind of miracle that you have to keep performing with your own body, minute after minute, or it forgets you.

Stand on a bridge for an hour and you will see it: a delivery flier stall, a child chase a rat chase a roach across a flooded deck, a filament-tender raise a hand and the drizzle curve around it, an old sessile woman in her wall-niche blinking slowly, blessing the turbulent bazaar flowing past her face with air that tastes of wet stone and salt.

She does not need you to believe in her. She is part of the floor. The floor is alive.
