In the Sunk Hamlet almost nothing lives alone.

That is the first thing you get wrong, when you come down the old service stair cut into the limestone and your boots hit the spring of root-mat over concrete. It feels empty. 

It looks empty.

The streets are still there — you can see the grid they poured for the mill-houses and the row houses and the boilerworks, all right-angled and sensible — but there are no crowds in them. No market stalls. No children shouting. Just long, dim avenues of air held up between pillars of banyan-root and strangler-fig, with roofs made of leaf layered over leaf ten feet above your head, dripping. You could stand in the main avenue and see two hundred yards in either direction and count maybe one hammock, one flickering bulb, one wisp of cook-smoke. 

You think: abandoned.

And then the air moves wrong and you realize every cubic foot you thought was empty air is occupied.

### The Loud Ones

The Hamlet is never quiet, because it is full of things that use sound to be solid.

*   **The frogs** live in the thousands. Not in a pond — in the air. Each huge waxy leaf, each bromeliad cradled in a crotch where vine meets rusted I-beam, is a private pond. One frog per cup of water, shouting *I AM HERE* with a throat inflated like a pale grape. At noon they are deafening, and at canopy-rain they all stop at once for eight minutes of warm shower, and then all start again half a tone higher.

*   **The cicadas** are architects. They don’t synchronize by time. They synchronize by *street*. The east street drones in F-sharp. The west street answers in G. Where their waves meet in the middle of an intersection, the sound cancels into a pocket of cool quiet — and that’s where the young of everything have learned to walk. Where it doubles and your teeth hurt, that’s a drop where root has rotted through into a basement cistern. The children of the Hamlet — human and otherwise — navigate blindfolded by this map.

*   **The moths** are the size of your open hand, the color of old paper, and they live as a single decision. Most of the day they hang under the leaf-ceiling like damp shingles, perfectly still. Then something — a gear squeal, a swift’s scream — spooks one and they all lift in a silent papery blizzard that blots the amber light pillars for three seconds. When they settle again they have all rotated a few feet clockwise. No one knows why.

*   **The swifts** cut the upper air. They came for the smokestacks. The stacks are still there, brick throats open to the sky, and inside them the air is always rising, warm off the digesting humus. The swifts nest in the soot-pitted courses and fall screaming through the dim shaft in knives of black, eating moths mid-air, never landing on the floor if they can help it.

*   **The beetles** are mirrored and stupid and loud as thrown gravel. They have learned that corrugated tin — still present under sheets of moss and vine — sounds exquisite when you clang against it. So they do. All afternoon: *clang. clang. clang.*

### The Industries

And then there are the ones who took jobs in the factories after people left.

*   **The ants** took over the electrical. You’ll see their highways first and think they’re pipes: thick, black, continuous rivers running along every conduit, every old belt-rail, every window lintel. Millions of them carrying leaf fragments, fungal fluff, slivers of rust, all in one direction in the morning, the opposite in the evening. If you put your ear close you can hear their feet ticking like static. They farm scale insects on the undersides of the door-leaves.

*   **The rats** forgot how to be rats. Their pelts are no longer just hair. In the endless damp their backs have been colonized by a tight, short moss that stays green even when they’re running. They look like moving clumps of the wall. They have lost their fear of open ground because they *are* open ground. They gnaw vine, not wire, and their droppings sprout within a day.

*   **The vine-things.** It’s not that there are carnivorous plants. It’s that some of the ordinary vines — the ones that replaced the stair rails, the ones that thickened into walkways between second floors — learned a twitch. A fly lands. The tendril snaps half an inch, pinning it. You only notice if you stare. The leaf is not a mouth. The whole walkway is.

*   **The rust-slugs.** Fat as forearms, the color of wet brick, leaving silver-brown trails. They have radulae hard enough to rasp the pitting off iron. You find them at night under the flickering alley bulb that no one claims to power, grazing on a boiler flank, their bodies pulsing with faint ingested current. Their slime tastes of blood and smells of ozone. The frogs won’t eat them.

All of this is dense, layered, vertical. A beetle lives on a leaf that lives on a vine that lives on a rusted catwalk that lives on a brick wall that is itself alive with liverwort. Nothing is just itself.

### The Few People

And spaced out among all that, sparse as raisins in a loaf, are the people.

There are maybe three dozen. You will not see them together. The Hamlet punishes crowding — the sound gets too loud, the condensation finds you, the mold finds your lungs — so they have learned to be solitary nodes in the green machine.

A woman keeps bee-boxes in a former storefront where the glass is gone but the lintel remains, now a mossy brow. The bees are dark, slow, drunk on tannic flower. She moves slowly among them bare-armed, because swatting summons moths. She talks to them at the volume you’d use in a library.

A man sleeps in a hammock strung between two gear-wheels that still turn. Every eleven minutes the great north-wall bucket wheel groans over, lifts a chain of water and frogs and blossoms up into the gloom and lets them fall again with a squeal that echoes off limestone. He has learned to sleep through the squeal and wake in the quiet between. His skin has a faint green undertone around his cuticles where spores have taken residence. He doesn’t pick at it.

There is an old couple who tend the lily-pond that used to be a boiler tank, cutting back fig roots with machetes that they must clean every night or find furred green by morning. Because in the Hamlet, **metal grows.** Leave a wrench on a damp sill and in a week you have to tear it free from hair-roots seeking the iron oxide. Leave it a month and the wrench is a handle with a green-black crust that breathes. The people’s tools, their jewelry, their belt buckles — all are pitted, all are being digested patiently. They oil everything constantly. It’s a losing negotiation.

They don’t speak loudly. They don’t need to. They know that sound is a wall here, and that light is owned.

### What Really Lives Here

If you stand at the bottom center at dusk and look up, you’ll see it. The rectangle of hotter, silent sky framed by surface trees that lean but never fall. Below it, the air goes particulate with spores after the canopy has rained. Pillars of light cut through it, and in those pillars dust and moth-scale and beetle-wing hang suspended, turning.

On the quarry walls — what’s left of them, soft and pitted and glowing faintly — the bracket fungi the size of tables begin to light. Not all at once. In patches, pulsing. They glow brighter when the bucket wheel squeals, when the generator hums through vine-sheathed wire, when the cicadas hit their peak. Sound makes light here. The fungi are listening to the factory that is still running on root-tension and water-weight and vine-pull, that no one turned on and no one can turn off.

That is the final inhabitant. Not the people, not the frogs, not even the forest.

The Hamlet itself.

A plant-mass that learned to be a city. An industrial cut that learned to be a lung. It inhales warm air off the lowlands at night, condenses it on every leaf, rains on itself for eight minutes at noon, sweats steam, digests its own bones, powers a bulb in an alley where no one lives, and hums — always hums — with a thousand throats saying *I AM HERE*, *I AM HERE*, *I AM HERE*, so densely packed that the empty streets sound full.
