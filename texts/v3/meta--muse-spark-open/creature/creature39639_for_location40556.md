Whatever lives here lives in water before it lives in anything else.

Not in it the way a fish lives in a river. There is no river to be in, no bank to define it. Things here live *with* the water the way you live with air — it is simply the condition. The ground is water held together loosely by roots and peat. The air is water waiting to fall. Every body here is a negotiation about how wet to be and for how long.

Start small, because small is most of what there is.

In the tea-colored sheet-water between the sedge pedestals, in every footprint puddle and crab hole and inter-tussock pan, there are fish no longer than your thumb. They are dark on top, silver underneath, invisible until they flick. Rainbowfish, hardyheads, gudgeons, thousands to an acre. They do not need depth. An inch is depth to them. They travel across the plain not by swimming downstream but by swimming *across*, during the afternoon rain when the pans connect for an hour into a single, ankle-deep sea miles wide. Then they are everywhere. Then the water shrinks again and they are sorted into their separate bowls, waiting. Their eggs are designed to wait too — sticking to peat, to reed stems, able to survive a souring puddle that goes warm and low-oxygen by noon.

Where there are that many small fish, there are things that live only to strain them. In the stillest pans, where duckweed and water-fern knit a green skin over the surface, it looks empty until you crouch. Then the lilies show you. Lotuses and waterlilies lift their plates on long, flexible stems that can rise six inches or fall six inches without snapping. Under them, hanging in the tannin water, are the bladders — Utricularia, hundreds of them per plant, each a trap smaller than a grain of rice. They wait with their doors open. A mosquito larva brushes a hair-trigger and the bladder inhales it whole. On the slightly higher ground, where the peat makes a fist above the waterline, the sundews do the same in air. Red threads tipped with glue, each drop acting as lens and snare. The water here is so pure, so leached, so old — laterite and kaolin giving nothing — that plants have learned to eat the animals. They flower in brief, startling pinks and purples low to the ground, and then go back to digesting.

Because the fish are so abundant, the frogs can afford to be loud. They are not hiding beside a single pool; the whole plain is their pool. They live spaced out like the trees, each male on his own tussock, his own floating mat, his throat inflated to a pale sac. One makes a sound like a knuckle rapping on wood, slow and deliberate. Another lays down a high, insect-thin peep-peep-peep that never varies, all day and all night. A third — bullfrog-large, mud-colored, buried to his eyes — lets out a deep, rolling, guttural bellow that you feel in your sternum before you hear. If you walk, they go quiet in a widening circle around you and then start again behind you, stitching the sound back together. Their tadpoles are not restricted to ponds; they develop in hoof-print puddles, in the water that gathers in the hollow base of a pandanus, in the seep at the foot of the weeping rocks where water beads straight out of stone.

And where there are frogs and fish, there are dragonflies that have had time to become enormous. Not swarming — there is no crowding here — but patrolling. Each one owns a long beat back and forth over open water, wings the color of weak tea and amber, beating so slowly you can see through the stroke. They hawk midges out of the humming lower air, they rest on a reed stem with their abdomen lifted to dump heat, they mate in a brief, linked heart over the sedge and separate again. Their larvae live for months down in the peat-water, masked predators, jetting forward by squirting water.

The larger animals have learned to wade, to be tall, or to float.

The waders are solitary by necessity. A white-faced heron will stand for an hour on one leg in six inches of water, neck folded, immobile as a dead branch, until a gudgeon moves. An ibis — white, or straw-necked, or glossy black-green — walks with deliberate, probing steps, its down-curved bill feeling through clay for crabs. Spoonbills swing their bills side to side through the water, eyes half-closed. They are never together. You will see one here, and half a mile off, as if placed by design, the next. Each needs its own pan, its own acre of shallow hunt. When one lifts, big wings beating slowly, its shadow runs far across the reeds and every frog under that shadow stops calling for two seconds.

Above them, the cranes and magpie-geese carry sound further. The brolgas — tall grey, heads capped red — call at first light in a trumpeting, rolling whoop that carries for miles across flatness with nothing to stop it. It is the only sound that seems to belong to the scale of the place. Magpie-geese gather in loose, noisy triangles, not quite flocks, grazing on spike-rush tubers, pulling the heads down with their feet. At night they roost in the paperbarks, hundreds of heavy birds bowing the thin branches.

In the mud itself, things live vertically. Fiddler crabs and ghost crabs excavate holes that are always full to the brim with water that seeps back in as fast as they bail it. Their mounds, each a small levee of pelleted clay, are the only topography for some creatures — a dry ridge an inch high where a spider can lay its silk. Long-necked turtles bury into the peat during the briefest drying, breathing through their skin until the next rain reconnects the world. Freshwater snakes — keelbacks, water pythons, lithe and olive — slip between tussocks leaving a V that stays for minutes after they are gone.

And then, in any water that stays still long enough to darken and deepen to your knee — a braid-channel that has held for a season, a pool shaded by melaleucas — there is the one animal that does not need to be tall or floating because it *is* the water. The crocodile. Not always the estuarine giant, though he comes up from the distant, equally flat sea when the wet connects everything. More often the freshwater one, two meters of muscle and patience, nostrils and eyes breaking the duckweed like three floating seeds. He has been here longer than the plain's present shape. He moves like the water moves: slowest of all when watched, suddenly elsewhere when not. He makes the ibis keep their distance from each other. He makes the fish stay shallow. He is why everything else is alert even when it looks asleep.

None of them crowd. None of them flock densely, or herd, or swarm. The plain is too poor in nutrients and too generous in space for that. Nitrogen is locked in peat, phosphorus is held in ironstone laterite where roots can't easily take it. So life spreads itself thin and stays. Each melaleuca holds its own island of shade and leaf-litter; each tussock holds its own frogs; each pan holds its own hunters. What looks empty from far away — that vast, shimmering, white-light openness — is, up close, absolutely occupied, inch by inch, but occupied sparsely, politely, with room to breathe.

At night, when the white sun is gone and the humidity finally condenses in earnest, the plain lights up differently. Eyeshine on the water: spiders, moths, the crocodile. Male fireflies — not swarming either, but spaced — pulse over the reeds at metronomic intervals, one flash every four seconds, each male offset from his neighbor so the whole plain blinks out of phase. The frog chorus deepens, the insect drone rises a half-tone, and something large rustles through sedge without ever showing itself.

If you were to live here permanently, you would not build. You would learn, like the heron, to stand still. You would learn, like the bladderwort, to be patient with hunger. You would learn to read a four-inch rise in mud as a mountain.
