Nothing with a face lives there. Nothing with a footprint.

At first you think: nothing lives there at all. That is almost true, and that almost is the whole of it.

What lives there has learned how to live without the things that living is supposed to require. Without water, without shade, without movement, without witness. It lives by staying. By refusing to die in a way that looks, from a distance, exactly like patience.

The oldest residents are the plants, and they are not residents the way a forest is resident. There are perhaps thirty of them in the whole pan, and each one lives alone.

The low shrubs live there. They are grey in that light, woody to the core, their leaves reduced to small wax coins that do not transpire, do not tremble. They do not photosynthesize so much as they *persist* at photosynthesis - a business so slow it could be mistaken for stillness. A trade of a few molecules a day with the dry air. They have pulled their lives inward until only the minimum necessary remains exposed. No new growth. No flower you will ever see. They live as an idea of a shrub lives, precise and final. Each one holds its circle of empty ground around it like a courtesy. The roots underneath, you understand if you kneel, must be vast and shallow and patient, tasting the hardpan for a dampness that never comes, and finding enough in that tasting to continue.

Closer to the stone lip, the tuft-grasses live there. They live as fists. Tight clumps of wire where a crack has collected a pinch more dust than elsewhere. They do not wave. When the warm sigh comes they shiver once, all together, and stop.

Lower still, the barrels live there. The succulents. They are the hermits. Ribbed and thorned, squat against the silt, they have turned themselves into jars. They live by condensation, by the cool of their own spines at a temperature that never quite cools, by coaxing a dew out of air that has no dew to give. Touch one and it is warm as the stone, unyielding. It has been that exact size for longer than you have been alive. It will be that exact size when you have left. Its life is not growth. Its life is holding.

Under them, and under everything, smaller still: the salt lives there. Not alive as you count alive, but active. The thin white crust lifts and settles with a breath so slow you need an afternoon to see it. *Tick.* A plate cracks. A plate reseals. It preserves. It encases the dust and the fallen waxy leaves that never rot and keeps them exactly as they are. And in the stone itself, in the pitted wind-smoothed lip, the lichen lives there — a dusting of near-black on near-black, so close to the color of rock you would call it stain until you realize it has a softness, a willingness to be damp for an hour every ten years.

And the dark lives there. And the warmth.

That is not a metaphor the way you think. The warmth in the pan is not weather, it is an occupant. It came and it stayed. It soaks into the hardpan by day-that-is-not-day and does not leave at night-that-is-not-night, and it lives in the stone and gives itself back to your palm when you press down. The dark does the same. It is not an absence of sun but a presence of dimness, even and held, like water in a bowl. It lives over the pan the way held stone is warm — completely, without edge.

The quiet lives there too. You will hear it after a minute, when your ears stop hunting for birds. It is not empty quiet. It is occupied quiet, smooth and finished, as if silence had moved in and arranged the dust to its liking.

And one more thing lives there, thin as ash.

At the eastern edge, where the three stones make their almost-natural shape — the large black one fire-cracked on one side, the two smaller ones set as windbreak, as seat — the gesture lives there. Someone knapped a flake of chert there, rested, was warm, left. No bone, no burial, no story. But the shape of having briefly rested remained, because in a place that has finished eroding, even a single night does not erode. The shallow depression where ash might have been, the edge dulled not by use but by time passing over it without touching it — that patience lives there. Not a ghost. Less than a ghost. The habit of a human choosing stillness, left behind like heat in rock.

You, if you sit with your back against the smoothed stone, live there too, for as long as you sit. Briefly, and correctly. Your pulse, that dry tick behind your teeth, becomes part of the inventory for a while. The pan does not notice you the way a wilderness would, with alarm or indifference. It includes you. For a few minutes you are another of the sparse, stubborn things standing apart with great consideration, needing your own circle of emptiness to survive, waxy and grey and complete.

That is what lives there: what can live without becoming more, without going elsewhere, without needing to be seen to continue.
