Nothing here lives *in* the place. Everything that lives here *is* the place.

You have to stay a while to understand the difference. At first glance you think the basin is full of plants. Then you understand it is occupied by a single habit of living, expressed in a thousand forms.

### The Weave

The primary inhabitant is not a species. It is a mass. A continent of plant-matter that learned, in the hot white trap of this new bowl, how to be ground and forest and bog and roof all at once.

It doesn't grow the way forests grow. It maintains.

Up on the rim, in a normal forest, a tree races for light and dies and falls. Here there is no race because the light has nowhere to go. Every leaf is already lit. So the plants here have stopped reaching and started holding. The house-sized philodendrons with leaves like translucent sails, the fig-stranglers that became their own trellises, the sideways trees that throw a trunk out fifty feet through empty air and then drop a curtain of red prop-roots to the lattice below — none of them are trying to get taller. They are trying to stay exactly where they are, perfectly filling their volume.

If you press your hand to one of the waxy trunks, it is warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. The sap runs slow and close to the surface in enormous, visible veins. You can feel the faint hydraulic pulse as it shifts water from a cup-leaf that is too full to a fern-mat that is drying. The whole lattice creaks once at noon when the heat peaks — one long, deep adjustment — and that is its heartbeat for the day.

A leaf will not fall here. It will thin until it is paper, and then the fungus will eat it from its spot without it ever detaching, and its sugars will travel sideways through white cord into the next frond over. The plants propagate by pressure. When two swollen leaf-bases press together long enough in the damp, the skin breaks and they fuse, or they split along a seam and make a perfect copy of themselves. Flowering is rare and pointless; many of the cup-flowers big enough to bathe in have no scent, no nectar. They are just cisterns. They open empty and green to catch wall-weep.

It is not a jungle. A jungle wants. This wants nothing. It has achieved closure. Water leaf to air to stone to leaf. Light in, heat held, mass never lost. It could keep this exact shape, this exact frond at this exact angle with that exact bead of water forever, if nothing intrudes.

You are the intrusion it has not accounted for.

### The Leftovers

Animal life was not planned for in that closure, so it persists only in the cracks of the system, in forms that have learned to be as plant-like as possible.

Low down, in the layer where you walk: the stone beetles. Each one is the size of your thumb, domed, black or deep amber, so polished they look dropped there. They do not scurry. They proceed a centimeter a minute across a leaf that will outlive them by centuries, eating the thin film of algae and pollen that settles on everything. When you breathe too close they stop and become absolutely still for an hour. You will think they are seeds.

On the verticals — the trunks and prop-roots that make the walls of your path — the bark-lizards. They are not lizards the way a desert lizard is a lizard. They have no hurry in them. Flat, lung-breathing things the exact grey-green of new bark, with lidless eyes and toes spread wide as leaves themselves. They drink condensation by pressing their chins to the trunk. They move only when the shadow of a warm fog passes over them. You will have looked straight at one for a long minute before it blinks, and then you will feel watched.

And high up, where the hot air finally ripples and rises over the lip, the only thing that truly moves: the veilers. You see them as pale kites, two, maybe three at a time, on wings so thin you can see the sun through the membrane and the leaf-patterns below. They are huge and utterly light — like manta rays made of rice paper. They circle for hours on the trapped thermal, never flapping, never calling. They don't hunt. There is nothing up there for them to hunt. Perhaps they came with the first wind that found the basin and have simply never found a reason to land, filtering pollen and motes through combs on their undersides. Perhaps they are waiting for the basin to make a beach or a cliff that it has not made yet. They cast a brief, cool shadow over you and are gone.

Fungus is the other citizen, the one you don't see until you look under. White and yellow cordage thicker than your arm laces through the entire lattice, tying root to root, digesting what the plants will not. It smells faintly sweet, like bread. If you sit still long enough, you will hear it — not a sound but a soft popping as it tightens or releases.

### The Gardeners Who Became A Ledge

And then there are the people. Or were.

You do not find them. You find where they learned to be unnecessary.

They were not many. A few dozen at most, at any one time. They did not come to conquer the basin; the basin was too new for conquest and too complete to need it. They came down the raw stone lip when it was still steaming, perhaps, and found a world that was already full.

So they did not clear. They tucked.

The person who slept in that shoulder-hollow in the leaf mass was small — you can tell by the way the living edge curled to fit a neck — and they slept there many times, until the sap varnished the impression smooth. The person who set three stones in a stair did it not to climb high, but to keep a root-bulge from covering a water-cup they drank from. The little shrines of rim-limestone, now roofs of moss, were never houses. They are too open, too perfectly sited for a draft of cooler air at dusk, for a view of the pale wall when it weeps black at dawn. Places to sit while water ticked from leaf to leaf.

They wore soft things that rotted without trace. They left no middens because there was nothing to throw away — the weave eats everything you give it within days. They learned to move like the beetles, slowly, on paths that are still paths only because the plants remember the pressure of feet and keep the weave slightly more open there, out of habit.

No one died violently here. There is no story in the stones of that. What happened is slower: they thinned.

Some of them left, back up the impossible lip, because a place that never erodes and never needs you is a hard place to stay human. Most of them stayed and let the maintenance take them. You find a cairn of fist-sized stones cemented by sap, and next to it a slight, human-shaped thickening in the root-mat where someone lay down for the midday heat and the air-plants found them interesting and covered them by a millimeter a day until covering and being were the same.

If you stay until the light goes flat and gold again, until the fogs lift off the leaf-cups in warm ghosts, you will feel them. Not as ghosts. As a faint indentation in the stillness where a person once learned to breathe at the same rate as a leaf filling with water. As the reason one hollow is exactly body-shaped and dry, while all the others are full.

The basin does not need animals. It does not need people. But it kept their shapes, the way it keeps everything — not as ruins, but as useful hollows. A stair that is also a root-buttress. A shrine that is also a water-tank. A bed that is also a leaf.

If you lie in it tonight, very still, you will feel the lattice adjust around you, creak once, and hold.
