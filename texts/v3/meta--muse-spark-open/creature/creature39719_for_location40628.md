They don't live here the way things live in an ocean.

Nothing in the Summit Ocean swims *against* anything. There is no against to push on. The water is too thick, too warm, too everywhere. To live here is to agree to be held.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. Or rather, that only one thing does.

### The Holders

The ones who hold the place together.

From a distance they look like kelp. Up close you realize kelp is a poor word for them, a shallow-water word brought too high. Call them Weight-Kelp, or Banner Ribbons.

They don't root. There is nothing to root *in* — the stone is too smooth, too old; it offers no cracks. Instead each one makes a decision. A young ribbon — and they are all young for a very long time — finds a slight concavity in the marble, a basin no deeper than your palm, and simply grows heavy there. It thickens its holdfast into a living stone, a knot of dense, amber-black tissue as heavy as lead and as alive as heartwood, until gravity, polite as it is here, agrees to keep it.

Then it grows up. Or out. There is no difference.

A mature ribbon is a road wide and half a mile long, translucent at the edges, opaque at the spine where a cord of pressurized water — not sap — holds it rigid. It doesn't photosynthesize; the light that filters down from the sun-patch is too diffuse, too warm. It drinks heat. The whole length of it is a solar collector for warmth, for dissolved minerals, for the slow rain of silt that never settles. Along its margins it grows frills, then fronds, then whole secondary ribbons that tear free and become something else.

And because it hangs in a water column that has no surface and no bottom, only *middle*, the ribbon never stops swaying. Even when the currents stall, it sways from memory. You hear it if you put your ear — if you can call it an ear — close: a creak like wet wood under strain, cell by cell elongating even as you listen. They grow while you watch. You can see yesterday's length in today's color, a paler band where new tissue hasn't yet darkened with heat-algae.

Nothing lives alone on a ribbon. The ribbons are continents for smaller lives.

The feathered ones — the Sighers — grow out of them and off them and beside them until you can't tell host from guest. Each frond is the size of a house, but it's not a leaf. It's a lung, a mouth, a hand. A single Sigher is a colony of a thousand mouths sharing one pulse. For hours they are open, vast feathered fans filtering the luminous silt from the water, each filament combed by cilia you can barely see. Then, on a signal no one has found — a pressure change, a cooling by a fraction of a degree, the dimming of the far glow — they close. Not fast. A slow folding, like an old book shutting. The sound it makes when a meadow of them does it together is the softest sound in that world: a collective sigh that travels as a wave across miles of dim water, closing meadow after meadow long after the first has finished.

Under and between them are the Lungstones.

You mistake them for geology at first. Terraces of pink and sulfur-yellow sponge, layered upon themselves into low cities, porous and humming. They are not one animal but a parliament of animals that decided to become architecture. Water is drawn through them — in through a million tiny mouths, out through chimneys as wide as doorways — and whatever lives in the water is kept. Whatever is alive is tasted and either kept longer or expelled as clean, slightly warmer flow. If you hover over a terrace you feel the push, the gentle jet of processed ocean against your belly. If you stay long enough, the terrace grows around you a little, not out of malice but out of habit. Everything here grows over everything else if you let it.

Stitching it all together is Stitchrope. Thick as your thigh, black-green, braided not by hands but by growth. It is not a plant and not an animal; it is the compromise they made. Vine-things that feel their way through the water by heat, finding one ribbon, then another, pulling them into a loose net so that when the turbidity storms roll through — those cold fronts of clear water that make all the kelp lean — the forest doesn't tear. It leans together. Cut a Stitchrope and it bleeds warm, slightly sweet water, and both ends spend the next week blindly searching for each other again.

And then there are the islands that don't want to be islands.

The Floatmeadows. Mats of liverwort, or something that learned the shape of liverwort because it was a good shape, a mile across and only inches thick, dark green and pocked with air-water pockets. They drift. They snag on the tops of ribbons and stay for a season, becoming busy with tenants — shrimp-things that are not shrimp, filter-worms, little walking ferns — until a current shift peels them free and they go again, shedding species like seeds. If you stand — hover — on one, it dimples under your weight and holds you. For a while you have ground. Then it forgets you.

All of these — ribbons, sighers, lungstones, ropes, meadows — are not separate. They are the same life, trying out different ways of being still. This is a place where moving is expensive and staying is lush. So most of it stayed, and grew into itself until the water is less an ocean than a greenhouse turned inside out.

### The Passers

Animals are rare here because to be an animal here is to be a heresy. To move volitionally through a medium that already moves you is a kind of rudeness.

But a few do.

You will know the Pallid Grazer is coming a day before it arrives. Not because you hear it, but because the forest gets nervous. The Sighers close early. The Stitchrope tightens. The luminous silt — which is itself half-alive, a soup of bacteria and larval things that never decided to settle — thins as if holding its breath.

Then it passes.

It is not a whale. It is the idea of a whale that the high water invented separately. Size of a whale, shape of a manta, color of old bone. It doesn't beat its wings so much as lean on the water and let the water agree to move it. Its mouth is a barn door, a silent, ciliated cavern that doesn't bite but engulfs. It moves through the forest not destroying it but mowing it, taking Sighers whole, stripping ribbons to tattered flags, inhaling lungstone chimneys and letting the sponge regrow in its gut. The wake behind it is a blizzard of torn fronds and startled tenants, and for months after its passage that wake is a meadow in itself. Everything blooms to eat what it broke.

It has no eyes you can see. It doesn't need to see in a world where distance lies. It navigates by taste, by heat, by the pressure-bow of the stone below. It passes once a year through any given territory. The forest measures time by its returns.

Smaller heresies travel with it, or after it, or despite it.

The Eyes. A shoal of glass minnows that are not minnows at all but single eyes with fins — literal, lidless, perfectly clear eyes the size of plums, trailing a filamentous tail that beats almost too fast to see. They don't school so much as share a thought. They are here and then they are gone, flicking through a Lungstone's excurrent jet and vanishing into the haze, seeing everything and remembering nothing. You look into one as it passes and for a heartbeat you see yourself from many angles at once, refracted through its body, and then it inverts — crossing one of those invisible stratified layers where water becomes glass — and for an instant you are looking at its mirror instead of it.

And above, always above, the Eclipse.

You never see it clearly. No one does. It lives higher than anything else can afford to live, where the water thins to almost-air and the light pools into that false sun. It is large. Large enough that when it drifts between you and the glow, the dim becomes dimmer for an hour. It doesn't flap or undulate. It occludes. Sometimes the ribbons far below, which have no eyes, lean a little away from it anyway. Whatever it is — a jelly, a ray, a folded place in the water itself — it filters on a different scale, straining the ocean-sky for blooms of pale flowers that open for forty-eight hours across a thousand square miles and then dissolve into snow. When it feeds, the snow falls sideways.

### The Remembered

The last ones who live here are the ones who stopped.

On a dome of soft basalt, steps cut square and shallow, each one filled with crimson crust. Not a stair for climbing — there is no up — but a mark that said *we were here and we knew down from up and chose this direction.* Limpets and anemones farm the edges now. If you put your hand on the stone, it's warmer than the water. It remembers being cut.

A circle of standing stones, too perfect. Each one leaning inward a degree more than erosion allows, each one a garden now, bearded with filter-feather and stinging lace. In the center the water is slightly clearer, slightly colder, as if the shape still pushes the ocean aside. Things that drift — Floatmeadows, Eyes, lost fronds — snag there more often than chance allows. It's a trap made of intention, still working after its makers dissolved.

And the tower. No door, no window, seams worn to shadows, top lost in kelp ribbons that have grown through it. It was not built to live in. It was built to be a point of reference in a place where distance stretches when you aren't looking, where you can pass the same split boulder three times going straight. A lighthouse with no light, because the whole ocean is light. An anchor for meaning.

No bones remain. No tools. The people who made these did not die here so much as they agreed, finally, to be grown over. Or they learned how to become silt — that luminous, never-settling dust that ticks against your skin, each grain a little animal, a little plant, a little light — and are still falling sideways forever.

In the warm, dim, endless middle, between stone that has learned patience and water that has learned to be sky, life lives by holding on, by filtering, by stitching, by drifting and by passing through. It does not hurry. The kelp grows while you watch, the lungstones hum, the feathered meadows sigh shut at evening-that-is-not-evening, and the ancient flattened earth breathes out heat through its living lace.
