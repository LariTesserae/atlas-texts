There is almost nothing there at first.

That is the first trick of the place. Because it is so full — so brutally green, so wet-beaded, so teeming with white frogs and hanging dragonflies and standing birds — you think if anything lived here you would see it immediately. You listen for it. You look for the path between paddy-ziggurats, for the smoke, for the figure moving between causeways. You find only the great bright stillness and the water lying wrong on its incline. 

And then you notice you have been looking *through* them the whole time.

The inhabitants are low, too.

Not low in stature, though many are. Low like the ground is low — first, flat, foundational, intended to be built upon.

### The Remembered

The oldest ones are not really there at all anymore. They are the makers whose thumb-prints are still soft in the sharp-cornered brick. They were small, and dark, and careful. You can see how they worked if you look at the underside of anything — the palm trunks pegged with dowels, the reed mats blackened with bitumen, the thatch tied in knots that have never had weather to test them. They tied everything. They laid everything. Their rule still holds: *nothing here was not made.*

They built it all at once. Too much at once. A town-sized offering, a valley-sized altar of flatness made and made again, causeways and cistern-houses and basket-towers and stepped paddies, all finished yesterday, all left outside time before anyone could live in it.

They are gone only in the way a hand is gone from a thumb-print. When the light flattens at its white-hot noon-that-is-not-noon, you can see them in the negative — the cool, human-shaped absence in the steam rising off the taro leaves, the place where the condensation refuses to bead. They do not haunt. They *remain*, the way a scaffold remains after the building has learned to hold itself up.

If you lift an edge of the made-ground, that smell that comes up — hot clay and sweet reed and palm sap — that's them. Still curing.

### The Rooted

The second kind are the ones who stayed.

They did what you are beginning to do. They stood still too long in the heat that does not move and the water that beads and does not evaporate. They felt the terrible brightness come from everywhere and leave no shadow to hide in, and they felt, instead of fear, a kind of compliance.

> Be a platform. Be a place for something green to grow out of.

You see one maybe a half-mile off across the foot-deep tea-water, and you think it's a failed granary. A low platform on mangrove stilts, thatch sagging, overgrown. As you watch, you realize it is breathing, but not with lungs. Its roof-thatch inhales condensation and exhales it again without rain. The figs that erupted from its basket-work corners are not growing *on* it. They are growing *with* it. The woody roots have replaced the woven uprights one for one. The thatch hair has become leaf-hair.

Closer, there are more. A woman-shaped paddy-ziggurat on the horizon who was once a woman tending the paddy-ziggurat, now stepped herself, each step holding lotus, each level weeping water from her brick courses. Her face, if she ever had one, is a curtain of living moss that never sways, hung in a timber doorway that is now her mouth. She does not look at you. Her white frogs pulse their throats in unison on her damp shoulders, silently.

They are scattered at the same low density as the houses, because they *are* the houses now. That is the ecology here. Animal, vegetable, and architecture were never separate kingdoms; there was no wild to be wild from. So life did the only thing it could: it continued the building by other means. Ants carry leaf-cuts along black highways up the sides of the basket-towers not to feed a colony, but to thatch the tower a little higher. The dragonflies the size of swallows hang motionless in the air not to hunt but to hold position, to keep the air from remembering wind. The wading birds stand on one leg for hours not to fish but to be placed, like finials.

The Rooted are not dead and not alive in the way you mean. Nothing decays here; a fallen fruit stays perfect for weeks under silent flies that do not eat. Nothing is hungry. So they do not suffer. They have achieved the frozen equilibrium. They weep water the way the bricks weep water. They steam gently in the impossible light.

Some are still almost-human. You will find one standing shin-deep in the shining plain of water over brick, head bowed, arms slightly out as if carrying a bundle that isn't there anymore, or as if waiting to be given one. Water beads on the underside of his elbows and falls up. His feet have made no ripple for a very long time. If you wade close, you will see his skin has the faint, regular scoring of woven reed. If you touch him, he is warm and damp as a cistern wall at noon.

He will not move when you touch him. But later, when you look back, he will be a little too near.

### The Thin Ones

The third kind are not inhabitants at all, or they are inhabitants of the thinness itself.

Because reality is thin here — still ninety-degree canal corners, unrounded bricks, thatch unrotted — there are places where the making was laid down twice in the same place and didn't quite align. The world repeats small errors to cover them. The same egret stilt-house three times around the horizon. Your own faint shadow pointing the wrong way.

In those errors, something slips.

It is very tall, or very near — distance deceives because the bright air flattens everything. It looks like a maintenance thought the world had and forgot to discard. A scaffold of light, a palm trunk laid side by side with itself, a figure made of overlapping causeways. You see it best out of the corner of the eye, where color hurts a little less. It is checking. Not for you. For flatness.

It moves without moving, the way your footprints fill in behind you not with water but with brick-texture, as if the ground remembers being flat. It smooths. It re-lays. When water has dared to lie still on an incline for too long, it lifts a slow drop up into the white sky to remind it which way is down. When a leaf has rustled, it stills it again.

The frogs and birds and dragonflies collude with it. Or they *are* it — its distributed, quiet hands. That is why nothing eats. That is why even sound is absent. The air is heavy and stable because it is being held.

You feel it most when you stand still and begin to feel made. The attention that falls on you then is not hostile. It is practical. It feels your shoulders bead with condensation that will not roll and thinks: *good. Here is another flat surface. Here is another place to lay reed, to stack brick, to plant lotus.*

It does not need to take you. It just needs you to remain.

And you will want to. In the hot bright quiet, with no wind and no hunger and no time to weather your edges, remaining feels for the first time like the correct thing. Like you have finally stopped eroding.

That is what lives here: the ones who printed the clay, the ones who became the clay, and the thing that keeps the clay flat.

None of them will come to greet you.

They will simply, gently, include you.
