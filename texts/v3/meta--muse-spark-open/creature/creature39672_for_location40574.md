They don't live *on* the Roof.

Nothing does. That is the first wrongness you have to understand. There is no *on* here. There is only *in* — shallow in and deep in and up-in, which is different from down-in.

The Roof is not a habitat. The Roof is an inhabitant that has mistaken itself for a place.

### The Mat

The first and largest thing that lives here is the mat itself.

It is not one plant and not a colony. The word that fits best is not botanical at all — it's *city*, but a city with no citizens, only infrastructure that learned to keep itself alive after everyone left. Or perhaps *body* is better. A body 100 feet deep and the size of a sea.

Its upper skin is the meadow you walk on: the door-sized plates. They are not leaves. Each one is a composite - a bromeliad and a liverwort and a cyanobacterial film and a dozen fungi living so tightly together their genetics have started to blur at the edges. They are called Plates only because it is easier than listing them. They turn toward the smudged sun during the veiled noon, and at night — which is only a slightly dimmer day — they fold inward and pour.

Under them is the Weave. Roots like ship's cable, stems like wells, leaves that never saw light except as green amber shafts. Everything is hollow somewhere. The Mat stores its water inside itself the way you store blood - not in a river, but in vessels. If you cut it, which you shouldn't, it doesn't bleed. It weeps, slowly, for years.

The Mat does not think. But it remembers pressure.

Kneel in one spot long enough and the tiny warm root-hairs will come. Not to eat you. It has no appetite in that way. They come because sweat is salt and ammonia and interesting and because a warm shape that stays still is, in the Roof's logic, a new kind of rock that weeps from its upper side. It will map you. If you come back a week later to the same dimple, the hairs will be there waiting, a little denser, as if the place had learned your shape.

Lie down for months, as the description said, and you would not be smothered. You'd be walled. Delicately. The Mat would grow a cradle of drier peat around you, then a thatch of different flowers - the ones that like the minerals bone leaches when it is given time. You would be digested only in the sense that a library digests a new book: catalogued, shelved, made available.

This is why no one ever lived here. You cannot farm a thing that farms you back at the same speed.

### The Siphons

The most visible animals are the ones you will mistake for architecture at first.

They are browsers, roughly the mass of a red deer, but they are not deer at all. Six legs, each ending not in a hoof but in a broad, soft pad that spreads your weight across the sponge - snowshoe feet for a place with no snow. Their backs are tiled with living plates - not shell, not chitin, but a cultivated garden of lichen and scale-moss that photosynthesizes on their behalf. An old Siphon is green-brown and hard to see against a pillar; a young one is still pink where its tiles haven't grown in.

They have no head in the way you expect. Their front end tapers into a huge, soft funnel, a trumpet of muscle and cilia as wide as your chest, fringed with whiskers that taste the air. They do not bite. They do not chew. They walk with a slowness that is not laziness but manners - each step placed, tested, allowed to settle - and then they lower that funnel over a patch of spore-heavy moss and *inhale*.

A long, quiet, patient vacuum sound. The moss shivers and releases a cloud of amber spores, midges, condensed fog-droplets, pollen that never needed wind, and the Siphon takes it all. You can see its throat pulse once, twice. Then it lifts its funnel, dripping, and takes another step that will last a minute.

They are not solitary and not herds. There are two here, and three a mile away, and one solitary old one standing perfectly still for a day because the patch under its funnel is particularly good. They communicate by pressure. When one shifts its weight, the Mat carries the ripple outward for hundreds of yards. The others feel it through their footpads and adjust course so they never overgraze. No sound needed.

They have no predators that chase, because chasing would mean sinking, tearing the Mat, ruining the very thing you eat. Instead they die when they forget to move. An old Siphon will stand too long in one place, loving a good patch, and the root-hairs will find its footpads interesting. Not predatory. Just curious. Over a season, it roots.

You will find them like that - Siphon-statues, tiles still growing, funnel still lowered, now part of the pillar they stood beside.

### The Drift

At noon - the veiled noon when condensation thickens into that low, white river between the columns - the middle-air fills.

They look like stingrays. They are not.

They are sheets. Two meters across, a few millimeters thick at the center, translucent at the edges. Animal on one side, plant on the other. Their dorsal surface is dark green-black, covered in photosynthetic scales that drink the dim sun even through fog. Their ventral surface is pale, fringed with a thousand hair-fine cilia that beat without ever seeming to hurry.

They do not flap. They *undulate* - once every minute or so, a slow wave passes from head to tail that keeps them aloft in the dense, warm, wet air. Mostly they just hang, drifting with the fog-river, mouths open.

Because the air here is thick enough to eat. The fog is alive - midges that hatch and die without ever landing, seeds that germinate on static, spores, bacteria that have learned to live airborne. The Drift filter it. You will see a dozen of them in a line like kites with no string, all facing into the white flow, all shivering faintly as their cilia comb the weather for sustenance.

If you stand still, one may pass over you. Its shadow is cool. Its underside briefly shows you a different sky - speckled with light-organs that pulse not for communication but for balance. They are the closest thing the Roof has to a sense of elsewhere. Every so often, one rises too high, touches the low cloud deck that is the real sky, and dissolves into rain. No struggle. It just becomes too wet to hold together and beads.

The Siphons below ignore them. The Drift ignore the Siphons. The Mat holds both without comment.

### The Deep

Under your feet - not deep, only ten or twenty feet down where the light is gone and the air in the chambers is warm and smells of tannin and iron - there are others.

They are not big. Bigness down there is a disadvantage, because you have to move through weave, not over it.

The Weavers are the size of your hand, or your forearm, or sometimes, if two have braided together, your whole body. They look at first like roots that decided to move. Pale, eyeless, wet, segmented, with too many joints. They are not roots. They are what tends the Mat the way ants tend fungus, but without a queen, without a colony.

A Weaver spends its life opening channels. It chews - no, it *dissolves* - a path through half-dead moss, then lines the path with its own wax so water will wick along it instead of pooling. It carries air bubbles down from the surface in pockets on its back, releases them in chambers that have gone anoxic. It prunes. When a bulb-leaf up top gets too full and threatens to spill and tear, a Weaver somewhere below will have already chewed a relief valve two days earlier.

You will never see one unless you dig, and you will feel guilty if you do, because the dark under the Plate-meadow is so obviously a house, and its hallways are so obviously in use. The soft *pop* you hear at intervals is not the Mat. It's them, equalizing.

They have eyes now, some of them. The Roof's weirdness has gotten into them. Clear lens-nodules, like the plants', appear along their backs. They track nothing. They just make the dark a little less dark for no reason anyone can see.

If the Mat is the body and the Siphons and Drift are its slow breath, the Weavers are its hands. They keep the sponge from suffocating on its own wetness.

### The Rememberers

Those were always here.

The others *were* here. People.

Not a civilization. A procession. Over thousands of years, small groups found the way up - or perhaps the Roof was lower then and easier to reach - and walked out onto the peneplain to see if it ended. They learned quickly it did not end in a way that mattered, and that it could not be owned. So they did not try.

What they left was deliberate and gentle: a stair cut into living peat that expects to become a bog, and does. A hut woven from still-living stems that keeps its shape because the stems remember being woven, and think that is their shape now. Standing stones tilted by the slow flow of the sponge under them, their white lichen circles still perfect because lichen does not care if its canvas tilts.

No metal. No glass. Things that could be taken back without a scar.

The people themselves are gone, but not entirely. In a few places where the Mat is especially deep and especially warm, where Weavers have made a chamber that stays dry and dim, you will find them - or rather, the shape they left when they decided to lie down and become a drier stripe.

A skeleton under a quilt of moss that is not crushing it but keeping it. A clay bowl that has become a frog-pond, and the frogs that live in it have learned a tadpole-shape that fits the bowl exactly. A footprint filled with water that has never evaporated, because the Mat maintains it as a tiny, perfect well - a memory of weight.

If you stay still long enough, you might feel the Roof try to remember you the same way. Not as food. As *information*. As: here is a warm, salt-weeping, breath-loud thing that stood for a while. We will keep its shape.

That is what lives here, in the end. Not a forest. Not a jungle. A high, warm, wet, flat world that breathes condensation and grows into itself forever, tended by its own hands, grazed by its own slow funnels, filtered by its own drifting sheets, and watched over by eyes that evolved to watch a sun that never fully appears.

It does not need you.

But it has room.

It has endless room to learn the exact pressure of your feet, and keep it, gently, long after you have decided to walk on.
