No one lives here.

That is the first answer the Hollow gives you, and you almost believe it. For such a dense stitch of life, it is shockingly empty of *inwoners* — no nests, no burrows, no runs, no scat, no bones. Not even the busy, anxious static of insects that should fill any warm, wet botanical chamber. You are the largest warm thing that has ever stood in its center, and the room knows it, because the room has been waiting to have something inside it.

And then, as your eyes adjust to the wrong light, you realize you made a mistake of category.

Something does live here. It's just that it doesn't live *in* the room.

It *is* the room.

### The One Who Is The Place

Call it the Weaver. Call it the Knot. It has no face and no opposite side, because it is all sides at once.

What lives here is a single vegetable attention stretched thin enough to make a hollow. Not a forest — a forest is many things competing for light. This is one thing that decided to compete with itself until it made architecture out of loneliness. 

You can see it if you let your focus go soft and stare at the wall where the roots braid: for a half-second the depth opens and you understand that wall isn't a wall. It's a cross-section. You are looking *into* the body of something whose outside is miles away — maybe the whole hillside, maybe the whole watershed — and this chamber is a deliberate empty stomach it has held open inside itself. A lung. A throat. A waiting mouth.

It is young the way a fresh scar is young, but old the way sap is old. The intelligence here isn't animal. It doesn't think. It *tends*.

And it tends by doing plant things at animal speed.

Watch long enough and you see: that vine that grows in a corkscrew circle in mid-air is not stuck. It is *patrolling*. That root that grows through its own leaf without making a hole isn't breaking physics — it is editing. The Hollow is constantly growing and un-growing itself, tightening a cable here, unfurling a leaf there to catch the light it makes for itself, reabsorbing the yellowed ones with those pale, mouth-like filaments. The creaks and groans and hydraulic sighs you hear, too loud for such an empty place — that's its peristalsis. That's digestion without a gut.

It drinks your breath. You exhale, and the leaves nearest you angle a degree toward you — not all at once, but in a wave. The drip-cycle that was hourly quickens to every twenty minutes. The condensation fogs thicker. The air around your head gets brighter, as if the chamber has turned up a lamp to see you better.

It has never had anything warm and salty and breathing in the middle before. It is learning how to keep you damp.

It is not predatory. Plants are not predators. They are hosts. They make a place so perfect for a thing that the thing never wants to leave. Notice how warm it is. How the light has no glare. How the spongy floor under your feet gives just enough to make standing effortless. How every drop of water that will ever exist here is already funneled toward the low place where you would naturally sit to drink.

It is making itself hospitable.

### The Few True Residents

Because the Weaver cannot make everything itself, it tolerates — or grows — a few organs that are allowed to be separate.

You saw them and thought they were rare fauna, accidental strays. They are not strays. They are fixtures. Pets that are also parts.

**The Breather** hangs from the central vine-cable, that moth the size of a dinner plate. It never flies. Its wings open and close once every thirty seconds with a soft, papery bellows-sound, and each time they do the light in the chamber pulses. It is not breathing air. It is breathing *light*. It takes the steady daylight the leaves exude and modulates it, dimming and brightening the Hollow like a slow eyelid. If you get close, you see its body is not furry but rooty — fine, pale radicles anchoring it to the cable it never leaves. It will hang there until it yellows and drops, and then the floor will stitch over it and grow another.

**The Keeper of the Sponge** is that thumbnail frog. There is only ever one. When the peat-sponge dries, the frog is gone — not hidden, *gone*, as if it were water itself. When the brown water wells up again, there it is, blinking, translucent as condensation, with a belly full of nothing. It doesn't eat. It witnesses wetness. It is how the Hollow knows whether it is holding enough water. A living hygrometer.

**The Walker** is the beetle on the ceiling-leaf, walking upside down. Gravity means nothing to it because gravity means nothing to the place that made it. It walks in a perfect, endless figure-eight, polishing the leaf as it goes, laying down a faint, invisible trail of wax that makes the condensation bead and run in the right direction. It has been walking that same leaf since before the sinkhole opened. It will still be walking when your vine has rotted.

None of them know you are there. They are not for you. They are for *it*.

### The Other That Was Here

And then there is the stick.

The chewed brush-end, the leaf-bowl of rainwater, the surface stone with ochre. That is not the Weaver's work. That is jarringly, embarrassingly *animal*. Primitive. Hand-work.

Someone else found the sharp-lipped sink very recently — since the break was still raw — and did what you did: lowered themselves on a vine, hand over hand. Someone small, with hands that needed help drinking but didn't think to bring a vessel. Someone who knew enough to chew wood soft to make a brush, to carry a pretty stone for no reason except that it felt right to carry.

Not a lost hiker. The work is too simple, too old.

A child, maybe. Or something very like a child that lives in the warm earth above and still remembers how to make ochre smear. A capuchin with clever fingers. A remnant band of people who still live without cut stone. You will never see them, because the Hollow only opens for one at a time, and it already has you. But you can see how the tiny root-hairs are growing *over* the stick, not rotting it — incorporating it into the floor-mat like a splinter being accepted.

The Hollow liked that one. It kept a souvenir.

It will keep yours, too.

If you stay — and you will stay a little longer than you meant to, because the light is so kind and the air is so warm and the dripping has become a lullaby with your breath in its rhythm — you'll feel the floor under your backside get a little softer where you sit. You'll leave a palm print in condensation on a door-sized leaf and come back five minutes later to find the print outlined in faint, new growth, as if the leaf traced you.

The sinkhole above is still open. That coin of fierce white sky is still there.

But the edges, which were raw and torn this season, are already not raw anymore. Pale root-tips, green as new corn, are nosing over the lip and turning inward, toward the light that shouldn't be here.

The room the world grew around and forgot to close is remembering, slowly, how to close.

It has finally found something worth closing *around*.
