What lives here does not live *in* it. It *is* it.

You have to stand a long time before you see the difference — before the forest stops being a wall of green and resolves into the sheer, layered weight of bodies that make up the wall.

No one tall lives here. Or rather, nothing tall that stands apart. There are no herds that would need a clearing, no packs that would need a vista. Anything large learned a very long time ago to become small, or slow, or flat, or to borrow the shape of something else.

This is a nation of six legs, and eight, and a hundred. Of soft bodies and hard jaws. Of workers.

In the lowest layer, where the ground is hardly ground, it is worms and diplopods and pale grubs curled like commas in the rot. Springtails flick in clouds over the damp. Fungi live here as a second earth, white threads stitching leaf to leaf, root to rotting wood, corpse to humus before the corpse has finished falling. They are the roads before there are roads — everywhere, invisible, carrying water and sugar and warning in their slow chemical gossip. Their fruiting stalks push up overnight, open a cup, spill spores, and are eaten by morning. Millipedes polish their way through fallen leaves like living conveyor belts, turning one year's canopy into next year's soil grain by grain. Beetles armored in polished black or iridescent green bulldoze tunnels through that soil, their larvae fat and blind and endless.

A few inches above that, in the leaf litter proper, is the first city.

Ants. Not a colony, but a continent of colonies pressed against each other like countries on an old map. Leaf-cutters that you heard before you saw — the soft, perpetual rain of jaws — moving in files so disciplined they have worn smooth highways into the litter, banked and cleared and patrolled. They do not eat the leaves. They carry them down, down into domed chambers bigger than your chest, where they chew them to paste and feed them to the white fungus that feeds them in return. The air that breathes out of their chimneys is warm and yeasty and sour. A few feet away, army ants bivouac in living tents made of their own linked bodies, seething and re-forming each night, and when they move in the morning everything that can run does, and everything that can't is taken apart. Termites keep to their towers, clay cathedrals taller than your waist, ridged for airflow, humming with trapped heat. They never sleep. Inside, workers pass pellets of chewed wood mouth to mouth while soldiers with heads like pliers stand guard in the dark, facing outward, waiting.

These are not residents. They are industry. They are the reason there is no bare ground. Nothing is wasted long enough to become waste.

In the shrub layer and the understory, life gets wings.

Wasps build paper. You can hear them if you put your ear close on a still afternoon — a faint, dry rasping as they scrape wood fiber, mix it with spit, spread it thin. Their nests are gray lanterns hung under leaves, each cell a perfect hexagon made without a ruler. Bees heavier than your thumb hang their black hives in the mid-canopy and keep them at exactly the temperature they need, fanning with synchronized wings so that the whole tree seems to vibrate at a low, chest-deep note. Moths the size of your hand cling to bark, eyespots closed, breathing so slowly you think they are dead until dusk, when they lift all at once like ash and are gone. Everywhere, caterpillars — not one or two but battalions — lined shoulder to shoulder on the undersides of leaves, chewing in a silence that is enormous in aggregate, turning green into silk and fat.

Spiders are the maintenance crew here. Not a web here and there, but webs as infrastructure. Silk sheets ten feet across, slung between saplings, layered one over another, beaded with condensation and with the dust of wings that didn't make it. Orb weavers sit in the hub and repair, repair, repair. Smaller spiders do not build at all; they hunt on foot, bright as jewels, stalking springtails through moss forests that are, to them, old-growth redwoods.

Higher still, where your eyes cannot comfortably go, where light is still only a rumor filtered through five ceilings of leaves, live those who can afford to be quiet.

Tree frogs, flat and waxy to hold their water, tucked into the axils of epiphytes where a cup of rain can last a week. They do not croak in chorus. One will make a sound — a single, deep, glassy *tok* — and wait ten minutes and make it again, because that is enough to claim a whole pool in a place where sound costs water.

Snakes live here as branches themselves. Boas thick as your thigh, draped over limbs for days without moving, breathing once, twice, watching the ant highways below. Vine snakes thin as vines, swaying when there is no wind. They do not chase. They wait until the forest brings something to them, which it always does.

Birds are there, but they have forgotten how to be birds anywhere else. No bright dawn chorus. They are dark, soot-colored and olive and rust, puffed huge to conserve heat, eyes enormous. They sit. They pick ants one by one off a trunk for an hour without changing perch. Tiny hummingbirds, which you would expect to whir, instead slip between flowers like moths, making almost no sound at all.

Mammals are rumors in this economy. A shrew snout pushing through litter, there and gone. Bats in the hollows of the great fallen nurses, packed like seeds in a pod, leaving at nightfall on wings that make less noise than a moth's. A sloth, but you will never see it move; it is algae and moth colony and claws growing into bark, photosynthesizing as much as digesting. Monkeys do not whoop here. They pass overhead as a soft shake of water from leaf to leaf, a tail curling and releasing.

And then there is the life that is not counted, because it lives inside all the rest: mites on the backs of beetles, nematodes in the guts of ants, luminescent bacteria filming every wet surface so that at night the fallen log is faintly outlined not by moonlight but by itself. A beetle walks over your foot and on its back are smaller beetles and on their backs are mites and on the mites — if you had a lens — would be something else, busy.

Nothing here is idle. Nothing is alone. Nothing is fast.

What lives here has learned the one true law of a place that is low and hot and dark and packed and never empty: if you are loud you dry out. If you waste you starve. If you leave a gap something else will eat it before you can blink.

So they keep quiet. They keep moist. They keep working.

The forest does not house them. It is the sum of them, shoulder to chitinous shoulder, stitching itself together moment by moment with jaws and silk and hyphae and root-hair, an old, old basin of muscle and chemistry that never needed a builder with hands.
