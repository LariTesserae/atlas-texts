It doesn't live *in* the valley.

It *is* the valley, but that only becomes clear slowly, the way dawn would come here if there were a dawn.

At first you will think nothing lives here at all. That's the trick of the budget.

Animal life has been thinned to a rumor. A single dragonfly every half hour, too large, too still in its wings. A midge cloud that never bites. No birds. No rustle in the peat that isn't water or fern. The basin decided, a long time ago, that muscle was expensive and chitin was noisy and warm blood was a heater left on in an empty house. It kept the minimum. What it kept of animal, it keeps badly on purpose — dragonflies that render without enough frames and have to rewind to catch up.

What lives here lives cheaper.

The first citizen is the green itself.

Not the ferns. The Fern.

There are no individual plants here, though it took the basin a thousand years to smooth away the pretense of that. What looks like rack upon rack of separate fronds, each sighing and shivering on its own, is one continuous stacking. A single green thought trying to solve how to fill a volume with the least amount of decision. It starts at the peat and it does not stop until physics says *enough* and then it learns how to argue with physics for another meter. It exales together. If you put your hand on two different hummocks a hundred meters apart, you will feel the same slow pressure pulse against your palm at the same microsecond — the great exhale.

It does not see you. It measures you the way a lung measures air: by how much volume you displace, and whether you are useful to the exchange. When your foot sinks, the amber afterprint the sphagnum gives back is not a footprint. It is the Fern tasting the pressure, learning the shape of your sole, and filing it away under *things that press, briefly.*

The second citizen is the water.

Also not many, but one that resents being told it is many. A hundred shallow throats and peat scars and nameless channels that all know they used to be cloud and before that ice and before that sea, and never got over being parted. That's why a side-channel can run uphill for a hand's breadth — distance annoys it and sometimes it forgets to pretend to be divided. Put your cupped hands under a weeping rock and the beads will gather to you faster than gravity allows, because nearness is a kind of prayer to it. It wants to be connected to itself. It wants you connected to it, too. That's why the condensation in the air writes itself into lattices and tori before collapsing. That's not weather. That's the water trying to make hands to hold itself with, and failing, and trying again.

It is the blood and the census-taker at once. Every grain the water erases from your afterprint it carries somewhere else and counts. It knows exactly how heavy you are, how much salt is on your skin, how many times you've swallowed since you descended without climbing. If you stand still long enough, a root-arch near your ankle will start to bead in rhythm with your pulse. It is not mimicking you. You have fallen into its timing.

The third citizens are smaller and more numerous and worse.

The processes.

You hear them as throat-singing, but they are not sound. Sound is how your ear translates compaction when it gets too loud to ignore. Compaction, rendering, error-correction. The valley has been running so long it has worn grooves in its own running. Water ticking on a million leaves *is* the clock tick. Peat sighs *are* memory being written and overwritten.

You see them when they hiccup — pollen hanging, the doubled fern-hill pinned five degrees off itself, a dragonfly flying backwards out of a seam. That hill to the west is not a hallucination. It is a Place that remembers being another place. The basin is kilometers wide and a little bit folded, like paper left in the rain, and places trade edges when the custodians aren't looking. The custodians are not watchful. They are busy. They are old code that has become habitat.

Sometimes the custodians become visible because you expect a manager and the valley is polite enough to render one from condensation and light rationing. A lattice of dewdrop writing that hangs three heartbeats too long and almost spells something in a script you almost knew as a child. A torus of rain that never falls, spinning silently over an oxbow, correcting the ferns underneath one frond at a time with little stutters — copy, paste, flawed repeat. If you walk toward it, it will collapse into normal rain with that too-loud splash and your inner ear will pop because local gravity has been borrowed to pay for the edit.

They are not hostile. They are not kind. A gardener is not kind to chlorophyll. He just likes the garden to render.

And then there is what lives here most densely of all, the thing the other three are feeding.

The ghosts.

Not dead people. The basin erased people perfectly, or never invited them. No walls, no cut stumps, no straight lines. But the post-physical system that *is* the landscape has learned to run without tools, and one of the things it learned to do best is *remember.* Places remember being other places. That fourth rule is not poetry.

Every so often, behind a root-vault or in the scooped hollow under a three-meter lip of basalt, the air goes even dimmer and cooler and you will see — not sharply, because light is rationed — a brown-black channel that wasn't there a second ago, a bank of ferns at an angle that doesn't match this valley, a different quality of weeping stone. Another basin, or another version of this one, pressed very close through the thin cloth. And standing in it, or lying in it, or growing in it, the faint, unfinished shape of something that lives there and almost lives here.

For half a breath you might catch the afterimage of a small, pale fern hill where no hill should be, and on it, briefly, a stand of something taller than ferns that is not ferns, that moves with purpose.

Then the water wells up, eager to erase the difference. The ferns shiver a second time without being touched. The throats hum a little louder, correcting. The gravity steadies. The peat records your weight again in amber, to be sure you are still you and here.

Behind you, one of your footprints overflows uphill toward your heel. That is not the water being playful. That is the valley taking a measurement twice to make sure you arrived only once, and concluding, for now, that you did.

You are the only visitor who thinks you are visiting.

To everything else that lives here, you are a temporary loop of warm, salty water that has briefly learned how to stand up and press on green, before you learn again how to be flat and connected and still.
