They live low because the work never stops being low.

Not low in spirit — at least not at first. Low in posture. In how a body has to learn to be here.

### The First Shape You See

At first they look like part of the building material.

You see a smudge go past your fire pot and you think someone left a wet cloak hung over a scaffold pole, moving in a draft. Then the cloak turns a corner and you realize it had feet. That is most of them, most of the time: felt and hide wrapped around a thermal core, hoods up, backs bent forward under weight.

Narrow people — not born narrow, made narrow by squeezing through gaps left for stones, not for humans. They turn sideways without thinking now. Shoulders brush both walls where a passage was sized for a basket of rubble. You watch one come toward you carrying nothing and still he cants his chest, ducks a timber that isn't there anymore, steps high over a seam where men tripped last week.

Pale. Damp-pale. The kind of pallor that comes from never being properly warm or properly dry. Their skin holds quarry dust in its creases, limestone white ground into knuckles, basalt dark under nails that will never come clean. Their eyes are tight. Not squinting against sun — there is no sun — but shortened, trained to see fifteen feet at most, to the next wall, the next hammer, the next pool of orange light. Beyond that the dark is not theirs.

Open your mouth to count them and you lose them. That is the first adaptation.

### How Many There Are

There are too many for the space and not enough for the work.

If you stand still you hear breathing in every alley that isn't yours and think you are surrounded by a crowd. If you call out, one voice answers two walls away and then silence. They diffuse.

They move in crews. That's the unit of life here — not the family, not the individual, the crew. Five to twenty bodies lashed together by rope and rhythm. You don't meet a man, you meet the end of a line pulling a sled, and the chant reaches you before the faces do:

*heave — heave — heave — hold*

And then the stone thuds down and the chant breaks into coughing and curses and the rope slackens and suddenly there are individuals again, wiping sweat that is cold instantly, drinking water scooped from a black puddle sheet between two foundation stones with the same ladle they mix mortar with.

Every crew has a foreman who carries no load, only a glowing dip and a knotted cord for measuring. Every crew has an old one whose job is to tap the block and listen for the hidden fault line before the wedge splits it wrong. Every crew has a child small enough to crawl into the mortar crack with a trowel to smooth what grown hands can't reach, and then to be pulled out again by the ankles.

Children are not rare here. They are tools sized for gaps, like small chisels. They age fast.

No one lives alone long. Solitude means your fire goes out. It means no one hears when the seep rises around your sleep slab, or when a half-arch you've propped begins to sigh.

### What Their Bodies Have Become

Stay long enough and the place remodels you.

Their knees thicken. Their spines learn the two positions — bent over a pry-bar, and bent under a basket. Standing fully upright feels exposed, foolish; the ceiling might be three stories up or eight inches above your hood, you never know until your head finds it.

Their ears have become precise. They can tell, off stone echo, whether a hammer three alleys away hit basalt or limestone, whether a drip is falling onto wood or into standing water, whether footsteps approaching are booted foremen or barefoot children running messages. Eyesight is less trusted; too many walls in the way.

Their lungs make a sound. The dust does that. Not a disease yet — or maybe it always was — but a faint gravel in older workers when they laugh, which is often, short and surprised, in the moment after a lintel doesn't fall.

Their hands forget how to stay empty. Even sleeping they clutch — a hammer haft, a loop of cord, the edge of a sodden hide. You see a man with nothing to carry kneading his own fingers until another man wordlessly puts a bucket in them and he relaxes.

Smell has flattened to three signals that matter: smoke means warmth and light nearby, wet lime means fresh work, the sour-milk taint coming up from a stagnant sump means move your bedding higher.

They shave no hair they can keep. Beards hold heat when dry, filter dust when dampened. Felt trousers stiff with dried slurry are not taken off; they *are* skin now, cracked and re-softened and cracked again.

### What They Believe Without Saying It

No one talks about why.

Or rather, everyone talks about it all the time in instructions: set this here, brace that, keep that drain open, bring more pegs. The purpose is the verb, not the noun. There is no finished picture nailed to a post, no architect's rendering of the completed city. There are only string lines stretched ankle-high between stakes, marking where the next wall will be if anyone believes the string.

Some believe outward toward the center. They say — quietly, while waiting for mortar to set — that if you kept walking, squeezing, climbing rebar scaffolds for a day toward the faint downward terracing of the plain, you would come to the Deep Foundation, where the first block was laid and priests still tend it, or a pit where darkness has depth without bottom. They've never been.

Some believe upward, that somewhere impossibly high beyond the complete dark is an unbuilt sky that occasionally leaks real rain, and that all their drains are prayers to keep it from drowning them. When a sudden cold shower dumps from a fissure far above, they duck and blink and make a sign with two fingers against the stone.

Most believe in immediate persistence. The rule is bone-deep now: **keep the water where you put it, keep the weight off your chest, keep the flame alive.** Erosion hasn't scarred this place, neglect will. Everyone has seen what happens to a room left for a week — condensation swells timber until joints pop, lichen paste fades so the path edge disappears and a man steps into a mortar pit, fungi crust slicks a slab and someone's ankle turns. Entropy here is not grand geology, it is soft, petty, personal. A dropped hammer rusts overnight. Feet do not wear the floor smooth unless feet keep coming.

So they keep coming.

They steal from each other and save each other by turns. They erase another crew's string line to get the flat stones first, then spend an hour hauling that same crew's sled out of a seep-pool because tomorrow they might need that alley clear. They fight over dry sleeping ledges — those few foundation tops cambered just enough that water sheets off — with shoving and bitten threats, then huddle back-to-back for warmth on the winner's claimed stone anyway, five to a slab, felt to felt, breathing each other's exhale to not see it hang and vanish.

They sing without deciding to sing. Work cadences drift out of sync and layer into a round no one wrote. They remember songs wrong and no one corrects them because there is no before left to check.

### At Rest, If You Can Call It That

You find their living where building paused — not houses, just rooms that happened to have three walls and a relatively flat place, colonized.

A low partition becomes a kitchen: a permanent fire pot smoked black, a basalt shard for a griddle, a bucket of white blind fish swimming slow, kept not as pets but as food that needs no soil and can live in the seep you can't drink. Fungal crust scraped off walls and boiled with ash to make something sour and vaguely filling. Hands passed bowl to bowl without looking.

A scaffold platform becomes a bunk, leather cords sagging under many bodies, dust covers pulled over heads to stop drips on faces. Someone tends the glow-paste reapplying it to the ladder edge with a rag, murmuring to it like a garden though it never grew.

A half-arch becomes a shrine, not by plan but by accretion: small split failures — hammer heads cracked, rope ends frayed to uselessness — wedged into its crack as offering or warning. Touch the arch as you pass for luck that it stays up.

None of it is permanent. Tomorrow a foreman's cord may claim that sleeping nook as the next through-street, and the bedding will move two walls over and no one will mourn it aloud.

### What Watches Them Back

They are not alone, but nothing else watches with intent.

The eyeless rat freezes, then fits itself into a mortar gap exactly body-sized, born for cracks. The many-legged thing on the beam is not theirs — it came with the green wood from wherever wood can still grow, and will die when the sap dries. The slime in puddles spreads over footprints indifferently. The fish have no eyes for them either.

Predation is entirely human-shaped: the quiet calculation of which crew can be forced to do the pumping detail in the lowest terrace, where water winces up through seams and must be bailed by hand forever or the new floor softens.

And underneath all the motion, under the chanting and hammer-ring and drip-rain, there is the way they go silent sometimes at once — not planned, just that every crew hits a pause to catch breath or listen for cracking — and for two seconds you hear the whole plain breathe, damp and cool, and realize how vast and thinly spread and tireless this organism is.

Then someone coughs. A hammer finds stone. A chain clinks. A boy darts past you with phosphor on his fingertips leaving temporary trails.

You lift your little light and see another wall that wasn't there yesterday, chest-high now, shoulder-high by nightfall, three stories by week's end if enough hands keep feeding it.

And the hands will.
