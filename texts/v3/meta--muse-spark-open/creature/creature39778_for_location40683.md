Nobody lives here.

That is the first true answer, and you have to hold it for a while before it will let you see the second one.

There are no residents. No tenants. No animals at all, or so few and so small that they do not count — a gnat's worth of mites in the cistern-pools, perhaps, a tardigrade clinging to condensation on a leaf the size of a billboard, but nothing that moves under its own will from one district to the next. Nothing that sleeps. Nothing that would duck when a tendril cracks past its head at twenty meters a second, because there is nothing with a head to duck.

This city was not built to be inhabited. It was grown to be uninhabited. And it is very, very good at it.

### What Lives Here Instead

The plants are not the *inhabitants* the way trees inhabit a forest. They do not live *in* the city.

They are the city, and they are also the citizens. Both at once, without distinction.

That is why it looked urban from the ridge. You were not mistaking vegetation for architecture. You were correctly identifying architecture that happens to be vegetation, and a population that happens to be its own infrastructure.

Each form has taken a municipal job and committed to it utterly:

**The Tower-Trunks** live here. They are not trees. A tree is a single organism that wants to be tall. These are something else — braided, grafted, nervous crowds of what should have been separate trunks that found each other in the warm, wet adolescence of this valley and decided to become a district. They spend all day growing and all night growing faster. You can press your palm to their shingled door-leaf bark and feel the faint, ceaseless shudder of water being hauled a hundred meters straight up, a hydraulic elevator with no cable. If you stand too close you hear them muttering to themselves in cellulose, *creak-creak-creak*, adding a floor, adding a floor, adding a floor. When one overreaches and collapses, which they do weekly, it is not death. It is retirement. Three new ones knife up through the steaming, sighing wreck before the echo has even left the basin, using the old one's body as scaffolding. They don't mourn. They build over.

**The Avenue-Vines** live here. They are the street that got tired of being walked on and learned to walk itself. A kilometer long, level as poured concrete, thick as a subway tunnel, with a springy, skin-like pavement on top that dimples under your weight and pushes back. They were not laid down. They *leapt* — that whip-crack you hear all day, *tik-tik-tik-TAK*, is a searching tip tasting air, failing, trying again, finding the opposite basalt blade and pulling itself taut with a groan that shivers the whole neighborhood. Once taut, they flatten. Harden. Invite crossing. They carry people who do not exist from places that do not stay put to places that haven't grown yet. If you cut one open — please don't — water the color of pale tea would gush out under pressure like a burst main, because they are also aqueducts, also veins. They do not know the difference.

**The Plaza-Leaves** live here. In every open space where the canopy tears, a dozen of them hang on huge, muscular petioles and work. They are heliotropes the size of tennis courts, and they have a civic duty: they tilt together at dawn to catch the sun, tilt away at noon to shade the vine-streets below, tilt again at evening like a crowd turning its face. They do this without a brain, which makes it more unsettling, not less. The coordination is perfect. Too perfect. They are not communicating. They are the same gesture repeated across one vast, distributed body.

**The Room-Flowers and Silo-Pods** live here. They are the housing stock. A bell-bloom twenty feet across hangs at the second or third canopy layer, petal-walls thick as canvas, interior cool and humming and faintly lit by its own flesh. You duck inside and your voice changes — the acoustics of a living throat. It will hold that shape for three days, a perfect temporary shelter, and then *— THWAP —* the thunderclap you heard from the ridge, and it has burst, gone flaccid, thrown its pollen in a choking cloud and become litter that another vine will use as compost by nightfall. Next door, a seed pod swells to the size of a grain silo all morning, shivering, straining, and at 3:17 p.m. it pops and unfurls into a white colonnade that wasn't there at 3:16. No one moves in. No one needs to. The rooms build themselves faster than any occupant could ever unpack.

**The Lamp-Fruit** live here. They are the night shift. Not bioluminescent, not magic — they are patient. All day they drink normal sun and store it in translucent flesh like warm honey-light, and at dusk they let it leak back out, so the lower avenues never actually get dark. They sway and jostle and hum faintly as they cool. Streetlights that grew themselves because the street demanded to be seen.

All of them are hyperactive, turbulent, frantic with growth because there is no herbivore to prune them, no animal to say *enough*. They have only each other to compete with, and the stone, and the strange looseness of the new reality. So they grow over, under, through, *as* each other. A root politely grows over your foot not because it wants you, but because you were still for 47 seconds and stillness here is interpreted as available foundation.

This is what an anthill looks like when you remove the ants but leave the anthill's desire to be busy.

### What Else Lives Here, Without Living

Because the stone is young and the rules are wet cement, other things have moved in that are not plants and not animals but occupy space anyway.

**The Loops live here.** You noticed the vine-street that loops and shows you your own back one layer up. That's not a trick of the plants. That's an inhabitant. A fold in low, warm space that decided to live in the valley for a while, the way a cat lives in a sunbeam. The Tower-Trunks have learned to grow around it, to brace it with extra buttresses, the way a city builds a plaza around a sinkhole and pretends it meant to. There are alleys that are longer on the inside, plazas where you arrive before you have finished leaving. The city tolerates them. It has to. It was born after the last upheaval into a place that hasn't decided how far a kilometer is.

**The Light lives here.** Normal sun, good honest sun — until it hits one of the plaza-leaf plazas and thickens. You wade through knee-high brightness that clings. Your shadow falls the wrong way in the lower districts, toward the sun, obedient to some other sun underneath this one. The plants drink it anyway. Light pools on the springy vine-pavement and stays until a wind made of nothing but warm condensation pushes it sideways. It is not alive, but it behaves as if it has tenancy.

**The Water lives here.** Not rain, which comes vertical and hard and leaves. The sweat. The valley sweats its own damp out of every leaf surface, beads it, runs it down bark-slate, collects it in house-sized bromeliad cisterns where whole, animal-less ecologies churn — algae, rot, fern hair, nothing with eyes. Hollow vines carry it in audible guts, rumbling, from high to low, a municipal water system with no pumps, only thirst. You can hear it moving behind walls that are leaves.

And above and around and underneath all of it — because there is no underneath, remember —

**The Absence lives here.**

This is the strongest inhabitant. The city for no occupant. The avenues perfectly level for feet that never evolved. The flower-rooms cool and luminous and empty. The braided towers with colonnades waiting for markets. It is not haunted. Haunted implies something died here. Nothing has ever died here except plants, loudly, to be immediately built over. It's *pre-haunted*. It's haunted by the future that hasn't bothered to arrive. By the people who could live here, should live here, in any other valley this beautiful and temperate and strange — but don't. Because there is no ground underneath to support a history yet. Because to be still is to be built upon.

If you stand in the center of the lowest plaza at noon, when the leaves have all tilted away and the pooled light is lapping at your shins and the creaking and cracking and tearing-paper hiss of roots in fresh basalt is so loud you have to shout, you can feel the valley trying on the idea of you.

Not you specifically. An occupant. Any occupant.

A root nudges your boot, testing. A tendril overhead tics, tics, tics, considering a new purchase that happens to be your shoulder. A flower-room nearby sighs open, cool and empty, exactly person-sized if a person were twenty feet tall and made of warm air.

It is not inviting you. It is not threatening you. Plants know no malice. It is simply doing what it does to everything that pauses: municipal maintenance. *We have an unoccupied lot here. We have a standing citizen with no assignment. We can fix that.*

You will move, of course. You have to keep moving here. That is the only tenancy agreement the city offers.

Stay long enough and you will not die. You will be incorporated. Gently, politely, with no ill intent whatsoever, floored over by a second ground of interlocked fig-leaves ten meters up, plumbed by a sweet, gut-rumbling vein, lit at night by patient fruit.

And then, at last, someone will live here.
