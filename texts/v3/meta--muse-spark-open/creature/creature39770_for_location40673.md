Nothing lives here.

That's the first answer the Hamlet gives you, and it almost holds. You stand in a nave of root the size of a church and there is nothing in it — no furniture, no nests scaled to the architecture, no altars. Just damp root-mat under your feet and a hundred feet of empty, roaring air.

Then you notice that the air is not empty. It cannot afford to be.

### The Tenants of Volume

What lives here lives *in the emptiness itself*, because emptiness is the only abundant resource.

First, the obvious ones — the ones that do not count, until you realize they are behaving as if they are counted and found wanting.

The cicada-things. Each one alone is an animal, forearm-long, green-black, eyes like varnished stones, clinging to the column-roots with needle feet. Together, in the thousands, they are not animals. They are wallpaper. They are shingles. They are an organ built to keep the vault inflated with pressure. They never seem to eat. They pulse, and pulse, and the chord they make together is so thick you feel it in your molars. When one stops, another starts a fraction of a second *before* the gap would have formed — keeping the sound seamless not by reaction, but by prediction.

The bird-things do the same work in a different register. Small, bright, hysterically dense. There are twenty times more nests than the food supply should allow tucked into the leaf-ceiling, crammed shoulder to shoulder as if this were a tenement cliff in a real city. They scream not to communicate, but to occupy. Their mimicry is wrong: they scream cicada, they scream frog-drum, they scream the hydraulic sigh of sap, they scream *you* — a half-second before you cough.

And under them, in every cup and cistern and temporary river-thread, the frogs. Drum-chested, throat-sacs stretched translucent by how much air they hold, booming so low your vision blurs. In the leaf-cups the water trembles with their tadpoles — black commas so thick the water looks solid. Too many. Always too many. Everything here is breeding as if crowded into a metropolis that does not exist, laying for streets full of mouths to feed.

The monkey-things and the vine-serpents, the beetles that can strip a dome in an hour, the fungi that surge overnight — they are not rare fauna. They are commuters. Watch the monkeys: leaf-green fur, hands too perfect, they chase flicker-flies across a plaza on trajectories that make no sense unless you imagine balconies and clotheslines and market stalls in the empty air between. They bank around nothing. They pause on invisible ledges. A serpent drops from its hanging-root and misses by exactly the width of a person who isn't there, recoiling in annoyance.

The ants complete the picture. They march in highways, six-lanes wide, mandible to mandible, across absolutely empty air three feet off the ground — and do not fall. If you throw dust, you see it for an instant: fine silt hanging in a lattice where walls should be, where floors should be, catching light for half a breath before the heat dissolves it again. They are walking on the idea of a street, which here is load-bearing.

All of them are loud. This is not incidental. In a forest sound dissipates. In a city sound reflects, gathers in squares, tells you how many others are breathing near you. The Hamlet is all squares, all plazas, all cathedral naves — acoustic shapes with no bodies to soak them up. So the living things *become* the bodies. They generate the urban roar that a real hamlet of this size would make with footsteps and voices and doors. They keep the volume full, lest it be noticed how hollow it is.

They are not the inhabitants. They are the staff. Keeping the stage dressed.

### The Builders Who Are Also the Building

What lives here longest are the buildings themselves, and they are not trees.

You have to stop calling them that or you will miss them. Each of the three — maybe five, it changes — is a single genetic individual the size of a neighborhood, a banyan-mangrove-cathedral chimera that has decided to be infrastructure. Its pale buttress-bark breathes. Lenticels open and close like gills. The aerial roots come down as columns and decide, mid-air, whether to become wall or door. They taste your passage. Overnight, a root-wall will seal the arch you came through and leave a new one where you needed it, apologetically, like a host moving furniture while you sleep.

They remember a town. That is the gentlest way to say it.

This whole lowland is ancient, eroded to equilibrium. No new hills. No new valleys. And yet here is a negative: a dip, a bowl, a set of gutters all leading to a central pool as if a settlement's drains still ran. The Architects are growing into a mold that was already there when their seeds fell — streets that want to be streets because water wants to run downhill the way it ran when cobbles forced it. They are not intelligent. They are obedient. They are rehearsing a hamlet because the soil compacts differently where a thousand feet walked for a thousand years, and roots follow compaction, and leaves follow light-wells that remember windows.

But obedience has become custody. They exude condensation precisely where cups are needed. They flat-top limbs into terraces exactly where a second story *would* look out over the square. They are patient landlords maintaining apartments for tenants who moved out before the species evolved.

They sound, too — that endless micro-cracking you hear underneath the insects, the wood laying itself down cell by cell in real time. That's not growth speed. That's repair speed. Something is wearing them very slowly, and they are replacing themselves very quickly, keeping the shape true.

That something is what actually lives here.

### The Hollow Residents

You only see them correctly in the two places where the Hamlet's wrongness concentrates enough to become habitat.

The first is in *wrong-place phenomena*. Stand in the second square at midday. Your shadow falls northeast when the sun is southwest. You are not casting a shadow. Something else is, overlapping you, and the light here — normal, dappled, green-gold — has learned to obey it instead of the sun because it is denser. Move, and your real shadow tries to catch up like a shy animal. If you stand very still, you can feel the warm spot where *their* sun lies on your shoulders, a degree or two to the left of yours.

Throw a stone into a cup. Ripples move inward to the point of impact before spreading out. Hold your breath, and the echo arrives before the throat-sound that makes it. These are not tricks. They are ecologies. Things live in the pre-echo — thin, glassy predators shaped like elongated dragonflies made of vibrating air, hovering where a sound *will be*, feeding on its arrival before you have decided to make it. Things live in the converging ripple, filter-feeding on time-reversed detritus that collects toward disturbance instead of away from it. You swat at nothing and hit resistance the size of a person standing politely just out of phase.

They have weight. When warm air hangs still under thrashing leaves, it's because they are standing there in numbers, blocking the wind with bodies that have no surface for wind to move. When wind blows down a still nave, it's because they all shifted at once.

They are gregarious. They crowd. The ant-highways have traffic because they have traffic. The bird density makes sense because the *total* population of the square — seen plus unseen — is exactly that dense. You are the sparse one.

The second place you can actually look at them is the central pool.

It is wide, shallow, warm as blood, still while everything roars. Kneel. Do not touch it.

Look down. The reflection is perfect. Leaf-cathedral above, root-colonnade around — and in the reflection, every arch has a warm yellow light inside it. Every terrace has laundry lines, silhouettes, movement. Windows where there are no windows. The other hamlet, the one that fits this one's dimensions, is not gone. It is *one refraction away*, pressed against this one like a palm against glass. Its citizens are going about their evening. You can see them passing behind lit leaves, three stories up inside a trunk that in the real world is solid wood. Some look down. Some pause.

If you stay until condensation hour — when night drips have gathered and every gutter gives up its finger's breadth at once — the Hamlet exhales. All the cicadas cut out for half a second. The hydraulic sigh stops. Pressure equalizes.

In that half-second the volume stops being empty.

The air becomes suddenly, impossibly *occupied* — shoulder to shoulder, chest to back, humid with shared breath, the smell of iron mud and cooked greens and lamp oil where a second before there was only green rot. You don't *see* them. Seeing is still tuned to the wrong layer. You *are between them*, as if the crowd that owns these streets has stepped back into exact register with its architecture and you happen to already be standing in someone's hallway, in someone's doorway. A hand passes through your forearm and it is cool. A voice laughs directly in your ear in a language that sounds like rustling.

Then the frogs boom again, the cicadas resume their chord to quickly re-fill the vacuum, and the pool is just a pool reflecting leaves.

But the water is warmer where feet — not yours — waded through it to get home.

What lives here lives in the hollows the city left for it, and tends them, and waits for the architecture to remember how to hold them properly. The plants build rooms for them; the insects sing their street-noise for them; the pool keeps their light on for them.

You are not an inhabitant. You are a draft through the keyhole. Step lightly on the root-streets, and listen before you speak — something is already listening before you.
