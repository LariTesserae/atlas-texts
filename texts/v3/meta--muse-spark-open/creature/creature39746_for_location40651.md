There is no one here in the way you mean when you say *someone lives here*.

No one has housed themselves in the bowl. There are no footprints worn into a path, no fire ring, no carved wall, no burial. Nothing has ever lived here long enough, or with hands clever enough, to want to change it — because to live here is to be in perfect agreement with not changing at all.

What lives here has achieved that agreement.

Under the roof — for it is a roof, that low lattice of dwarf willow and creeping heather at your knee, prostrate and knotted and bonsai'd by the cool into a thatch — is a single, continuous animal.

At first it looks like many.

They are moths. Small ghost moths, high-altitude moths, the kind that should only live a week in summer. Here they live as a pelage. You crouch with your face inches from the breathing peat, in that blue-grey starlight dusk, in the cold exhalation off the peat-water, and you understand the green you thought you saw from above was never there. Every leaf, every cranberry runner, every thumb-high fern frond and sphagnum star, every prostrate willow stem has been thatched over.

Pale as wet paper. Grey as lichen. Thick-bodied and densely furred against a cool that never lifts, never quite freezes. Their wings fold like little tents over their backs, not bright, not patterned for display, because there is no distance here to display across. Their feathered antennae are always moving, just barely — tasting the saturated air for the same few molecules, over and over.

There are so many that they touch. Shoulder to shoulder, wing-edge to wing-edge. When you breathe out, the faint warmth doesn't scatter them. They ride it. They shift infinitesimally closer to one another and become still again.

This is their way of being: not flying. In any other place a moth is defined by flight, by a frantic attraction to light. Here the bowl is bounded. Above the moss-browed rim is only cloud and killing wind. Light does not enter except as a used-up blue dusk. There is nowhere to fly to, and no need. Food is under their feet and over their heads — the perpetual dew that beads on every surface, the willow catkins that swell and never quite open, the thin fungal bloom that moves like frost over the peat. Mates are a body-length away in any direction. So they have given up flight as a habit. Their wings are intact — if you were to lift one, which you won't, because to lift one is to feel how reluctant it is, how it clings with its hairy legs — you would see the wings are still muscled, still capable. They simply do not use them. Generations in the held breath of the bowl have taught them that stillness is more efficient than motion.

Listen, if you hold your ear close enough that the moths could, if they wanted to, touch your cheek.

It isn't silent. The bowl has fooled you with its drip... drip... drip five seconds apart. Under the willow roof there is a different sound: a dry, papery rustle with no wind behind it. A field shifting itself. It is tens of thousands of spiracles opening and closing. The bowl breathing through all those tiny furred bodies at once. Inhale. Exhale.

Among them, because nothing in nature is ever truly just one thing, are the attendants.

The beetles — there are very few, perhaps one for every thousand moths. Dark, flat, slow as cooled wax. They move through the pelage and the pelage parts for them and closes behind them without alarm. They are not predators in the way you are thinking. They drink the same dew, they eat the dropped scales, they lay their eggs in the damp thatch and the moths lay theirs right beside them, pale beads glued to the undersides of leaves. You will see a beetle pause and groom its antennae fastidiously while moths crowd its back, using it as ground.

And deeper still, surfacing only when the peat-water sighs — the salamanders.

They are pink, blind, the color of things that have never needed color. Their eyes have filmed over generations ago in this dusk that never required sight. They are not many. Perhaps three, perhaps five in the whole forty-stride bowl. They live in the black, cold, still water under the floating sphagnum mattress, breathing through their skin. Once in a long while one will push its blunt head up through the moss, open a mouth that seems too large for its head, and take — without lunging, without hunting — whatever is directly above it. A mouthful of moth and moth-fur. The moths around the hole do not flutter. There is a brief ripple in the living pelage, a dimple, and then theyshuffle shoulder-to-shoulder again and the spot is gone. The salamander sinks. It doesn't need to eat often in that cool. It is in no hurry. It is as much a part of the water as the peat is.

Nothing else.

No birds. A bird would need sky. No mice. A mouse would need dryness and seed and somewhere to tunnel that isn't already water. No predators that come and go — because nothing comes and goes. The level never rises. The level never falls. The water weeping from the old, soft, handled basalt is drunk by the moss and held in the sponge and exhaled as mist to condense on the rock and weep again. The moths hatch, live, lay, die, and within hours the moss has taken the bodies apart so softly you never find a corpse. The number stays the same.

You thought, watching the little ritual on the back of your own hand — three or four moths patiently carrying sharp-struck basalt flakes in their hairy legs, overlapping them like shingles on your skin the way they do over their pupae — that you were seeing intelligence. Intention. Someone making something.

You weren't.

It is not intelligence. It is older than that. It is habit become texture. It is what happens when a living thing has been in one perfect, wet, cool, dark, small and absolutely full place for so long that it has stopped trying to leave any mark, and instead has learned only to lightly cover what is vulnerable — a pupa, a burrow, the strange warm skin of a visitor — with a flake of stone, and then abandon it. Ten thousand times at once, then abandoned, then repeated exactly the same way.

That is who lives here: a crowd that is one crowd. A fur that is moths. A few beetles moving slowly through the fur. A few blind pink mouths opening and closing in the water under the fur. All of them practicing stillness so perfectly that the bowl has achieved what no valley ever does.

Equilibrium. Held. Unchanging. Waiting for nothing.
