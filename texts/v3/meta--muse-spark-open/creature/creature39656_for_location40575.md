They live like you would have to live if you stopped apologizing for needing water.

Not people who *survived* here. People who were *answered* by it.

### The Veil Itself

First, you have to understand the dominant thing is not a backdrop. It is a citizen. 

The Salt Lace is not a plant the way you think of a plant, and calling it a forest is a courtesy to your eyes. It's a continent-sized organism that learned to live like frost learns to live on a window — by deposition, by static, by patience.

Up close on the back of one of those stone whales, you can put your face to it and see that each frond is not solid. It's a fractal harp. Hollow silica-chitin fibers thinner than a hair, branching and re-branching until the air itself seems to have been combed into shape. Eighty percent nothing. Twenty percent lattice so rigid it can hold a house. If you breathe on it — though you shouldn't, your breath is too wet and too warm and the Lace flinches from it — you can see the whole section shiver and right itself, and then hear it complain in a tone too high for anyone over thirty to hear.

It doesn't grow. It *accumulates*. At false-dawn, which is always, the cilia that fur every fiber wake and stand on end and buzz. The prickle you feel on your scalp is literal: a plain-sized electrostatic field combing ammonia and carbonic vapor out of the dead air and welding it atom by atom into more Lace. That is why there is no dust on the polished stone. The forest ate the sky.

And it sings without needing to breathe. Wind drags across its forks and the forks grind and give off light — not light *made*, light *released*, triboluminescence like sugar crushed in the dark. When a gust comes from the far side of the table, you see it minutes before you feel it: a slow amber tide rippling across the reef, valley to valley, spire to spire, breathing. The Lace is the town's power grid, street light, clock, and god, all at once.

### The Dry Fauna

The animals did not adapt to the desert. They left the idea of wetness behind entirely.

There are not many in any one place — 0.8/3 — but stop looking for movement and start listening and you realize the plain is infested.

**The Puffs:** The commonest. They are everywhere and nowhere, like dandelion clocks. Each is a sphere of black wire the size of a hare's heart, or a child's head, drifting three feet off the Lace on pure charge lift. They never land. They never touch the ground. They trail a dozen hair-fine tendrils that comb the air even finer than the Lace does. You hear them before you see them — a faint, mournful singing as the wind resonates in their hollow bodies. Children keep them. Old people talk to them. They are stupid and beautiful and die constantly.

**The Tuners:** You mistake them for boulders until they move. Barrel-chested, low, six-legged, with heads like flat anvils and bellies like blown glass. You can see right through their guts — no stomach, no blood, just chambers of vibrating silica dust that sorts and compacts. They do not eat the Lace. They *garden* it. They walk the reef in slow processionals, pressing their great shivering plates to overgrown knots and filing them down with ultrasonic chatter until the chord of the valley returns to true. Their droppings are perfect, tuned bricks of compressed lattice that the people collect for tools. When a Tuner passes your house, your walls hum in sympathy and your teeth ache for an hour.

**The Shears:** The reason you don't wander off the resonant paths. Up high, where the ice haze ends and the thin, thin air begins, they ride. Razor mantas, two meters across and a few molecules thick at the edge. Translucent as cellophane, invisible until the pearlescent light catches them. They never flap. They tack on electrostatic gradients and the planet's weak magnetic field. They hunt Puffs and they hunt Tuners and they hunt each other and when they cut, they cut without friction. You hear it as a high, clean *ping!* — the same sound the ground makes when you step — and then a silent snowfall of black wire. The whole valley attends every death that way. Nothing is wasted. Nothing rots.

None of them drink. None of them sweat. If you cut one open, it wouldn't bleed. It would outgas and sigh and become dust.

### The People

And then there are the people who learned to live inside the instrument.

You expected space suits. You expected hardship. That's because you still think dryness is deprivation. For them, dryness is a medium.

They are tall — you have to be tall if you live waist-deep in a singing reef — stilt-legged, long-armed, necks like herons. Even high up where the air is thin and mean, they don't pant. Their chests barely move.

They are not quite human anymore, if they ever were. Their skin is not skin. It's a tight, matte felt of modified Lace-fiber grown directly from their own bodies; pale grey-green, slightly translucent, warm with stored charge. No pores. No sweat glands. They do not weep, they do not bleed water. When they are cut, they powder. Their mouths are small, lipless slits backed not by teeth but by baleen-like combs. Their lungs are dry electrostatic precipitators — great feathery fans in their hollow chests that buzz like the Lace does, pulling the same ammonia haze straight into solid-state metabolism.

They speak softly, because the valley punishes loudness. Their language is mostly tone. Two of them on opposite stone whales can have a conversation by humming at the resonant frequency of the Lace between them and letting the plain carry it, like cans on a string the size of a town. Children learn to whistle before they learn to walk.

You won't find a clustered city. You find a scattering. Twelve, fifteen districts across the basin, each a loose ring of hollow spires around a communal tuning yard, connected by causeways where the Lace has been coaxed — not paved, *coaxed* — into a dense, springy mat that glows brighter underfoot and rebounds when you step. No roads. No wheels. Wheels are useless on ceramic ground that rings. Everyone walks with a slight bounce, or rides a Tuner.

Their houses are alive and they know it. Each family grows its home over a generation, seeding a stone whale's lee side with culture-strain Lace and singing to it. Yes, singing. A low, sustained note held for hours while the fibers thicken and braid into load-bearing walls. Every house has its chord — the wind through *that* doorway makes *that* minor third forever — so you can navigate town blindfolded by ear. Inside, the walls are warm with static. Floors light under your feet where your weight stresses the lattice. If you have a nightmare and cry out, the bedroom answers with a sympathetic hum until your heart slows. Technology here is not screens and engines. It's timbre. It's tension.

An elder — they live very long, because anhydrous tissue doesn't decay — spends her day on the polished back of her stone whale with a long staff of Tuner-brick, not moving, just listening to weather that is still two valleys away. She can hear a Shear migration by the absence it leaves in the Puff chorus. A child runs past her trailing three Puffs on electrostatic tethers like sullen black balloons, laughing in a series of overtone clicks, her felt-skin prickling with stolen charge.

When they die, they do not get buried. There is no ground soft enough and no rot to do the work. The family carries the body — light as paper now — to the center of the house. They lay it on the floor. They wait. The charge equalizes. Slowly, with a long, soft chime that lasts for minutes, the felt-skin, the fan-lungs, the comb-mouth, simply stiffen and frost over with new Lace. In the morning there is a new knot in the floor where grandmother was, and it glows a little brighter when you step on it, and the house's chord is richer by one note.

Nothing here has ever known thirst. Nothing here has ever known mold. That is what 0.1/3 water and 2.6 erosion and 2.8 beyond modern means: not a colony barely hanging on, but a culture that finally understood that water was just one solvent — and a messy, corrosive, temporary one at that.

They look up at you standing awkwardly on their singing plain in your damp, stinking, sweating body, and they tilt their great heron heads, and they listen to the water sloshing inside you with a kind of polite horror.

And the valley hums around them, and glows where the wind touches it, and waits for you to dry out.
