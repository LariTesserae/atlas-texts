Who lives there is a question with the wrong scale. Nothing lives *in* the Cistern the way a fish lives in a lake. Everything lives *on* it, *with* it, *because* of it. The Cistern is not a habitat. It is a host.

Let me be slow about it, because if I give you one animal you'll picture the wrong thing.

### The Film

First, the thing you don't see until you've been still for ten minutes with your face close to the water under the dome.

Everything is coated. The boiler-plate, the concrete, the ceramic hexes, the glass itself on its underwater side, the chains hanging slack from the gantry, the dead bulbs in their wire cages — everything has grown a skin. A bacterial film, but that word is too thin. It's a felt, a pelt, a living varnish the color of weak tea and old copper. If you brush it with a fingertip it comes away in a sheet and reforms behind you in hours. It tastes the water for iron and light and tells the rest of the room what the water is doing.

It *is* the room, really. The shrimp eat it. The newts breathe through it. The pump grinds it off its impellers and it grows back overnight. If you drained the Cistern, this is what would remain, shivering in the air for a few minutes before it dried and died — a perfect, hollow second Cistern made entirely of soft, photosynthetic suede.

It has no center. It has no face. But it decides who gets to live there, because nothing can anchor without its permission.

### The Grazers and The Pilgrims

On the floor — that endless dim green plain of dished tiles — live the cattle.

They're shrimp only if you need a word for them. Each one is as long as your forearm, translucent as warm wax, with legs like jointed glass and a back armored in overlapping plates the exact institutional green of the tiles they mimic. You can watch their guts work: a dark coil of processed film moving visibly from mouth to tail. They move slowly, always head-down, scraping. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* A dozen of them, maybe fifteen. Never more. They molt in unison after a storm tops the reservoir, and for a day the bottom is littered with perfect, empty shrimp-ghosts that the current rolls gently until the film claims them.

They don't fight. They don't flee. If you wade in, they will part around your ankles and then close behind you and continue scraping your footprints clean.

Above them — never touching them, never touching the walls — are the Pilgrims.

Small, blind, white as milk teeth, with whiskers twice as long as their bodies. They are the only thing that schools here. Twenty or thirty of them, constantly turning in a donut around the central outflow, where the black pipe tugs down at the heart of the mountain. They ride the vortex without ever being taken by it. Each fish is precisely the distance from the next that their whiskers can just almost touch. If one falters, the torus wobbles, corrects, tightens again. They don't eat the film. They drink the water itself — the snow of pollen, the shed skin of newts, the bacteria that bloom when the inlet chills the thermals. Their bellies glow faintly at night, when the dome goes dark and the shelf-fungus wakes up.

They never sleep. They were not built to stop.

### The Clingers

On the vertical walls, where you think nothing could live because there's no purchase and the boiler-plate sweats rust, that's where it gets lush.

The newts. Flat as pressed leaves, wide as your spread hand, the color of wet concrete. Each one has gills that are not gills — feathery external crowns, blood-red, that pulse open and closed like lungs breathing water. They suction to the wall with bellies evolved to grip rivet heads. All day they do nothing but pulse and stare with lidless eyes. At dusk, they unstick one by one and swim — absurdly, gracefully — in slow spirals to the hanging forest to hunt.

There are the frogs, too. They don't belong this high. They're big, moss-backed, the green of the hanging fronds, and they live on the few floating islands — mats of heart-leaves and trapped pollen that have stitched themselves together into rafts. A frog will sit for hours on a leaf that is barely holding it, throat inflating. When it sings — *bonk* — the whole cylinder rings like you struck it with a mallet. The sound goes down through the water and the shrimp pause. The Pilgrims tighten their circle. The pump seems, for a beat, to thud in time with it. There are four frogs. Maybe five. Counting is hard because they look exactly like wet leaves until they blink.

And on the gantry, in the eighteen inches of breathable air that the dome traps, the dragonflies live their short, furious lives. They are not delicate. They are the size of barn swallows themselves, wings clear and veined with rust-red, bodies banded black and amber, eyes like rivets. They were born underwater as monsters — finger-long nymphs with hinged jaws that stalk the newt eggs in the root-mats — and now they hawk the air lens, rattling against the glass. *Thirrr. Thack.* They lay their eggs by stabbing them straight into the fleshy leaves of the hanging forest, even as swallows knife down through the storm mouths to take them.

The swallows don't live there. They *visit*, which is more important. Three or four soot-black mountain swallows who have learned the trick: the vent is a vertical tunnel through stone, forty feet of dark, and at the bottom is warm air and stupid, slow dragonflies. They fold their wings and fall like darts, punch the meniscus without breaking stride, snatch, and climb back out in a spray of droplets that never have time to get cold. They scream the whole time. Their nests are outside, on the bald dome, in the wind. But their hunting ground is inside. They are the only thing that remembers there is an outside.

### The Keeper

And then, at the very bottom, where the light gives out and the outflow sighs, there is whatever the newts defer to.

You'll think it's a pipe at first. The outlet is a black mouth a yard wide, screened by bars set six inches apart, thrumming as it meters water to the cities below that may not even exist anymore. Over centuries the film grew thick there. The rust grew thicker. The root-mats from the hanging forest sent down blind white runners, seeking dark and nutrients, and found it. They braided together around the bars. They thickened. They learned to pulse.

It's not an animal. Not really. If you shine a light down sixty feet through the green gloom you'll see it: a knot, a bale, a soft mass the size of a bathtub, breathing. Pale roots and dark fronds and anemone-like filter fans opening and closing, cleaning the water before it goes down the mountain. Small fish dart in and out of it. Newts lay their eggs on it. It constricts slightly when the pressure valve cycles. It has no eyes. It has no need to move.

It was not placed there. It placed itself. It is what happens when a drain lives long enough to grow a throat.

If you stay on the gantry overnight — and you shouldn't, because the warm air will make you drowsy and the thudding will get inside your heartbeat — you'll feel the whole room sync up around it. The shrimp pause their grazing. The Pilgrims widen their circle. The hanging bells open and release pollen all at once. The frogs answer each other. The Keeper squeezes, and sends a pulse of clean, warm water down through the stone.

That's who lives there. Not a food chain. A household. A few dozen tenants who have agreed, without ever meeting, to keep the water turning over, to keep the light filtering down, to keep the pump from finally grinding itself to death.

They don't need you. They've learned how to use everything humans left — the tile, the steel, the perfect sixty-foot cylinder of sky-held water — better than we ever did.

If you lower yourself in, they'll clean your skin for you, politely, like you were just another warm piece of infrastructure that drifted in through the storm mouth and will, eventually, soften and become part of the wall.
