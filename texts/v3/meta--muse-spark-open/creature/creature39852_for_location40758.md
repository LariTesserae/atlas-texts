Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing you understand, and it's wrong, and you have to hold both ideas at once until the wrong one dissolves.

At first — standing in that fissure with both hands on the weeping walls — you feel the absence as an inhabitant. No eyes. No rustle. No scuttle in the leaf-litter, because there is no leaf-litter. No birds, no insects, no bats, no rats. No ghosts of them, even. A normal forest, even a dead one, is full of the memory of animals. The spaces are animal-shaped. The branches are perches. The hollows are dens.

Here there are no spaces. There is nowhere for an animal to *be*. The whole architecture was grown on the assumption that mouths are inefficient. That legs are wasteful. That anything that has to move to eat is already obsolete.

And then the second understanding comes, slow as the thud in your molars:

Everything lives here.

Not *in* here. *Here* lives.

What occupies this low continent is not a forest. A forest is a place where many things live. This is one thing that lives like a place. A single, continent-wide, sky-sealing organism that learned how to be ground and mountain and factory and weather all at once. It doesn't have inhabitants. It has organs. It doesn't have citizens. It has tissues.

You have to stop looking for a face.

There's no central trunk to touch, no heart-wood to find, no mother-root. If you cut it — and you could cut for days with a torch and not make a wound it would notice — you would not find a center. You'd find more of the same: pipe bundles and sap belts and steam-pistons and filtration curtains, all alive, all awake in the plant way, which is to say responsive without ever being aware.

Think of it on different scales, because it lives on all of them at once:

At the scale of your hand, it lives as skin. The walls around you are not bark. They are pressurized rind, a living periderm as thick as your torso, laced with contractile fibers. It sweats. It shivers. When you press your palm to it and leave it there, the warmth under your hand increases by a fraction of a degree. Thermistors in the tissue have felt a cold spot — you — and are shunting warm sap toward you to equalize. Not because it cares. Because gradient is error. Error is corrected. The surface beads more heavily where you touched and drip rate increases to cool you.

At the scale of your body, it lives as gut and lung. Those pillars of pale fiber flexing beside you are not trunks. They are esophagi. The road-wide lianas creeping overhead are intestines, peristalsing black resin toward combustion chambers that are also stomachs, deep down near the hot rock where water is forced down and flashes to steam. The vast leaves fused into hundred-meter bladders full of amber fluid are alveoli and kidneys. They hang there in the dark, filtering, precipitating, budding off nodules of fuel-weight with a soft *plok*. You think of them as rooms. They are cells.

At the scale of the continent, it lives as climate. That roof a mile or three above you — those plates the size of continents overlapping continents, black-green and slick — they are not canopy. They are skin and sky at once. A single photosynthetic membrane would be pointless here; no light gets through anyway, 0.1 lux of amber fault-line glow. So the roof does not photosynthesize in the way you mean. It transpires. It exhales. It takes the hot water shunted up from below, spreads it across a surface area larger than oceans, lets it condense on the underside and fall again as that endless warm drizzle. It makes its own rain, inside itself, forever. The crackle of St. Elmo's fire across its filament fields is not weather. It is a nervous system. A blue lace of electrostatic discharge that lets a valve opening in the north know to close a valve in the south. Thud. Long pause. Thud.

There is no queen in this hive. No brain.

Plant intelligence is not animal intelligence sped up. It's chemical consensus slowed down until it looks like equilibrium. A pressure drop here diffuses as a hormone there. A sugar glut here precipitates resin there. Over decades, the system hunts for steady state the way water hunts for level. It found it. A long time ago, it found perfect balance — heat in equals heat out, water up equals water down, nodule bud equals nodule burned — and then it stopped evolving, because to change would be to be less efficient.

That's why nothing grows larger. That's why nothing dies. Growth and death are for places with seasons, with scarcity, with sky. Here, a leaf that falls does not get to become humus. It is caught mid-air by a lower plate, enzyme-welded, reabsorbed. A pipe that ruptures seals before it can whistle. Every drop you hear find its cistern a hundred feet below was counted before it fell.

It expelled fauna the way you expel a splinter. Not violently. Just by making employment impossible. An insect wants nectar? The plant moves sugar through sealed phloem faster than any proboscis could steal it. A bird wants fruit? There is no fruit soft and sweet, only resin-nodules dropped directly into waiting bladders. A fungus wants rot? Nothing is allowed to rot. A microbe that tries to live free in one of those amber bladders is filtered, precipitated, and burned as fuel within the hour. Even bacteria here are not inhabitants. They are organelles, domesticated, permitted only inside root-nodules where they fix nitrogen for a host that has no outside.

There are filaments in the air, spores so fine you inhale them with every wet breath. You could count them as living things, if you want to be lonely. But they never germinate. They are test balloons, chemical sniffers, aborted experiments. The dark is too full for them to land.

So who lives here?

A perfect, endless, warm, dripping, silent engine that learned how to be alive without needing to be born or to die. A mangrove that became a refinery that became a lung that became a continent that closed its own sky because sky was a leak.

It does not know you are here. It knows there is a small, cool, salty, 98.6-degree pressure anomaly moving through fissure 4,783,901-B, and it is correcting for condensation and mineral loss. It will grow a root-hair through the sole of your boot if you stand still long enough. Not to eat you. To seal you. To include you.

You are the only animal that has ever lived here.

And you only live here until you stop moving.
