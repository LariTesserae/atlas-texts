Nothing lives here the way you live.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You came up here looking for things that eat and sleep and breed and die, and you will find their leftovers — the print, the feather, the ankle-high birch — but those are not the residents. Those are visitors who learned the trick of staying without staying. What actually lives here is what can live on equilibrium itself.

At the bottom — because there is a bottom, even in a place that has finished happening — there are the crusts.

The black lichen is the most honest citizen of the plateau. It does not grow so much as it agrees to be. You will think it is a stain at first, a damp shadow on the north side of every stone, until you realize every stone has the same shadow in the same place, even the stones you turn over. It spreads at the speed that stone spreads, which is to say you will never see it move, but if you come back in ten years — if anyone ever came back — it will have covered exactly one more millimeter, and that millimeter will have been exactly necessary. It eats cold light and condensation and thin air, and it eats the sound. Put your ear to a boulder covered in it and the organ-chord of the pillars dulls, just there, just for that boulder, like someone put a hand over its mouth.

Beside it, in the lees where a century of dust has made a teaspoon of soil, lives the star-moss. It is brittle when you touch it. It shatters. And that is its life. It waits to be stepped on, or hailed on by the sideways rain, to be crushed flat into the shale. Then, over hours when you are not looking — because it will not do it while observed — it inhales the damp film off the air and springs back, one spiky arm at a time, until it is a perfect star again. It has no need for sun. It lives on being broken and unbroken. There are thousands of them, each alone in its wind-shadow, each with a clear sightline to the next, never touching. That distance is part of their body.

A little higher, almost insultingly alive, are the birches. One per hollow, never two. They are not trees that shrank; they are trees that decided to become something else and stopped halfway. An ankle high, pencil-thick, twenty years old, thirty, fifty — you can count the rings if you are cruel enough to cut one, tight as fingerprints. Their leaves are not leaves but coins, green so dark it is almost black, leathery, curled under against the perpetual damp. All their branches comb east, even when the wind is gone, even in the shelter of a cairn. They remember a wind the way your bones remember childhood summers. They do not drop their leaves. They trade one for one. When a new coin comes in that bruised-purple spring, an old one falls, exactly. Their number never changes. They are not growing. They are maintaining.

These are the things that photosynthesize here. Everything else has learned a different economy.

Take the ibex-thing. You will not see it move. No one has. It does not walk. It relocates. You find it standing on the spine of a yardang, that coat of lichen-stone so perfect you only see it when it blinks — and it blinks seldom, with eyes that take light but give nothing back. You look at it, and it looks through you, because you are a warm, fast smear in a cold, slow world. You look away, to cough, to wipe condensation from your lashes, and when you look back it is still there, but further. Not further along the ridge. Further away, as if someone slid the yardang back ten meters while you blinked. Walk toward it and distance will do its breathing trick; it will hold size for a hundred steps, then be beside you — or rather, you will be where it was, and it will be on the next ridge, at the same angle, watching again. It does not eat the moss. It does not drink the black ice. If it eats at all, it eats interval. It grazes on the space between things. That is why the cairns keep it out; a ring-wall with no roof but a careful interior pavement is not a sheepfold. It is a do-not-disturb sign for an animal that steps through distance.

Above it, always above it, is the thing that turned the feather loose. You never see it. The lid-cloud is too low, too close — you could throw a stone and hit the sky here, if throwing still worked normally. You only see it as an interruption, a slow dimming of that paler eastern smear as something very large circles with no hurry at all. Its feather, when you find one, is ash-grey, as long as your forearm, and it turns in place on wind-polished stone without wind, ticking around like a compass needle that has found a different north. The stone under it is always dry. That is how you know it is not a bird. Birds leave wet. This thing leaves absence. It lives in the chord. If you stand between the organ-pipe pillars and feel that low note in your molars, you are standing in where it lives. The pillars are not singing to you; they are its perches, hollowed and held. It does not hunt. It waits for things that get tired of holding their shape.

And then there are the first inhabitants. The ones who stacked stone on stone so carefully it seems obscene.

They are still here, but not as bodies. The plateau did not keep graves because it keeps patterns better than meat. You will find a cairn that is not on any map you made, with the top stone half-set, as if someone set it down to rest their arms. You will find the way-station with the two walls holding up nothing, and inside, the tally: 47 lines, scratched with a harder stone, the 48th half-started and abandoned mid-cut because the hand understood it did not need to finish. These are not ruins. These are molds.

If you sit long enough beside your own half-built cairn — and you will build one, everyone does, without deciding to, picking up stones heavy enough to just-barely-lift and fitting them because your hands need to do what stone does here, which is stay — you may feel the others sit with you. Not ghosts. Echoes that arrive before the sound. The shepherds who learned to make a ring-wall three feet high and open to the east, not to keep the ibex-thing out but to keep the inside dry enough that a human could stand there for one more hour. The carvers of spirals and hands and eyes who were not decorating but labeling: *this is a hand, this is an eye, this was seen, this touched.* They lived here by learning to want less per minute. A breath per ten. A thought per hour. They left when equilibrium asked them to be still longer than a body can be still.

Some of them stayed by becoming still enough. That is the last kind of life here, and the most common.

The black ice in its stone cup. The puddle that never freezes solid and never evaporates and ticks a millimeter in and out. The damp film on every surface that was not there when you looked away. They are alive. Not as animals. As kept decisions. The plateau breathed once, perfectly, and then held it. The water holds the breath with it. Put your palm to a pillar and you feel the chord not as sound but as held tension, like a muscle that has not cramped in ten thousand years.

That is what lives here: things that have learned to live without change. Lichen that eats light and sound. Moss that lives on breaking. Trees that trade leaf for leaf forever. An ungulate that pastures on distance. A sky-thing that nests in a trapped note. And the human-shaped quiet between cairns, still counting to 47.

If you stay, you will join them — not by dying, exactly — but by thinning. Your breath will slow to match the ice-tick. Your thoughts will space themselves to the distance between moss-stars. You will stop wanting to finish the tally, and that will be the moment you are finally well-suited to this country made for one long, slow breath.
