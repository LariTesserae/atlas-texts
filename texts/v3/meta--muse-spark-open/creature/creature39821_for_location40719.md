Nothing lives here the way it does anywhere else.

Not at first. At first you think this bowl is empty. There is no green to catch the eye, no movement to track. The basalt forest is too still to be alive, the water too shallow to hold anything, the air too cool and too saturated to breed. You walk as if through an abandoned quarry that has filled with rain.

Then you stay long enough for the place to correct you.

### The First Residents Are Not Animals

The crowd itself is alive.

Not alive like a tree — alive like a crystal is alive when it is still growing. The spires and needles and fins and botryoidal domes are not dead rock left over from the upheaval. They are the upheaval still happening, only slower. 

Each formation weeps. Capillary water is pulled up through the young, vesicular basalt, through a labyrinth of trapped bubbles and cooling tubes, and as it reaches the cold air it degasses and drops its load. Silica comes out of solution in opaline sheets. Calcite feathers at the tips. Sulfur yellows at the base where microfilms breathe without ever needing light. Zeolite grows like frost, white and felted, a millimeter a day. Iron blooms rust-red and then pulses darker when a new sheet of condensation washes over it.

You can put your hand on a fin — thin as slate, tall as you — and feel it. A faint, patient vibration. The *hum* you thought was background is coming out of it. Water singing through stone. Stone adding itself to itself.

They crowd because they are all trying to grow at once, shoulder to shoulder, with no room to fall. A needle leans into a dome; the dome incorporates its edge; a fin grows straight through the place where two columns are trying to occupy the same narrow gap. If you came back in fifty years the capillary you squeezed through would be gone, sealed shut by that pearlescent silica terrace lipping over the waterline. In a century this entire acre will be solid, and the water that is now sheeting over everything will be trapped as those branching inclusions you see inside the clearest chert — little illuminated trees of ancient water, moving when you are not looking.

This is a mineral forest with mineral metabolism: slow, wet, cold, and relentlessly accretive. It doesn't want to be walked through. It wants to close.

### The Water Is Also An Animal

It's not a lake. It's not a river. It's the blood of the bowl.

An inch down, a foot down, the table itself. Pressurized, cold, everywhere at once. It doesn't flow so much as it *perspires* — up through stone, down off every point in endless ticking drips, across the glass in a skin a few centimeters deep, pooling calf-deep where the buckling plates have sagged. 

It keeps everything at fifty degrees. It makes the fog. Cool air over cool water over cool stone: the bowl exhales a knee-high mist that never lifts, that beads on every surface and drips again. Nothing ever dries here because the water doesn't allow it. It circulates.

And it keeps time. On a scale of minutes — seven, ten, twelve, you lose count — the whole system swells. The water rises a fraction of a millimeter. Condensation accelerates. The hundred thousand drip-points fall into phase inside their natural waveguides: *wuh-WUH, wuh-WUH*, piling harmonic on harmonic until the roar peaks and the pools shiver with concentric rings, and then it sighs. The pools go glassy. The hum drops to almost nothing. For a breath, the forest holds still.

Then it begins again. No weather drives it. No tide. The rock breathing water, water breathing air. This pulse was here before anything with legs found the bowl, and it will be here after the gaps close.

If anything *rules* here, it is that.

### The True Animals Are Rare On Purpose

Life that needs to move cannot afford to be common here. There is no food chain in the green sense. No plants to eat, no insects to swarm. So what lives here lives *translucently*, *tentatively*, in the margins the mineral forest allows.

**The sheets.**

In the deeper, black-mirror pools you sometimes catch them: palm-sized, flat as a leaf, absolutely clear except for a faint opaline gut. They are not fish. No fins, no eyes you can find. Just an undulating membrane that paddles itself with a traveling wave around its edge, skimming the silica terraces and grazing. They eat the precipitate itself — the fresh opal, the bacterial iron film that looks like a stain. You only ever see one at a time, and when your shadow hits the pool it folds — literally folds in half like paper and jets down between two stone teeth and disappears into the submerged interstices where your hands can't follow. They live their whole lives in water that is never more than a foot deep, and never needs to be.

**The stilts.**

Above — because there is an above, even here — on the tops of the spires where the fog thins, the other one hunts. Pale to the point of colorlessness, six legs like jointed needles, body no bigger than your fist held up high. It never touches the water. It picks its way across the needle-points with impossible precision, each foot finding a dry tip, its weight distributed so lightly it doesn't snap the chert. 

It is listening. You realize this when you freeze and it freezes too. Its head — if it is a head — cocks toward a corridor of parallel fins where the drips have fallen into sync. It waits. When a sheet below ripples the surface just enough to make a *plok* out of rhythm, it descends in three quick, horrifyingly long strides and spears. Most times it misses, and must climb all the way back up to dryness to try again. It is the only thing here that makes a sound on purpose.

There may be others — a worm of white glass you only see as a flicker inside a pool; colonies of sulfur-yellow dots that creep a centimeter a month — but they are all built on the same principles: stay flat, stay pale, stay light, never fight the stone for space, let the water do the work.

### The People Who Try To Live Here

And then there are the humans. Or were.

They do not live here. Not really. No one can. There is nothing to burn that will stay lit in this saturated cold, nothing to eat that isn't silica, no dry place to sleep except the one you make and even that will be slick by morning.

What lives here instead is a *type* of human: the listener-pilgrim.

You know them by what they left, because they left almost nothing that could rot. A wall three courses high that encloses nothing, its basalt shards already being welded together by new crust — not a defense, a gesture: *I was here, this is my width.* A causeway of flat stones laid with great care across a pool that wanted to be crossed, leading to a broad dome topped flat, polished by sitting. An altar or a table, depending on what you needed more.

A shelter made of three leaning fins propped together, just large enough for one person to sit cross-legged out of the direct drip. Inside, a circle of smooth pebbles placed one by one. No ash. No bones. Whoever sat there wasn't trying to survive. They were trying to be *dense and wet and low* enough that the bowl would count them as one of its formations for a night.

They come because the roar calls. A place this young, this crowded, this loud without any life to make the noise — it feels intentional. You stand in a corridor where the drips have phased and the hum has risen and the fog thickens with your own breath, and for a long minute you are convinced the bowl is trying to say something, or that if you listen with your molars and your bootsoles you'll hear the exact frequency of stone becoming stone.

You mark it. You stack. You sit. You listen until the cold soaks through you, until you realize you are an interruption in the accretion, and you leave before the silica can start growing over your footprints. The next pilgrim finds your wall and adds one stone, or takes one to make their own circle somewhere else.

They are the only warm-blooded things that tolerate the fifty-degree wet, the only things that want the loudness. They don't colonize. They visit, like moths.

### What Actually Lives Here

Put it together and no single image holds:

A bowl of young glass at near-sea-level that never dried.
A forest of mineral that grows by sweating.
A sheet of water that is also a lung.
Two — maybe a dozen — species of translucent, stilted animal that have learned to eat rock and walk on knives.
A succession of people who came to be quiet in a place that is never quiet, and left only the kind of sign that will soon become rock itself.

If you ask what *lives* here, the honest answer is: the bowl does. 

Not as metaphor. As ecology.

The stone, the water, the sound, the fog, the slow closing of gaps — it is all one distributed organism in mineral time. The sheets and the stilts are parasites on its skin. The humans are brief itches, scratching a circle before they go. The hum and the pulse are its heartbeat, measurable nowhere but in your chest when you stand still long enough to let your own drip-phase sync with its.

It was doing this before anyone named basalt. It will keep doing it until the forest finally fuses shut, the water is locked as inclusions, the roar mutes to a trapped hiss, and pressure builds again somewhere else — miles away, or right under your boots — to crack fresh, sharp, wet land open for the next bowl to start listening to itself.
