Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing you have to understand, and it takes a while to understand it because the place is so crowded.

You come in expecting life to be sparse at 19,000 feet. You expect it to be thin, tough, holding on — a cushion plant in a crack, a lichen the color of rust, a single alpine spider under a rock. You expect the usual high bargaining with existence.

There is no bargain here. The terms are wrong. The ultraviolet comes down unfiltered and sterilizes. The chemistry of the basalt and old andesite gives nothing back. The water — and there is water, that is the trick of this bowl — cannot be kept. It condenses out of valley air on the cold stone, beads and runs for an hour, and then is gone, sublimed or evaporated or frozen into a pane as clear and hard as glass that cracks at 4 p.m. like a gunshot. Nothing that needs continuity can live on intermittency like that.

So there is no lichen. No moss. No black crust. No insect hits the side of a cairn. No bird lands. You listen for the hum you always hear in a living place, even a desert place, and there is no hum. Only ticking stone, hissing frost, the drip every few minutes from an overhang into a pan no wider than your palm.

And yet the floor of the cirque is full.

It is full the way a house can be full after everyone has left it. Three hundred yards across, walled on three sides by those rounded, finished cliffs, and the floor is shoulder-to-shoulder with cairns. Hundreds of them. Three to four feet high, hand-stacked, different hands, centuries apart.

They are what lives here. Or more precisely, they are what *stays* here, which at this altitude is the same thing.

They live the way stone lives when it has been given intention and then left alone.

Each one has a slightly different way of being upright. Some are tight, thoughtful, each flat shale piece interlocked so that the whole tower leans very slightly into the prevailing wind and holds. Those are the older ones, or the more patient builders. Some are loose, already slumped at the shoulder, a pile beginning to remember that it was once just scree at the angle of repose. Some are capped with quartz cobbles that act as little suns — they catch that violent white light at noon and throw it back, and they hold a crescent of frost in their shadow long after the rest has gone to vapor, as if they are breathing slower than everything else.

They are not alive. They don't grow. But they participate in the only cycle this bowl allows: the water cycle.

At around eleven, when the valley air pushes in through the southern mouth and fails against the cold walls, the northern sides of the cairns darken. Condensation beads on them grain by grain until the gray shale turns black. You can watch a single tower sweat. The water runs down its stacked edges and gathers at its foot, where someone, sometime, made a low circle of stones around a natural scalloped basin to keep it clean. The pan fills, a knuckle-deep lens that holds the whole dark blue sky perfectly until the sun takes it again.

For twenty minutes in the early afternoon the summit cloud spills over the western wall like dry water — not rain, just suspended droplets — and the light goes pearlescent and flat and the cairns lose their shadows. Then they look most like inhabitants. Without shadow you lose scale; the one fifty feet away could be ten feet away. The thin air bends a little. The far cliff seems to inhale. In the fog-damped quiet you hear your own heart and, underneath it, the faint creak of those little panes of ice forming and re-forming in every pan, between every pair of stacked stones where a drop got trapped.

That's what lives here. Not a species. A population.

A hamlet of attentions. Each cairn is one person's answer to the same unanswerable elevation: *I was here, I carried this up, I made this stand for a little longer than I will.* No one ever lived here to tend them. That is why they count as residents. They have outlasted every builder. They have weathered into the same state as the cliffs — rounded, settled, finished. Nothing is collapsing, nothing is new. Erosion has done its work and stopped, and left this density on purpose.

If you kneel — and you will, behind one of those single-course windbreaks that barely breaks the wind — and you look close at the water in its little stone-lined pan before it evaporates, you will see that it is absolutely clear. There is nothing in it. No wriggling thing, no green thread, no speck. Even the things that can live anywhere — tardigrades, rotifers, endolithic bacteria — have not found tenure here. The water is too brief and too pure, distilled straight out of air onto glassy rock and gone. This is one of the few places on earth that is truly sterile and open to the sky at the same time.

So the cairns keep the water instead. They keep the shadow for the frost. They keep the soot-smear of old fires in pits lined with darker rock. They keep the green stain of a copper offering weathered to nothing. They hold the only memory this bowl retains, which is human memory, left as weight and balance.

When night comes in an hour the temperature will drop ten degrees in ten minutes and every damp stone will freeze and every pan will crack again, loud as rifle shots in the cathedral quiet. The starlight will be sharp enough to cast shadows. The cairns will tick and settle, almost touching, none of them moving. And none of them dead, either — because nothing here has ever been alive in the way you mean.
