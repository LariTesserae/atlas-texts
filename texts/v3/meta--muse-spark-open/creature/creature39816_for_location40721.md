They don't live there like people live in a town.

They live there like lichen lives in rock. Like mold lives in bread. Like a thought lives in a cracked skull — not *on* it, but squeezed *into* the geometry that allows it to exist at all.

If you stood on the central gantry long enough for your eyes to adjust to the blue dusk, you would stop seeing "a city" and start seeing a stratification. A reef turned on its side.

### The People are not the most numerous thing there.

First, because there are not very many of them — maybe three hundred at high season, fewer when a support cable shears and takes six dwellings with it — but that's not why. It's because in the Horn, humans are not the biomass. They are the scaffolding.

You can spot them by their stillness. Everything else in the fissure is in constant, frantic motion, but the Crackborn have learned to not move.

They are low, broad people, even the children. Barrel-chested out of necessity; at this pressure your blood wants to forget how to carry oxygen. Their lips are always cracked, not from cold but from dryness — the skin around their mouths and nostrils is white with salt-scar, and their eyes are huge and wet in a way that looks almost diseased, until you realize the moisture is rationed there on purpose. They blink slowly. They speak rarely, and when they do it is a low, close murmur, because the thin air doesn't carry a shout. All their songs are hums you feel through the iron.

No one is old. The air peels you whether you are careful or not. Their lungs, after twenty years, are the color of the wall — soot-black with iron flecks. But they are not sickly. They are adapted in the way a deep fish is adapted.

Watch one for an hour and you see the choreography:

**The Stokers** live highest, closest to the seam where the sky leaks in. They tend the Furnace, which is not theirs. The Furnace was there before the town — a natural chimney in the basalt where geothermal heat breathes out, which the first founders capped with a kiln and never let die. It chews powdered rock and thin wind and gives back the two things without which the crack is a tomb: heat and a trickle of current. A Stoker can tell by the thrum in her molars if the draft is wrong. Their whole liturgy is keeping it lit. *If it goes out everything dies* is not a saying. It's the first thing children memorize.

**The Net-Tenders** are the inverse. They work in darkness. At sundown, when the temperature in the crack drops two degrees and the stratospheric snow begins to fall — that faint, glittering hydrocarbon frost that smells faintly of kerosene and bad decisions — they climb, hand over hand, up into the lattice of cables and throw the black aerogel sheets across the seam. The sheets are not nets in any normal sense; they are static fields on a frame. By morning they are white with frost. The Tenders scrape them with bone knives into sealed kettles, where it is boiled without boiling — in a closed loop, under pressure, because *no open containers. No steam without a hood. Spill nothing.* One kettle is a day's drinking, washing, cooling the lathe. You will never see a Crackborn pour. They decant with syringes.

**The Salvors** are everyone else, which is to say everyone. There is no ground here. A family that has lived in alcove 14-B for three generations will wake to a *ping* — a rivet head popping as the mountain, still young, still pushing itself up a millimeter a year, stretches — and without discussion they will unbolt their tin wall and carry it two bridges over to where the rock has opened a new, slightly more stable scar. Houses are verbs here. The belt drive that runs the entire length of the fissure — a single long loop of old mining cable that every lathe, drill, and coil-winder taps with a leather belt — is constantly being re-tensioned. A half-built weather-drone that hung in the assembly frame yesterday will be gone today because its aluminum ribs were needed to shore a sagging walkway. Progress, here, is not building up. It's staying even.

They eat what they make. Which is mostly nothing. A paste rendered from glass-moths, a mineral broth, the eggs of doves. They trade less. The one thing that leaves the fissure, sometimes, is a completed drone — a fragile thing of foil and wire that catches the wind outside and rides it down to the world below carrying a photograph, a measurement, a warning. They do not leave. To go down to thick air would drown them as surely as staying here would drown you.

You thought they were the city. They are not. They are the gardeners.

### The City is the Animals

The reason the air in the fissure is dim even at noon is not shadow. It's life.

There are no plants. There has never been a green thing in the Horn; there's no water for it, no soil to root in. What lives here skipped photosynthesis entirely and went straight to the other economies: eat heat. Eat dust. Eat each other.

It is layered like shale:

At the warmest level, pressed flat against every pipe and duct that carries Furnace heat, are **the Doves**. They are not doves, really — you call them that because you don't have a word. Pale, translucent gray birds the size of your fist, with twice as many feathers as a dove should need, layered like insulation batting. They do not coo. They do not fly inside the crack; there isn't room. They shuffle, shoulder to shoulder, by the dozens, so still you think they are part of the pipe lagging until the pipe cycles and they all shift six inches left in perfect, silent unison. They drink warmth through their feet. At night they exhale a powder of keratin dust that feeds everything below.

Below them, on the sheer basalt itself, are **the Skaters**. Six-legged, flat as a hand, their carapaces the exact iron-red of the stone. You only see them when they move — a soft *tik tik tik* across the glass — because when they stop they are invisible. Their bellies are not legs; they are heat exchangers, pale and veined, that they press to the rock. They graze. Not on algae — on the bacterial film that grows on condensed hydrocarbons, on the Dove dust, on the microscopic iron oxide that blooms on every surface. Children let them walk across their hands to warm them.

In the air column itself, so thick you breathe them, are **the Glass-Moths**. They are the plankton of the fissure. No bigger than ash flakes, wings of living mica that catch the amber bulb-light and throw it back. They hatch, swarm, mate, and die in about an hour, drawn irresistibly to any heat or light. Every evening the bulbs go cloudy with them, a snowstorm in reverse. This is the harvest. The children go out with oiled trays and sweep them by the handful, still twitching, and press them. One handful is a thimble of clear, tasteless oil that burns without smoke. One handful is also a mouthful of protein if you are not too disgusted to eat it. Nothing is wasted.

Along the cables and rope bridges — the nervous system of the town — run **the Rats**. Blind, naked-tailed, with whiskers longer than their bodies. They never touch stone. They are born on the wires, die on the wires. They run in endless, counter-flowing lines, gnawing the crust of mineral and salt that deposits on insulation. Their droppings are not droppings; they are bricks. Gray-green paste, still warm, that the people collect and the wasps steal.

And down at the very bottom, where the V of the walls narrows to a wedge you can't stand in, where all heat pools, are **the Builders**. You hear them before you see them, a low papery rasping. Wasps the length of your thumb, but they are not wasps; their abdomens are swollen with iron they have eaten off the walls. They chew the Rat paste, the Dove dust, the dead Moths, the slag from the Furnace, mix it with paper from their own wings, and build. Tiered, hexagonal nests the exact size and shape of the human houses above them, stacked like wasp cells, hung from chains of their own silk that they anchor directly into basalt with a spit that etches glass. Tear one down and they will build it a foot higher by morning, where it's a tenth of a degree warmer. The people learned how to hang a house from them.

That is the loop, closed and frantic: Furnace heat grows Dove, Dove dust feeds Skater and Moth, Moth oil runs the lamp that draws more Moth, Rat cleans wire and feeds Wasp, Wasp paper insulates Furnace house and becomes Dove nest. Droppings become paste become paper become insulation become droppings. A desert reef.

And it never stops. It can't. The walls shift, the wires sag, the frost falls differently this year than last, and everything — human, dove, skater, moth, rat, wasp — tears down and rebuilds a hand's width over. Tourists would call it squalor. Ecologists would call it a climax community at extreme compression.

Stand on that swaying gantry again. Look up at the bright seam of impossible sky. Look down at the compacted bottom — not rock now, but centuries of fallen slag, bone, bent scaffolding, old nests, all of it still shivering, ticking, shuffling.

The rule of the White Horn is the rule of every place where there should be nothing at all:

Life doesn't Thin Out.

It folds in on itself until it is dense, and quiet, and industrious, and strange — a city in a wound that the mountain didn't mean to make, kept alive by people who have become indistinguishable from the animals who keep them alive.
