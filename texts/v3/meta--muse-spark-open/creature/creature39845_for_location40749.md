What lives in the Cut is not adapted to it. It is being made *by* it, the way a hand is made by the work it does.

At first from the rim you think: people. Just people, very many of them. And they are people — unmistakably human, small-statured and broad through the shoulders from hauling, pale in a way that has nothing to do with sickness and everything to do with light that never quite reaches the lower terraces except as reflection.

Then you descend and they stop being a crowd and start being a texture, and the texture resolves into kinds that only exist here.

### The Stone People

Everyone works stone whether their name is for it or not, so you learn them by how the stone has marked them.

There are the **Cutters**. You hear them before you see them, that patient *thuk thuk thuk* all along the raw walls. They are not strong in the way lowland farmers are strong. They are dense — low centers of gravity, thick forearms veined and dusted white to the elbow, knuckles swollen from the vibration of stone mauls. Their eyes are always narrowed, not from suspicion but from staring at a single plane of rock inches from their face for hours, looking for the grain. Many of them are half-deaf in one ear, the ear turned toward the wall. When they speak to each other on the ledge they don't shout; they tap codes with their hammers — two taps, pause, three — and lean their foreheads briefly against the cool wall to feel the reply travel through the rock.

There are the **Carriers**, mostly younger, both sexes, barefoot or in rag-wrapped soles that grip the slick stone. They have backs like shelves. A Carrier child by ten learns to balance a basket of spoil on a padded ring on their head and move without spilling, down switchbacks too narrow for hurry. Their calves are knots. Their lungs are what you notice if you walk beside one — a shallow, rapid breath that never quite gets deep enough in the pooled cold air at the bottom. At night they cough together, a soft chorus behind partition walls, and fall asleep sitting up because lying flat makes the dust settle.

There are the **Layers and Mortar-Mothers**. They are older, or injured Cutters whose wrists gave out. They sit in rows in the alleys mixing mud mortar with their feet, shaping the hand-baked brick that never quite fires hard in the damp. Their hands are always dark, polished as the inner stones by touch. They know every wall by feel. They can walk blind through their terrace and tell you whose house is whose by the temperature of the stones.

None of these are professions exactly. In the Cut you will be all three before you die, if you live long enough to be old — which few do past forty, not from violence but from wear.

Physically, they have converged.

Because the air is always a few degrees too cold and always faintly damp, no one ever truly sweats and no one ever truly dries. Skin stays cool to the touch, slightly clammy. Hair is kept cropped close or shaved entirely, because lice love packed places and because condensation dripping from a fringe will drive you mad by midday. Faces are broad and wind-burned on the upper terraces, almost bloodless white on the lowest levels where a person might go weeks without direct sun. Eyes are dark and have deep lines at the corners from squinting against glare off wet ash-colored stone.

They are quiet because the Cut requires quiet. A child learns before speech that if you yell the wall yells back louder, and everyone looks at you. So language has gone soft and close. They speak in murmurs, in hand-signs over the loom, in nudges. Songs exist but they are humming songs, chest-voice songs meant to carry three feet and no further — work songs timed to hammer-fall, lullabies that are mostly breath. The loudest voice in the city belongs to the Water Callers, and even they do not shout.

### The Water Keepers

Water is intermittence made flesh.

No one owns it, but some remember it better than others. Old women and a few old men whose joints ache before rain keep the cisterns. They are the ones who go out after the sheet-rain and open the flat stones over the runnels with hooked sticks, guiding the sudden brief torrent into the scooped basins at the foot of each stair. For an afternoon the Cut is loud with children — the only time children are allowed to be loud — shrieking and splashing in water ankle-deep and brown with rock dust, before it is gone again.

In the morning, before any cut begins, you see the other rite: figures moving terrace to terrace with rags, wiping condensation from lintels and sleeping mats and slate roofs into jars. A family can get a cup a day that way. It tastes of stone. In the sheltered north-facing corners where algae makes the rock slick and black-green, the Lichen Tendrils work. They cultivate the edible crust with bone scrapers, misting it with mouthfuls of that collected water. They speak to it. Not prayerfully. Practically. *Grow here, stay.*

That fig tree in its courtyard — the only true tree — has a keeper who has never left its shadow. He is a young man with a withered leg who cannot carry. All day he picks off insects one by one, props leaves toward the sun with string, talks low to the handful of wasps that pollinate it. The fruit when it comes is not eaten as food. It is divided by knife-tip and passed like news.

### The Packed Ones

The strangest adaptation is not bodily at all. It's spatial.

People in the Cut do not have a concept of empty room. Privacy is not a wall; it's a turned shoulder, a curtain of woven reed pulled two feet from a sleeping bench, an agreement not to look. Families of five sleep in a cell the size of a lowland shed, stacked on platforms, breathing each other's condensation. To be alone is faintly shameful, a sign you have no work. Even lovers press close in alleys where anyone could brush past, because there is nowhere else, and no one looks directly — eyes are kept down in the narrow lanes as courtesy.

Because of this, touch is constant and meaningless and therefore deeply meaningful. A hand on the small of your back as you pass means *I see you, go ahead*. Children ride on hips until they are heavy, then on shoulders, then underfoot, always in contact. An infant that cries too long three terraces over is hushed quickly not from annoyance but from a collective wince — sound carries so well off damp stone that distress is literally shared air.

Grief and birth happen in the same small rooms, curtained off while life continues inches away, hammering never stopping. The dead are not buried — there is no soil to bury in, and no space to waste. The dead are carried up the wide switchbacks at dawn, wrapped in their own blanket, to the flatland above where the wind is. What happens there you don't see from below. Those who return come back quieter and get straight back to work.

What also lives here, because people make room for it, is sparse:

The thin stone-cats, the color of ash, bellies never full, who slip across roofs and are never owned but are welcomed in grain lofts because they kill the glossy black beetles that live in the barley beds. To kill a cat is not punished by law — there is no law written — but the alley will go silent when you pass for a month.

The pairs of hardy sparrows that nesting in holes deliberately left in terrace walls when they were laid. Seeing one with nesting material is good luck for the household that left the hole.

The heavy grey rock-eaters, forearm-long, utterly still on quarry faces absorbing daytime heat into their cold blood, worshipped by children who are forbidden to touch them. The old say the lizards know where the next good seam is because they vanish into it first. The lizards tolerate humans the way stone tolerates water — indifferently.

And everywhere, on walls, in beds, in jars: lichen, barley shoots, succulent leaves swollen with stored water like green thumbs. They are tended as if they were also citizens, given names by terrace. They are not decoration. They are the reason there is still a next season.

If you stay one full day you understand the cycle that binds them: water beads and is wiped, stone thuds and is carried, barley lifts a millimeter, carriers ascend empty-handed and descend full, mothers grind lichen cakes on flat stones with a rhythm like breathing. No part is dramatic. All parts interlock because if one stops, the packing becomes crushing rather than holding.

And at night, when the thousands of hearth pits bloom warm and normal against damp rock, you see what really lives in the Cut. Not just the bodies.

It is a compact. Unspoken, unfinished, like the walls themselves.

*You will be cold together. You will be damp together. You will be crowded beyond comfort. You will tap and haul and lay and wipe. And in return the Cut will hold you inside its new, sharp, still-growing walls and keep the wind off you, and tomorrow there will be a little more Cut than there was today.*

From the rim the light of all those quiet fires reflects off wet stone and looks for a moment like something breathing.
