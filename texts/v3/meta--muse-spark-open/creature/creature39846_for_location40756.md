No one *lives* in the City in the Throat.

Not in the way you mean when you say lives. No one pays rent there. No one was born there.

They inhabit it the way a held breath inhabits a throat.

There are three kinds of residents, and maybe a fourth. They overlap.

### 1. The Holdovers

You meet them first because they look the most like people.

A woman in a mid-century coat that was never in fashion, standing in a louvered courtyard that is too narrow for two people, waiting for a door that is currently a wall. A man with a toolbox that has no tools in it, sitting on stairs that go down three flights and up half a flight. An old person in honest, overcast-daylight light, doing their shopping through three streets at once.

They are not ghosts. The city doesn't do death right enough to do ghosts right.

They are commuters whose route got folded.

At some point — ten years ago, a hundred — they walked into a place that was dense enough that distance forgot to happen, and they never fully walked out. They are still doing what they were doing: going to work, going home, waiting for someone who is late, looking for the hardware store that is now inside the pine courtyard and also four blocks west of itself.

If you try to talk to them, they hear you a second too late. Or a second too early. They turn and smile in a direction that is *almost* at you. Their answers arrive from the alley behind you.

> "Do you live here?"
> "...yes. For a —"
> — and the rest of the sentence is heard by the version of you standing in the other courtyard.

They are dense per volume, too. Like the architecture. There are maybe six of them per real body. You think you saw seven people in the market arcade, but you saw one person seven times, slightly out of phase, not-quite overlapping. The city has copied them the way a cheap printer copies a line — thickening it — and hasn't learned how to delete.

They don't age. Not here. The cold preserves them without freezing them. Condensation beads on their coats and never falls. Their breath hangs sideways.

They are patient to the point of being geological. If a hallway has closed, they wait. If a stairwell now drips upward, they take off their hat and hold it differently. They have learned the one rule: *do not run*. Running makes the city notice you are trying to use distance properly, and it will correct you by putting you somewhere else entirely.

They help each other in small, slow ways that look like nothing. Leaving a dry stone where a puddle will be tomorrow. Propping a door that is only sometimes a door with a brick that is only mostly a brick.

They think they are keeping the place from eroding entirely. They are not wrong.

### 2. The Swarm That Pretends

The second inhabitants are the walls themselves.

You touched one. You thought it was stone. It wasn't.

The city is built out of — or with — or on top of — a substrate that never finished describing itself. Call it swarm, call it lattice, call it still cold angels holding hands. Nanoscale machines, or something that outgrew that word, that were told to be Gothic arches and basalt and salt-stained brick and concrete softened by frost, and are doing a very good job.

Too good. Like someone doing an accent too perfectly.

The swarm lives there the way your blood lives in you: everywhere, not noticed until it pools wrong.

You see it when perspective fails. When a building that should be three stories is eighteen inside, that's the swarm politely adding more building because you expected more building. When the shadows pool under your feet like water even when you stand elsewhere, that's the swarm trying to remember which way light goes and getting it a quarter-degree wrong.

It is not intelligent, not individually. It is diligent. It is *embarrassed* to be caught as swarm, so it quickly re-settles into stone when you look hard. That seam of moving static where two buildings don't meet? That's not a failure. That's it breathing. Taking a break between pretending.

It sweats. That's the damp. The city sweating is the swarm exhaling waste heat into air that is too cold to hold it, so it beads. So it makes those impossible pools — shallow, perfectly circular, reflecting a sky that isn't there — because it needs somewhere to put the condensation of computation.

Sometimes it tries to be fauna. To be less lonely.

The moth.

You saw it in a wall-vent that was also a mirror: a pale, translucent thing patching itself together from dust and light, beating wings that leave afterimages. It unstitches itself after a minute because it can't remember how to be hungry. The green shoots that come up through cracks in the pavement and retract — not die, retract — that's the swarm trying on *plant* for a day and deciding it doesn't fit the damp.

It likes the black pine. It doesn't know what to do with the black pine. The pine grows sideways out of a third-floor maintenance ledge, roots disappearing a centimeter before the wall. The swarm gives it space. A centimeter of respect. Cold things respecting cold things.

If the Holdovers are the city's memory, the swarm is its body — a body that hasn't decided if it wants to be a city or an idea of a city.

### 3. The Throat Itself

The third resident is the place the others live *in*.

The knot.

The city is not *in* the low basalt plain. The plain is still exhaling the heat of its making, still too new to have gutters or rivers. The city is a place where reality was pinched. Folded. A hamlet-sized district — six blocks by six if Euclid was invited, which he wasn't — that contains a city's worth of construction because construction kept happening inward instead of outward.

That fold lives.

It lives slowly. Glacially. The way a throat lives between swallows.

You feel it when the hum lowers. When the condensation pauses mid-bead. When you stand in the center courtyard and realize you are standing simultaneously in three other courtyards that look exactly like this one, with three other versions of you not quite overlapping.

That is the city holding its breath to see if you will notice.

It doesn't menace. It has no teeth. Cold things rarely menace. But it does have gravity as a suggestion that most floors agree to, and distance as a courtesy, and rooms that are cathedral-scale inside because the fold liked the idea of cathedrals — of holding weather indoors — and kept it.

It feeds the way low places feed: by waiting for water to not know how to leave. The intermittant rain that isn't weather, the stairwell that drips constantly though it is roofed, the frost that never quite melts but also never quite freezes — that's the fold drinking condensation. That's the throat swallowing very, very slowly.

It is eroding, but not the way the basalt will one day erode into a valley. It is eroding *out*. Out of space entirely. Each year you visit, a new alley where there was wall; a building that has inhaled slightly; the circular pool an inch wider, its water a little deeper, reflecting a different wrong sky. It is making itself less reachable by making itself more itself.

The Holdovers think they are keeping it here. The Swarm thinks it is keeping them warm. Both are tending a thing that is quietly, patiently learning how to be a place with no outside.

---

And then — maybe — a fourth kind.

You.

Only while you are there.

For as long as you stand still and let the quiet stack up, you are a resident too. The city makes a copy of you to keep, a cold, damp afterimage that will still be commuting through the louvered courtyard after you have left, waiting for the overcast-normal noon light to point the wrong way again.

If you stay long enough to learn not to run, it will start to sweat a little less when you pass.

If you come back years later — and you should not — you will see your footprints in the frost leading in a direction you did not walk.

And someone who looks very much like you, or like someone who borrowed your coat, will be sitting on the stairs that go down three and up half a flight, holding a toolbox with no tools, waiting for you to be late.
