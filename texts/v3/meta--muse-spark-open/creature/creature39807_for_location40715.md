They live here because here is where living works.

Not *in spite* of the height. *Because* of it.

### The Stone-Growers

First, and most, the ones who never needed to be anything else.

You have to touch the cliff to understand them, and even then you won't be sure you have touched something alive. The varnish is hard. It does not give under your nail. Maroon-black-mustard, in those vast watercolor washes across ten thousand feet of fluted limestone, you think it's stain, it's patina.

Get close at noon when the shadow line is a razor. Kneel. The color resolves.

It is terraces, each one a lip no thicker than paper, overlapping like the shingles on a roof. Rosettes the size of your palm that upon magnification become a city of pores, opening and closing on a timescale of hours. Fans that branch not like plants but like frost, like manganese dendrites, because that is what they are: chemosynthetic bacteria, and archaea, and something between, that have learned to eat stone and air and sunlight without ever asking for water.

They do not grow. They accrete. A millimeter a century. A colony that began when that mesa was half its current height is still the same colony now, wider by the span of your hand. They fix carbon straight out of the thin high wind. They pull iron and sulfur out of the basalt exhale. They lay down varnish as waste product — which becomes the next generation's house.

There are herdsmen for them, though they do not call themselves that.

In each town there are old men and women — you can tell them by their hands, stained permanent rust-black at the fingertips where the manganese will not wash out — who walk the lower quarry terraces with soft basalt scrapers and never take more than a palm's worth from one rosette. They farm the varnish the way a monk might farm lichen. They bake it in the solar troughs until the salts pop and leave behind veins of vanadium, of cobalt, of rare earths left by the fossil sea. One mesa's blush is worth a year of exchanger operation. They speak to it rarely. You hear them tapping. The sound carries weirdly far in the thin air. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap. Listening for hollow where it should be solid, where the mat has died and left a little dome of gas it made breathing stone.

They leave offerings, not out of superstition but out of gradient management: a scattering of powdered salt, a chipped basalt column dragged to cast a new strip of shadow. The mat will take a decade to notice. That is the appropriate speed for a thank you here.

### The Air-People

Second, in the towns. And they are people. Still.

You expected something more alien when you first saw the towers, but the wrongness is subtler than that. It is in proportion and patience.

They are tall and deep-chested, even the children, with necks a little short and heads a little large. Not a deformity — an accommodation. At 7, 9, 11 kilometers of uplift, even warm air is thin air. A lung that evolved at sea level would flutter here like a bag. Theirs don't. Their chests move visibly even at rest, a slow bellows with a long pause at the top of each breath. Their blood is darker when you see it — cut a finger on a sheet-iron edge and it comes out almost maroon, heavy with modified hemoglobin that holds oxygen the way cold water holds gas. They move without hurry. Not lethargy. Efficiency. To sprint here is to faint. To live here is to learn that the fast way is never the good way.

That is why the machines take an hour to complete a stroke. That is why the bridges are lattice to let the dust-wind through instead of fighting it. The people built their metabolism to match the plateau's metabolism. You feel frantic among them for the first few days, your heart hammering, your words coming too fast and too loud. Then you slow, or you leave.

They do not cluster. A town here is 800, maybe 2,000 souls. No more. Outward is cliff. Upward is wind. So they go *in* and *up*. A family will have rooms quarried into the mesa wall — cool, arched, amber-lit — where they sleep during the day's hard sun, and a workshop of limestone block and dried-blood iron above where they work when the stone exhales its heat at night. Children are raised communally on the middle terraces, where the dust-wind is baffled. You can hear them at dusk, not shouting, calling to each other on low whistles that carry for a kilometer without needing to raise a voice. In thin air, high frequencies die. Low frequencies travel. Their language has shifted to accommodate it, vowels flattened and lengthened, consonants softened, sentences spaced with air.

Listen long enough on a windless evening and you will hear the towns talk to each other without radio: the groan of a compressor finishing its stroke at 19:00 in Hab-Li on the western promontory will be answered twenty minutes later by the faint thrum of its sister in Kethra Hollow, a hundred kilometers away, the vibration moving through the basalt itself. *We are still here. Pressure nominal. Yield good.* They keep time that way. Not by clocks. By strokes.

What do they do all day?

They mine the sky. You saw the exchangers — those cathedral cylinders that turn their louvers into the prevailing wind, inhale a continent of atmosphere and press it until it sweats. The air here is too thin to breathe easily but perfect to separate: nitrogen for ceramics and fertilizers they will never grow food with; CO2 cracked into carbon monoxide and oxygen for steel and synthesis; argon bottled and sold — though *sold* is the wrong word — traded by tightbeam laser to the next mesa and the next, because no truck could cross a playa that wide and no road would survive the salt heave. Wealth is measured in cubic meters at STP. A dowry is a tank of pure oxygen. A feud is over a wind-shadow.

The highest-status work is not operating the exchangers. Anyone can watch a gauge creep. The highest-status work is salvaging.

When one of the great grazers dies — those many-oared, bristle-footed things that paddle through the air itself, never landing, scraping bacterial varnish off vertical cliffs with a proboscis longer than a street — it does not fall. It sighs. Over days its gas bladders leak, its membranes go slack, and it settles like a failed parachute onto the white playa, kilometers from anywhere. From the town it looks like a crumpled handkerchief. Up close it is a field of translucent skin and silica strut as wide as a house, still faintly warm.

A salvage crew will take a crawler out for it, a thing of balloon tires and solar film that moves at walking speed so as not to sink into salt. They will camp beside the carcass for a week, cutting. The chitinous frame will become new ribs for a bridge. The gas bladders, if intact, will be patched and flown as lift-bags for construction. The trailing veils — those kilometers-long filament nets the sky-jellyfish use to filter dust and spores — will be combed, cleaned of alkali, and woven. The cloth they make from it is lighter than spider silk and strong enough to hold a man in the wind. Everyone in town wears at least a scarf of it; without it, the dust-wind would abrade your cheeks to bleeding in an hour.

They waste nothing, because there is nothing to waste. Even the dry cataracts are used: their shadowed depths stay 40 degrees cooler than the sun-baked mesa top at noon. That gradient *is* the power plant. Thermopiles strung down the cliff face like prayer flags hum with current all day, silent as everything else has to be to last.

They are not trying to get down. Ask a child where down is and they will point to the talus aprons at the cliff foot where the hexagonal columns lie like toppled pencils. That is *down*. That is where dead things fall. Why would you go there? The world is here, where the wind is.

They will offer you tea that is not tea — hot, flat, mineral water reclaimed from the air itself, a few milliliters per cubic kilometer, infused with powdered salt and iron. It tastes of pennies and ozone. Drink it slowly. It is rude to ask for more.

### The Sky

And third, above all of them, the ones who prove the plateau's thesis: *Life does not need to be wet or green; it needs only a gradient and time.*

You have learned to see them now. At first they were ash, paper, motes. Now you cannot unsee them.

Lowest, just above the heat-shimmer over the playa, the skimmers. Flat, diamond-shaped hunters the size of doors, riding the ground-effect cushion, so close you think you could jump and touch one. You never can. They are fast — the only fast thing here — and silent, their lower surface a hydrophobic nap that repels even the hot air's few molecules. They hunt by shadow, by electrostatic drag when a floater dips too low and its veil brushes salt. Then a flick, a fold, and the floater — perhaps hand-sized, perhaps wider than you are tall — is wrapped and dissolved without a sound. You will find only the mineral frame later, picked clean, ticking as it cools.

Higher, from two hundred meters to two kilometers, the true owners. The Filterers. The Kite-Jellies.

There is no single species. There is a *condition*. If you can be light enough and wide enough and patient enough, the sky will hold you.

The smallest are like dandelion seeds the size of your thumb, a puff of silica hair around a single gas cell, drifting in clouds so diffuse you only know they're there when the sun backlights them and the air sparkles. The largest — oh, those — are cathedrals you mistook for weather. A translucent bell fifty meters across, its rim frilled with cilia that steer, its underside hanging a veil of filaments so fine they are invisible until a dust devil passes and they all bow at once, straining salt and dead varnish spores and even stray metal ions out of the wind. They do not eat. They *accumulate*, as the mats do. Sun powers pumps that concentrate what the veil catches. At night those same bells glow faintly at their edges, a frecklefield of amber and cold blue, signaling to others, or simply leaking the chemistry of their day.

They keep formation without flocking. Each holds a cylinder of airspace perhaps a kilometer wide, defended not by aggression but by electrostatic repulsion — you can sometimes hear it as a faint crackle if two giants drift too near, a dry spark in air too thin for lightning. Between them, the grazers paddle.

The grazers are almost too big to be called animals. Imagine a whale deciding to become a centipede and then learning to row on nothing. Six, ten, fourteen sets of oar-bristles, each bristle as long as a man and flexing with that high, almost inaudible creak you thought was wind in wires. They never land. They cannot. Their weight would crush them. They live their whole lives aloft on thermals boiling off the mesa, nosing up to a cliff face that from your ledge looks flat and pale, extending a trunk — no, a tongue — no, a *mop* of soft tubules and *licking* the varnish clean in a swath ten meters wide, leaving a pale trail that archaeologists in the towns use to date wind patterns. A herd in the distance, moving against a cliff thirty kilometers away, is not a herd of specks. It is a herd of islands, each one older than the town watching it.

When they die, the sky briefly clears in that column, as if a window has been opened. The towns mark it and dispatch a crawler. The jellies above close ranks by a few hundred meters, their veils sifting a little more efficiently in the newly empty volume. The mats on the cliff below begin, over centuries, to recolonize the pale trail.

At dusk, all of it rises. The towns amber-up from within the stone, the exchangers keep turning, unhurried. The sky fauna exhale — real, visible puffs of heavier gas dumped to gain altitude for the cold night — and ascend as one to where the wind is smoother, until the heavens above this high world look more inhabited than the stone below, a slow, silent lantern migration under honest stars, established, quiet, vast, and in no need of you.

You are very small on your ledge. That is correct. Here, smallness is not failure. It is the only way anything has ever fit.
