No one lives here.

That is the first thing you have to understand, and the last thing you will keep forgetting.

Nothing with a face lives here. Nothing with eyes, or legs, or a mouth that opens and closes to eat. Nothing that sleeps. Nothing that looks back at you when you look at it. 

There are no tenants in this city. The city is the tenant.

What lives here is the city itself — not as metaphor, but as biology. A single, distributed, quarreling population of plant-things that were told, a long time ago, *become architecture*, and never stopped trying.

You have to stop thinking of it as trees.

### The Founders Are Still Holding

The oldest residents are the ones who were grown to be place.

They are banyans and ficus and cousins with no lowland name, selected for obedience and longevity, for wood that wants to fuse. You can tell them by their composure. While everything around them thrashes and erupts, they hold their shape.

Their trunks are roads. Not like roads — they *are* roads. A dozen saplings were braided together while green, their cambium coaxed to knit into a single organism with a flat back. They kept growing wider, not taller. The bark on top wore smooth and hard under its own weight and weather, callused into flagstones that are still alive. You can see the grain running lengthwise for a hundred yards. If you put your hand to it, it is warm.

Their roots are walls and railings and colonnades. Aerial roots were trained down like curtains, then thickened, then lignified until they are indistinguishable from pillars, except that they still drink. They still put out shy, bright leaves from their sides, as if remembering they were supposed to be trees.

These are the Basilicas, the Courtyards, the Bridges. They do not think. They endure. Their job is to stay woven while the world tries to pull them apart. They creak constantly under tension, a low, structural groan you feel in your molars. They are dying very slowly, from the inside out, drowning in the water they cannot stop collecting. When one finally gives way — and they do, every hour somewhere out of sight — it does not fall like a tree. It collapses like a building, with a roar of snapping beams and the sudden emptying of all the pools it had been holding on its roofs and balconies for years.

And even as it falls, its seeds — if you can call them that — are already striking. Because the Founders do not reproduce by fruit. There is nothing to eat their fruit. They reproduce by layering. Any branch that touches any other branch for long enough grafts. Any root that finds wet rock roots. The city copies itself by contact.

### The Lungs Live Above You

If the Founders are the bones, the things that live in the canopy are the breath.

You will not see them at first because they are the size of houses and they move so slowly you mistake them for architecture. Then one inhales.

They are pitcher-flowers, or lung-flowers, or something for which you have no word. Fluted cones of fleshy, veined tissue as big as water towers, mottled red and cream, hanging from the scaffold of the upper canopy on vines thick as your torso. They hang closed and still for minutes at a time, gathering heat. Then the seam around their lip loosens, and they sigh open.

It is not wind. There is no wind up here, only this. A vast, warm exhalation rolls down through the leaves, smelling of cinnamon and wet iron and overripe sugar. It carries visible clouds of spores — not dust, but wet, heavy flecks that glitter and then fall, too heavy to stay airborne. So the plant pushes them. It has to. With no birds, no bees, no bats, the forest invented its own weather system.

The whole plateau breathes arrhythmically. One lung to your north exhales while two to your south are inhaling with a soft, hydraulic wheeze. The air shivers. Mist that was hanging still suddenly streams sideways. Leaves flutter without wind. This is how pollen moves here: by appointed gale, from cathedral to cathedral, in warm, spore-thick sighs that make the air briefly unbreathable.

They are not intelligent. They are punctual. They open and close on turgor pressure, on heat, on water weight. But standing under them, you get the terrible feeling of being inside a chest.

### The Light-Thieves Live Between

In the fierce, turbulent thicket between the Basilicas — the wild sea that was never told to be a city — lives the understory that refused to be understory.

Here the residents are small, vicious, and brilliant.

The Lens-Leaves are the most obvious. You spot them by the burn marks. They grow in rosettes the size of doors, translucent as jellyfish, each leaf cupped and filled with clear, filtered water. They do not photosynthesize much themselves. They are hydrologic lenses. They track none of the sun — there is no direct sun, only diffuse, high, cloud-scattered light — and focus it. An inch-wide spot of brutal white light hangs in the steaming air beneath each leaf, wandering slowly as the leaf shifts. Where that spot touches a rival vine, the rival scorches, blisters, withdraws. You can hear the faint hiss where they work. The ground beneath a grove of them is speckled with blackened circles, as if the forest has cigarette burns.

Next to them, the Dissolvers. They look innocent: pale green filaments, fine as hair, waving from the bark of otherwise ordinary lianas. They are not roots. They are not vines. They are contact-digesters. When the filament brushes a neighbor's bark, it adheres and exudes acid. Not much. Just enough to soften, to make a wound that weeps sugars. The filament thickens there, drinks. It does not kill quickly. It farms a sore. You can see whole columns ringed with these pale halos, slowly being girdled by what looks like Spanish moss. The air around them smells faintly of vinegar.

And everywhere, the Seekers: the pale, wet, fast tips. While the Founders groan, the Seekers sprint. Shoots with no chlorophyll at first, blind white asparagus spears, nosing forward at inches per hour — fast enough that if you stand still and stare, you can see them move. They quiver in the air, tasting humidity, tasting chemical gradients, tasting shade. When two tips meet mid-air, they coil instantly, fuse, thicken, and begin to lignify into a new arch, a new rail, a new accidental bridge. Most of these bridges go nowhere. Most are strangled within a week by Lens-Leaves or Dissolvers. Some make it.

This is the turbulence you hear. Not growth as metaphor for slow time. Growth as weather. Crack, pop, groan, sigh, splash. A trunk splitting. A new graft popping into tension like a gunshot. A leaf-basin tipping.

### The Water Itself Is Alive

And in every cupped leaf the size of a swimming pool, in every cistern held in the elbow of a braided road, in the endless weeping sheets down the fresh, bubbled basalt that never had time to become soil — there is the last, smallest population.

Mats of algae, of cyanobacteria, of biofilm slick as oil, red and green and black. They are the only things here that do not try to be big. They live on light and steam and dissolved rock. They make the slick that makes every step treacherous. They tint the waterfalls pink. They are food for nothing. They die and become peat in the same puddle they lived in, and the next generation of Seekers roots through them.

They are the forest's memory of an ocean.

---

Nothing here knows you are here. No leaf turned to you. No lung timed its breath for you.

You are the only animal on a mountain that was made to be a city without animals, high in the hot clouds where rain never starts or stops. The rock beneath you is still young enough to cut your hands. The bridge beneath your feet is still trying to become a wider bridge. The courtyard below you is already being unmade by something pushing up through it.

The inhabitants will not make room for you.

They will simply continue — holding, breathing, burning, dissolving, seeking, dripping — until the basalt cools and softens, or until the clouds finally wring themselves dry, or until the weight of their own water finally pulls every last grown cathedral down into the thicket, where it will be taken apart by filaments and lenses and fast white tips, and grown over, and grown again.
