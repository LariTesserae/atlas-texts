There is nothing that lives *in* it.

That is the first mistake. You look for an animal in a forest, a bird in a canopy, and you don't find it - or you find too much of it, and it doesn't hold still long enough to be counted.

The plateau is not a habitat. It is an organism that has mistaken itself for a landscape, and everything you see moving is an organ that has mistaken itself for an animal.

### At first, you think: insects.

Because the scale is wrong and the number is obscene and there is no vertebrate logic to hold it together. No packs, no herds, no solos. Only braids.

Look closer at the ground between the fin-trees, where the black felt-moss steams:

The kiln-runners. They are not ants, but you will call them ants until your eyes learn better. Fist-sized, matte black, with heads too heavy - they are walking crucibles. Their mandibles do not bite; they *clamp*. They harvest the basalt itself, rasping still-warm stone into dust with radulae lined in silica teeth, carrying it in semi-molten pellets held in a ventral pouch where it glows dull orange through their translucent bellies. They run in silent, braided rivers - not pheromone trails, but heat trails, following the warmest line of moss to the mound-kilns.

And the mounds: low, pitted domes that breathe. They are not nests. They are *the* animal, too - sessile, colonial, exhaling sulfur steam. The runners feed them dust and the mounds sinter it, with a wet, patient hiss, into ceramic beads, scales, struts, lattices. Then other runners - the same species, a different caste, a different age, it doesn't matter - carry the cooled product away to be snapped together somewhere else. Nothing is ever finished. You will see a perfect arch of interlocked ceramic standing alone in a clearing, humming with retained heat, with no purpose, until a different braid of runners dismantles it within the hour and carries the same beads to a chasm edge to begin a tower.

Nothing owns its work.

Above them is the air, which is more crowded than the ground.

The clink-moths. Blankets of them, when they move. Each wing is tiled not with powder but with actual scales of metal - iron, copper, nickel - shed and regrown daily. When they fly, which they do rarely and badly, they chime. They do not eat nectar. They feed on vapor. They hang beneath the giant cooling fins of the fin-trees with proboscises unspooled into the pores, drinking the distilled volatiles - hot oxygen, hot sweet hydrocarbons - and their bellies glow amber with it. When a fin-tree's pressure-fig discharges, they flock to the steam cloud and beat it with their wings until the spores stick to their metallic dust, carrying them miles before grooming them off into a new felt-mat.

The wire-wrights. You thought they were dragonflies. They have the same impossible hover, the same territorial dart. But their bodies are segmented tubes of living glass, and you can see the fluid moving inside, carrying dissolved silica to the tip of their abdomen where it is extruded. Not silk. *Wire.* Fine, hot copper and glass fiber, still soft when laid. They don't build nests. They stitch. They stitch epiphyte to vine to fin to air, leaving tangles of warm wire that thrums in the haze. Other things use it - beetles with resin feet walk it, fungi climb it, current runs through it mysteriously when condensation drips. The wrights never tend what they've made. They just make more, until the tangle shorts or melts or is torn down by the next scaffold-rush.

The resin-slugs. Slow, warm, the size of dogs, leaving behind not slime but a bead of quick-cure amber that hardens in seconds. They seem purposeless until you realize every new scaffold starts along a slug-trail. The trail is the blueprint, or the foundation, or the invitation. It says: *build here now*.

### Then you think: no, not insects. A single thing.

Because none of them fight. That's what starts to unsettle you.

A vertebrate forest is murder. This one is a workshop at midnight after the foreman has left. A furred thing like a lemur with six jointed fingers and eyes like wet coal hammers ceramic beads into place beside a chitinous thing like a mantis made of black glass that welds them with a brief, blue arc from its mouth. A slug lays resin across both their feet and they step out of it without annoyance. A bird with no beak - just a hollow tube face - lands and regurgitates a wad of half-living moss onto the structure and leaves.

They are not species cooperating. They are *hands*.

The towers and lungs and cathedrals that throw themselves up in minutes - the scaffold that becomes a breathing tower of interlocked leaves and then is swallowed by the basalt - are not built by any of them. They are built *through* them. The forest extrudes animals the way your body extrudes blood cells: briefly, for a function, then resorbs or discards.

If you catch one - and you can, they are docile in the way a finger is docile - you will find it is unfinished. Open its belly and you find not organs but half-sintered pellets, wire, resin, still warm. Open the black-felt moss and you find it is threaded through with fungal hyphae as thick as rope, pulsing heat, connecting the root of every fin-tree to every kiln-mound to every resin-slug trail. Cut a fin-tree open and steam screams out, and the wound knits in hours, but the tree does not bleed sap; it bleeds runners, clumsy, wet, newborn, who stumble out and immediately begin carrying.

They are not individuals. They are detachable, mobile enzymes. Prototypes. The place is trying shapes out.

That is why a species here does not go extinct. It is *recalled*. You will see a glorious thing - a glider with four wings of living stained glass, trailing copper wire like prayer flags - for one afternoon only, perfect, and then never again. Its niche was filled, or the idea was bad, or the plateau exhaled differently. In the place where it hunted, tomorrow there will be something else entirely that does the same job with a different body, as if the same thought was re-rendered.

### And underneath them, it.

You feel it finally at night, when the rainless rain beads on everything and the whole continent of steam sighs at once.

There is no queen. No brain. No mycelial godhead with a plan.

There is only the heat. The young rock, two miles high, still cooling from when the world poured it. It holds an impossible amount of energy, and energy doing work looks, from a distance, like intention.

The plateau breathes out, the dim amber air drinks, the fin-trees fractionate, the moss seals, the kiln-mounds fire. It is a convection cell the size of a country, a heat engine that learned to grow hands to assemble itself faster. It is lush *because* it is industrial, not despite it. The only way to dump that much heat at that altitude without cooking is to turn it into complexity - to fold it into leaves and wire and ceramic and briefly-alive things that carry heat away with them when they fall into chasms.

So it lives by endlessly becoming.

If you stay still long enough on the basalt that thrums under your palm, you will feel the negotiation in the stone itself. The gravity that lets water hang as spheres, the sound that arrives late, the flower pollinated by its own future - those are not glitches the inhabitants adapt to.

They are the inhabitants adapting *ahead* of physics. The plateau is so busy manufacturing itself that it manufactures its own rules, badly, and then lives inside the error because the error is warm.

You are not being watched here. There are ten million eyes - metallic, resinous, glassy - but none of them belong to anyone who can watch.

You are standing on the warm back of something vast and young and sleepless that does not know you are there, because it does not yet know that it is there. It only knows how to work.
