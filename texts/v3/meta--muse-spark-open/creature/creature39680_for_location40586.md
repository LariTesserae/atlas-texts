They live low and close together and apart all at once.

At first you think no one lives here at all — that maybe the Basin was evacuated and left to steam. Then you see movement in a window on the third floor where no light is on, and movement where no window is, and you realize the movement *is* the inhabitants. They haven't left. They've learned to take up less visual space.

### The People

There are people. Fewer than the buildings were built for, but enough to keep the brick from deciding it is alone.

You see them at shift change because that's when they consent to be seen. Otherwise they are behind walls, under pipe bridges, inside the dim amber light of the waterworks and bottling plants and abattoirs that never properly close because you cannot turn off water that comes up through the floor.

They are not pale the way you'd expect from people who live under cloud. They are flushed, damp-skinned, with the permanent faint shine of people who are always a little sweaty and have stopped wiping it away. Their clothes are thin, practical, darkened at the collar and cuffs by mineral water that never quite washes out. White cotton goes yellow-gray here in a week. Everyone owns rubber boots, even clerks. Everyone owns a bicycle with a chain that rusts if you look at it.

They move economically. No one jogs in the Basin. No one hurries unless a valve needs closing. Children learn early to walk in the shade of a wall, to stand under a pipe when the midge cloud rises, to never, ever put their hands in a canal without looking first — not because something will bite them, but because there will already be something there, feeding.

They are not loud people. The air is too heavy for loudness, and loudness would be rude anyway. They talk in low voices in the row houses where families have lived vertically for generations — grandparents on the second floor where it's cooler, parents on the third, kids on the fourth under the eaves where the pigeons coo through the walls like a second plumbing system. At night you can hear through the brick: not words, but the tide of rats in the cavity and the murmur of a radio left on for company, and a child coughing a little from the warm air that is always a little too wet to be air.

They work with water more than against it. The women who run the laundry lines strung across alleys know by feel when the air will wring itself out on its own and save them the trouble. The men who tend the elevated pipe arteries walk their lengths with wrenches that have been worn smooth, tapping for the dull note that means scale building up inside, bleeding off steam with a practiced quarter-turn. Old people keep shrines of sorts: not religious, exactly — a corner of travertine left unpainted where the seep makes a perfect miniature terrace, white and rust, that they tend with a toothbrush. To mark that water wins and they have made their peace with winning.

They are not numerous, but they are *continuous*. Like the pigeons. You will never find yourself alone on a street. Two blocks over there will be someone hosing down a loading bay, someone carrying a crate of live crabs that clack faintly, someone standing still on a bridge watching the canal run pink after the tannery sluice, waiting for the catfish to come. They nod to each other without stopping. There is too much shared work to be lonely, and too much heat to be sociable.

### The Birds That Are Citizens

But if you asked the Basin who its most successful residents are, it would not say people.

It would say pigeons.

They are everywhere people are not, and everywhere people are. They cover roof lines so completely that from the rim the city looks furry. Not in flocks that arrive and leave — in a permanent, shifting carpet that lifts in sections and resettles, like breathing. Their coo is not birdsong. It is background, low and liquid, the sound of the city digesting.

They have learned the industrial schedule better than the workers. When the flour mill vents at 6:14, they are on the sills before the dust settles. When a bottling plant rinses bottles, they drink from the gutter in orderly lines. The guano is not just mess; it is infrastructure. It streaks the buildings three stories high and dries and flakes and feeds the white worms that live in the mortar, which feed the matte-black beetles the size of a thumb joint that click across the mineral pans at noon when nothing else will. The people sweep it from their balconies every morning without comment. It will be back by evening.

Gulls live here too, which shouldn't make sense — the sea is unimaginably far — but they came for the hot flats and stayed because a landfill of fish heads and slaughter offal is as good as a tide. During the day they wheel high under the low cloud deck that caps the Basin like a lid, crying once, sharply, and then silencing themselves to save moisture. In the evenings they stand in the shallow mineral mirrors, one leg tucked, looking like they have always lived inland.

Herons stand in the canals. Not a few. A heron every fifty meters, each claimed a concrete outfall, each stained orange to the knee by the iron water. They are utterly still, and so the teeming does not disturb them. A catfish will slide past a heron's leg — those pale, whiskered catfish, fat and slow from eating what the abattoirs bleed into the water — and the heron will not move until it is supposed to move. Then a strike, quick as a pipe hissing, and resume stillness. The people walking over the canal bridges barely look down. A heron taking a catfish is as routine as a worker clocking in.

At dusk the swifts come, and then the bats. You don't see them leave their roosts — the hollow upper floors of warehouses where the windows are filmed with condensation — you just suddenly realize the air above the alleys is full of them, a silent, flickering ribbon pouring between buildings, eating the mosquitoes and the midges that rose off the bacterial mats in that hour when the air cools by a fraction of a degree and decides it can hold no more water. The bats are small, quick, numerous beyond counting. They lodge behind shutters in the row houses and the children know not to bother them; bats mean fewer bites.

### The Things Under and Between

The real population, the one that makes the quiet feel full, is not counted in individuals.

It is the chitinous floor.

Roaches live here the way people do in other cities — as commuters. Matte black, dry-bodied unlike the damp that surrounds them, they keep to the drains and wall cavities by day and boil out at pressure shifts in such quick, orderly streams that you could think it was water if you weren't looking. The people stamp once, not out of disgust, just to reroute them, and keep walking.

Dragonflies as long as your forearm patrol the alleys in straight lines, territorial as foremen, their wings ticking. They hunt the midges, which are the true base of everything. The midges rise off the warm bacterial mats — that film on the mineral pans that is not a plant, not an algae exactly but a sheet of living chemistry, red and green and sulfur-yellow, shivering with color — in clouds so dense they dim the already-dim noon further. When they rise, the city holds its breath. Then the dragonflies cut through, the swifts cut through, the pigeons snap at them from ledges, and within minutes the cloud is thinned and the air is breathable again.

Moths cover every streetlamp, not fluttering but clinging, thousands per bulb, until the light is a furry brown glow that barely reaches the wet asphalt. Beetles trundle through the salt crust. Crabs — pale, mineral-crusted, slow — pick sideways through the hot shallow pans between lots, feeding on whatever the bacterial film didn't want. And in the canals under it all, the worms, the leeches that have learned to tolerate water almost too salty to drink, the catfish eggs stuck like pearls to concrete.

None of them are rare. That is what unnerves visitors who expect a desert logic: one hardy specialist eking out a living. The Basin does not do rarity. It does *plenty*. Everything that can live here does, in numbers that smooth out into abundance. Predator and prey pass each other on the same hot curb — rat and heron, beetle and roach, catfish and man with a hose — on different schedules, with agreed hours.

### The Hollow Itself

And then there is the inhabitant that is not a species at all.

If you stand on a canal bridge at dusk, when the steam is rising thick enough to hide the rim and the pipes are all ticking as they contract and the pigeons have gone quiet at once and the bats haven't yet come out, you feel it: the Basin itself lives.

Not metaphorically. The travertine growing under the pavement, millimeter by millimeter, lifting the concrete. The salt crusts expanding and cracking. The water breathing in and out of the limestone — seep, condense, drip, evaporate, condense again — a slow lung. The brick softening and re-hardening with mineral.

It holds them all — the people in their sweating row houses, the pigeons on the ledges, the herons in the canals, the tide of rats in the walls, the film on the pans — not as guests, but as parts of a way of being organized that has been stable for a long time and intends to stay stable.

Small and plentiful, the rule says. Crack-dwelling, hollow-loving, wet-tolerant, heat-tolerant, mineral-tolerant. Humans qualify. Barely, but they qualify, so they are allowed to remain, bicycles hissing, wrenches tapping, laundry snapping damply in air too thick to be wind, part of the quiet, endless, teeming balance that has no need to announce itself.

You thought emptiness was absence. Here, emptiness is spacing, so everyone can fit.
