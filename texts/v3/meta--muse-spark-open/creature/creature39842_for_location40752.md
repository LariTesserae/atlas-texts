They live by negotiation. Nothing here can be owned, only bargained with for a few hours at a time.

### The Small Gods

**The Chorus** - you hear them before you understand they are alive. 

Millions of them. Fist-sized, amber-skinned, hanging like fruit from the underside of every vine. They are not quite animal, not quite plant. Their backs are photosynthetic shingles, matte grey-green, warm to the touch. Their bellies are a translucent throat-sac, stretched taut over a resonant fiber drum. They do nothing but drink light, drink air, and sing. One note each, held for minutes at a time, powered by the slow contraction of wax-muscles around an internal heat engine. Alone, it is mournful. Together, they build that deafening pressure-field you felt on the ropes - a chord so dense it becomes texture.

They are the clock of the rift. When the sun shaft moves, their pitch shifts. When the wind changes, they compensate. The other fauna tune to them. The people do, too. When the Chorus stutters, something big is growing or dying nearby, changing the tension of the mat.

Cut one open and you find no blood. Just layered paper lungs and a coil of coppery fiber where a heart should be, still twitching, still trying to resonate even on your palm.

**The Mirror Swarms** - Elongate beetle-things, hand-long, with no mouth you can see. Their elytra are not just shiny; they *are* mirrors, perfect, flat, vacuum-deposited wafers of biological glass. They spend the day in swirling, screaming flocks in the updraft, flashing noon light back down into the canopy depths, farming the plants beneath them. They are shepherds. At dusk, they descend and crawl, leg-by-leg, over the leaves they illuminated, licking off the thin bloom of resin-sugars with a tongue like felt.

When threatened, the swarm turns as one and becomes a strobe, redirecting sun into your eyes until you are blind. The locals wear their shed elytra as signal mirrors, as knives, as earrings. You find them everywhere, untarnished, long after the animal that made them has been eaten.

**The Kites** - If the beetles are the day-shift, these are the night. You think they are dead leaves caught in the thermal, until you see them change direction. Dry, veined, triangular things with four wings set at right angles, no head, no tail, just a central slit-mouth that is always open. They ride. They never flap. They surf the womb-warm air rising out of the rift, mouths agape, filtering the ash-seed clouds that burst hourly from the puff-plants. Each one carries a static charge, so seeds stick to their wings like iron filings, then fall off kilometers away when the charge bleeds out.

They squeal. Not a metaphor. The air whistling through their slit-mouths at speed makes a high, thin keen that adds the top octave over the Chorus. Children of the rift can call them down by humming that exact note with cupped hands.

**The Weavers and the Worms** - The engine room.

The fiber-worms are as thick as your thumb and as long as your arm, pale as candle wax, completely blind. They chew. That is their entire theology. They chew the waxy wood, the resin, the dead leaf-paper, even the basalt if it's salted enough, and excrete perfect, cold, structural thread. Their tunnels are everywhere - a three-dimensional subway system that holds the whole mat together for the brief hours before it grows over them again. Leave your pack on the ground for ten minutes and you will come back to find it laced to the floor with white guy-lines, a worm politely incorporating it into the neighborhood.

The weavers are what come after. Spider-things but not spiders - six legs, no abdomen, just two huge resin-glands on their forearms. They follow the worms and plaster their tunnels with a hot, quick-hardening lacquer that smells of cinnamon and solder. They follow *you* and try to do the same to your boots, your hair, your outstretched hand. Not malice. Manners. Loose things must be tacked down. Unincorporated things are rude.

You learn to keep moving and to thank them gently when they try.

Other things flicker at the edge of taxonomy:

- Things like tumbleweeds made of jointed legs that roll uphill.
- Things like hummingbird moths whose wings are living solar panels, vibrating so fast they are a blur, whose larvae are indistinguishable from leaf buds.
- Things that look exactly like translucent amber leaves until they bite, and then look like leaves again while they digest you from the inside with silicone oil.

None of them drink. Ever. They are sealed systems. They trade water molecules back and forth like heirlooms, never losing one to the air if they can help it. To spill is to die. To leak is to be devoured for your humidity.

### The People of the Scab

They are not *of* here, not originally. No one evolves in a place that only existed for a geological blink. They came down from the pan, generations ago, because the pan killed everything slower.

Now they are lean and loud and lacquered.

You know them by the adaptations they have made without changing their bodies much at all:

Their skin is dark, dry, polished-looking. They oil themselves daily with the refined sap - that low-volatility silicone resin - until they shine. It seals them. Without it, they would desiccate in hours in the womb-warm updraft. With it, they can sweat *inside* their own sealed layer and reabsorb it. They smell faintly of hot plastic and cloves.

Their ears are always plugged with soft amber wax, except for a single small horn of hammered mirror-elytra they hold to the side of their head to hear each other speak over the Chorus. Their language is clipped, percussive, designed to cut through drone - mostly consonants, hand taps on the resonant leaves for emphasis. They don't whisper; whispers don't exist here.

Their eyes are rimmed red, not from disease but from seed-fluff. They wear close-woven masks of feather-fern fiber when they work, which is always. Work is tending.

Because they have learned the only true skill this forest allows: *you don't cut, you coax.*

An old woman will sit for an hour with a questing vine-tip the thickness of a finger, breathing on it, warming it with her hands, dabbing it with a paste of ground salt and worm-spit, convincing it to turn left instead of right, to become a lintel instead of a noose. A boy will tend a singing bulb-throat the way you would tend a goat, moving it to a better-lit vine, shushing it when its note wavers. They farm air. They have strung nets of hair-fine glass fiber - harvested from a plant that extrudes it at night - across the thermal columns to condense what little moisture rises from their own breath and bodies at night, then wring it into clay pots where it is immediately sealed under a layer of oil. One pot of true water is a family's wealth for a month, used only to soften food or to anoint a newborn's mask.

They live in clusters of three or four basket-huts, lashed to whatever part of the mat is most stable *today*. The huts have no floors except the living mat, no foundations. When the mat buckles at night - and it always does, groaning, popping, lifting a whole hillside a meter by morning - they roll up their walls, carry their fire-stones, and walk to where it has settled.

Their children are fearless in a way that looks like insanity to you. A toddler will fall into a chew-tunnel and be gone from sight, and the mother will not panic, because she knows the worms will push the child out somewhere else, lacquered but unharmed, and everyone will laugh over the clicks. They learn early not to be still, to pat the weavers away, to never swat at the Kites. To be loud.

They do not think of the rift as hostile. They speak of it as a body that arrived and is still learning how to be a body - restless, itchy, overgrowing. They call it The Scab, or The Stitch. They believe - or they act as if - that if they keep tending it, moving with it, giving it shape, it will one day finish growing and settle, and then there will be soil, and then there will be quiet.

Until then, they live inside a drum, oiled and masked and shouting kindly to each other, stitching their own clothes to the world and then carefully unstitching them again before the world decides to keep them.
