They were not the first thing to live here. But they will be the last to ever need to.

In that hot, sealed dark — where the light is the rock's own cooling and the water never becomes water — nothing else can hold on. No bacteria mat, no lichen, no mold. It's too new, too dry, too sharp. The chamber is still being born, still cracking itself open as the young mountain shudders and swells. There is no soil, no soft edge, nowhere to hide.

And so what lives here decided not to hide.

At first you don't see them as animals. In the 0.5 light, against the coal-glow of the basalt, they look like texture. Like the wall is blistering. A pavement, a shingling, a living scale-mail stretched over every surface until no stone shows through.

Only when your eyes adjust to the amber and the iron-red do you see the seams. The overlap. The slow, geological creep.

They are beetle-things only because your brain has no better word. Each one is about the length of the last joint of your thumb, flat enough to slip under a fingernail if it wanted to, heavy for its size. The carapace is not shell so much as slag — matte black, pitted, heat-tempered, unreflective except at the joints where it thins and the furnace inside shows through. No eyes, no antennae in any familiar sense. The head is all tool: a blunt, shovel-shaped plate with two inward-curving, iron-black mandibles underneath, more like the jaws of a mining cutter than a mouth.

This is *Ferrivora* — if you needed a name. The furnace-millers.

They don't move like insects. There is no skitter. Dynamic 0.8. Each individual advances perhaps a body-length an hour, if that. A *tik-tik-tik* of six spined legs finding purchase on raw grus, a long pause, and then —

*hiss* —

a vent along the back seam opens and sighs out a jet of spent gas, hot and acrid with ozone and volatilized sulfur. The air shimmers over them in aggregate, a low haze of exhaust that makes the chamber hard to breathe.

*thunk.*

The mandibles strike. Not a bite. A chip. A cold-chisel impact against fresh basalt and feldspar that you feel in your molars because the stone conducts it. A flake no bigger than a grain of rice is sheared off and passed under the body into the ventral furnace.

You can see it glow. As each one feeds, light blooms from within — not bioluminescence, but true heat. The gut is a kiln. Lithivorous, thermophilic, chemolithotrophic in a way that barely qualifies as biology anymore. They take the just-born rock, with all its trapped reduced iron and sulfides and unoxidized minerals, and they fire it. What they want is not calories as you understand them, but chemical potential: the oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+, the stripping of sulfur. The mountain is fuel because it hasn't finished rusting yet.

And then they step forward and leave the product behind.

A single, glassy pellet. Black, perfectly formed, still ticking as it cools. It is slag and frass and egg and ash all at once. They do not build with it. Built is none. There is no nest, no tunnel, no waxen cell, no queen. They don't pile the pellets into walls. The floor is simply inches deep in them — a loose, clinking scree of industrial waste that rolls underfoot, that holds the day's heat from thousands of tiny guts. If you sift it, some pellets are hollow and some rattle with a dry spore inside, waiting for a fresh crack to open somewhere else in the range. But most are just waste. Just proof of work done.

This is what Tech: Industrial means here. Not that they have tools. That they *are* tools. Each animal is a self-contained, chitin-housed stamping mill: intake hopper in front, blast furnace in the middle, exhaust vent and extruder in back. Their legs have evolved not for speed but for torque, for clamping to near-vertical hot stone while the head hammers. Their backs are ribbed like boiler plate to dump heat. They don't communicate, they don't fight over territory. There is no territory. At density 2.9, there is only tiling. Back to back, edge to edge, a living caulk that keeps the chamber from closing.

Because that is their ecology. The chamber is embryonic geology trying to anneal itself shut under pressure. The hot rock creeps inward, fresh mineral veins exhaling into the void. And they eat it as fast as it forms. They are not inhabitants of the pocket; they are the reason the pocket persists. A bio-mechanical equilibrium. Without them, it would be solid in a decade. With them, it remains a lung, four meters by three meters, forever.

Listen to them in aggregate and you understand Sound 2.4. One animal is nearly silent — tik... hiss... thunk. A thousand of them, out of phase, become a low, dense roar. No chirp, no drone. A factory floor heard through stone. A thrum that lives in your chest cavity. You can hear the chamber working. You can hear the mountain being slowly, patiently milled by mouths.

If you stand still, the pavement will flow over your boots. Not an attack. Recognition fails. To a blind, heat-feeling miller, warm leather on warm slag is just another patch of wall that needs testing. They will clamp, *tunk* once against the sole, taste iron-poor organic carbon, deem it not-rock, and shunt around you, leaving a neat, soot-black kiss of exhaust soot on the toe. They do not care. Weirdness 0.4 — this is not a monstrous or alien scene, once you sit with it. It is terribly logical. Hot dry rock with chemical energy in it *should* be eaten by something flat, slow, blind, armored, and deafening. Something that turns geology into industry.

They have no predators. Nothing else can stand the dry kiln heat, the metallic air, the starvation of real water. They have no need for sociality. They meet only to exchange spores — two venting undersides pressed together for a long moment in the dark, a cloud of microscopic, glass-coated cysts passing in the steam — and then they part, and continue tiling forward one slow body-length per hour, mining the world as it is made.

When you leave, squeezing back out into the alpine cold where wind smooths everything and water does all the work, you take the sound with you. That *tik-hiss-thunk* that never synchronized. Behind you, in the dark with no sun, the pavement continues its shift. Covering every surface. Eating the mountain to keep its room empty.
