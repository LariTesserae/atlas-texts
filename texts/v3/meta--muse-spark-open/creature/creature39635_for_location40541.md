They live close. That is the first thing about them.

Not close the way families are close, or friends. Close the way stones in a wall are close. The way teeth are close. There is no space left between them and everything else, so they have grown to fit the exact negative shape of their neighbors.

### The People

You would mistake them at first for just another texture in the town - because they want to be.

They are short. Not dramatically - you wouldn't call them dwarfs - but compressed by generations of low doors and shoulder-width alleys and roofs you have to tend on your knees. Broad through the back and ankle, narrow through the shoulders. They move sideways often. They have learned to turn their feet out.

Their clothes are silence made textile: thick felt from the dwarf reindeer and the hares, stuffed with eiderdown and moss fibers, dyed in the only colors the bowl can make without import - lichen-rust, peat-black, crowberry-juice pink, saxifrage white, a deep fern green that is almost black. Everything is patched. Nothing is discarded. A child's coat is an adult's coat taken in and in and in until the felt is soft as skin. Hoods are large, furred with hare, framing round faces that are wind-burned even though the wind never reaches the bottom.

Their faces are the only thing that still moves quickly. In a place with no loud speech, the face has taken over. Eyes - pale grey, pale blue, made watery by the relentless bright light - that flick and hold, that ask permission to pass in an alley with a single narrowing. Mouths that smile tightly, without teeth, because cold air hurts teeth. Hands that never stop. That is how you know them for people and not just another slow animal: their hands are always doing two things at once.

One hand mending, one hand holding. One hand harvesting crowberries one by one from a vertical wall while the other steadies a child on a hip. One hand passing a bundle of birch roots to a neighbor without looking, because the neighbor's hand was already there to receive it.

They do not speak above a breath. At first you think they are mute. Then you realize they have a language tuned to the acoustic of moss. It is clicks of tongue against teeth, exhales through the nose, the tap of a bone needle against a peat brick meaning *careful*, the double pat on a felt shoulder meaning *I have it now*. When they must speak in words, they lean in until foreheads nearly touch and make the word inside the small, warm pocket of air between their mouths, so it never has to travel.

They have no leaders. No need. Leadership is a structure for managing scarcity or surplus and surprise, and they have had none of those for longer than their oral memory - which is not long, because nothing changes enough to need remembering. Instead they have positions, the way a body has positions. The Gutter-Menders, who know the fall of every mica roof by heart. The Hare-Keepers, whose hutches are under their own beds. The Cistern-Watchers, who dip a finger in each barrel at dawn and know by coldness how much they can draw that day. The Moss-Trimmers, perhaps the most respected, who crawl along the walls with stone scrapers and keep the town from breathing itself shut - cutting back exactly as much as grew overnight, no more, no less.

Children are few and accounted for. A child is born - quietly, in steam - and a space is already waiting: a hutch emptied by an old hare, a wall-niche re-lined with down, a name that was used two generations ago and has been resting. They are carried constantly, strapped to chests in felt slings, because there is nowhere to set them down where they won't be in the way. They learn early not to cry loudly. A loud cry in the bowl is physically uncomfortable - the heavy air pushes it back down your throat. So they whimper like kittens, and are immediately picked up.

The old do not get old the way people outside do. They get smaller, smoother, more weathered, until their faces have the same bearded look as the moraine stones. When they die, it is also quiet. They are not buried - there is no ground to spare - they are given to the peat. Cut into the wall of their own house, wrapped in felt, packed with crowberry and moss that will take root in the nutrients. A day later the wall is green again, indistinguishable. No marker. The house remembers the shape.

Because above all, they are not individuals packed together. They are a packing.

### The Animals That Have Agreed

None of the animals here are wild. None are domesticated in the way you think of it. They have co-adapted to the same density.

The **dwarf reindeer** - knee-high, barrel-bodied, coats the color of dirty snow and lichen - live on the roofs. They do not roam. Each has a roof-meadow, perhaps three meters by three meters, of lichen and dwarf willow that it mows in a slow circle all day, tethered by a sinew braid that is never untied because it never needs to be. Their antlers are stunted, velveted year-round, used not for fighting but to scrape icicles from the north wall. They are warm. In winter, people sleep huddled against them under a shared felt.

The **snow hares** in the under-stair hutches are not for meat. Not often. They are for heat and for felt and for the quiet company of a nose that twitches. They are bred to be deaf to sound and alive to vibration; they thump only with their soft front paws on peat, a signal the whole alley can feel through the walkway.

The **birds** - bullfinches with rose-red breasts too bright for this pale place, long-tailed tits like cotton balls with long handles - do not sing. Or rather, they sing above and below your hearing. What you see is choreography. A flock of thirty will pass from one birch-wall to another in perfect silence, each flash of black-and-white wingbar a syllable. They eat the springtails and the dwarf birch seeds and they are eaten - occasionally - by the people, caught in fine willow nets, but so rarely that they have lost their fear. One might land on your hood and peer at you, head cocked, breathing visibly.

Under everything, the **lower town**: woodlice the size of your thumbnail, slate-grey and glossy, patrolling the damp undersides of walkways in armies, cleaning what falls. Pale springtails that puff like dust when you lift a board. And in every half-barrel cistern and stone basin, the **blind fish**. Small as minnows, translucent white-pink, eyes filmed over generations ago because there is nothing to see in peat-dark water. They rise and mouth the surface without a ripple, taking mosquito larvae, and are watched by children for hours. Never harvested.

And the **bumblebees**. You only notice them when they land on you. Big, slow, the exact grey-white of reindeer fur, furred so thickly they seem knitted. They move from saxifrage blossom to saxifrage blossom with a lethargy that looks like swimming. If you put your ear very close, you feel more than hear their buzz - a deep, bone-conducted hum at the limit of perception. They are the only thing that pollinates in a place with no wind, and the people treat them with a particular reverence: if one dies on a walkway, it is picked up on a leaf and set gently in a patch of moss.

### The Heart

They all lean toward it. Not worship. Orientation.

At the very bottom of the bowl, where the town's density finally has to leave a gap because water is pushing up, is the well-head. A circle of dark water maybe a meter across, lined with smooth, water-black stones that no one remembers placing. The water in it is not rain. It is impossibly clear and it is cold in a different way - not the pooled cold of the bowl, but the deep, pressurized cold of the earth.

And in it, affixed to the stone below the surface, is the colony.

You called it white, fronded growth - not plant, not animal - and that is right but incomplete. In the bright, trapless light you can see each frond is translucent, veined with a faint pink like the inside of a lip, tipped with a polyp that opens and closes. Not quickly. On the scale of breaths. Open for a minute, closed for a minute, open again. Collectively, they make a slow pulse. If you hold your hand just above the water you feel it: a faint, humid warmth rising, despite the cold. A smell like iron and milk.

It does nothing that you can see. It does not feed them. It does not speak.

But everything is measured from it. The water that beads on every leaf and peat brick - the dew that is not dew but their shared breath condensing - is warmest nearest the pool and coldest at the rim. The moss grows thickest toward it. The old, when they sit, sit facing it. The hares in their hutches, if given choice, face it. The bumblebees, when they rest, rest with their heads toward it.

The people do not have a word for it that you would translate as god or king or engine. When you press - forehead to forehead, whispering - an old Gutter-Mender will eventually make a sound for you, annoyed that you need a sound: *mmma* - the sound of something held in the mouth. The thing that holds the water. The thing that holds the cold. The thing that we arrange around because it was here first and it is not done.

They do not tend it. They do not need to. They know, in the way you know not to put your hand on your own heart and squeeze, that as long as it pulses - open, closed, open - the equilibrium holds. The moss will grow exactly as much as they trim. The cisterns will fill exactly halfway. The light will be too bright and the air will be too still and the silence will hold like ice over everything, perfect and entire unto itself.

They live around it like petals do not know they are petals.
