What lives here is what can live on wet stone and warm air.

Not much, by the count of kinds. Everything, by the count of bodies.

### The People

There are people the way there is water — not as visitors, but as a condition of the place. They have been here long enough to stop being new to it.

They are not a hardy mountain people in the way lowlanders imagine mountain people. No ruddy cheeks, no thick furs. If anything they are pale and a little softened, the way things get that never fully dry. Their hair takes on the air. Their skin smells faintly of iron no matter how often they wash, which is often — everyone washes constantly here, with water that is always close and always warmish from the rock. Laundry is a daily sacrament. Hands are clean, nails are clipped short because of the isopods. Everyone learns as a child not to leave food out, not because of shame but because in ten minutes it will be found.

They are short to medium, mostly, broad in the chest and ankle. Not by genetics — newcomers get this way within a year or two. It's the stairs and the stone. You walk everywhere on a slight grade that never seems to be a grade until your calves tell you. You carry water even when you don't think you're carrying water — two buckets to the roof tank, the dehumidifier bucket to the gutter, the gutter bucket to the cistern. You carry new brick, reclaimed brick, brick dust in sacks. The old women who sweep the guano paste in the mornings have forearms like millworkers.

Their clothes tell you immediately how long they've been here. Newcomers wear boots that try to keep water out. Residents wear boots that expect to be wet, soft leather or felted wool with soles cut to grip slick travertine, or open sandals with clay-draining footbeds even in winter. Newcomers wear jackets that try to stay dry. Residents wear boiled wool and oiled canvas that darken at three o'clock and steam dry by four, without comment. Everyone owns a tin. A tin box for papers, a tin cupboard for flour and sugar, a tin chest for anything that could mold. Doors are left ajar even when it rains. Privacy is not about doors here; it's about noise and about whose condensation channel you're allowed to tap.

They live slow and close. Families are not large — there is no room — but households are. A flat is never just a family. It's a family plus Mrs. Deluca's cousin plus the apprentice who minds the press on the second floor plus the old man who knows how to re-cut a gutter deed. Sound carries through wet stone so well that everyone knows everyone else's schedule by ear: the press starts at six, the pigeons go up at six-fifteen, the scrape of the street crews at seven.

They have trades that only exist here.

**Water-readers.** Old, mostly women, who can tell by the beading on a tile whether the aquifer is high or low, whether your wall needs another weep hole, whether Mrs. Deluca upstairs is draining her sink illegally into your channel. They are consulted like lawyers when deeds are written — and every building has a water deed, a formal path: *this much from the rock goes this way to this gutter to this cistern to this overflow*. To block or steal water is the only serious crime. Everything else can be negotiated.

**Gutter-cutters.** Not plumbers. Stoneworkers. They walk with long-handled basalt chisels and re-deepen the canyons in the streets a millimeter at a time. Their work will outlive them by a century. They measure in generations. "Fifty years 'til the next cut," they always say, and they are always right because fifty years is what people can imagine.

**Cistern-keepers.** Usually children and the very old together, a pairing that makes sense once you see it. They climb. Every roof has a tank, a shanty, a jury-rigged balcony under the tank. Someone has to check the lids, skim the bacterial felt before it gets too thick, make sure the overflow drips into the right gutter, untangle the laundry lines from the wires that have become thatch. Old hands remember where the weight is safe; small feet can go where weight isn't.

**Scrapers.** At dawn, before the steam. Teams with flat wooden shovels and hoses, municipal and informal and indistinguishable. They scrape the night's guano and dust paste — pale, ammonia-sharp, gritty — off every ledge and sill where it will not be allowed to become soil, and they sell it by the cartload to the Brickworks. The children who do this for pocket money come home with white knees.

They don't farm. There's nothing to farm. The City makes things: bearings, bolts, wire, tile, glass. The hum you feel through your soles even four blocks away is not a metaphor. The machines are bolted to bedrock and the bedrock carries. Men and women tend lathes that have been in the same building for ninety years, replaced bearing by bearing until no original part remains. They make more lathes to make more bearings. The closed loop that feeds the birds also feeds them — starch and oil and crumbs on hands, shared, breathed in, reworked into brick dust, into new brick.

And they listen. At three o'clock when the deposition comes and the sun comes back and the whole Table steams, every human for a thousand miles stops, without deciding to, to listen to every bird go silent at once. Then they smile a little, private. Warm feet. Dry tin.

### The Birds

If the people are the water, the birds are the weather.

You were told there are no trees, so you imagine a few hardy pigeons. What there is instead is a sky that has texture. At dawn it doesn't lighten so much as fray — a fur of wings, a static, a low cloud that resolves on second look into individual bodies rising from every ledge, every wire, every cistern lip at once. Not thousands. Millions. The density is such that the air between buildings has a constant faint tick of feathers on air, like paper being shuffled two streets over.

They have sorted themselves not by species as lowlanders count species, but by *place*.

**The Ledge Pigeons** are the base stock, the true citizens. Big, slate-grey with iridescent necks gone dusty from limestone. They don't coo so much as mutter, a low rolling murmur that together becomes the chord you sleep through. They nest in any horizontal crack where two bricks don't quite meet, where a lintel has slumped. Two eggs, white as salt, always. Their chicks are ugly and blind and fed on paste made from scavenged crumbs and bacterial felt and their own filtered guano — because there is nothing else. You learn not to look up with your mouth open under an overhang.

**The Wire Starlings.** Smaller, dun and sharp, they don't roost so much as bead. At dusk every wire that thatches an alley becomes suddenly thick, then knotted, then impossibly heavy — hundreds per yard, shoulder to shoulder, chattering in a high, metallic hiss that sounds exactly like wet wires shorting. They are thieves of wire and thread, lining nests made entirely inside wall cavities with stolen copper strand and laundry lint. If your radio cuts out, it's starlings.

**The Wall Swifts.** You almost never see them still. They live in the vertical. Thin as knives, soot-black, they pour out of expansion joints and weep holes and drainage cuts like smoke being pulled backward into a wall. They never land on the ground — they can't; their legs are vestigial little hooks good only for clinging to brick inside a crack. They drink on the wing from the momentary sheen of deposition before it becomes drip. Their nests are saliva and brick dust glued deep in the dark where no human hand will ever reach. You know them by the flicker: a whole district taking off at once when a press backfires, a wave of shadow that has no bird at its center.

**The Table Gulls.** Big, white, screaming. They have never seen the sea and have no need to. They work the Edge where the aquifer pours off columnar basalt in sourceless waterfalls, riding the kilometer-high updraft without moving their wings. Inland, they walk the flat roofs like watchmen, picking off the thumb-sized isopods that stray too far from the damp. The kids at the cliffs throw scraps just to watch them catch without diving — a neat, arrogant tilt.

There are smaller things too: the **Cistern Wrens**, dun-colored flickers that live their entire lives inside cisterns and wall cavities, entering through overflow pipes, nesting in the felt that grows on the inside of tanks. You only see them when a whole district takes off and they spill out for a second like chaff before vanishing into the next wall.

They should not have enough to eat. They do, because the City sheds. Every balcony with a crumb, every sill with fat-spattered dust, every warm vent exhaling starch from a bakery that has been baking the same bread for eighty years. The birds eat the City's leavings, and each other’s eggs and dead, and the insects, and the bacterial felt when desperate.

No raptors. The air is too crowded and too low for hawks to stoop. Once in a few years a falcon gets lost on its migration, comes screaming down to the Table, finds no perch, no thermal that isn't full of brick exhalation, and leaves again by evening, bewildered.

At night the cooing never actually stops. It just thins into the drip and the hum until you can't pick it out.

### The Creepers - What Moves Underneath the Cooing

The ground itself moves if you stare long enough.

Not the stone — the carpet on it.

**The Pale Woodlice** are the true bulk. Isopods the size of a thumb joint, blind, translucent grey-white like wet paper, each segment edged with fine, vibrating hairs. In the service levels where the stone sweats constantly, they cover the walls in a slow, eating tide. They rasp the black-green bacterial felt and the rust-colored bacterial felt off the rock with specialized mouths, leaving behind a faintly paler trail that dries for an hour before the felt grows back. Lift a loose tile and a hundred will curl, then uncurl, then resume. They are food for everything. They are the sheep of this place. Children keep them in tins as pets and are gently told to put them back.

**The Black Gutter Beetles** own the drainage cuts. Glossy, long-legged, up to two inches, they run in such numbers that the bottom of a gutter seems to flow in the wrong direction even when dry. They click. Each beetle makes almost no sound, but ten thousand mandibles and tarsae on limestone make a dry rustle like rice being poured, forever. They eat dead birds, dead isopods, dead beetles, guano paste, fallen feathers. They are undertakers. At steam hour they cling under the rim and wait out deposition; if you wash them away with a hose they'll be back in an hour, in the same number, as if you'd never hosed.

**The Limestone Moths** are the camouflage. Flat, powdery, the exact grey-black-ochre of old lime and brick dust, with wings that when folded are indistinguishable from a mineral bloom. No one sees them fly because they only fly in the glassy minute before deposition, when the air is supersaturated and everything is about to weep. Then for thirty seconds the walls themselves seem to detach and flutter two inches and settle again. They lay eggs in the guano paste; their pale, fat larvae eat mold in grout.

Under all that, smaller: **silverfish** like quicksilver in the tins, **mites** invisible until you rub your eyes too much, **red bacterial felt** that is not animal at all but behaves like one — spreading overnight across a freshly scraped wall, retreating from a stripe of direct sun by noon.

All of it damp, all of it patient, all of it without hurry. Nothing swarms here. Swarming is for places where food comes in seasons. Here food comes always, a little, forever.

### The Lean Gods

**Cats.** There must have been cats from the beginning, brought up to kill something that turned out not to exist. They failed. No cat can control a biomass like this; a cat that ate pigeons here would explode.

So they changed jobs.

They are lean, wet, half-feral, allowed everywhere and owned by no one. You see them trotting along a wire-thatch with perfect balance, or sitting on a warm cistern lid grooming the damp from their fur forever. They do hunt — a flick of a paw and a starling gone, a beetle crunched — but mostly they are left alone because they *try*, and because they kill the one thing people do fear: the **stone rats**, rare, pale, blind things that follow the aquifer tubes and chew through copper wire not for food but for the salts that condense on it. A stone rat in your wall can short your press and flood your flat. A cat that lives in your service canyon is insurance, and you leave it milk that is really water with a little starch, and it pretends to thank you.

Dogs are rare because a dog wants to chase what it can catch, and here it can catch nothing without falling off a roof or into a cistern. The few that live here go mad from the cooing for a month and then learn to sleep through it, lying with their chins on warm stone like people.

### What It All Is

It should not work: a city with no plants feeding animals with no predator.

It does because the sun still hits warm rock at a mile high and the air still gives up its water at three o'clock and bacteria still make a living on wet limestone and people still drop crumbs. The base of the food chain is not a forest. It's condensation and skin and brick dust and time.

If you stand on your third-floor balcony at steam hour and watch the far factories disappear and reappear, you can see it for a second as one thing: the weep running, the isopods rasping, the beetles clicking, the pigeons muttering, the swifts flickering, the presses humming through your feet, Mrs. Deluca emptying your dehumidifier bucket for you and scolding — all one slow metabolism, spread flat across a table of stone that holds heat like a brick out of the kiln.

It doesn't roar. It accretes.
