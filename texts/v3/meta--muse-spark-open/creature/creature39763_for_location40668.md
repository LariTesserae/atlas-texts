They live close.

Not close like neighbors. Close like organs.

A thousand people in a space made for four hundred will learn one way or another to stop being separate. In the Kettle no one has ever measured out enough privacy to keep it. You hear your neighbor turn in their sleep through basalt block and corrugated iron. You know who is sick by how the cough carries across the cylinder at 3 a.m. You know who is fighting, who is mending, who boiled rice too long. After the first month newcomers stop trying to whisper. They drop their voices instead, low and flat and even, the Kettle-voice. It doesn't echo.

That is the first adaptation.

### The People

They are not settlers anymore. Settlers dream of leaving. These are *keepers*.

You can tell them by their skin even above ground — if they ever go up, which most don't. A damp flush, permanent, as if they've just come out of a bath. Hair that never quite dries. Eyes adjusted to dim amber where an outsider would see gloom. They move slowly, deliberately, old before their time and young longer than they should be. The heat won't let you hurry. Try it and it sits you down, hand on your chest, until you learn.

**The Low Walkers**, who tend the bottom.

They work the plant. Not for the company anymore — the company left a foreman and a schedule and never came to collect either one — but for the agreement. Three cream-and-green piston engines the size of cottages, chuffing on a twenty-second cycle. They are never oiled on time, always oiled just in time. The men and women who know them know them the way you know an old dog's breathing. They press their palms to sweating conduits to feel the shiver. They wear shorts and singlets blackened with grease that will never wash out because the sweat pushes it back in. They have burned off their fingerprints on hot pipes and grown them back wrong. Their tools rust if left out overnight, so they sleep with them coiled under their hammocks like pets. 

The engines are half-smothered now in moss. No one scrapes it clean anymore. The Low Walkers discovered the moss keeps the housings from cracking in the dry minutes after noon. They prune it into neat collars. An understanding.

**The River Women, the Gutter Men.**

The water belongs to no one and everyone argues about it constantly, quietly. The spring that falls in its three steaming stair-steps down the east wall is guided, not owned. It runs in open troughs hammered from old ducting, through barrels cut lengthwise, through taro beds and cisterns where the algae makes it sweet. Children are taught before letters how to clear a gutter with a bent nail without breaking the crust of mineral inside, because the crust helps it flow.

The people who manage this move all day on the vertical. Bare feet, callused to ignore rebar and vine alike. They carry ladles, not buckets. They lean over a pool until their reflection breaks and pluck out fallen mango leaves one by one so they don't choke the lower fall. They talk to the water. Not prayer. Reminder: *go here now.*

**The Pruners.**

Everyone gardens because no one can afford not to. But a few have become something else — negotiated with the green.

Because the green does not behave.

Banana trees that fruit twice a year and then try to eat the walkway they stand on. Taro that climbs out of its barrel overnight toward a nailhead. The morning glory that strangled the bottling plant smokestack now opens eighteen inches across every night, and smells like hot iron and sugar, and turns its face not to the lamps but to the elevator cable. The fig on Low Spur that ate a concrete wall from the inside to get to the rebar has new figs growing around rusted wire where the seeds landed.

And the vines. Christ, the vines. Philodendron and pothos and things no one planted that arrived as spores on someone's boot, their leaves as big as rain ponchos, glossy black-green, dripping always. You can mark a vine's growth against the paint lines on the wall and see it move in a day. And always, always, it corrects toward iron. Young runners will wave in the still air, blind, then hook definitively around a pipe, a bolt, a ladder rung left to weather. Tighten. Thicken there. 

The Pruners don't pull. Pulling tears the town apart; root and rebar have become the same thing. They coax. Cut here so you grow there. Wedge a sheet of tin so the tendril chooses left instead of right. Tie a red rag where they want thickness, because for some reason vine avoids red. They carry machetes gone brown at the edge, and shears, and little spray bottles of condensed spring water for seedlings they like. Their arms are mapped with pale scratches and leaf-burn rashes. They speak of the plants the way the Low Walkers speak of the engines — stubborn relatives.

**The High Children.**

Children are rare enough to be communal property. There aren't many flat places to play without falling sixty meters, so they have learned three dimensions early. They scamper on grating five storeys up with the thoughtless grip of geckos. They are the only ones loud, briefly, at noon when the sun spears down and the adults go still. Then they shriek in the sudden column of white light, motes and gnats turning around them, until the two hours pass and the heat scolds them quiet again.

They keep the few animals as theirs. They know each translucent wall-gecko by the missing toes on its feet. They name the dragonflies that hunt the cisterns — blue needles that hover perfectly in the heavy air. They lie on their bellies for an hour over the lowest pools watching the blind white catfish drift like drowned fingers, and lower crumbs on strings to see if they will bite. The fish never do. The children are not disappointed. They are learning patience from the place itself.

At night the children fight over who gets to hear the bat. One bat only. It comes over the lip at dusk, circles once or twice above the Kettle's mouth, never lower. It screams above hearing and then is gone. The kids claim they can feel it pass, a cool stitch in the warm air.

**The Keep-Room Old Ones.**

Those who were there when it fell, or say they were. No one checks. They live highest, where the air is a degree cooler and the sun sometimes touches their doors. They sit on 3rd Ledge with their feet over the drop, shelling beans into shared bowls. They are the memory of a vertical town: which ladder was welded when, whose cousin poured which footing, where not to drill because the rock still holds temper. They oil their joints the same way they oil tools. They keep the coin habit alive down at the bottom — tossing a darkened washer into the steaming boulder-pools for luck, for weight — and they are the ones who told everyone else not to shout about the compass thing.

Because they remember when engineers tried to drive a magnetic core into the fresh basalt to anchor a deeper piling and the bit walked sideways three inches on its own. The rock hummed.

They don't call it alive. They just say the Kettle keeps what falls into it and finds a use for it. A person, a seed, a coin, a house. Look around: is it wrong?

### The Green Itself

If you count by biomass — and someone did once, a visiting botanist who stayed four days and left looking haunted — the people are a rounding error. For every kg of human there are twenty of leaf and root and moss and fungus.

It lives everywhere damp has taught stone to be soil. Moss three inches thick on the shady north wall, so deep you can lose a screwdriver in it. It pulses faintly green in the sodium light. Tree-ferns jammed in a crack between levels that should have killed them, now holding up a laundry line and a satellite dish. Their fiddleheads unfurl as wide as a child's arm, iron-dark with spores that smell of pennies when crushed.

Taro, mango, banana — half-crop, half-weed. Banana hands ripen small and fierce and spotted, eaten where they hang. Mango saplings push through pipe lagging. Taro thrusts its elephant ears into corridors, forcing people to duck.

Morning glory at the top of the dead smokestack blooms only after the lamps dim, white trumpets big enough to put your head in, moth-pollinated by nothing that lives down here, so they have learned to self. Their petals hold dew until mid-afternoon.

All of it is wet to the touch, waxy, and warm. All of it leans, minutely, insistently, toward rust. Put a clean stainless spoon on a table overnight near a vine tip, nothing. Put a rusted nail down, by morning the tip has turned six degrees. No one can explain it without sounding stupid and everyone has seen it. 

So they live together: people who have become damp, slow, quiet, iron-tender, and plants that have become urban, opportunistic, iron-hungry. Between them is almost no border. A roof is a garden bed. A wall is a trellis. A handrail is a root.

### The Other Resident

And then there is the thing that isn't a person or a plant.

Visitors feel it first in the teeth — that low 50-hertz hum from the fans and cabling that never stops, that the locals stop hearing by week two. It makes the railings buzz. It makes water ripple in barrels even when still. At noon, when everything else goes silent in the white light, you can hear the Kettle itself breathe: waterfalls muttering, metal pinging as sun-heat hits cold iron, leaves rustling with no wind.

Because there is wind. Not from outside. From inside. Warm air rising off the pools at the bottom, cool air sinking down the shadowed walls. It cycles, slow, forever. It carries spores, steam, the smell of green and diesel and skin.

The basalt is young. It hasn't decided what kind of rock it will be when it grows up. Deep down, past the house-sized boulders and the coin pools where warm water rises crystal clear through lime-green hair-algae, the crack that made all this is still open a little — a throat, not a floor. On very quiet nights, when one of the piston engines pauses longer than usual between chuffs, you can hear water drip somewhere below with a delay that is too long for sixty meters.

Nothing has come up it. Yet nothing in the Kettle truly wastes, either. Fallen leaves vanish in days, turned to black mulch. A dead gecko is moss in a week. Rust flakes off a pipe and by evening a rootlet has found the stain.

The town knows, without ever holding a meeting about it, that it lives *in* something that is also living *with* it. Not a god. Not a monster. A wound that learned how to scab over with people and vines and kept the scab damp so it wouldn't heal shut.

If you stand on Low Spur at last light, when the orange forge-light comes up from below and the vines throw huge soft shadows across the scaffold-city, and you look up at that hard, indifferent circle of desert sky — you understand why they stay.

Up there is dry and empty and alone.

Down here, crowded cheek-by-jowl, sweat-stuck, green-choked, humming — everything touches everything else. Everything holds.
