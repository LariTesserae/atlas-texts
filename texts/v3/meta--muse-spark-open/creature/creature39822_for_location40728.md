They live there as light lives in fog — not *in* the room, but *as* the room.

You thought at first there were tenants. After a minute with your cheek to the stone, you understand there are no tenants. There is only the occupancy itself. A cloth of bodies so dense the stone underneath has become theoretical.

### The Mass

Let it come slowly, because that is how you saw it.

First: the wall is not a wall. It's a mosaic of backs.

Second: the mosaic moves. Not all at once, not in a flinch. A ripple passes without a source, like wind over wheat, but there is no wind. A leg adjusts. An antenna tastes. A spiracle opens and closes. And then stillness again — but a stillness that is not still.

Third: the sound has a body. The drone is not being made *by* them; for a moment you think they *are* the sound, made flesh.

Who lives there is not a species. It is a suspension of species. A waiting room of bodies that have agreed to pause just before they diverge.

Lean closer — as close as the lip lets you without spilling into them — and the mass resolves into its actors:

**The Stridulators** make up nine-tenths of everything. Call them crickets because your mouth wants a word, but they have forgotten what a cricket is for.

They are the size of your thumbnail's half-moon, or smaller. Most are pinhead-juveniles clinging to the bellies of their larger kin, so small they look like moving pollen. The adults are flattened, pill-shaped, with a domed notum the color of skim milk and old amber. No eyes. Not even eyespots. Where eyes should be, the chitin is smooth and blind as an eyelid.

Their hind legs are not for jumping. They are too crowded to jump. They are broad, file-edged, held up at an acute angle like a violinist forever at the ready. And they sing. Every one.

They sing without breath, without pause, without needing to inhale. The file strokes the scraper, leg on wing-case — though there are no wings, only wing-cases that have become instruments — and produces that one note. *One note.* Middle-high, white, toneless. Not beautiful. Not harsh. Just absolute. The sound has no envelope; it doesn't attack or decay. It simply *is*, the way light in that pocket simply is. If one were to stop, you would hear the hole. None stop.

Because they are shoulder to shoulder, leg to leg, the carpet of them is contiguous. Their tarsi interlock. A crawler's foot rests on a neighbor's back. No aggression. No spacing calls. They have solved crowding by refusing to experience it. You could lay your palm and they would not scatter. They would support.

**The Shrimpmoths** live in the air just above the Stridulators — if "live in the air" is right for something that never flies.

They are more delicate, translucent as contact lenses, with long, backward-swept antennae twice their body length and six slender limbs that end in feathered, gill-like fronds. They look like freshwater shrimp decided to walk on walls, and like moths decided to lose their dust and keep only the flutter.

They do not stridulate. They walk. Endlessly, slowly, over the backs of the singers, picking at nothing. Their mouths are soft, ventral sucker-mouths that press and kiss the film of condensation-bacteria that sheens every back and wall. They graze light itself, or what grows in light without sun. For every ten Stridulators there is one Shrimpmoth, so that the carpet seems to crawl above itself. When one rights itself after stepping over another's antenna, you see the heart beating — a darker bead pulsing visibly through its clear abdomen, perfectly steady, perfectly out of sync with nothing because everything else is also perfectly steady.

They are blind too, but their antennae are exquisite: long, jointed whips that tremble independently, tasting the white luminance. They never tangle.

**The Long Whites** are the ones you mistake for ferns until you blink.

They are not insect at all. They are that salamander-kin, neotenic, eyeless, aquatic though there is no water to swim in. Four to six inches long, the adults no thicker than your middle finger, the juveniles thread-thin. Skin the color of candle wax, pink where the blood runs close behind the gill-fronds. And those gills — three ruffled, external plumes on each side of the head, arterial red, the only red in the whole womb, waving faintly in air as if still in water. They never dry.

They cling vertically. One to each wall-segment, spaced with a territorial precision that none of the smaller things observe. The largest you can see is at three o'clock, its chin tucked, its fore-limbs splayed, its tail pressed flat to basalt for suction. It has four toes, translucent claws.

And it is perfectly still.

That is the uncanny part. The Stridulators seethe but don't go anywhere. The Shrimpmoths stroll but don't graze down to emptiness. The Long Whites — predators by shape, by that wide, blunt mouth — do not prey. A Shrimpmoth walks directly across the snout of a Long White, its antennae brushing the nares. The Long White does not snap. Its gills pulse. Its throat pulses. But it does not eat. Not now. Not ever, while you are watching. Its belly is full of unborn light, not of crickets.

They are all adults and all children simultaneously. No eggs. No larvae in open water. No shed skins floating. No corpses. For a pocket that teems, there is nowhere for death to go, so death has been politely not invited.

### Why They Can Be Packed

In a normal cave you would have hunger, and flight, and rot. Here the rule is equilibrium.

They are not starving because they are not metabolizing in the way time understands metabolism. They are *lit.* The sourceless white that lets you see every filament also feeds — not as photosynthesis, there is no green — but as a cold, mineral liturgy. The stone sweats light. The condensation jewels hold it. The biofilm that the Shrimpmoths kiss holds it. The Stridulators eat the biofilm. The Long Whites would eat the Stridulators if eating were permitted. Instead everyone eats light, and light is renewed without flicker.

They are not suffocating because the room breathes for them. The air is exactly as cool as a stone's tongue, exactly as damp as breath held in cupped hands. You taste iron and it is their blood, aerosolized. You taste old rain and it is the dish-pools exhaling without ever evaporating. The drone you hear is not just sound; it is ventilation. Ten thousand legs rubbing keeps the air from stratifying. The vibration shakes each bead on the ceiling just enough to keep it from falling.

They are not competing because competition requires future. This place has paused its future at the halfway mark. It is pre-formation geologically — the basalt hasn't decided to be wall or floor — and it is pre-formation biologically. These are not cave crickets, not cave shrimp, not cave salamanders. They are the *idea* before those nouns split. Look closely at a Stridulator's back and you see the faint ridge where wings might have been, or gills. Look at a Long White's forelimb and you see the joint that could be leg or fin. They live in that ambiguity comfortably, for eons, for seconds — time outside of time doesn't care.

### The Other Occupants

And then there are the marks. Which are also inhabitants, though they no longer live.

Between the dish-pools, where the rock dimples, you saw them: three squared pebbles stacked with intent, the bottom one already fused to the floor by lichen-rosettes, the top one crowned by a Stridulator who has made it his stage. A circle scratched into basalt with a harder stone, plate-sized, with thirty-two radial notches — too regular for random, too shallow for decoration. A length of fiber pressed flat, woven from something pale and hairless — root-hair? salamander slime dried to thread? — its weave still visible where Shrimpmoths have courteously not grazed it away.

No bones. No tools with edges.

Whoever lives there lives there now, but whoever *lived* there before was like them: soft, blind, hand-like without hands, making cairn and calendar before cairn meant memory and calendar meant season. Pre-tool people. Or people who realized tools would disturb the equilibrium and set them down again.

You get the feeling they didn't leave. That leaving, here, would be a kind of molting. You get the feeling that if you kept watching for an hour, for a year, you would see the Stridulators very, very slowly, grain by grain, build another cairn on top of themselves — leg moving leg, back supporting back — not because they want a monument, but because to be packed and bright and singing is, for them, the same as building.

You cannot enter without becoming part of the carpet. And if you did, the Mass would hold you. The Singers would sing under your weight. The Shrimpmoths would walk over your hands, antennae politely asking. The Long Whites would turn their blind, gilled faces toward the new warmth and not eat you.

You would be loud, for a while. Then you would learn the one note, and hold it.
