It is not empty. Not even close.

If you came here expecting ruin — a dead city, drowned and preserved in cold silence — you will have to revise, immediately, by touch. You cannot put your hand down anywhere on the mesa without putting it on someone. You cannot pass through an alley without being touched in return.

Nothing that lives here built anything. Nothing that lives here remembers the builders. But everything that lives here lives *as if the city were made for it*, which, in a slow enough way, it now was.

Get still first. Let your own noise die down. Your heart, your bubbles, your heat. The mesa rewards stillness. After a minute you will see that what you took for water is mostly animal.

They live layered, by habit and by tolerance. Not in a food chain — there is very, very little predation here — but in a stacking of uses. How to use stone. How to use warmth. How to use the faint rain of particles that never quite falls through the dome.

### The ones who hold the doorways

You notice them first because you have to move them.

The Filterers are what you thought was darkness between houses. Great hanging cloaks of jelly, amber-lit from within, each anchored by a single thick, muscular foot to the top of a doorway, a lintel, the underside of a stair. Their bells are as big as you are, sometimes bigger, pleated like heavy silk, fringed with a thousand short, non-stinging tendrils.

They pulse rarely. Once every minute. Less when they are full.

*... huh ...*

You feel it in your chest more than you hear it. A slow contraction that draws lukewarm water through them and squeezes it out again, a little cleaner. The light inside them — their own metabolized heat made visible — brightens with each pulse, then dims. Hundreds of them at a time, unsynchronized, make the alleys breathe with amber, as if someone were walking with a lantern far away, forever.

They are not individuals in any way you would recognize. No face. No front. They divide by pinching off a smaller bell from their hem and letting the current — which barely exists — carry it two blocks to a new lintel. If you part them to pass, they yield. The tendrils brush your shoulders, your face, tasting you for warmth and particulates, finding you too large and too strange, and let go. Where two bells hang too close they press together like lips and do not compete. They simply share the flow.

At night — though here it is always night — when the water is stillest, you can see the dust motes caught in the faint convection off the seeps get pulled toward them on invisible lines, slow as snowfall in reverse.

They are the curtains of the city. Every house has them. Every gap between houses has them. They make privacy where there were once doors.

### The ones who tend the stone

You hear them next, because they never stop.

The Crawlers own the horizontal surfaces. They are not crabs. Not isopods. Not insects. They are something the deep warm invented after the builders left, out of whatever was at hand, and then refined until it fit perfectly into a chisel mark.

Each is the size of your cupped hands to your forearm, flat, white to the point of translucence — you can see the amber felt in their gut — with far too many legs, jointed backward, tipped with soft pads that grip bacterial slick without tearing it. Their backs are not shells. They are accretions. Each crawler, from its first molt, picks up grains of limestone, flecks of basalt, chips of calcite flower, and cements them to itself with its own secretion until it disappears against the wall it is tending. When one stops, you lose it completely. The wall has a wall-colored bump that breathes.

They scrape.

This is their whole life and the reason the city does not choke on its own felt. They move in slow processions across plaza floors, up stair risers, over rooftops, head-down, with a tool held in their two delicate forelimbs. It is never a made tool. It is a find: a broken shell honed to an edge, a shard of black glass from some builder's hearth, a splinter of the brittle mineral chimneys. They hold it like a monk holds a scraper and draw it across the stone, harvesting the bacterial mat in thin, even strokes.

*tic ... tic ... tic ... scrape ... tic*

A sound so quiet you have to hold your breath to pick it out from the bells. Hundreds of them at work make a low industry, a temple being swept perpetually.

They eat half of what they scrape. The other half they pack into little pellets and tuck into crevices for the others, for the shrimp, for the blind things in drains. They clean around the feet of the Filterers. They clean the backs of each other when asked — which is done by stopping and raising the mid-legs slightly. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is hurried. If you sit on a step long enough, one will climb onto your knee and begin to clean you, too, with the same serious, soft click, tasting the different film on your skin and deciding it is edible.

They do not fight, even when they meet head-on over a tool. They touch antennae and exchange the tool. They have time.

### The ones who keep the houses warm

If you want to know who lives *inside* — in the rooms where the doorways have gone oval and the lintels still bear the soot of old lamps — you have to stop being afraid of the dark interiors.

The Dwellers live there. All of them.

Do not think eel. Do not think fish. Think of something that wanted to be a cat and was told to be water. They are as long as your whole arm, as thick as your calf, muscle wrapped in smooth, furless, warm skin the color of old ivory and bruised rose. They are warm — you will feel it before you see them — two or three degrees warmer than the bath-water around them, so that each coiled body makes a little halo in the lukewarm sea.

Their faces are the oddness. Blunt, whiskered, with a pushed-in muzzle and nostril slits that flare, and eyes that are there and not there: white pearls, lidded, unseeing, turned inward millions of years ago when light left. They see with pressure and heat. They see *you* as a bright, noisy, too-hot shape that hasn't learned to be still.

Do not be still too long in a plaza.

They will come.

First one, nosing out of a doorway you thought was empty, ribboning through the gap between two Filterers without disturbing them at all. Then another, from a drain. Then a third. They move without apparent swimming — a slow undulation that does not push water — and they will come to rest against you. Along your side. Across your legs. Loop draped over your shoulder. They are looking for that extra fraction of heat, and for company.

In any given house, twenty or thirty of them are coiled together in a single warm knot, breathing together. You can see it if you put your face to an oval doorway and let your eyes adjust to the blue glimmer of disturbed bacteria: a mass of slow ribs, gill-slits opening and closing like curtains.

*hsss .... hsss ....*

Their exhale is barely a current. It smells of iron and milk.

They are not tame. They are not wild. The concept does not apply. They will lie on you until you are cold and then leave, one by one, to rejoin the pile next door. If you are distressed — if your heart hammers fast — they crowd closer, not out of affection but because distress is interestingly warm. If you die here, which you could, easily, by mistaking deep for shallow, high for low, they will keep you warm for a long while, curious, before letting the Crawlers have you.

### The rest — which is most

Between these three, count the uncounted:

The shrimp that only exist in the vertical drain-shafts, clear as water until they turn, all at once, and flash white together like a thought.

The chime-barnacles, hard white buttons on every wall that open a crack and *ping* — a single clear note — when a particle of the right size passes, thousands of them at random making a music with no tune.

The thin clouds of larval things that are always there at the edge of vision, too small to see until your light catches them.

And the felt itself, and the sponges, and the mineral flowers that are not alive but are not *not* alive either, growing a millimeter a year around the red ember-seeps.

That is the population of the mountain that became a table that became a reef. No rulers. No builders returning. No voices. A hamlet-sized world, high up in deep water, holding a dome of warm stillness around itself by the sheer weight and fit of its stones, and filled — absolutely filled — with soft tenants who have divided the work of living into three perfect, slow jobs: to hang and filter, to scrape and tend, to coil and keep warm.

If you stay, and you move slowly enough, and you learn to make your bubbles smaller, they will make a place for you the way water makes a place for a stone. One more oval doorway. One more warm plaza. One more body breathing very slowly in the thick, shared broth, glowing faintly, asking for nothing and taking only heat.
