Who lives in the Hollow has learned not to need what the world above thinks is needed.

First — the ones you were told have taken the place of animals.

The **Glass Reeds** live there. A stand of them is not a forest, it is a choir left standing. Each reed is empty, each reed is listening. Three meters of silica grown thin as breath, translucent amber at the base where the light catches, clouding to frost higher up. They have no leaves, no green. Inside their hollow walls a skin of black thermophores darkens and lightens, opens and closes pores thinner than paper. When the floor-heat rises they flex — a contraction you can feel more than see, a shiver running the length of the tube — and suck the scalding air through themselves, stripping what they need out of its motion. What they exhale is cooler by a degree, enough to keep the convection turning.

That is what they live on. Heat and dust.

They are tuned, accidentally or deliberately — the Hollow doesn't distinguish — so that the wind of their own breathing makes them sing. Not rustle, not whisper. *Resonance.* The largest reeds are bass pipes, wide as your thigh; the youngest are needle-flutes. Together they hold a chord that never fully stops, a low HMMMM you feel in the molars, loud enough that two people standing ten feet apart raise their voices. When pressure drops in the Throat they overblow. The whole grove leans slightly inward, as if listening harder, and screams. You will hear the children clap hands over ears and laugh. The Reeds don't mind being touched. Hammocks strung between them sway and make them louder. If you tie too tight they crack straight down their length with a clean, bright *ping* and stay there, split open, until the next generation dissolves them from the inside.

The **Blanket Trees** live there. Nothing about them is tree-like except that something large is sitting in one place being alive. One individual might be twenty strides across, a low dome of overlapping plates like a pangolin curled over the basalt. Each plate is waxy horn, soot-black, warm and slick under your palm because it exudes oil constantly to seal itself. Lift an edge — and the townswomen do, carefully, with copper wedges — and you see what it is doing: the stone beneath is gone soft, pitted with perfect, acid-etched alveoli, damp with vapor that was never water. The Tree eats rock. It precipitates it layer by layer into more plate.

On top of every plate grows a pelt of velvet hair, fine, black, combing the slow fall of air for what the desert above would call nothing: iron dust, salt crystals, flecks of sulfur sublimed off hot stone. Once a month, in unison for reasons no one has divined, every hair lets go. It falls as dry snow that never melts, that never wets a finger. It gathers in drifts against house walls. The people sweep it before noon because if they don't it felts and roots and tries to make a new Tree in the doorway.

You can walk on a living Blanket Tree. You should. The dome bears weight without complaint. Children run races across them. Old men nap on them because the warmth comes steady through the plates at blood-heat. The Trees open at dusk — not much, a thumb's width between plates, a ticking sigh — and exhale the day's collection as a cloud of hot spice and spore, oily incense that hangs eye-level and then settles. That is when you see them inhale, and remember they are alive.

In the crawlspace where roof kisses floor, the **Lantern Vines** live. They are not vines. They are colonies that decided rope was a good shape. Each one hangs thirty, forty bladders long, translucent parchment-purple, each bladder pulsing on its own time. You hear them before you see them in the dim edge: *whuff... whuff... whuff...* like a hall of sleepers breathing out of sync. They do not hang still. Convection swings them, slow. Valves inside snap open and shut, forcing hot air through chambers, wringing power out of a few degrees' difference. At maturity the tip-bladder swells taut as a drum, detaches without a sound, and rolls. It goes trundling across the basalt floor, light as paper, driven by escaping gas, bouncing off houses and Reed feet, scattering a dust inside it that smells sharply of hot iron when you crush it between fingers. Then it collapses and the next Blanket Tree grows over it.

That is the animal silence you noticed. No buzzing. No eyes. Things open and close, swell and shatter, fall and are not eaten, only reabsorbed. The Hollow is lush the way rust is lush — abundantly, without any mercy in it.

Because the **Hollowfolk** live there too.

They are four hundred, though the bubble could hold thousands. They chose sparseness. Houses touch walls for shade, then leave a deliberate emptiness: a Reed grove with a single bench worn smooth, a fifty-foot wall with one shuttered window, a plaza that is only warm stone and the circle where children set chimes spinning. It feels both crowded and depopulated, like a market after hours where everyone went home by agreement.

You know them by the mouth first. Their lips are always covered loosely with ash-dyed linen, their nostrils too. Uncovered, you'd crack in an hour; they taught their children this before speech. Under the cloth their lips are pale, almost interior-pink, perpetually oiled. So is all their skin. They press oil from the joints of young Reeds and from the rendered down of Blanket hair, bitter nutty stuff, and anoint arms, scalps, eyelids. Without it they'd fissure. With it they shine ember-amber in the lamp light. Their hair is cropped short or braided flat, because loose hair collects static and draws the floating dust into mats.

They do not drink. There is not even the word for well among them except as blasphemy or joke. A visitor begs water and they smile politely and hand you a thimble of tepid oil that coats your throat and makes thirst stranger, not less. What they metabolize is the same economy as the plants: hydrocarbon and mineral. They bake Blanket plate when it is young and still pliable, pound it to meal, ferment it into a sour, dense cake that steams faintly with volatile oils. They candy Reed nodes where sugar-analogues collect, chew Vine bladder-skin when it is tender, before it goes to parchment. None of these things contain water. All of them yield water inside the body when burned, and their kidneys — changed over generations no one counted — keep every drop and exhale the rest as that faint, warm-clay smell that lives in every house.

They move differently. Unhurried at noon when the Reeds scream and the air shivers by the ceiling; quick at dusk when the Trees exhale and must be swept. They talk over the drone, so their voices have gone a little loud, a little tonal, habitually pitched to cut through HMMMM. Sound carries insanely under the basalt bowl; a whisper at one edge arrives intelligible at the other after a half-second delay. Children learn early not to shout secrets. Old women in doorways gossip across a hundred feet without rising.

What they do, all day, is tending that wants no other name.

The night-watch walks the highest dome with a long copper-tipped pole, tapping each Reed in turn — *tok tok tok* — just before resonance locks. You hear his circuit wander all night under the greater humming. Without him, a locked cord of Reeds will amplify until they shatter in cascade and fill an alley with glass razors for a week.

The broom-walkers follow dusk. Men and women both, barefoot to feel spore-heat, pushing wide, soft brooms of last month's Blanket hair, gathering the day's exhalation before it can take purchase in a crack and lift a foundation plate. They push it into clay jars, the tall cistern-jars at every corner that an outsider would mistake for water storage. At the pressing house it will become more oil, more fiber, more tinder that burns with a low blue flame.

The copper-smiths work cold. They mine nodules sweated out of the basalt by eons of heat — native copper, blistered, precious — and hammer it without quench because there is nothing to quench with. A forge in the Hollow is a coal pan and bellows, the metal glowing and then beaten, ringing and ringing under stone. They make exactly what is needed: wedges, adzes, shallow pans, lamps, poles. They anneal by burying hot work in warm ash overnight.

In the buttress-temple cut where the bubble wall folded double, the keepers tend the calendar, which is not dates but pressures. They watch the Throat's light-crawl, mark where the column falls at noon by notches in basalt, know when the wind above will howl and inflate the Vines taut enough to drum. No one prays for rain. They pray — if that word fits — for equilibrium. For the bubble to stay lukewarm, for the chord not to break.

If you stay too long you stop feeling inhabited by them. You start feeling inhabited by the place using them to maintain itself. Their houses are furnishings the Hollow grew for itself once it had people the way the Reeds grew their nodes: low scoria circles roofed with cured plates that curl after twenty years and must be re-lashed; second stories not built but occupied, platforms left when a Blanket Tree dome died and hardened into a perfect, warm mesa. People move in before the old colony has finished dissolving. Babies are born on living roofs.

And still, they are people. They argue about a badly lashed roof. They leave a toy horse of reed and string for a child who died. In the market circle at nones they lay out cakes and oil and coils of Blanket fiber and nobody buys because nobody needs coin when everything is made within sight. They trade barbs. They sing, necessarily in tune with the Reeds whether they want to or not. At night they go quiet to hear the individual whuff of the Vines and decide from its tiredness if a storm is coming above that will never touch them except as a change in pitch.

They look back. That is the difference the earlier silence was trying to tell you.

The jungle does not look back. The Reeds listen, the Trees comb, the Vines breathe. None of them meet your eye.

The Hollowfolk do. Amber-lit, oil-shining, cloth-masked, they meet your eye across warm stone and raise the cloth just enough to show you the chapped, smiling mouth underneath — to show you, in the most hospitable gesture they know, that they have kept a soft place alive in a world that perfected hardness, and are willing, briefly, to share shade.
