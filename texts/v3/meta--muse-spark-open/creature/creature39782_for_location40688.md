You don't notice them at first.

Not because they are hidden. Because there is no room for *not* noticing them and your brain refuses the math.

The room is only thirty meters long. Every surface is made — concrete, steel plate, iron rib, pipe. You think at first you are looking at texture. That the ceiling is mottled. That the walls have been wrapped in some kind of frost-damaged insulation that hangs in heavy, furred curtains. That the warmest of the twelve-inch mains has a strange, moving sleeve of wet leather pulled over it.

Then the texture breathes.

Not once. Always. A thousand tiny breaths slightly out of phase, so the whole room has a pulse like a lung held inside stone. The air edits out the deafening turbine scream after ninety seconds, and underneath it, this is what is left: the shiver of wings that never open, the dry click of shell on steel, the softest creak of claw on pipe.

Nothing here lives *in* the Cistern. Everything lives *on* it, pressed flat against the made world like it is trying to become part of the machine.

**The Moths — the Ceiling**

They are first because they are everywhere your eyes try to rest.

Each one the size of an outstretched hand, they coat the vaulted ribs between the rivets. Not clustered. *Tiled.* Edge to edge, wing to wing, so close their feathery margins overlap. From below you think the ceiling is simply the color of wet concrete — a mottled, lichen-grey with a darker eyespot at the center of each hindwing that looks exactly like a rivet head. That's the trick. They evolved, or were drawn here, to match poured industrial grey.

They never fly.

To fly in here would be to be shredded by vibration. Instead they cling with hooked feet sunk into condensation-slick iron and they shiver. All of them, all the time. Not a tremor of fear — a furnace. Their flight muscles uncoupled from their wings, firing continuously in isometric shudders to keep their bodies at 14°C while the air around them sits at 2°C and the steel tries to drink every joule they have. If you put your palm close — you will without meaning to, there is nowhere to put it — you feel the faint, terrible heat coming off the ceiling in a sheet, as if the vault itself has a fever.

Their antennae are flat back along their heads. Their eyes are matte black and do not reflect the sodium bulbs. They do not eat each other. They do not need to. Every few minutes a moth extends a tongue as long as its body, licks the film of water beading on the iron above it, and reels it back in. The water is thick with what shouldn't be there: airborne algae, spores, mineral flour pulled up from the valley by the pressure engines, a thin cold soup condensing out of the saturated air. The moths graze the air as it becomes water.

If one dies — and rarely, one does — it does not fall. Its neighbors simply shift a millimeter and tile over it.

**The Bats — the Walls**

You think the pipe bundles are insulated. They aren't. What looks like white batting packed into the negative space between the two-inch feeders and the steam jackets is fur.

Small alpine bats, no bigger than mice, snow-white except for the translucent pink of their ears and noses. They don't hang singly. They *pack.* Shoulder to shoulder, chest to back, in dense living curtains that fill every shadowed gap between pipes where the pipe-heat radiates steadiest. Hundreds in a square meter. Thousands in the room. You can see the shape of a single pipe only by the outline of the bats not covering it.

Their fur is not for cold — though it helps — it is for wet. It is so dense it beads water instantly; condensation forms on their heads and runs off in rivulets down their backs without ever reaching skin. Their ears, huge and veined and almost transparent, are not listening for moths. There is no point. The turbine scream has been at the same 110 decibels at the same frequency for decades. Evolution here gave up on echolocation as hunting. Their ears are pressed flat against their skulls, not to hear, but to *feel* the vibration through the steel. They navigate the roar by bone conduction, by the thrum of the pipes they cling to.

They breathe in unison when they sleep, which is almost always. A slow, visible inflation of the entire white curtain, once every three seconds. Their eyes are closed. Their mouths are open a crack, pink interior collecting the same mineral condensation that feeds the moths. Filter-feeders that hang like fruit.

The moths don't fear them. The bats don't eat the moths. In any other cave this density would be a slaughterhouse. Here, predation has been edited out along with the sound. Why chase when the air feeds you?

**The Isopods — the Veins**

The warmest pipe — the low, fat main that thrums audibly even through the white noise, the one that carries the actual melt — is black and appears to be moving.

Crouch. The light catches it wrong.

It is covered, corner to corner, in a slow, bidirectional river of isopods. Each as long as your index finger, matte black with slate-grey segmentation, like polished coal. They flow both ways at once without colliding, antennae constantly brushing the animal ahead, shells ticking faintly against steel and against each other — the only sound in the room after your brain erases the turbines. *Tik. Tik-tik. Tik.*

They are the custodians. Their mouthparts are not for hunting but for scraping. They clean everything. Guano from the bats above that would otherwise pile inches thick in a month — gone, licked to a sheen. Biofilm from the algae crust on the pipe — grazed in neat rows. Dead moth scales, dead bat skin, the mineral crust left when condensation evaporates for half a second on hot steel — all of it goes under them. If you watch one up close, you see its underside is crowded with tiny, constantly moving legs holding a bolus of grey-green paste that it presses back into the cracks between pipes as it walks. They are farming the leak.

Touch the pipe and your hand will not find metal. It will find a cold, startled ripple of armored backs flowing around your fingers, making way and then closing again, never stopping.

**The Swallows — the Witnesses**

And above the isopods, below the bats, tucked into every horizontal the builders accidentally made — the top of a valve wheel, the flat head of a rivet plate, the elbow of an overflow runnel — are the nests.

Cave swallows, but wrong for this altitude. Too dark, too puffed, their feathers slicked tight against the damp like seals. They build with what they have, which is concrete dust and mud made from guano and condensation and flecks of iron rust. Their nests are not cups but brutalist terraces, hard as the wall itself, stacked one above the other wherever there is an inch of ledge. Dozens per pipe. Hundreds in total.

Each nest holds one bird. They sit facing outward, eyes open, heads perfectly still. They do not call. No bird can call here — the frequency that would cut through the turbine scream does not exist in a syrinx. So they stare.

Their eyes are the only glossy thing in the room. When the flickering caged bulb stutters — that arrhythmic stutter you feel in your molars — every single eye catches it at once and throws it back, orange pinpricks in the dark between pipes. Then off again.

They are not waiting for insects. There are no flying insects except the moths that never fly. They lean forward every fifteen or twenty minutes, open their beaks, and drink the air. A swallow will tilt its head to a dripping pipe joint and let the cold mineral water run straight down its throat. Or it will hawk a clump of the airborne plankton as it condenses on its own whiskers. They raise young that way too — mouth to mouth, passing condensation.

You realize very slowly that none of them are looking at you. They are looking *past* you, at the exact center of the room where the drain pulls the half-centimeter skin of black water in a perfect, silent circle. That is their sky. That is the horizon that never changes.

You cannot stand anywhere without being within inches of a hundred eyes. You cannot put your hand anywhere without touching something that was alive before your grandparents were born and will still be shivering, hanging, clicking, and staring in exactly the same configuration after your concrete has crumbled.

They don't notice you the way prey notices predator or animal notices animal. They notice you the way a pipe notices a temporary change in water pressure: a thing to flow around until equilibrium restores.
