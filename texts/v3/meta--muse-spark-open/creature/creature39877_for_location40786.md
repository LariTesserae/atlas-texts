They live close together because there is no other way to live here.

Not close the way animals crowd around a waterhole. Close the way cells crowd in a body. In the Low Country, to be alive is to be touching something else that is alive, braced against it, feeding it, being fed by it, shading it in return.

There is no one ruler here. No apex. If you pulled the whole continent up and looked for a king you would only find a committee that has been in session for ten thousand years and never taken a vote.

### The Ones Who Are The Place

First, the towers themselves.

You called them trees because you have no better word, but they are not trees the way an oak is a tree. Each one is a colony that decided to be a building.

From the outside: thirty meters of fluted, grey-green flesh, pleated like a cactus to swell and shrink with the night's dew. Bark that is not bark but a living skin, waxy, dust-filmed, pocked with breathing pores that close to pinpricks by noon. Dozens of trunks from one basal cauldex the size of a house, rising and re-joining, fusing where they rub into arches and buttresses and hollow columns big enough to walk through.

From the inside: they are mostly air. Chambers and galleries run through the pith like lung alveoli, some dry and papery, some slick with stored water, some packed with a sweet, fermenting pulp. Every surface exudes. Sap wells up in gutters along the ribs and runs down to cisterns at the branch axils — shallow cups formed by the plant itself, lidded with a waxy leaf that lifts at dawn to catch dew and closes at noon to keep it.

They are not individuals. You can't count them. Where one ends and the next begins is a matter of opinion, because their roots have grafted underground into a single mat, and their canopies have grafted overhead into a single roof. What you walk through is one organism the size of a country that has learned to pretend to be a forest so it can have more surface area.

They do not think fast. They think in seasons, in the slow hydraulic sigh of water moving from root tip to rosette. But they remember. Break a branch and the whole sector seals its pores for a week. Cut a fissure-avenue too wide and new stilt roots will race to close it within a month, thin as fishing line at first, thick as your thigh by winter.

They flower almost never, and when they do the entire district does it at once — a sudden eruption of pale, bell-like blooms from the high canopy that open for one cold night, fill the air with a heavy, yeasty scent you can taste on your teeth, and are gone by sunrise, already being dismantled by everything that can fly, crawl, or glide to reach them.

They are the landlords. Everything else lives in the rooms they make.

### The Ones Who Work The Vertical Rain

The dew economy has its own guilds.

High up, where dawn comes first, are the Combers. Felted epiphytes — grey, hairy spheres that look dead — unfold at night into feathery nets three times their daytime size. They don't root. They hang from bark with a single holdfast and live entirely on what they can condense out of the air. By midnight each filament is beaded with water, by 4 a.m. each bead has run down to a central tank leaf at the base. If you squeeze one you get a mouthful of slightly salty water. Nothing does. To puncture a Comber is to be marked by its smell for days, and everything predatory knows that smell.

Below them, the Catchers: black-green vines with leaves shaped like funnels, like spoons, like cupped hands. They don't bother condensing much themselves. They steal. Positioned under Combers, under rosettes, under any drip line, they intercept the tick-tick-tick of dewfall hand-to-hand and channel it down their own grooved stems. A single vine can water a columnar trunk from ten meters away.

Lower still, where light is amber and cool, the Filters — sheets of lichen and liverwort so thick they look like industrial carpet, sealing the floor of every understorey street. They are the last catch before the basalt. What water escapes everything above ends in them, and they hold it like a sponge for hours after the sun has taken it from everywhere else. Step on it and it sighs. Tear it and it bleeds clear.

And moving through all of it, constantly, are the Groomers. Not a species but a job. Tiny mites, springtails, iridescent beetles no bigger than rice grains, in numbers that make the bark look like it is shifting. They clean. They scrape dust from pores, eat dead Comber filaments, prune fungal hyphae, keep the gutters clear. Without them the dew system silts up in a season. The towers feed them with wax scales shed deliberately from their skin. It is salary.

### The Ones Who Move Without Sound

Animals here have made the same bargain: noise is water, movement is water, heat is water. So they have become quiet, slow, and cool.

In the root-dark — the basements between stilt-roots where air never moves — live the Processors. Fist-sized, glossy black beetles with shovel heads that do nothing but patrol the runnels of compacted leaf litter, rolling balls of dung, shed skin, fallen flower, dead Groomers. They bury each ball in a side chamber inoculated with white fungus, and farm it. The fungus does the chemistry they cannot. In a week the ball is a soft, protein-rich cake. They eat half, leave half for the towers, whose root hairs invade the chamber to absorb the leachate. If you crouch in the dark you will hear them, very faintly: the dry rustle of chitin on dust, like someone turning pages far away.

Sharing those low streets are the Salt-Lickers: blind, pale, salamander-things about as long as your forearm, with heads like shovels and feathery external gills they can fold flat against their necks to keep moist. They travel nose-to-tail in files of twenty or thirty, following scent trails centuries old, lapping the white salt fur that forms in hollows, excreting the excess through glands on their backs that crust over, then rubbing that crust off on special scratching-post roots the towers grow just for them. The towers want the salt moved away from their bases. It's payment for passage.

In the middle tiers — the broad, springy platforms where branches braid together — lives the real city traffic.

There are the Ribbon-Runners. You'd call them rodents, but they have the wrong social life. Each one is small enough to fit in your palm, sandy-grey, with enormous black eyes and hind legs built for leaping, and ears that can fold shut like wallets. Alone they are nothing. Together they are a river. Colonies of ten thousand pour along fixed highways of interwoven branch, in two lanes — outbound empty-handed, inbound carrying. They carry pith scraped from fresh growth, seeds winnowed of chaff, the honey-paste grubs that live only in the ferment chambers, fragments of dead gliders to line their nurseries. They do not squeak. They drum. Two quick taps of hind foot on resonant wood: *go. stop. danger above.* The wood carries it further than air ever could, and costs almost no water.

They gnaw nursery hollows into living wood that never rots, because the tower immediately seals the wound with fast-growing cork, making a perfect, sanitary, climate-controlled apartment. In return the Runners defend their sector viciously from borers, chewing invading beetle larvae out of the bark all night. You can see old battle scars: branch platforms pocked with healed-over craters.

They are not intelligent the way you are. They are intelligent the way a city is — no single Runner knows where the grubs are, but the river does.

Above them, launching from tower to tower without a sound, are the Gliders. Not birds. Not bats. Something older that solved flight separately, when there was still open sky. Leathery wings stretched between elongated ribs, bodies light as dry leaves, heads dominated by vast, convoluted nostrils like conch shells. They are rebreathers. Every exhalation is tumbled through spirals of moist membrane before it leaves, so little water is lost even in flight. They hunt at dusk and dawn, snatching Runners who stray from cover, plucking beetles off leaves, sometimes clinging to a trunk and inserting a long, hollow tongue into a sap cistern to drink. They roost in thousands, packed flank to flank in the shaded streets between fused bases, hanging by hooked thumbs, breathing together so slowly you can barely see it. Disturb a roost and they don't shriek. They exhale all at once — a warm, sour gust — and vanish on silent wings.

On the trunks themselves, perfectly still, are the Watchers. Lizards with water-filled throat sacs that pulse faintly, like pale lanterns. They don't move for hours. They are ambush display artists. When a rival enters their patch of bark they don't chase. They inflate the sac, which flashes — not with color, with *light*, a quick ripple of iridescent blue-green made by bacteria they farm in the sac fluid — and deflate. The message is clear and costs no chase. Females choose the male whose light holds steadiest, which means he is best at holding his water.

### The Ones Who Wait

Predators here cannot afford to be wolves. They must be monks.

The Bark-Mantis hangs on vertical trunks, as long as your forearm, the exact grey-green-dust color of cork, with forelimbs folded so perfectly they look like lichen. It does not stalk. It picks a drip line where Runners must pass and becomes part of the wood. Its metabolism drops so low its skin is cool to the touch. It can wait a full day and night, swaying slightly as if in wind, until the vibration of feet tells it something is within reach. Then one strike, too fast to see, and it is eating, already becoming bark again. It drinks nothing. All its water comes from its prey, which it exsanguinates completely and leaves as a dry husk the Groomers will take apart before noon.

Higher, the Ribbon-Snakes — which are not snakes but limbless, burrowing cousins of the salamander-things that have taken to the canopy — move with glacial patience through curtains of hanging moss. They are as thick as your wrist, patterned like sun through leaves, with heads that can unhinge impossibly wide. They hunt Gliders at roost by tasting the air for the faint ammonia of packed breath. They never strike while the Gliders are awake. They wait until the coldest hour, when the Gliders are torpid, and slide among them like another hanging vine.

Even plants predate here. There are pitcher rosettes the size of buckets tucked into branch forks, not for insects but for dew — and for the occasional Runner that slips. Their rims are slick with wax that only becomes treacherous when wet, at dawn, when everything is scrambling for water.

### The Whole

What lives here, then, is not a list. It is a single trick learned a thousand ways: *how to be wet without water.*

Every creature rebreathes its own breath. Every plant catches its own shadow's condensation. Urine is not excreted; it is wrung dry inside the body and the salts wiped off later. Feces are dry pellets, immediately claimed. Even death is dry — a Glider that dies in flight mummifies before it hits the lower tiers, where beetles dismantle it fiber by fiber and the tower reabsorbs it through its roots the same week.

If you stay long enough you stop seeing species and start seeing neighborhoods. That fused pair of towers with the broad platform between them is a Runner borough, noisy with foot-drums at shift change. That dark fissure-avenue where the salt fur is thickest is Processors and Salt-Lickers only; Runners avoid it because Gliders watch it. That sun-blasted upper vault where Combers hang thickest is almost empty of animals at noon — too hot, too exposed — but at 3 a.m. it glitters with condensed water and everything in a half-kilometer radius is there, drinking in silence, shoulder to shoulder, predator and prey suspended by thirst truce.

There are no humans here. Not yet. If you came you would learn, as everything else has, to move low, to speak rarely, to eat a fallen leaf for thirst, to listen for the ticking of dew falling from hand to hand to hand in the dark, and to understand that this desert is not empty because it was never allowed to be. Life arrived at the same time as the rock cooled, and simply refused to leave any of it unused.
