You don't pack for this city the way you pack for other cities. There is no street map to learn, no currency to exchange, no phrasebook that will help much. You pack like you're going to be a guest inside a lung.

This is not a ruin. This is not an abandoned place. This is working infrastructure that happens to be beautiful, and it will not pause for you. Everything here is intentional - the dimness, the spacing, the silence - and your job as an ordinary, unmodified human visitor is to not break the intent.

Here's how you do that, and how you come back healthy.

### 1. Recalibrate Your Senses Before You Go

The first shock of this valley isn't visual. It's proprioceptive.

**Light:** Spend a few days before departure living in dim amber light. No overhead LEDs, no screens at full brightness, no direct sun. Get a pair of warm amber work lights and live under them. Your eyes need to remember how to see without shadows. Here, the sky is a lightbox, not a light source. Contrast is almost zero. Depth perception goes strange. Edges soften. You can stare straight at the sun's position at noon and see only a brighter bruise. People get headaches the first day because they're squinting for shadows that never come. Bring eye drops. The air isn't quite dusty, but it's perceptually dry - that warm haze tricks your lacrimal system.

Do NOT bring a high-lumen white flashlight. The towers are matte for a reason - they drink the dim light and keep heat down. A 2000-lumen headlamp swept across a building face is rude at best, and at worst you'll bleach the gardens clinging to it. Bring a dimmable amber-red headlamp, <50 lumens, with a true low mode. You will almost never need it.

**Sound:** This place is profoundly quiet. Not silent - *hushed*. No engines, no HVAC hum, no city drone. You'll hear the rustle of thousand-pound curtains of bromeliads shifting to face wind, the soft *pop* of pitcher plants opening at dusk, the basalt ticks as it cools, and, if you're close enough to a large tower, a slow inhale that you feel as a pressure pop in your inner ear. Newcomers start talking louder to fill the void. Don't.

Practice silence. The locals communicate with economy. If you travel with a partner, agree on hand signals. Your voice will carry kilometers in the still air.

**Scale and Distance:** This is a valley kilometers across with only 10-20 structures in it. Between them is nothing but ankle-deep moss and open air. Your brain, used to reference points, will badly misjudge distance. That tower that looks like a ten-minute walk is 3km away. Weather really does appear an hour before it arrives - you can watch a rain shaft approach like a curtain.

Train: Go somewhere flat - salt flat, prairie, beach at low tide - and practice judging distance without buildings. Bring a laser rangefinder if you must, but don't rely on it. The gravity fields bend light a little.

### 2. Your Body In That Air

The air is breathable. Sea-level-adjacent pressure, warm (28-30C), humid but not saturated, like breath held. You won't need a rebreather. You *will* need to manage water, heat, and pressure in ways you aren't used to.

**Clothing:** Forget anything waterproof. Forget anything heavy. Forget synthetics that don't breathe.
- Dark, loose, long layers. Long sleeves and pants despite the heat. The locals wear dark dew-collecting clothing for a reason: dark surfaces radiate and condense. You won't be as efficient, but black or charcoal loose linen/merino will help you catch dew and shield you from the diffuse UV that still gets through the stratocumulus.
- No exposed skin if you can help it. Not for cold - for abrasion. That moss mat is springy and soft, but underneath are angular, unweathered scree plates. One fall on the short talus at the base of the scarps will fillet your knees. It's a young valley - nothing has been rounded by time.
- Footwear: Tall, soft, absolutely waterproof-from-below boots with ankle support, but breathable uppers. Like bog boots or jungle boots. You will be standing in a living sponge. Water wells up around your ankles with each step. You want to stay dry from ground seep and still let your feet vent heat. Bring gaiters. Bring extra socks in dry bags. Trench foot is a real risk if you stand still too long in the moss.

**Water:** Do not expect to find a stream, lake, or tap. This is an endorheic basin with no rivers, by design.
* Take water only from the air, as they do.* That is not a metaphor. It's Rule #2 and it's also hydrology.

Rain comes sideways, hard, warm, and brief - 20 minutes and it's gone, never pooling. The towers collect it in moments with capillary skins that go pitted and dull. You cannot collect that fast by holding out a bottle.

You need:
1. A large, passive condensation collector. Simplest: a 1m x 2m piece of dark, hydrophobic fabric (silnylon with a DWR coating, or better, a fine mesh like a fog net) strung between poles at night. You will get liters by morning. This is non-negotiable for stays >24 hours.
2. A personal dew-rag - a dark bandana or shemagh you wear on shoulders/head. In the morning, wring it out. That's ~100-200ml free.
3. A way to drink from the pitcher plants *without* harming them, which is: don't. Those barrel-sized pitchers are the city's cisterns and the glider's drinking fountains. They are not yours. The locals will notice if you dip into them. You can refill your condensation net water by holding it under a tower garden drip - those once-a-minute drops from hanging forests. Sit under a beard of epiphytes on the leeward side of a tower and just wait. It's slow, clean, and permissible.

Bring oral rehydration salts. You will sweat more than you realize because the humidity fools you into thinking you're not losing water. You are.

**Pressure and Gravity:** This is the part that gets ordinary humans.

The towers breathe - literal nanoscale pores opening and closing. When a big one inhales, you feel your ears pop. If you have any Eustachian tube dysfunction, sinus congestion, any cold at all - **do not go.** The slow pressure oscillations will leave you in agony and you won't be able to clear it.

The transit threads - those invisible held places high up where gravity is politely told to lean sideways - are not for you to use casually. You see them as heat shimmer between towers. A pod or a person steps off and falls *horizontally*. It is silent.

As an unmodified human, you have no field harness. Do not try to step onto a thread. Without mediation, the gradient at the edge will hit your vestibular system like a slap. Travelers report immediate vertigo, nausea, and a lingering sense that "down" is wrong for hours. If you must move between towers, do it on the ground, on foot, through the moss. It will take hours. That's correct. The city is *supposed* to be sparse so the wind can pass through - Rule #3. You're meant to cross the emptiness slowly.

And the Stillness - that half-kilometer-wide column between the four largest towers where rain falls slowly, stops, rises, and evaporates, where dust hangs forever and glows faintly at night with captured charge.

Give it a wide berth. At least a kilometer. You will be tempted. It looks magical. It is a standing low-gravity well and a static trap.
- Don't fly a drone into it.
- Don't throw things into it.
- Don't walk into it to feel what low-gravity feels like.

Dust in there never settles because it never *can*. It's charged, fine, basaltic and biological detritus, held in suspension for weeks. Inhale that and it won't come out. Bring an N95 or better and wear it if the wind shifts from that direction. If you see that manta-like thing - the big, slow filterer that turns circles in the column - stop, watch from outside, and leave. It is either an animal or a maintenance system and either way it does not want company.

Add to your kit: anti-vertigo meds (meclizine), electrolyte chews, pressure-equalizing earplugs for fliers, and a small pulse oximeter. The air is warm and still enough that heat exhaustion sneaks up without you noticing.

### 3. How To Move and Where To Sleep

There are no roads. No cars. The ground is for growing - Rule #1.

That means:
- **Do not pitch a tent that crushes the moss.** That moss-ferns mat is not decoration. It's the primary ground-cover condenser and soil-retainer. Every square meter you smother is damage. If you must camp on the ground, bring a hammock. Sling it between... nothing. There are no trees. So bring a freestanding hammock stand or, better, a tiny bivvy that you move every night, or ask politely (with gestures) to shelter on a tower balcony. The locals occasionally allow it if you are clean, quiet, and small.
- **Never drag anything.** Lift and carry. The hanging gardens have rootlets and stolons that can stretch meters across what looks like empty air on invisible cables. Trip on one and you'll rip a 10-year-old vine wall.
- Walk on the moss with soft steps. It's ankle-deep and forgiving, but beneath it is that young, sharp scree and sometimes voids. Use poles. Test before you put weight. The valley was torn open a few million years ago - geologically yesterday - the walls are still sheer, slabs still settling.

Sleep will be odd. No true night, no true dark. Perpetual late-afternoon amber-gray, luminous without being light. Bring a true blackout eye mask and earplugs not because it's noisy, but because the lack of darkness will prevent melatonin. You will feel jet-lagged without moving.

### 4. How To Behave Around the City and Its People

The buildings are alive in the way a skin is alive. That matte dark weave that drinks light and shifts polarization is nanoscale, healing, opening pores the size of pinpricks. When it rains, it goes pitted all over.

- Do not touch the building skins if you can avoid it. Oils, sunscreen, DEET - they foul the pores. If you must stabilize yourself, use the back of a clean hand briefly.
- Do not apply sunscreen with oxybenzone, or strong insect repellent, before approaching a tower. If you need sun protection, use physical zinc on face only, or just cover with dark cloth.
- Do not take samples. Not a leaf, not a pitcher of water, not a fleck of wall. People have tried to peel a bit of that skin. It doesn't come off cleanly and the field system notices. You'll be politely but firmly escorted to the valley edge and not allowed back.
- Be quiet, be dim, be sparse. That's literally the city's four rules:
  1. Do not build on the ground.
  2. Take water only from the air.
  3. Keep the city sparse, so the wind can pass through.
  4. Keep the light dim, so the heat doesn't build.

You following those rules visibly is how you show respect. Don't bring a bright camp lantern. Don't cluster your gear. Don't try to be helpful by clearing moss.

The people: You'll see a figure tending a vine wall kilometers away, or two silhouettes falling sideways between towers. They wear dark, loose, dew-collecting clothing. They are not loud. Approach slowly, from below if on a balcony, hands visible, no sudden light. A simple open-palm greeting is usually returned. Don't shout up. If they don't respond, move on. Privacy is the culture here; density is intentionally low. Assume every person you see is busy keeping the climate machine running.

Animals are rare - large, solitary gliders with translucent wings who live their whole lives on one tower, salamanders as long as your arm that only move after rain. Leave them alone. The ecosystem is engineered to discourage swarming insects and flocking birds. That means there is little redundancy. If you kill the one glider on this tower, you've killed that tower's glider population.

### 5. What To Actually Pack - The Short List

For an ordinary human, 2-4 day stay:

- Dark, loose linen/merino layers, long
- Bog/jungle boots + gaiters + 3x merino socks in dry bags
- Wide-brim dark hat
- Amber-red dimmable headlamp + blackout eye mask + earplugs
- 2x fog-net panels + collapsible poles + cord (your water plant)
- 2L soft bottles + 500ml wring-rag/bandana + ORS
- N95 masks (for Stillness dust events)
- Meclizine, ibuprofen, blister care, antihistamine
- Light trekking poles
- No-perfume unscented biodegradable soap (use far from towers/gardens, or not at all)
- Small binoculars (8x, dim-light capable) - for watching the transit threads without entering them
- Rangefinder if you're bad at distances
- A journal, not a recorder. Electronic hum is noticeable in that quiet.

What *not* to bring: drone, bright lights, ground tarp that kills moss, aerosol sprays, loud generator/battery bank with fan, offerings of food/water (they have their own perfect cycle).

### 6. The Feeling You Should Expect

Most travelers prepare for danger and forget to prepare for affect.

This place is not hostile. It's not welcoming either. It feels like standing inside a greenhouse the size of a city that was built before you arrived and will continue breathing after you leave. The towers rise like dark trees, bearded with forests, crowns lost in haze. The young cliffs stand sharp and blue-black. The whole valley inhales condensation and exhales vapor, gardens opening and closing, gravity leaning invisibly.

You will feel very small, very *visible* - because you are the only thing that doesn't fit the airflow. You will feel an urge to whisper, to not disturb. Follow that urge. That's your best navigational tool.

Don't try to understand how the gravity negotiation works. Don't try to photograph the Stillness to prove it. Just sit in moss up to your ankles in warm, wet-stone-smelling air, watch droplets fall straight down everywhere except in that one golden, glowing column, and let the city breathe around you.

Leave no crushed moss, no stolen water, no bright footprint in the light field. When you go, walk out the way the wind goes - through the gap in the saw-toothed basalt walls - so the city can close behind you without having to route around you.

And when you're back under hard shadows and traffic noise, you'll miss the amber haze more than you expect.
