You should prepare to visit this place like you would prepare to visit a newborn star that is pretending very hard to be a mountain.

That is not a joke. Everything about it is contradictory to what your body expects from a high place at night, and your body will be wrong faster than your mind can correct it. This is not a hike. This is not a desert. This is fieldwork on a planet that hasn't finished loading.

Here is what your intuition needs to know, and what to do about it:

### 1. First, understand what it *is*

This is not an ecosystem. It is a construction site after the workers have left but before the building has cooled.

*   **High, but not cold.** Your entire life, height has meant cold and wind and snow. Here height means thin air and heat radiating from below. At 30-35C in darkness, your brain will tell you you are safe, comfortable, almost sleepy. You are not. You are standing inside a kiln with the door open to vacuum.
*   **Dry, but not desert-dry.** A desert still has water in its history — dry riverbeds, air that remembers humidity. This air has *never* had water. It is chemical zero. It will pull moisture out of you actively, like silica gel. You will not feel like you are sweating, because sweat cannot form. You are just desiccating.
*   **Empty, but not quiet.** Your ears expect life at this temperature. Insects, wind in plants, birds. You will get none of it. You will get mineral noises instead: random gunshot cracks, sighs from collapsing tubes under your feet, wind through 50-meter glass needles that hums like someone running a wet finger around a glass. The silence is so complete it becomes a pressure. Don't trust it. Geology is happening while you blink.
*   **It builds ruins before it builds life.** Those huts, corridors, towers — your pattern-matching brain *desperately* wants them to be buildings. They are not. They are cooling accidents, gas bubbles and salt chimneys and tipped basalt colonnades. They are incredibly dangerous because they invite you to expect shelter, stability, intention where there is none. That perfect corridor will end in a fresh crack glowing orange-white. That cozy beehive hut is a thin shell over a vent that could outgas at 200C without warning.

You are the first soft thing this place has ever met. It has no adaptations for you.

### 2. Your Body Will Fail In Three Ways At Once

You need to prepare for altitude, heat, and dehydration *simultaneously*, which almost never happens on Earth.

**A. The Air:**
At this elevation the air is "so thin it barely holds itself together." You cannot acclimate to this. There is no slow hike up, no base camp. This is effectively 7,000-8,000+ meters equivalent, or worse, plus industrial heat. 

You need:
*   **Continuous supplemental oxygen.** Not a bottle for emergencies — a closed or semi-closed system with a regulator, pulse ox monitor, and alarm. A cannula will not be enough in exertion or when a vent belches CO2, sulfur, or ozone-heavy gas. Bring a full-face or at least nose/mouth rebreather with CO/CO2/H2S filtration. The metallic ozone smell described is a warning: this air is not just thin, it is occasionally toxic.
*   **Practice breathing under load.** You will be walking on sharp, tilting clinker and salt. At low O2 and high heat, even standing will raise your heart rate to 120. Do stair climbs in heat with your rig before you go. If your oxygen fails, you have minutes before bad decisions, not hours.
*   **Ear protection that you can still hear through.** The plateau inflates. Air pressure pops. Subsonic collapses happen under you. You need to feel that pressure change, but also protect against sudden thermal cracks right next to you. Soft filtered earplugs, not industrial muffs.

**B. The Water:**
The fact that your sweat evaporates before it beads means your normal thirst/hydration feedback is broken.

You need:
*   **Pre-hydrate for 48 hours before insertion.** No alcohol, no caffeine, salt-balanced electrolytes.
*   **No exposed skin, not for sun but for water loss.** You want a full-body wicking base layer under a loose, light-colored, non-melting shell. Think caving coveralls, not shorts. Your mouth will dry in minutes — bring a hydration bladder with a bite valve you can sip from every 2-3 minutes without thinking, and add a rebreather-style humidifier or even a simple shemagh / humidifying mask over nose and mouth. It will feel stifling in the heat, but it will keep you from exhaling all your water into the void.
*   **Carry 2-3x what you think you need, and turn back at 2/3.** There is no water to find. No condensation. No cactus. If you drop your bladder and it leaks onto warm basalt, it will hiss and be gone. Bring electrolyte tablets, salty snacks, and lip/eye/nasal moisturizer. Your eyes will dry out. Bring sealed goggles — not sunglasses — because ash is still hanging and a sudden fumarole gust will scour your corneas with warm glass dust.
*   **Watch your urine, watch your brain.** If you stop peeing, leave. If you get headachy, giggly, or suddenly convinced those salt stacks are trying to tell you something, you are simultaneously hypoxic and dehydrated. That is the killer combination here.

**C. The Heat:**
30-35C in the dark feels wonderful until you realize the ground is the radiator.

You need:
*   **Boots, not hiking shoes.** Real mountaineering or foundry boots with thick, insulating, puncture-proof soles. The description — warm basalt exhaling heat, sugary fresh fractures, glazed slag bowls, orange-white fresh cracks that you can feel heat from tens of meters away — means the ground temperature is wildly uneven. You can step from 35C air onto 80C rock without seeing any change. Thin soles will cook your feet. Ankle-high will fill with razor clinker.
*   **Gloves. Always.** Everything is sharp. Those needle ridges are a meter thick at the base and 50 meters tall — they are glass knives. Salt tiles are curling and ticking, ready to snap. Basalt flakes exfoliate with a *ting*. You will want to touch things. The warm halite towers, the smooth slag bowls — they feel inviting. Many will be 50-70C. Thick leather or Kevlar-lined work gloves.
*   **A thermal layer system you can ventilate instantly.** You will be hot while walking, then dangerously hot if you stand near a fresh fissure. Bring a way to dump heat — pit zips, vented back — but also a way to insulate if you have to stop moving. Hypothermia isn't the risk; heat exhaustion is, and it sneaks up in the dark because you don't see yourself getting red.

### 3. Navigation Will Gaslight You

This place is big, dark, and has no reference.

*   **The light lies.** The only reliable light is upwelling from fissures — cherry-red seam-light, pulsing, throwing double shadows behind every spire. It is low, diffuse, and from *below*. Your entire visual system evolved for light from above. Shadows will be inverted. Holes will look like bumps. A glowing crack will blind your night vision for 20 minutes, and there is no moon to readjust to.
    *   Bring a **low-red headlamp** and keep it LOW. Do not use white light unless you have to. You want to preserve your adaptation to the ground-glow. Bring a backup. And bring a tall walking pole/staff — you will need to probe ahead for hot fissures, thin salt crusts over voids, and glass edges hidden in shadow.

*   **The scale lies.** "A raw tableland the size of a country... stretching out of sight in a tangle of ridges and basins, town-to-valley-to-range" — you will not be able to judge distance. No trees, no buildings, no dust haze. A 10-meter tower and a 100-meter tower look the same at night. The horizon is actively *moving* as the plateau inflates centimeters while you watch, enough to make your inner ear lurch.
    *   You **must** have a non-visual tether. Compass will be useless near so much fresh basalt and magnetic anomaly. GPS may not exist in this universe. For a multiverse traveler, this is where you set your anchor/beacon *before you take a single step*. Drop it, test return, confirm lock. Set a hard turnaround timer and a hard distance: e.g., 800 meters max on first visit. Every 100 meters, place a small, *deliberately artificial* cairn — glow stick, reflective tag, IR beacon — so you can find your way back. Do not pile salt slabs. Do not add to the false architecture. The plain is already confused about whether it is natural or built. Don't make it worse.

*   **The map doesn't exist yet.** Because erosion hasn't happened, there are no trails, no watercourses to follow, no stable landmarks. That perfect corridor of basalt columns that runs dead straight for 100 meters? It ends in nothing. That inviting basin glazed smooth as foundry slag? That's a sag where the crust was plastic last week — it may still be warm and ready to sag again. Treat every invitingly smooth or geometric feature as suspect. If it looks too perfect to be natural, it is natural practicing to be perfect, and it has no concern for your weight.

### 4. Your Mind Is The Least Prepared Part

This is the hardest briefing.

*   **It is 0.6/3 wrong.** Nothing overtly impossible, just improvisational physics. Warmth where there should be frost. Darkness that radiates heat. A silence crowded with potential. Your brain will try to resolve this into story: "someone lives here," "this is a test," "those stacks are a message." They are not. That "faint discoloration — rust-red or sulfur-yellow fractal ferns" is chemistry, not a warning, not a growth. That waist-high stack of balanced salt slabs... might be the only intentional thing on the entire plateau, made by someone who didn't need footprints. *Do not chase it.* Do not spend hours looking for more. Do not move it, add to it, or take one as a souvenir — salt that precipitated from hot gas is fragile and scientifically irreplaceable, and if something did place it there, you don't want to announce yourself by rearranging its furniture.

*   **You will feel watched because you are the only watchable thing.** With no life, no wind in leaves, no animal ticks, your attention will turn inward and then outward again, magnified. The humming spires, the ticking salt, the *creep of the horizon* — your vestibular system will tell you the world is breathing. It is. Not metaphorically. The tremor through your soles is real upwelling. This can cause intense awe, intense dread, or intense loneliness in different people. None of it means you should stay longer to understand it.

*   **Time is deceptive.** Geologically busy means the ground you stood on an hour ago might have a new crack. Lava tubes collapse a kilometer away and change the air pressure in your ears. Do not camp near any tower, wall, hut, chimney, or fissure edge, no matter how sheltered it looks. Camp on high, dull, cool basalt away from glow, away from resonance. If you must sleep — and you could, because it's warm dark like an incubator — set proximity heat and gas alarms. Otherwise, plan for short insertions: 2-4 hours maximum, then return. This is not a place to spend the night, even though night is all it has.

### 5. Practical Checklist For An Ordinary Human

**Wear:**
- Insulated-sole mountaineering boots, broken in
- Full wicking base layer + loose abrasion-resistant outer layer
- Leather/Kevlar gloves, goggles, humidifying face wrap
- Pulse oximeter on finger, gas detector on chest

**Breathe/Drink:**
- Closed/semi-closed O2 + H2S/CO/CO2 filtration + spare canisters
- 4-6L water in insulated bladder + electrolytes + backup sealed bottle
- Nasal spray, eye drops, lip balm

**Move/See:**
- Red headlamp + backup + chemical light markers
- 2m probing staff, heat-resistant
- Personal multiverse anchor/beacon + short-range IR waymarkers
- Thermal monocular or handheld IR thermometer — invaluable here. Point it ahead and you will *see* the hot seams before you see the glow.

**Survive:**
- Hard turnaround time (set before you go, obey it)
- No solo first contact — if you must go solo, tell your rig your plan and set auto-retrieval on vitals
- Bring nothing that melts easily, outgasses, or is alive. Spores, seeds, microbes — this is pre-life. You would be a contamination event on a planetary scale. Sterilize soles and gear as if for a Mars sample return, in both directions.

**Remember:**
- Do not step on salt fern crystal growths.
- Do not shelter in hollow scoria huts or fumarole chimneys.
- Do not follow straight corridors.
- Do not touch orange-white fissures, even from tens of meters if wind is towards you.
- Do not stay to watch the horizon inflate. If your inner ear lurches, you are standing on active inflation. Move to higher, cooler, older basalt.

When you leave — and you should leave hungry, wanting more, not exhausted — take nothing but notes, photos in low red light, gas readings, and the strange, quiet feeling that you were briefly the only witness to a world learning how to make itself.

It will not miss you when you're gone. It hasn't invented missing yet. That is its mercy and its warning.
