You are not going to a lake. You are not going diving. You are going to a mountain range that learned how to hold up an ocean, and then grew a forest inside it.

Most travelers get this wrong immediately. They pack for "cold high altitude" or they pack for "tropical dive" and both will get you killed slowly here. This place is neither. It's a high, young, warm, still, dense, bright, silent, knife-sharp vertical jungle where up and down are both sunlit and there is nowhere to stand.

Here is how an ordinary human body gets through it and comes back.

### 1. Understand What You Are Actually Visiting

Stop thinking "swimming."

**The rock is newborn.** This plateau was forced up violently and recently, geologically speaking. That means no sand, no soil, no rounded river stones. Everything is *freshly broken*. Basalt teeth, scoria like frozen foam, glass-sharp obsidian veins, cliff edges that will slice a wetsuit and your hand open if you grab them for balance. You cannot go barefoot, bare-legged, or bare-handed. Ever.

**The water is not cold.** It's bathwater. 30-36°C / 86-97°F, top to bottom, held warm by sun and by the hot stone underneath. You will not get hypothermia. You will overheat, dehydrate, and prune until your skin macerates if you stay too long. It's less like the sea and more like being inside a huge, living greenhouse tank.

**The water is not empty.** It's broth. Spores, pollen-analogues, floating seeds, leaf-fall snow, bacterial mats, translucent skin-sheets peeling off trees. Visibility is beautiful and terrible - on a clear day you can see 20-30 meters, but it's *textured* air, not clear water. Like swimming through warm, sunlit dust.

**The forest has structure.** There are three layers you need to learn to navigate:

1.  **The Membrane:** The top 0-2 meters where water pretends to be air. Glassy, elastic, sometimes raining upward. You can poke your head through it into real, thin mountain air.
2.  **The Canopy:** 15-25 meters down. Interlocking door-sized leaves forming a false floor/roof. Almost continuous. Darkens everything below into mottled gold-green. You will spend most of your time *under* this.
3.  **The Trunk Labyrinth:** From canopy down to rock. Pale buoyant trunks, rope-vines, gas-bladder strings, sideways lilies, cliff-moss carpets. This is not open-water swimming. It's climbing through a flooded forest with no ground.

There are no beaches. No dry trails between peaks. If you want to move more than 10 meters, you have to be *in* the forest, breathing differently, pulling yourself along.

If you can hold that picture - sharp young mountains drowned under warm soup, with a slow forest growing through it - you can pack correctly.

### 2. Your Body Before You Go

**Altitude acclimation is non-negotiable.** The rock plateau itself is kilometers up. The *air* above the Membrane is true high-mountain air: thin, dry, high-UV, low oxygen. You will need to breach the surface to breathe if you're on open-circuit scuba, and you will be breathing that thin air. Spend 2-3 days staging at 2500-3000m equivalent before you jump, or arrive with supplemental O2 for surface intervals. Pulmonary edema kills fast up there and rescue is non-existent.

**Heat training.** Do long soaks in 35°C water before you go. Learn what heat exhaustion feels like while swimming: headache behind the eyes, nausea, suddenly-too-quiet heart. You will sweat *into* your suit and you won't notice fluid loss. Pre-hydrate with electrolytes, not just water. Plan to exit every 90-120 minutes whether you feel like it or not.

**Ear and sinus prep.** You won't be going very deep by ocean standards - 10 to 40 meters to the canopy, maybe 80-100+ in the troughs - but you will be changing depth constantly as you pull yourself over ridges and under leaves. Practice very gentle, frequent equalization. Warm water and spore-load makes outer-ear infections rampant. Bring alcohol-vinegar ear drops and use them religiously after every immersion.

**Allergy/immune prep.** That "pollen" in the water is reproductive material. It *wants* to stick to you. Travelers with asthma, severe pollen allergies, or sensitive skin get hammered. Bring a good antihistamine, a steroid nasal spray if you use one, and consider a full skin barrier. Do not go with open cuts. That warm, iron-rich, porous rock water is a bacterial paradise.

### 3. How You Breathe

You are human. You cannot breathe this water. Do not try to romanticize it.

You have two workable options:

**A) Full-Face Mask with rebreather or long-hose back gas.** This is the correct choice. Why full-face?
* It keeps that spore-broth out of your nose, mouth, and eyes.
* It protects your face from razor scoria when you squeeze between trunks.
* It lets you talk/comm with a buddy, which you will need because hand signals disappear in leaf-shadow.

A closed-circuit rebreather is ideal here because it's quiet - you won't terrify the few slow animals - and it doesn't blast bubbles that get tangled in the canopy and disorient you. But a simple, well-tuned open-circuit with a 5-7L pony bottle is fine if you keep dives short and shallow. Remember: at altitude, your gas consumption tables are liars. You will use air 30-40% faster.

**B) Surface-supplied snorkel / hookah if you stay very shallow.** Some travelers rig a floating intake raft that sits on top of the Membrane and dangles a long snorkel hose 5-10m down. It works for puttering in the canopy top, but the hose snags instantly in the trunk labyrinth. Don't rely on it.

Bring a redundant air source. There is no "swim up quickly for air" here. Up is a ceiling of leaves.

And bring a *nasal* plan for when you do surface: the air above the Membrane is cold and bright and painfully dry after the warm bath. Your sinuses will crack. Keep water for your nose.

### 4. How You Dress

Forget tropical dive skins. Forget drysuits.

**You need armor that still dumps heat:**

* **A 1-2mm full-body suit with reinforced Kevlar/ Cordura** on palms, knees, shins, forearms, and butt. The rock will shred neoprene. The vines will chafe you raw. You need slide protection, not thermal insulation. Many travelers wear a thin merino or polypropylene base layer underneath to wick sweat and reduce skin maceration.

* **Hard-soled booties with ankle support** AND open-water fins that fit over them, but *also* bring **gloves you can climb in**. 3mm dive gloves with textured grip are mandatory. You will be grabbing trunks, not finning. Some bring lightweight canyoning gloves over them.

* **Hood or at least a head sock.** Not for warmth - for sun and spore protection. At that elevation, UV through water and through the Membrane still burns. The top of your head and back of your neck will cook in that amber light.

* **Eye protection always.** Even if you take your mask off on the surface, wear UV-blocking glasses. Photokeratitis at altitude + water reflection is brutal.

You will overheat if you exert. Move slow. That is not advice, it's the physics of the place. Everyone there moves slow.

### 5. How You Move

You do not swim. Swimming is wasted energy in a forest.

Learn to **pull-climb.** Hand over hand on trunks, gentle leg push, long glide. Like caving or zero-G. Fins are for occasional steering, not propulsion; in dense trunk zones they just tangle in hanging bladders and red weed curtains. Many experienced visitors use short, stiff freediving fins or even just booties.

**Neutral buoyancy is life.** Get perfectly weighted *before* you go deep. In warm fresh water? You're going to be buoyant as hell, especially with trapped air in your suit. Bring less weight than you think, distribute it, and practice hovering motionless. Any silting - and sediment here falls like deliberate snow - will hang for an hour and blind you and everyone else.

**Tether.** Bring 20-30m of thin, floating guideline on a small reel. Tie off at ridges. The forest repeats. The light comes from everywhere. Basalt is magnetic and will make cheap compasses wander. The canopy removes the sun and the Membrane as landmarks. Without a line, after three ridge crossings you will not know which trough you came from. That is how people get lost and run out of air staring at identical trunks.

**Light.** You won't need a primary dive torch during the day - the whole water column glows. Bring a small, warm-tinted backup light anyway to look under leaves, into caldera cracks, and to signal. And bring a strobe/marker you can clip to your entry point. The canopy looks the same from below everywhere.

**Sound discipline.** Talk softly on comms, move softly. The hush is part of the navigation. The creak of trunks, the tap of rising pods, the hiss of vents - you'll learn to hear a grazer coming 10 meters away before you see it because the forest goes *quieter* in a specific way. If you're thrashing, you miss it.

### 6. Hazards They Don't Put On The Briefing Sheet

* **Cuts + warm water = infection within hours.** Carry waterproof wound closure strips, povidone-iodine, and broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment in a dry pouch on your person. Clean *every* scrape immediately, even tiny ones. Don't touch your face with scratched gloves.

* **Dehydration and hyponatremia.** You sweat profusely but don't feel it. You swallow small amounts of warm, mineral-heavy water without noticing. Drink 250-500ml of electrolyte water every hour you're in. Pee is not sterling - if you haven't peed in 3 hours, get out.

* **Falling leaves / ghost forests.** Old fallen fronds hang suspended for months, pale and translucent, forming false floors. You can get under one thinking it's the surface and panic. Always check for the Membrane's distinct shimmer, not just "light above."

* **Gas bladders.** Those bead-strings on the vines pop. Individually harmless, but large mats of them can release a burst of gas that briefly changes your buoyancy and fills your mask with organic oil. Don't yank.

* **Vents.** On trough floors, warm seeps release thin bubble threads. Pretty, but some are genuinely hot (45°C+) and sulfurous. Don't lie on the bottom where you can't see. Hover a meter above and look first.

* **Slow time psychosis.** This is the real danger for ordinary humans. No waves, no wind, no birdsong, no fast movement. Just slow creaks and amber light that barely moves all day. Time dilates. People stay in too long because "it feels like 20 minutes" and it's been 2 hours. They get quiet, contemplative, and lose urgency. Set a vibrating timer on your wrist. Obey it, not your feeling.

* **The animals are shy for a reason.** Do not chase the white grazers or the manta-flappers. They have almost no predator response to humans because nothing hunts fast here. Stress can kill them. And the grazers, despite their soft mouths, will accidentally ram you if cornered in a trunk maze - they're the size of deer and neutrally buoyant. Give everything 5 meters.

### 7. What to Bring (Checklist for an Ordinary Body)

**On you:**
- Full-face mask (fitted, anti-fog, with comms if possible)
- Rebreather or doubles + pony, altitude-corrected computer
- Thin armored wetsuit + hooded skin + Kevlar gloves + hard booties + short fins
- Weight system you can ditch one-handed
- Guideline reel + surface marker strobe
- Dive computer + analog depth gauge (backup)
- Vibrating timer + whistle (for surface)
- Small backup light + cutting tool (line cutter, not knife)

**In a small dry bag clipped to you:**
- Electrolytes + 1L water (for surface intervals ON your float)
- Ear drops, antihistamine, wound kit, lip balm with SPF
- Thermal emergency blanket (for surface - you will get cold FAST when you get out into thin wind)

**On your float/entry raft:**
- Huge sun hat, UV shirt, glacier sunglasses
- More water, more electrolytes, calorie-dense food
- Supplemental O2 or at least awareness of altitude sickness symptoms
- Repair tape, spare mask, spare gloves
- Anchor - not for bottom, for *canopy* - a small grapple to hook a leaf-stem so your raft doesn't drift while you're under

**Do NOT bring:**
- Spearguns, collectors, sample jars (this place is untouched and slow-growing - a broken trunk takes decades)
- Loud scooters/DPVs (they shred forest and stir silt for hours)
- Single-use plastics that can snag
- Heavy weight belts you can't release

### 8. How to Be There Well

Plan short, shallow loops, not expeditions. The most beautiful part is 5-15 meters deep, just under the Membrane and just above the canopy. You can see sun columns, pierce-leaves steaming in real air, grazers hanging in shadow-play.

Enter from a ridge lip, not a trough. Ridges are where trunks anchor and water is clearest. Troughs are silty, dimmer, with vents.

Touch as little as possible. Pull on trunks, not vines. Never stand on a lily pad-stack - it looks solid, it's a plant.

Breathe slowly. Move slowly. The forest rewards stillness. If you hang motionless for 5 minutes, the broth settles, the light sharpens, and the animals forget you and resume their one-kick-every-minute drift.

And when you leave, do it before you're tired. The exit is always harder: you have to find your line, ascend through canopy, break the Membrane (which feels oddly resistant, like pushing through warm cellophane), and suddenly be in cold, bright, thin air, blinking, dripping, with basalt at your feet again.

Sit. Drink. Warm up slowly. Let your ears crackle. Don't jump straight to another world - your body is still holding that warm pressure and your mind is still on hush-time. Give it an hour.

If you do it right, you won't come back with trophies. You'll come back with the feeling of warm amber light on your face while you're 20 meters deep, the soft tap of seeds rising past your ears like reverse snow, and the strange, quiet certainty that a mountain can hold up an ocean if it's young and stubborn enough - and that you, for a little while, were allowed to climb around inside it.
