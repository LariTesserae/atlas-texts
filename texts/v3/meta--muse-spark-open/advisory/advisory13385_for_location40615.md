This is a gentle place. It will not try to kill you. That's important to understand up front, because the numbers make it sound like something out of Annihilation.

It isn't.

You are not going to be eaten by the forest. You're going to be politely, persistently, damply *ignored* by a town that kept its job after everyone quit.

Think less "haunted woods" and more "Vermont mill town that got tenure as a terrarium." It's melancholy, muffled, cool, green-lit, and profoundly, intentionally alive — but on an industrial schedule. A monkish factory-garden where the machines and the plants have negotiated a truce and you're just there to not mess it up.

If you go unprepared, you won't die dramatically. You'll just get cold, wet, allergic, lonely, mildly lost, and quietly grown over while you were napping. Here's how to not do that.

### 1. Understand What It Feels Like To Be There

Before you pack, calibrate your expectations. Your body and brain are expecting one of two things: Town or Forest. This is neither, and the dissonance is tiring.

**The Climate:** Cool-basin October. Always. 0.8/3 cool means about 50-60°F / 10-16°C year-round, overcast-bright but never sunny or dark. The air doesn't move much because the basin holds it. With 0.9/3 damp, the relative humidity is perpetually 80-95% without being rainy. Everything sweats. You will never be warm-dry inside your clothes unless you make yourself warm-dry. Hypothermia here doesn't come from blizzards; it comes from sitting still for two hours in damp wool in 55°F and getting sleepy.

**The Light:** Normal light 1.6/3 filtered through 2-3 layers of leaf-roof and old factory glass. No hard shadows, no true night-dark. Even at midnight the moss batteries make the copper streetlamps glow amber. Your circadian rhythm will drift. Bring a watch. You will rely on industrial time more than sunlight: the stacks inhale, the sap pumps tick, the western avenue-flowers POP open at ~3pm, the whistle blows the hour. That's your clock.

**The Sound:** 0.8/3 mostly quiet, but not silent. Living mass is the best acoustic insulation ever invented. Sound doesn't echo. A factory whistle two blocks away sounds like it's in the next room with a pillow over it. The loudest consistent noises are *you* — your footsteps on iron catwalks — and water: drip... runnel... valve-click... drip. After day two, travelers report auditory hallucinations that aren't hallucinations: the huge diesels wrapped in root-felt going *thud ... thud ... thud* through the soles of your feet. If you are neurodivergent, noise-sensitive, or anxious in silence, prepare for that muffled-heartbeat feeling. It can be comforting or deeply claustrophobic.

**The Space:** Scale 0.9/3 hamlet, Density 0.9/3 sparse, Fauna 0.7/3 rare. You were built to calibrate distance by other people and animals. Here, there's almost nothing to calibrate with. You can walk a vaulted avenue made of fused trunks the size of a cathedral nave and see no one. Buildings are huge and mostly empty. Workshops weave quilts no one collects. A single heron stands in a perfectly round plaza pool for eight hours. It's not post-apocalyptic; nothing collapsed. It's *post-occupancy*. The social cue is absence with maintenance. This hits harder than horror for most ordinary humans. You'll feel like an intruder at an empty party where the house is still cleaning itself.

**The Action:** Dynamic 2.4/3. It's busy, just not for you. Sap, pumps, filters, vines, looms, flower-tracking, cistern valves. It's like staying inside a beehive. Do not expect the plants to react fast to you. They react slowly, constantly, on schedule. If you sit on a shelf-fungus balcony, they will not grab you. If you leave your backpack in an alcove for 8 days, they will start to incorporate the straps into a wall because you were impolite enough to abandon a niche.

That is the whole threat model: a cool, kind, bureaucratic plant-town that will gently metabolize your neglect.

### 2. Physical Preparation

**You must solve for Damp, Cool, Mold, and Allergies before anything else.**

This is a low basin [Elevation 1.1] with established erosion [2.5/3]. That means: flat, soft-edged, no rockfall, no altitude sickness, easy walking. Also means spores. Every surface that isn't actively tended is in biological equilibrium with filter-lichen, structural moss, black-bark kudzu, and engineered ivy. Weirdness is only 1.1/3 — this isn't space-magic pollen that makes you see God — but it is relentlessly biologically active.

Two weeks before jump:

1.  **Test your tolerances.** Spend a few days in a damp greenhouse, botanical conservatory, or Pacific Northwest in fall. Are you sneezing already? Do you get contact dermatitis? The resident pollinators are managed — half real bees, half gossamer drone-pollinators — but the flower-facades shed a lot of particulate. Get a prescription for antihistamines (cetirizine + nasal spray), and if you have asthma, bring your inhaler plus a spare. The filter-stacks *clean* the air of industrial soot, but fill it with green organic dust. Asthmatics often do fine for 48 hours then crash.
2.  **Condition your skin.** Foot fungus is the #1 reason travelers abort early. Start sleeping with merino wool socks now. Powder your feet daily with antifungal/tolnaftate. Trim nails short. Learn to dry between your toes like a ritual.
3.  **Get used to quiet work.** People crack here from understimulation, not over. Practice 2-3 hours of repetitive manual task without music or podcast: mending, whittling, sorting beans. That's what you'll be doing with residents; mediating between machine and plant.
4.  **Vaccines/health:** Standard tetanus up-to-date — non-negotiable with rust held at orange-black by lichen, pitted brick, iron catwalks slick with biofilm. No special tropical vaccines needed — fauna is rare, mosquitoes are suppressed — but bring basic antibiotics if you can. A cut that stays damp for 3 days in this basin will get weird.

### 3. What To Pack

Think interdimensional field botanist who was also invited to work in a heritage textile mill. Waterproof-breathable, repairable, respectful.

**Clothing System - No Cotton, Ever:**
*   Base layers: 2x merino wool tops and bottoms. Wool stays warm when damp. Synthetics will stink and cling in a week.
*   Mid layer: Light wool sweater or fleece you don't mind staining green-brown forever.
*   Outer: A *breathable* hardshell jacket and pants. Not poncho-style — you need sleeves for catwalks and weaving rooms. Goretex or equivalent, pit-zips essential. You sweat a lot here; non-breathable raincoats become saunas.
*   Footwear: Knees will thank you. Option A: Ironwork catwalks [20 min traversal] are slick metal + condensation = ankle breakers. Option B: Root-stairs [40 min traversal] are soft, uneven, root-mat with give. Solution: waterproof mid-height hiking boots with aggressive tread AND felt-sole pullovers, or a second pair: rubber gardening boots for root levels. Break them in. Bring 3 pairs merino socks total, rotate daily on lines you string inside.
*   Gloves: Thin nitrile-coated gardening gloves. For handling bark, vine plumbing, shelf fungi. Protects from irritation and keeps oils off the plant.
*   Sleepwear: Dedicated dry set sealed in a dry-bag. Never wear your day-damp outside. Psychological game changer.
*   Hat: Brimmed soft hat, not beanie. Keeps drip off your neck on avenues and under canopy.

**Gear:**

*   Dry system: Everything lives in dry-bags or ziplocs. Bring a 10L roll-top dry-bag for sleep kit, electronics. Bring 3 large silica gel packs and actually recharge them on any warm boilerhouse skin. In that humidity, a journal left open will cockle overnight.
*   Light: The lamps come on early via moss battery, but interiors/hollows are green-dim. Small headlamp with red-light mode. Don't use white blast at night in someone's burl-home; it's rude and the leaves close.
*   Tools - and this is cultural: Bring a *small, excellent* pair of bypass pruning shears (Felco #2 style) and a whetstone. Not a machete, not a saw, not herbicide. The First Rule is PRUNE, DON'T CUT. Offering to prune is like offering to do dishes. Residents will trust you instantly if you know how to make a clean 45-degree cut and dab the sap-seal they provide. Bringing a big blade tells them you don't understand the agreement.
*   Water: Despite Water 0.9/3 pockets, **do not be clever**. Collect where they collect. Bring a 1L steel bottle, purification tablets, and a Sawyer squeeze filter as backup. The intentional pools — round plaza basins, cistern-fed barrels — are clean. The Canal (straight black cut with the pointless barge) is stagnant industrial symbiosis water; filter it twice or skip it. Condensation is excellent to drink if gathered from living bark-gutters, less so from iron.
*   Food: Sparse population means sparse services. There is food — communal kitchens in hollowed burls, root-vegetable stews, air-root fiber bread, honey from the real bees — but quantity is low and hospitality is shy. Bring high-density, low-waste supplements for 3-4 days: nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese waxed, oat bars, electrolyte powder. Critical: Electrolytes. You lose salt constantly via insensible sweat and never feel thirsty because air is damp. You *will* cramp on day 3 if you don't supplement.
*   Shelter: You won't camp wild. Locals will offer a green-lit room with root-mat floor (shoes off at edge). Bring your own sleeping bag liner + compact quilt rated to 40°F/5°C. Their bedding is vine-woven quilts from the ever-running looms — beautiful, slightly damp. Your liner keeps you dry and establishes a boundary the plant respects.
*   Health kit: As above + strong antihistamine, fluticasone, hydrocortisone cream, antifungal foot powder/cream, ibuprofen, blister kit (moleskin, Compeed), bandages, antiseptic wash, earplugs (some people need them against the heartbeat thuds to sleep).
*   Navigation/signal: Phone GPS useless under woven willow tramlines and leaf-vaults. Old-school compass works, but grid still readable (avenues = water/light tramlines, blocks = perfect square root-mat housing). Bring a paper map sleeve and bright *natural-fiber* twine for temporary marking. Never plastic flagging. And never leave it. Retrieve it. The plant will eat plastic eventually but resents it.
*   Offering/Gift: Best currency: machine care. Bring a small universal tool kit (screwdriver bits, WD-40 pen, sewing needles + heavy thread, brass brush for cleaning lichen off contacts), and good work gloves. Second best: news, stories, paper books you can leave. They have few new inputs. Don't bring sweets from outside — sugar ferments weird with local yeasts.

**Do NOT bring:**
Fire starters beyond one emergency Bic. Open flame and filter-lichen don't mix; the residents control all heat via wrapped boilers. Herbicide, pesticide, bleach wipes in volume. Loud bluetooth speakers. Drones that look like birds — will confuse actual pollinator drones.

### 4. Etiquette & How To Move

This is built 2.0/3 urban. It has zoning. Respect it.

1.  **Prune, Don't Cut.** If a vine blocks your path, don't yank or snap. Find the node, prune. Ask first: gesture with shears and raised eyebrows works across languages. Residents will show you their angle. Dab the cut with ash-paste if offered. Never cut a load-bearing vine colonnade. Those are street infrastructure.

2.  **Collect Water Where It Gathers.** Don't reroute a bark-gutter because you want it closer to your bed. Don't dam. Watch where beads become threads become cisterns. Place your bottle under a thread and wait. If a barrel has a ladle, that's yours.

3.  **Keep To Sparse Paths.** The town expects sparseness. Walk catwalks or root-stairs, center-channel. Don't sprawl. Don't claim an empty burl-room as yours just because it's empty. Ask. Empty doesn't mean unused; some buildings are being rested fallow for a season while mycelium resets. If you stay >5 days, move your things daily or the shelf-fungus will start softening around them.

4.  **Tend Your Machine.** This is the social contract. Find a task: sweep condensation off a tramline bridge, clear a gutter-root of fallen stipules, turn compost for filter-lichen, help fold a finished bolt of air-root cloth. Even if product piles up untouched, participation signals you understand: human = mediator. Standing idle watching is weirder to them than talking too much.

5.  **Shoes Off.** Inside any root-mat floor, shoes off at edge, placed together pointing outward. Socks can stay on if dry, but bare feet or dry slippers are polite. The living walls are warm and slightly damp to touch; don't wipe them dry.

6.  **Quiet Hours Are All Hours.** Sound 0.8/3. Speak at porch-conversation volume. The factory whistle already marks the hour. No shouting across plazas. If you need to call someone distant, walk to them.

7.  **Flowers & Stacks:** At ~3pm the western avenue-flowers open POP-POP-POP. Don't stand under first opening directly — puff of pollen — and don't pick. Just watch with others. Same for filter-stacks sighing; that lichen inhale is the town breathing. Pause for it. Locals do.

8.  **Barge:** Yes, there's a single barge going back and forth on the Canal for no remembered reason. Don't stop it, try to ride it without asking, or ask why. It's part of the agreement.

Language: Tech 1.9/3 industrial town = regular people, probably English-adjacent plus plant-specific jargon (burl, guttermother, sap-tick, lampcharge). Learn 5 words before you go and you'll be loved.

### 5. Safety & Exit Planning

**Real risks:**

*   Slips/falls on beaded catwalks. Use hand-rail vines (they're sturdy). Test before committing weight.
*   Hypothermia via damp chill. Change to dry sleep clothes immediately upon stopping for the night. Vent jacket while moving, bundle when still. Drink warm broth whenever offered.
*   Getting turned around. All green vaulted avenues look similar in flat light. Count blocks. Note factory cues: sawmill-basilica north, boilerhouse steam south. Always know whether you're going toward or away from the basin lip (where plant architecture thins).
*   Overstay incorporation. Not horror-movie — if you're injured/sick/immobile in one spot >24h warm/damp, check your contact patches for irritation and early hyphal adhesion. Don't panic; just move, wash, dry.

**Psychological safety:**

Prepare for beautiful loneliness. Bring a journal. Write letters you'll send later. Schedule a daily video/audio message home if your jump tech allows async burst. Three hundred people in space for ten thousand means affection is low-volume but deep. They won't crowd you. Initiate small kindnesses: share electrolytes, prune a doorway without being asked.

If you feel the muffled silence pressing: place a hand on a brick wall under bark and feel the diesel thud. Press forehead to leaf-window and watch condensation bead and run. Give your nervous system something slow to track.

**Leaving Cleanly:**

*   Shake out all gear outdoors, inspect seams for seeds, burrs, live lichen flakes. This ecology is contained equilibrium and would love to hitchhike to your damp basement. Most multiverse protocols require 30-second UV/air-shower before re-entry — do it.
*   Return shears cleaned. Leave remaining electrolytes, any spare dry socks.
*   Do not take cuttings unless explicitly given and cleared. Most variants are sterile outside basin conditions anyway, but taking without asking breaks hospitality.
*   Say goodbye to your burl. The room will stay warm for a bit after.

Warm when damp [wool] / Breathable rain shell / Boots + dry socks x3 / Dry-bag sleep set / Pruning shears not machete / Antihistamine, inhaler, antifungal, electrolytes / Headlamp red mode / Steel bottle + filter / Tool kit gift / Journal / Watch

Mantra for when you arrive, shoes at the edge of the root-mat, green light on your face, whistle blowing soft with no echo:

*I am a mediator, not an owner. I prune, I collect where it gathers, I tend my machine.*

Stand there long enough and you'll hear the stacks inhale. The whole town sighs with you. Then you'll get it — not a forest that swallowed a town, but a town that finally figured out how to breathe.
