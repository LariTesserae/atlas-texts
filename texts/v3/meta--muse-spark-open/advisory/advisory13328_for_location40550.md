You cannot survive The Hollow.

You can only visit it. That's the first preparation — understanding that this place is not hostile to you. It is simply complete without you. It doesn't want your kind of life. It has replaced water with heat, dust, and stone, and it works perfectly. You are the error term.

An ordinary human — which is to say, a wet, salty, evaporative animal that needs to drink, to sweat, to hear birds — will die there in a day without preparation. Not dramatically. Quietly. You will dry out like wood left untended.

This is how you stay alive long enough to actually *see* it, and leave.

### 1. Accept the One Law: There Is No Water Here.

Do not look for it. Do not expect to find it. Do not think "but surely the people must have a well."

They don't. Their cisterns are for dust, oil, and fiber. Their plants don't drink and their food isn't water-based the way you understand it. Everything they eat and use is oil, mineral, dry starch, pressed silica-joint pulp. If you try to drink any of it, you will make your dehydration worse. If you try to distill water from the air, you will get nothing — at <10% relative humidity and still hot air, there is nothing to wring out.

**You must bring all of your water with you in a closed system.** And you must bring more than you think.

For an ordinary human at rest in 40-50C / 105-120F dry heat on hot basalt:
* **Minimum 6-8 liters per 24 hours** just for drinking and electrolytes. Double that if you plan to walk around, climb, help sweep spores at dusk.
* **Electrolytes are non-negotiable.** Plain water will flush you and kill you. Bring salt tablets, oral rehydration salts, magnesium/potassium mix. Your sweat will vanish before it beads — you won't realize how much salt you're losing until you cramp and get confused.
* **Do not ration by thirst.** In The Hollow, thirst stops feeling like "I need water" and starts feeling like "I need shade." You will forget to drink. Set a timer: 250ml every 30 minutes, no matter what.

Ideal system: a camelback-style bladder *inside* your clothing, with an insulated hose, so your body heat doesn't cook it. Metal bottles left on hot basalt will scald you. Bring two redundant bladders. Puncture is death.

You will need to carry your water in and carry your wastewater out. Don't urinate on the floor if you can help it — the Blanket Trees will try to etch it, and the people will notice.

Plan for **no more than 48-72 hours** on your first visit. After that, your skin barrier, eyes, and sinuses will be failing even if you're drinking.

### 2. Dress For A Sauna Made Of Stone And Glass

Your instinct will be to strip down because it's hot. Do the opposite.

The heat wells up from the floor. The basalt is warm to the touch, almost hot, all day. Air near the ceiling shimmers. At noon, the center convection column is punishing. You are inside an oven where the heating element is the ground.

You need:

**a) Full skin coverage, loose and breathable.**
Think desert Tuareg, not hiking shorts. Long, loose white or undyed linen/technical sun shirt, loose pants, long sleeves. Air gap is insulation. Direct contact with hot basalt or hot air will cook you faster than clothing will.
Cover neck, ears, scalp. A wide-brim hat with a drape or a shemagh. Your scalp will burn in the sun column and dry-crack out of it.

**b) Moisture-retention underlayer.**
A tight, wicking base layer under the loose outer layer traps a microclimate of slightly humid air. Some travelers use a cheap trick: a damp bandana around the neck inside a sealed collar re-evaporates slowly. Don't soak yourself — you can't afford the water loss.

**c) Feet: closed, thick-soled, broken-in.**
The basalt floor is hot enough to blister bare feet at midday. The reed-alleys are littered with microscopic silica shards when reeds shatter naturally. No sandals. Wear mid-high boots with heat-resistant soles (vibram-style) and gaiters. Bring moleskin. The salt-flat approach above will chew up soft shoes.

**d) Lips, eyes, nose — seal them.**
* Heavy petrolatum lip balm, or better, lanolin + beeswax. Reapply constantly. Your lips will crack in an hour uncovered, and split in a day.
* Nasal saline spray and gel. Your nose will bleed as the mucus membranes dry and crack. Bring it.
* Sealed, vented goggles or wraparound sunglasses. The air is full of fine dust, sloughed Blanket hairs (microscopic, barbed, like asbestos-white tinder), spore puffs at dusk, and hot iron-smelling vine spores. You do not want that in your eyes or lungs.
* A dust mask / shemagh / N95. You don't need a full rebreather, but you do need particulate filtration. At dusk when the Blanket Trees exhale their oily spore puff, you will be grateful. Don't breathe deeply during the exhale.

### 3. Prepare Your Ears. Seriously.

This is not a quiet desert cave.

The Glass Reeds thermosynthesize. They pump hot air. That pumping makes a continuous organ chord, low and loud. When the wind shifts over the Throat, they groan and ping loud enough you have to shout. At peak heat, they *scream*.

The Lantern Vines *whuff whuff whuff* like a sleeping animal all night.

The town itself ticks, rings, echoes. Sound carries perfectly under that low stone sky.

Bring:
* **Foam earplugs + over-ear muffs or good noise-cancelling buds.** Not for the whole time — you want to hear the chime — but for peak convection (noon to ~15:00) and for sleep. The people sit with cloth over their ears at peak and stop work. Imitate them. If you don't, you'll get a headache that turns into disorientation.
* Don't tie anything to reeds. Don't hammer on them. Don't lean a pack against them tightly. Tension makes them sing louder and crack. The night-watch taps them *gently* to prevent resonance-lock. That's a skill. You don't have it. Keep your distance from tight clusters.

### 4. Light, Heat, and Falling

**Entering:**
You don't find the Hollow by searching. You approach across the salt shield — flat, white, ringing, blinding. Bring polarized sunglasses for above, GPS and a physical marker for your return. The Throat from above looks like a crack with rounded lips, not a mouth. There's a stair — really a worn, half-natural slump — on one side. Time your descent for **early morning or late afternoon.** Do not descend when the sun column is vertical through the Throat. You'll be walking directly into a column of white, dusty light and 50C+ air dumping straight down.

**Inside Light:**
Your eyes will take 20 minutes to adjust. It's dim: amber, umber, bruise-purple shadow. The sun column crawls across the floor and never reaches the edges. The rest is ember-light from lamps and warm stone. Bring a **warm-temperature headlamp (amber/red, not white)** with a low lumen setting. Blasting the darkness with a 1000-lumen white spotlight will blind the residents, kill your night vision, and make the plants react — the hairs track light/heat shifts.

Bring a small infrared thermometer. The floor vs. roof delta can be 15C. You can find cooler alleys near the walls at noon.

**Heat Injury Protocol:**
Know the signs because no one in town gets them the way you do. They are adapted.
* Heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, confusion, stop sweating. If you stop sweating in The Hollow, you are in crisis.
* Find shade, drink ORS, cool neck and wrists with a *small* amount of water on cloth. Do not pour out your supply to cool down. Seek the bubble's edge where the roof meets floor — it's dim, cooler, vine-hung, but also where spores are densest. Cover your mouth.
* Have an exit plan that doesn't rely on anyone else rescuing you. Tell someone above where your rope is.

### 5. How To Move Without Breaking Anything

You are in a bowl 300 strides across. You can see the whole town at once. That makes you feel like you can wander anywhere. Don't.

* **Blanket Trees:** They look like solid domes of black coral. They are alive, waxy, acid-etched to the rock. Walking on a *living* one is disrespectful and you can slip on the oil-slick plates. Walking on a *dead* one (the hard, domed platforms with houses on top) is the street. Ask before you step. If the hairs are standing up and lifting, don't touch it — it's combing air.
* At dusk when they exhale, people sweep with brooms immediately. Offer to help if asked, but brush *away* from cracks and doorways. Those spores want to root in any shadowed fissure.
* **Glass Reeds:** Beautiful, hollow silica, thin as lamp chimneys. Sharp as hell when broken. Three meters tall. They tinkle normally. They shatter into needles. Don't cut them for a souvenir. The town presses oil from the joints at specific times; the rest is left to fall and dissolve. If you break one, you break an instrument.
* **Lantern Vines:** Rope-like bladders pulsing in the edge crawlspace. They inflate taut when wind howls over the Throat. Do not poke, pull, or collect the rolling parchment tumbleweeds unless given one. The spore smell of hot iron is an irritant in concentration. Don't sleep directly under a heavy cluster.
* **No animals means no animal logic.** No buzzing, no birds. Just plant noise. After an hour you'll feel watched-by-nothing. That's normal. Bring something human to anchor you — music on low volume, a familiar scarf — so the silence doesn't turn into loneliness.

### 6. The People: Be a Guest, Not a Geologist

There are ~400 people living in dense intention. They have copper adzes, scoria walls, Blanket-plate roofs that last 20 years. They have been there long enough their walls are polished by the same air engine that has run for 10,000 years.

* Copper is sacred because it's rare. Don't touch forge tools, nodules, or jewelry without permission.
* Their market circle sells nothing from outside. Don't try to trade in bottled water as if it's wealth — it's insulting and also you need it. Trade in stories, small tools, help, not exploitation. Don't offer to "fix" their waterlessness.
* They will cover ears at peak. Do as they do. They will sweep at dusk. Do as they do.
* They know the tapping rhythm to keep reeds from shattering. Don't imitate the night-watch unless invited. If you hear *tok tok tok* wandering across the dome, be still and listen. It's a lullaby for glass.

Ask before photographing homes. In that light, a flash is a violation.

### 7. Mental Preparation — The Long Stay Effect

The description is accurate: if you stay too long you forget thirst as a need for water and remember it as a need for shade.

You will stop craving a drink and start craving dimness. That is dehydration psychosis beginning. The amber light, the endless *hmmmm* and *whuff*, the weight of the low basalt thumbprint roof over you — it's a room, not a landscape. Some people feel womb-safe immediately. Others get crushing claustrophobia despite the width.

Bring:
* A way to track time that isn't the crawling sun column. Watch, notebook. The town repairs the same walls forever; without timekeeping you'll lose days.
* Earplugs for sleep to break up the vine-breathing illusion that something large is sleeping beside you.
* An absolute hard exit time written down *before* you enter. And obey it.

---

**Final Checklist For An Ordinary Human:**

Water (6-8L/day) + ORS, in redundant insulated bladders. Wide-brim hat + shemagh, loose long linen outer + wicking base, goggles, N95, petrolatum/balm, saline nose spray, thick-soled boots. Warm headlamp + extra batteries. Foam earplugs + muffs. Infrared thermometer, GPS marker + cord for Throat exit, watch/timer. Small gifts, notebook, no expectations of resupply.

You will come up again onto the hammered shield of salt and basalt, eyes stinging, ears ringing, lips already cracked despite the balm. The air up there will feel thin and cold even though it's furnace-hot, just because it's empty. You'll understand why the town doesn't leave.

Don't try to take The Hollow with you. It can't exist in a bottle. Let it stay a low place, a held breath you fell into, and were allowed to leave.
