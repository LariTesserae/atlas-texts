This is not a ruins world. That's the most important thing to understand before you go.

You're not visiting a place where nature *won* and people left. You're visiting a place where people and plants made a deal a long time ago and both kept it. It's inhabited, industrial, alive, and it is in perfect balance. Which means you, as a visitor, are the destabilizing element. Your job isn't to survive it. Your job is to not get in its way long enough to understand it.

It's a factory-town terrarium the size of a thumbprint, running at 84°F and 95% humidity forever. Here's how to prepare.

### 1. Prepare Your Body Before You Arrive

The air hits first. Travelers describe coming down that shallow roll off the plain and walking into a wall of warm soup. It's not desert heat, it's *wet* heat. The kind that doesn't make you sweat so much as condense.

**If you have any respiratory sensitivity, take this seriously.**
Every brick wall is a 20cm-thick vertical bog. Moss, fig, philodendron, fern, fungus, all alive and sporulating. The town smells like hot brick, steam-oil, jasmine, wet earth, and faintly of mildew. People who live there don't notice. You will. For the first 12-24 hours your sinuses will feel full, your lungs will feel shallow, and you may get a low headache from the constant low-frequency thrum of the mill and turbines vibrating through the masonry.

Preparation:

* **Acclimatize if you can.** Spend 2-3 days before your jump somewhere hot and humid with daily sauna. You want your sweat response working. People who arrive pale and dry from climate-controlled transit get heat exhaustion in two hours because they don't realize they're overheating - the sweat never evaporates.
* **Allergy and asthma meds:** Bring cetirizine/loratadine, your inhaler even if you "rarely need it," and saline nasal spray. Talk to your doctor about a short course of antifungal nasal care if you're prone to allergic bronchitis. The spore load is *enormous*.
* **Hydration salts, not just water.** You will lose electrolytes without ever feeling like you sweated. Bring oral rehydration salts. Drink before you're thirsty.
* **Mosquitoes are a medical issue, not an annoyance.** They're not swarming in clouds, but they are constant, especially at dusk near any canal or drainage ditch. This is standing-water, warm-temperate, bird-light country - that means mosquitoes fill the niche. Assume they can carry whatever mosquito-borne illness exists in your home continuity for hot wetlands. DEET or picaridin 30%+, permethrin-treat your clothes *before* you go, and bring a head net if you plan to sit still near water at dawn/dusk.

You won't get trench foot in a day trip, but any blister or cut will try to get infected immediately. Bring proper wound care and keep feet dry when you sleep.

### 2. What To Wear: Dress Like a Factory Gardener

Forget hiking gear. Forget anything "tactical" or waterproof in the heavy sense. You'll cook.

**Do not bring:** Jeans, cotton hoodies, canvas sneakers, leather boots that can't get wet, anything you aren't willing to stain with algae and rust forever.

**Do bring:**

**Clothes:**
* Quick-dry synthetics or merino/synthetic blends. Light long sleeves and long pants, loose. You want skin covered from mosquitoes, hot pipes, and brushing against walls that will soak you and scrape you, but you want air movement. Think jungle mill-worker: breathable work shirt, light ripstop pants.
* A light rain shell that actually breathes, or better, a poncho you can throw on for the 3-4pm downpour. It *will* rain. Every day. Briefly, violently, then it drips for three hours. An umbrella is actually useful here in the alleys, more than a hood - the rain is vertical and heavy, and you need ventilation.
* 3-4 pairs of thin merino socks in zip bags. Change them.
* Shoes: Closed-toe, quick-drain trail shoes or light jungle boots with good grip on wet cobble and algae-slick concrete. NOT waterproof membrane boots - they will never dry out. You want shoes that get wet and then dry. Bring a pair of cheap camp sandals/slides for indoors/at night.
* A wide-brim, quick-dry hat. The sun isn't savage but the light is heavy and reflected off wet everything. And it keeps leaves and drips out of your neck.

**Skin:**
* Antifungal powder. Gold Bond or similar. Groin, feet, under chest. Every day. This climate grows fungus on grout in real time; it will grow on you.
* Zinc oxide for any chafing.
* Unscented everything. Scented lotion = mosquitoes.

### 3. What To Pack — And How To Pack It

Water and electricity live together here in a way that would give an OSHA inspector a stroke. Pipes sweat, vines grow over transformers, bare bulbs hum behind wet leaves. The town manages this. You don't know its workarounds.

* **Everything in dry bags.** Not "water resistant." Silnylon stuff sacks, zip-locs, a proper dry bag for electronics. Your backpack will be rained on, dripped on, brushed against a weeping wall, and set down in a puddle even if you're careful. Humidity alone will condense inside lenses and phones overnight.
* **Electronics are semi-expendable.** Bring your phone/camera, but bring a hard case, silica gel packets, and expect charging to be weird. The power is real - industrial electrified - but it's local, humming, cage-enclosed 220-ish. Ask before you plug in. Don't run extension cords across wet alleys. A small, good power bank is better than assuming an outlet.
* **Light, not heavy tools.** A small pocket knife, a headlamp with red light for night (the sodium yard-glow never goes out, but alleys are dark under canopy), zip ties, a little roll of tape. Do NOT bring a machete to "clear vines." You will offend everyone instantly and likely damage a structural root-mat that is literally holding a building up.
* **Ear protection.** This is loud in a way people don't expect. Not bangs, but *drone*. Mill turbines + drying fans + water chutes + steam valves on timers + the ticking of a million drips. It layers and it doesn't stop at night. Foam earplugs for sleeping are essential or you'll be awake at 2am listening to a sluice gate creak and a generator buzz.
* **Water and food:** Do not drink canal water, ditch water, gutter rivers, or rain barrel runoff. Even the creek that looks clear is warm, slow, full of taro and hyacinth and industrial memory. Bring a 1L bottle and purification tablets/filter, but plan to ask locals where their potable tap is. Most row-houses have one, fed from the water towers. Eat what they offer - it's mostly canal greens, taro, small fish, mill-canteen stew, bought-in staples. If you have a sensitive stomach, bring bland backup food.
* **Paper will die.** Notebook? Rite-in-the-Rain or it will pulp. Passport/papers in a zip-loc on your body.

### 4. How To Move and Behave

The town is half a mile across. You cannot get truly lost, but you can get *stuck* in the social sense. The grid is warped around boiler houses and canals. Alleys jog. Cobbles heave. Roots make trip ridges. Gutters are ankle-deep canals themselves.

Movement:

* Walk slow. Test footing. Algae on concrete + wet brick + steel plates over canals = slip hazard. People who live there have a flat-footed shuffle. Copy it.
* Don't touch sluice wheels, valves, or hand-cranks unless asked. Water level is managed constantly. Moving the wrong gate floods a basement workshop or drains a dye-race.
* Watch for overhead pipes. They're low, lagged, hot, and vine-covered. A vine on a pipe is not decoration; it's insulation. Don't pull it.
* Staircases are exterior iron, often wet. Use the handrail. Vines are not handrails.
* If you want to stand and watch the heron in the canal - do it from the bank. Don't wade. Don't move it along. Animal stillness is rare and valued here.

Social Protocol - This Is The Most Important Part:

This is a working hamlet-city. Everyone gardens because gardening is maintenance, like shoveling snow up north. Every morning people sweep cobbles, cut vines back from doors and windows, clear gutters with hooks. If they don't, they lose the house.

Rules:

1. **Don't pick, peel, or "tidy" anything.** That mat on the wall is load-bearing. That sapling on the roof is holding the tar down. That reed in the ditch is filtering water to the taro patch. Ask before you even pluck a flower.
2. **Cutting back is a skill, not a favor.** If someone hands you a sickle or secateurs and shows you where to cut, that's an honor. Cut exactly where they show you, no more. Cutting too deep overheats the wall and the mortar washes. Not cutting lets damp into the electrics.
3. **Sweep.** If you're staying in a room or on a balcony, sweep it. Keep your threshold clear. That's how you say thank you.
4. **Talk low, listen.** It's a loud town but people speak at normal volume over it. They are used to the thrum; they will think you're shouting if you raise your voice to compete with the turbines. Lean in a little.
5. **Don't romanticize, don't pity.** Don't say "oh it's so overgrown, you must want to clear it" or "it's so beautiful, you must never leave." Both miss the point. It's neither ruin nor paradise. It's infrastructure that photosynthesizes.

A good opening line is not "What happened here?" but "Who taught you how to cut that wall?"

### 5. Real Hazards For An Ordinary Human

You won't be eaten. There's almost nothing big to eat you. The dangers are boring and industrial-botanical:

* **Electric + water:** Old humming transformers in cages, ceramic insulators dripping with vines. Don't duck under a cage, don't lean on a conduit. Assume any wet metal near a bulb or line could be live.
* **Rot and rust:** Railings look solid but may be half-bark. Test before you put weight. Wooden walkways over marsh behind the dye works are particularly soft.
* **Heat exhaustion:** The wet 80-84°F will lull you. You won't feel "hot" until you're nauseous and headachy. Rest in shade, drink salts, eat. The mill exhale is hotter near the south walls - don't nap there.
* **Respiratory funk:** If you wake up wheezing, leave the wall-side bed and sleep more central with a little fan if you have one. Don't try to tough out a mold reaction.
* **Getting wet and staying wet:** The daily rain is not dangerous, but hypothermia is not the risk - maceration is. If your feet stay wet for 48 hours, your skin softens and cracks. Dry your feet at night thoroughly. Powder them. Change socks.
* **Cats, ants, mosquitoes:** The ants are in endless lines on mortar. They don't bite much but they *are* in everything. Keep food sealed. The thin pipe-rack cats are not strays to pet - they're workers. Watch, don't grab.

### 6. A Light Checklist

On your person, leaving the rim:

* Long light pants + long sleeve shirt, already permethrin-treated
* Quick-drain shoes, dry socks in bag
* Hat, headlamp, earplugs
* Passport/papers in chest zip-loc
* Phone in case + power bank + silica
* Water bottle + salts + purification
* DEET/picaridin, antihistamine, inhaler, antifungal powder, blister kit, small first aid
* Rain poncho/umbrella
* Small gift/trade: good work gloves, sharp secateurs, decent coffee, or fresh fruit from the plain - something useful, not plastic trinkets

At night:
Take everything off the floor. Hang it. Wipe condensation off your bag. Run your headlamp over the wall before you lean against it - check for ants' highway. Put earplugs in. Listen to the town tick.

### 7. Leaving

Don't take cuttings, seeds, or "just a rock" from the spillway. This biome is equilibrium. It doesn't need exporting, and agricultural plain officials *really* don't want that kudzu-fig seed mix spreading up out of the basin.

Sweep your room. Clear the drain in your shower if there is one. Thank the person who showed you the sluice. Walk back up that shallow roll slowly. The air will lighten. The thrum will fade. Your ears will ring a little from its absence.

You'll drip for a while even after you're dry.

If you do it right, you'll remember not a lost city being swallowed, but a place that figured out how to let the jungle carry the grid, and the grid carry the jungle, and the people, in the middle, pruning both so neither falls.
