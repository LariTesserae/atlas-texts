You do not prepare for this place like you prepare for a planet. You prepare for it like you prepare to walk across the inside of a lung in the dark while it's working.

It's not hostile. That's the important thing to get right before you go, because everything about it *feels* hostile. The dark, the wet, the deafening insectile roar, the carpet of moving backs. Your brain will scream PARASITE / INFESTATION / LEAVE. But nothing there wants you. That is much worse for planning, because it means there are no threat displays to read, no predator to avoid, no safe season. It just goes on living over you until you break.

An ordinary human can visit. An ordinary human should not *stay*.

Here is how you visit and come back intact.

### 1. Fix Your Expectations First

This is not an adventure landscape. There are no vistas. The basin is so wide and so low and the cloud is so close that you lose depth perception after twenty meters. Everything becomes haze and sound. No sun ever rises. No night ever falls properly. It's a perpetual deep twilight lit from below.

You are going to a cool, damp, dark room the size of a small country where the floor is alive and the air is full of things the size of your hand that treat you as a boulder that got warm.

You go there to crouch and look and listen, not to hike. If you want to cross distance, plan for 0.5 - 1 km per hour at best. Not because of fitness - because of footing, because of noise, because of needing to watch every step.

If you have:
* severe entomophobia, trypophobia, or acoustic sensory issues
* asthma / severe allergies
* a history of panic attacks in enclosed/open spaces

...do not go without a minder, and seriously consider not going at all. This place finds phobias like water finds cracks.

### 2. Your Body Will Be Wrong For It

Prepare your body before you bring it.

**Cold + Wet = Hypothermia Without Drama.** Air temperature sits a few degrees above the stone. The stone is always sweating. The air is at or near saturation. You will not feel cold until you ARE cold. Shivering starts late. You lose heat by conduction through wet boots, by evaporation off your face, by sitting too long to look at something.

* Train: spend 3-4 hours sitting still outside in 8-12°C / 46-54°F rain in waterproofs. Learn what your hands do when they get slow.
* Pre-hydrate and eat salty. You will sweat more than you think under waterproofs, and the faint ammonia in the air makes you breathe shallow without noticing.
* No cotton. Ever. Merino or synthetic base layer, insulating mid-layer you can vent, waterproof shell top and bottom that actually breathes. Bring two pairs of dry liner socks in double-zips even for a two-hour visit.
* Your skin will soften fast. The varnish-skin organisms shed compounds that irritate abraded skin. Cover every cut with waterproof plaster + tissue glue. Moisturize hands/face beforehand so cracks don't open.

**Air:** It's breathable for humans. That's a trap. It is full of mist-water droplets, shed chitin dust, spores from the amber jelly, metabolic ammonia from millions of overlapping animals, and fine silica/salt dust where crystal gardens are being grazed. One clean breath feels fine. Ten thousand will give you a chemical bronchitis that lasts weeks.

* You need a reusable half-face respirator with P100 / P3 filters. Not a paper dust mask - it will wet out in 20 minutes. Bring spare filters in sealed bags.
* If you wear glasses, use anti-fog or a full-face respirator. The condensation will blind you otherwise.

**Ears:** This is not negotiable. 

The basin is not "noisy". It is saturated. The stridulation, clicking, popping, skittering stacks into a continuous 75-85 dB roar with ultrasonic components around 90+ dB that you feel as pain behind the ears and teeth. The fliers' echolocation bleeds down into human hearing as a whine that triggers pure anxiety. After 30 minutes without protection you will get tinnitus, headache, nausea, inability to think.

* Foam plugs rated 32-33 NRR *under* active noise-cancelling earmuffs. Test the combination at home over loud insect recordings. Wear them 10 minutes *before* arrival so your baseline is quiet, not shock.
* Keep one ear slightly cracked if you need radio, but never both open. Wind + rib-resonance events can spike briefly very loud and will stun fliers - it will stun you too.

**Eyes / Light:** Human eyes will adjust to the dark in ~30 minutes and you will see surprisingly well: the living varnish glows faintly blue-green when bruised, dying fliers glow in the belly, crystals catch starlight. That is enough.

Do NOT bring a big white headlamp. White light washes everything out, kills your dark adaptation, and triggers a feeding frenzy ripple in the varnish - you slap one puddle of jelly and a wave of light travels for ten meters and everything carnivorous comes to look. You will be standing in a dinner bell.

* Primary: dim red headlamp on lowest setting, with strobe disabled. Red doesn't trigger the varnish as badly.
* Secondary: very low amber penlight for looking into pans/cracks. Shield it with your hand.
* Photographers: no flash. If you must shoot, use long exposure on tripod with red focus assist. Accept grain.

### 3. Gear Checklist For A Short Visit (2-6 hours)

Think caver, not hiker:

**Feet and hands are everything.**
* Knee-high waterproof boots with aggressive soft rubber sole. Not hiking shoes - the siltstone hexagons fill ankle-deep with silt and chitin slurry, the basalt ribs are polished and stream with invisible bacterial slime. You *will* slip. Gaiters help keep grazers and gravel out.
* Trekking pole or better, a short caving staff. Use it to test pans: some palm-deep pans have solid bottom, others are silt traps that swallow your boot. Use it to part crab-things instead of your foot.
* Thin waterproof work gloves with good dexterity. Nitrile underneath if you have cuts. You will touch stone constantly to steady yourself. Bare hands leave oils and salt that scar the varnish for days and you inhale whatever you touched.

**Navigation and time:**
* GPS works, compass may not. There are magnetic veins that make water run uphill a few centimeters. They swing a needle 10-30 degrees near ribs. Mark waypoints.
* Bring a physical line if you plan to move more than a few hundred meters from your entry point: 200m of dull 5mm cord, biodegradable flagging. The haze erases landmarks. Everything looks the same after rain. People - experienced people - walk in circles here because every rib looks like progression.
* Watch + alarm. Time dilates in the roar and dark. Set a hard turn-back time no matter how interesting that chime-garden looks. Also set a shorter check-in: every 30 min stop, drink, wiggle toes, check fingers for cold/white.

**Pack contents:**
* Respirator + 2x spare filters + wipes
* Earplugs x3 sets + ANC muffs + spare batteries
* Lights as above + spare cells
* 1L potable water. **Do not drink the basin water.** It looks sky-clear and perfect. It's supersaturated with dissolved basalt, iron, silica, salt, and a soup of lithovore metabolites. Locals drink it because they are made of it. You will get violent cramps and vomiting. Even filtering won't take out dissolved metals. Use it only to rinse.
* Salty snacks you can eat with respirator half-lifted, quickly, downwind. No crumbs dropped - you'll be mobbed by cleaners within seconds and learn regret.
* Small insulated sit-pad. You WILL crouch for a long time. Cold stone will take the heat right out of you through your knees.
* Zip bags for trash. Everything out, including tissues. This ecology runs on scarcity. A single energy bar crumb is a meteor strike.
* First aid: blister kit, waterproof plasters, antihistamine, cough drops, eye rinse ampoules, emergency thermal blanket.
* Whistle and personal locator beacon. Shouting is useless under the roar.

Leave at home: perfume, sunscreen scent, bright colors, loose dangling straps that a flier wing can snag, drones - the fliers will kill a drone in seconds and then descend on the wreck.

### 4. How To Move And Be There

**Arrive still.** When you translate/port in, don't stride forward. Stand. Breathe through filters. Let the sound hit you with muffs on. Look at your feet. They're already being inspected. Wait 5 full minutes. Let the nearby animals decide you're stone-warm and boring. Let your eyes adjust to red.

Then move like you're in a shrine full of sleeping mice.

* **Step high, step vertical.** Lift your boot straight up and put it straight down. Dragging crushes grazers. Crushed grazer = burst amber jelly = bioluminescent ring = every opportunistic predator in 15 meters converges on your boot, including fliers dropping from above.
* **Never step on crystal gardens.** Those hair-fine needles, quartz rosettes, hollow chiming tubes - they are not decorations. They are farms. The scrape marks show where herds tend them. Stepping on one is like kicking over a village's winter store. The inhabitants won't punish you; they'll just abandon that hollow for years. Walk around, even if it's awkward.
* **Follow footpaths you didn't make.** The grooves worn in basalt by millions of tiny feet are the best trails. The animals have already solved drainage and slip. Walk *beside* the densest traffic, not in it.
* **Don't touch the varnish if you can avoid it.** That leathery rust-red-black mat that puffs to amber jelly in mist IS the base of the food web. Touching it when dry does little. Touching it when swollen leaves a glowing handprint that spreads. I've seen a handprint throb for eight minutes, drawing a spiral of crab-things inward. If you must steady yourself, touch bare polished basalt, not the mat.
* **Rain protocol:** When the seven-minute drizzle starts - and it will, without warning - just stop under an overhang or tilt your hood down. Don't try to push through. The animals shift modes instantly: wings fold, herds clamp down, trickles start under stone where there was no water. That is the most dangerous footing moment - new sheet-flow over slick biofilm. Count to 300 after it stops before trusting slope again.

**Fliers:** Give them airspace. They are blind, loud, and frantic. They navigate by screaming and by those pale static threads you only see after rain in absolute dark. If you wave your arms you'll get struck - not attacked, collided with. A flier the size of a gull at speed will knock you down on glass.

* If a cloud descends to feed, crouch, tuck elbows, look down. They'll wheel overhead and snap at each other, not you. Do not throw anything. The sound of impact makes them swarm.
* If a resonant hum starts in a basalt rib - a low thrumming you feel in your molars - step OFF the rib. Sit on siltstone. The resonance stuns fliers and makes them drop like stunned fish. It will make your jaw ache and your vision swim. It passes in 20-60 seconds.

### 5. Anomalies Are Not To Be Tested

That paragraph about stones humming, water running uphill, varnish flashing, static threads - travelers get mystical about it and start poking.

Treat anomalies like tidepools: observe, don't interfere.

* Humming ribs: don't press your ear to them, don't hammer them. Just move off.
* Uphill water: pretty, small trick. Do not try to dam or channel it. Magnetic precipitates concentrate there. You don't want that dust in your filters.
* Static threads post-rain: genuinely beautiful - pale filaments crawling a foot off the ground, discharging the plain. Don't walk through the densest curtains. Travelers report raised arm-hair, metallic taste, headache. Could be ozone. Could be something else. Step around.

Nothing here is supernatural for the locals. It's all metabolism. You are the guest who doesn't know which plate is hot.

### 6. Psychological Survival

The hardest part isn't physical. It's the texture.

After an hour, your brain stops being able to parse the roar as many animals and starts hearing it as one huge animal. You will feel watched despite nothing having eyes in any direction that cares about you. The lack of green, of soil smell, of bird-song - the complete absence of plant signals - registers as "dead" to a primate brain, while your eyes scream "alive, alive, everywhere alive".

Expect:
* A strong urge to brush yourself constantly. Fight it unless something actually bites (almost nothing does). You'll just smear jelly and irritate your skin.
* Irrational guilt/disgust when you inevitably crush something. You will. Mourn once, step cleaner.
* Awe flipping to dread for no reason when your lamp catches a whole field of quartz rosettes at once. That's normal. Name it: "I am having an awe response. My boots are dry."

Mitigations that work:
* Bring a human anchor: a low voice recording of a friend, a photo under clear tape inside your cuff. Glance at it during checks.
* Talk aloud periodically into your recorder/mic, even muffled by respirator: "I'm at the second rib, pan is clear, breathing okay." Hearing your own narrative breaks the bell-roar trance.
* Limit your first stay to 90 minutes. You can always come back. Overstayers get careless, cold, and deaf to their alarms.

### 7. Leaving Clean, In Both Directions

The basin erases footprints in hours, but your body remembers.

Exit protocol:
1. Move back along your flagged line. Don't cut fresh path on the way out even if you think you remember.
2. At your transition point, before you leave: brush boots vigorously, bag and seal all flags/cord/trash. Pick off any hitchhikers - isopod-things cling to warmth and will die screaming in your ship/home and foul you with ammonia. Check cuffs, hood, pack seams.
3. Decontaminate: shower thoroughly, wash clothes on hot, wipe boots with alcohol. Quarantine filters/bags sealed until you can incinerate or deep-bury them far from local watersheds. Do not introduce basalt-dissolving microbes anywhere else.
4. Rest your ears. 12 hours of relative quiet. Hydrate. Expect mild sore throat and tiredness. If cough persists >48h, tell a medic you were exposed to heavy arthropod/organic dust and mineral-rich mist, not "an alien planet" - they can treat that.

---

What does it feel like if you do it right?

You stand in a shallow bowl of old stone under a ceiling of cloud that has never been a sky, and for a while the roar stops being noise and becomes pressure, like being underwater. The mist beads on your sleeves. A herd of slate-colored isopods moves over each other like a living pavement, leaving bright trails. Under an overhang, sack-colonies inflate and sigh. Crystal gardens chime. When you accidentally brush the amber jelly it blooms green-blue under your fingertip and a wave of light runs away from you farther than you expected, and you pull your hand back and apologize without speaking.

Cold drips down your neck. Fliers scream beyond your protected hearing. Your footprints glow for a minute and then herds come and polish them flat.

And when you leave, turning off your red lamp for a second to let the basin be dark again, you understand it doesn't need sun, doesn't need soil, doesn't need you to think it's beautiful. It just is - damp and dense and dark - complete without permission, busy in a way that has nothing to do with day.

You felt very small. In the good way.
