This isn't a wilderness you survive in. It's a house you are invited into. A bowl maybe 40 meters deep and 100 across, and every single surface has been touched, smoothed, sealed, and re-sealed by human hands for generations. Think less "ancient village" and more "one enormous shared organism made of brick and living people."

An ordinary human can absolutely live there for weeks. But you will be miserable, rude, or dangerous there for the first 48 hours if you don't prepare to be a *useful* guest instead of a tourist.

Here is how to prepare:

### 1. First, Re-Calibrate Your Brain

**You are not going Outdoors. You are going Indoors.**

Most travelers prep for "desert" or "ruins." This is neither. There is no horizon, no bare ground, no wilderness. The sky is a bright ragged circle straight above you. Every wall is someone's floor. Every flat roof is someone's yard. Every stair is worn hip-smooth because a thousand feet have sat on it to shell beans and talk.

That means:
* **There is no privacy and there is no solitude.** On an 8-foot wide terrace there is a hearth, a loom, sleeping mats, pigeons, a child, a grandmother grinding salt, and now *you*. Laundry lines cross three stories above the drop. Shutters touch shutters. Sound echoes off hard plaster so everyone hears everything. If you are quiet, people will come check on you because silence means "fallen or sick."
* **Density is vertical, not sprawling.** It will feel crowded like a bazaar that never ends, even though the whole hamlet is only the size of a large apartment building. If you are claustrophobic in elevators, tight alleys, or night markets, practice now. The cure there is not to withdraw - it's to sit on a step, be visible, and join the murmur.
* **It is loud and it never stops.** Hundreds of pigeons in wall-cotes, goats' hooves clicking, looms clacking, children yelling ring-to-ring with slap-back echo, the endless *tick tick tick* of condensation into pans, grain pounding, water pouring. And when wind hits the rim, the whole bowl hums like a jar you blow across. Bring a tolerance for noise, and unlearn the instinct to whisper.

If you need alone-time to regulate, plan for it *within* presence. Bring knitting, a whetstone, shelling work - something to do with your hands in public. That counts as "with others" here.

### 2. Your Body in the Bowl

It is not quite like any desert you've felt.

**Heat + Damp:** Outside the bowl is dry desert. Inside is a kiln that sweats. From ~11:00 to 14:00 the sun goes straight down the throat and white lime plaster explodes light back at you. You squint even in shade. The air sits, thick, warm-brick warm that lives in the walls. Then from trapped cooking smoke, goat sweat, hot dust, it flips: cold desert air sinks at night, condenses on every wall, and by dawn the whole place glitters and drips. Corners grow moss. Wood stays swollen. Plaster smells mineral-wet.

Prep for:

1.  **Light blindness & heat soak.** Bring a wide-brimmed hat with a dark underside, good sunglasses *and* a light scarf you can drape over head/shoulders to cut bounce-light. Wear loose, light, long sleeves/pants in bone-white or dusty colors - not black, not synthetics that trap damp. Cotton or linen is perfect; the locals live under damp sheets at midday for a reason. Expect to be sweaty all the time without ever feeling "wet."
2.  **Deceptive hydration.** There *is* water everywhere - beads running down walls into finger-wide runnels, jars, cisterns with stone lids, black ceramic pans left out on ledges. Do NOT assume you can just drink. That water is the whole reason they exist and every drop is assigned. You will get sick if you sip from a random pan, and you will deeply offend if you waste it. Bring your own 2-liter bottle/canteen, fill only where offered, and ask: "Where may I fill?" They will show you the guest jar.
3.  **Slip and fall.** Condensation + polished plaster + cupped stairs = skating. No amount of reading prepares you for how smooth those center-worn steps are. Bring soft-soled, grippy shoes that get wet - not boots, not flip-flops. Bare feet are common on the cool damp flagstones at the bottom, but keep shoes for the rings. Walk slow in the morning when everything drips.
4.  **Flash-flood.** When it rains - every few weeks, sudden hammering wall - the bowl becomes a funnel. You'll hear it roar on the rim before a white sheet falls on you. Every stair becomes rapids. Every courtyard pools. It drains through throats under the lowest floor, but for an hour it is genuinely dangerous to be caught in a crevice-lane with a goat. When you hear that roar, get up high, get under a lintel, secure your pack, and *do not block a drain.* Afterwards the brick stays cool and dripping for days. That's repair time.

Health kit: electrolytes, blister care, anti-fungal powder, earplugs for sleeping *if* you must - but use low-grade ones so you can still hear calls - and a small headlamp with red mode. Night is very dark after the amber fades.

### 3. What to Bring, What to Leave

You want to arrive as someone who already knows the Three Rules.

**The Three Rules:**

> 1. Keep the surfaces sealed.
> 2. Never block a runnel.
> 3. Make sound.

Everything else flows from that.

**Bring:**

* **A gift of utility, not novelty.** They have almost no metal - a few copper rivets, one heirloom iron knife. Don't bring flashy tech. Bring: a bag of fine slaked lime or high-quality white plaster pigment if you can get it, good palm-fiber twine, a hand-sized smooth river stone for burnishing plaster, beeswax, dried mint/pepper seeds, hard candy for kids. If you can carry it, a new wooden plaster trowel or a small sack of straw chaff for mix will make you instantly beloved.
* **Your own plaster skill.** Seriously. Practice before you go: mix clay-lime-straw-water to peanut-butter consistency, apply with your palm to a rough wall, smooth in circles. You *will* be asked to help re-plaster after rain. Every person knows how. Doing it badly but willingly is better than watching. Watch a few videos on earthen/lime plaster - then do it with your hands.
* **Clothes you can work in and get filthy.** You will crouch, carry jars, move pots with pomegranate saplings and fig roots, wring cloths at dawn.
* **A cup.** Everyone carries one. For water poured in a silver arc jar-to-jar. Having your own means no one has to lend theirs.
* **Something to make sound with.** Not a Bluetooth speaker. A small drum, wooden clacker, mouth harp, your voice. Morning, evening, while you walk the crevice-lanes, you call, sing, clap. Travelers who go silent make people anxious.

**Leave / Don't Bring:**
Metal-heavy outdoor gear, tent poles, stakes that could gouge plaster. Harsh soaps - waste goes to sealed pits that feed the figs that feed the goats. Bring plain olive-oil soap, tiny amount. Drones, loud recording rigs, bright chemical lights. And anything that leaks oil/dye onto lime.

### 4. Arrival and How to Be a Good Guest

You don't "enter a site." You descend into someone's home.

**Descent:** Come down slow, mid-morning after water-carrying but before the brutal 11-2 column of sun. Wear your hat. Touch the wall - it's expected. People will be sitting on swooped steps, watching.

First ten minutes:
1. Greet ring by ring. You don't need their language perfectly; cadence matters more. Loud enough to echo slightly, smile, open palms. "Salaam / Hello, may I pass? Beautiful water / beautiful plaster today." Compliment the surface - that's like complimenting someone's cleaned kitchen.
2. Ask for water and for where to put your body. "Where should I sit/sleep so I don't block water?" They *will* place you. Accept the placement even if it's close to pigeons or a hearth. That 8-foot terrace assignment IS your bedroom, kitchen pass-through, and social office.
3. Unveil nothing, move nothing. Those hundreds of pots stacked on ledges, olives jars with pepper plants, grape vines on twisted fiber making green tunnels - each is placed to chase sun as it tracks, to catch condensation, to not block a runnel. Don't "tidy."

**Living on Cycle:**

* **Dawn (~5-8): Water Hour.** Everyone unveils black pans, wrings cloths, empties runnels into cisterns, feeds vines. Get up, help carry. Lightest job, most important. Learn which finger-wide groove is a runnel and NEVER step on, sit on, or set a bag across it. Your house can be tiny, but water must pass.
* **Midday (~11-15): Stillness.** Work stops. Animals pant. People sleep head-to-toe under damp sheets in shade. Do not try to be productive. Lie down, nap, listen to cicadas in the fig trees overhead - five or six great figs on the second ring where the big runnel runs, roots cracking plaster, leaves loud in wind. If you fight this, you'll get heat exhaustion.
* **Afternoon: Repair.** Something always needs re-patching where last rain peeled plaster like pages - you'll see walls with five generations of faded handprints underneath. Mix, palm-apply, smooth. Move a pot a foot to follow sun.
* **Evening: Smoke Hour.** A hundred tiny hearths light. Smoke gets trapped in the bowl for an hour, sweet/stinging, until cold air punches through at dusk. If you have asthma, have a bandana ready and sit near the western curve where it clears first. This is talking/cooking/story time.

Eat what's offered: flatbread, figs, peppers, goat milk, pigeon eggs sometimes. Refuse nothing politely? Take small, eat all. Offer to grind salt, mend net, card palm fiber. Children will teach you faster than adults.

Sleep: on mats, often communally, under a fig branch or loft reached by notched pole. It will be damp-cool at 3am. Keep your bedding off the lowest flagstones where the bowl breathes. Use earplugs only half-in - you need to hear morning calls and kestrels stooping for pigeons at first light - cats stalking clotheslines three stories up - geckos snapping moths.

### 5. The Weird Gentle Parts

Don't poke the curtains in the lowest ring. That old, dampest plaster grows thin translucent white sheets, brittle as moth wings, that hang where no one hung them and chime when brushed. They're still plaster, still lime. The condensation sometimes beads and crawls *up* a curve for a second before gravity remembers. Old women say the Bowl drinks when thirsty. Just notice it. Don't collect samples aggressively, don't slap explanatory science on it in front of hosts. Say: "The Bowl is breathing tonight." They'll nod. You understood.

This place isn't decaying. It looks melted, licked smooth by water/feet/wind/hands re-plastering every spring - corners rounded, drainage channels cut exactly as deep as needed. It feels *finished*, in-balance. Your job is not to fix or improve it, only to keep it sealed for the next rain.

### Quick Pack List for the Multiverse Traveler

Worn: loose linen pants/shirt, scarf, brim hat, grippy soft shoes
Packed: 2L bottle + tin cup, olive soap, electrolytes, first-aid + anti-fungal, red headlamp, light sleeping sheet/mat, lime or plaster gift + burnishing stone + twine, instrument/voice, notebook that tolerates damp, plastic bag for waste to pack out

And the real prep: practice being heard, being crowded, being useful.

Stand on the bottom flagstones - cool and damp under bare feet - and look up: stacked rings of made life, honeycomb-dense, dripping, green in its jarred pockets, gold-white in sun, worn perfect. It's a hamlet the size of a room that contains a world. It wasn't found. It was made. And it keeps being made, every day, by everyone inside it.

If you remember to seal a crack, step over a runnel, and sing while you walk - you'll be welcome to help make it for a little while too.
