You are going to a place that is not hostile, but is profoundly indifferent to you. It will not try to kill you. It will simply *not help you stay alive*, and will quietly punish you if you misread it.

This is a high peneplain — the eroded ghost of a mountain range. Think Tibetan Plateau crossed with Scottish moor crossed with Icelandic lava field, smoothed for a billion years and then lifted 10,000 - 15,000 feet into the air. Prepare for it like you would for high-altitude backpacking in a cold desert, plus deep winter silence, plus unreliable physics.

An ordinary human can live there. People *do*. But you are a visitor tuned for a louder, warmer, lower, more Newtonian world. Here’s how you stay safe, sane, and welcome.

### 1. Your Body Is The Problem

At 2-3 miles / 3-5 km elevation, the air is 40-60% as thick as you’re used to. It’s cold, damp, and UV-blasted.

**Before you go:**
*   Spend 3-5 days acclimatizing *before* you step onto the plateau proper, if your transit method allows it. If you can arrive at ~2,500m and walk up, do it. If you have to portal/drop in at altitude, plan to do NOTHING for the first 48 hours except breathe.
*   Get prescribed Acetazolamide / Diamox for acute mountain sickness. Bring ibuprofen, anti-nausea meds. Talk to your doctor about dexamethasone as an emergency backup. If you have asthma, anemia, heart conditions, sleep apnea — this is not the trip for you without medical clearance.
*   Train for walking on soft ground. The moss-carpet is like walking on a wet mattress all day. It’s exhausting on ankles, calves, and knees. Hike in bog, sand dunes, deep moss if you can find it.

**What you will feel:** Headache, breathlessness on any incline, piss constantly, sleep poorly, wake up feeling you haven’t slept. Mild euphoria/confusion. Your breath and heartbeat will be audible. That’s normal at first. If you get a wet cough, confusion, ataxia, blue lips — you have edema. Descend immediately. There is no hospital there.

**Pack for your lungs and skin:**
*   Supplemental oxygen is not optional for a first visit. Bring at least one 2-3L lightweight canister with regulator + pulse-dose conserver for sleeps and emergencies. You will wean off it as you acclimatize, but you want it for nights 1-3 and for any climb onto a ridge.
*   Pulse oximeter. Check twice a day. If you’re below 85% at rest after day 2, descend.
*   Sunscreen SPF 50+ and serious UV lip balm. UV at that altitude + pale sun + snow glare + thin cloud = sunburn in 20 minutes, even when cold. Bring glacier sunglasses or Category 4 goggles. The light is flat and the air is crystal; snow blindness is a real risk.
*   N95 / P2 dust masks, at least 5-10. Not for pollution — for spores. On calm warm afternoons the ferns and bladder-plants release electrostatic clouds of spores and seeds that hang and corkscrew. They are not toxic, but they will trigger brutal hay fever, asthma, and eye irritation if you’re sensitive. Locals are adapted. You are not.
*   Waterproof everything. Not water-resistant. Waterproof.

### 2. Cold, Wet, Never Dry, Never Drowned

It is 35-45°F / 2-7°C in the day, below freezing at night, all summer. It mists every morning, drizzles most afternoons, freezes every night. You will never be soaked by a downpour. You will slowly get damp in every seam and then stay damp.

**Clothing system:**
*   Base: Merino wool, head to toe. No cotton. Bring 3 sets. You will sleep in one.
*   Mid: Fleece or light down jacket. Compact, warm-when-wet.
*   Shell: Gore-Tex or equivalent hardshell jacket + pants with full side zips. You need to vent while walking and seal when the fog rolls in.
*   Feet: Knee-high waterproof boots with aggressive tread but soft sole. The basalt is polished glass when wet. The moss hides ankle-breaking holes. Bring gaiters. Bring 4 pairs of wool socks in ziplocks. Bring foot powder and treat any hot spot immediately — a blister here infects fast and you walk 10 miles a day whether you want to or not.
*   Hands/Head: Thin liner gloves + waterproof overmitts. Wool beanie + sun hat with brim. Buff/neck gaiter you can pull over nose and ears. Your extremities will be wet and cold constantly.
*   Tent: 4-season, free-standing, low-profile for wind. Footprint essential — you will pitch on stone or saturated moss, never dry dirt. Bring extra guylines and stone-anchors (stakes won’t hold in moss/basalt). Or plan to sleep in city outskirts — see Culture.

**Water:**
*   The plateau is *wet* but there are no lakes. Water appears as seep-springs on cliff bands, braided clear creeks over pink gravel, and cisterns in every basin-city. It’s all shockingly cold and mostly clean — it’s snowmelt filtered through basalt and moss.
*   Still filter it. Use a Sawyer Squeeze + SteriPen. Giardia is unlikely (no animals), but the bladder-plants and their decay leach unknown organics. The locals drink cistern water that has settled. Do the same if invited.
*   Carry 2L minimum, 3L if crossing a ridge. Creeks vanish into sinkholes without warning. A valley can be dry 2 hours later.
*   Insulated bottle + thermos. Drinking near-freezing water all day will chill your core. Warm it. Bring tea, bouillon, electrolytes. You lose salts fast from diuresis at altitude.

### 3. Navigation Will Lie To You

This is the most important part. The plateau is physically vast and honest. The *edge* where it thins is not.

Up on the ridges, distance telescopes. In low basins near cities, gravity and light sag. You will experience:

*   False horizons: a ridge that never approaches, or snaps closer when you blink
*   Shadows falling the wrong way for a few steps
*   Light pooling in low spots, a second pale sun ghosting the real one at dusk/dawn
*   Uphill water, stones hanging at apex, voices arriving early
*   Straight streets that bow around empty air

The inhabitants have learned to live *below* the sag. They don’t build high, they worship weather towers rather than use them, they leave courtyards empty.

**Do what they do.**

1.  **Never navigate by sight alone.** Bring a mechanical compass, but *do not trust it* in sag zones — it will wander 10-15°. Bring a GPS with pre-downloaded topo, but expect ±50m drift and altimeter errors near basins. Best is hybrid: GPS for general, compass for sanity check, and *dead reckoning with cairns*.
2.  **Mark your path.** Carry 100m of biodegradable flagging tape (muted green/brown — not neon, locals will remove neon) and small cairns. When crossing fog or any place where the air looks “low” or shadows misbehave, place a marker every 50 steps and look back. If the marker moves, stop.
3.  **Travel on local time:** Move in late morning. The fog lifts 10am-11am. Stop by 4pm before the afternoon snow-dust and before dusk, when the breakdown gets visible. No travel at dusk or night unless you are inside a walled city. At noon the sag is weakest. That’s your window for crossing bad ground.
4.  **Listen to the wind-chimes.** Every courtyard has slate shards hung to tinkle when the air “forgets how to hold sound.” If you hear unnatural silence, ringing without wind, or your own footsteps echo *before* you step — get below the ridge, get to stone shelter, wait an hour. Locals go inside. Copy them.
5.  **Avoid the empty geometry:** If a street bows for no reason, walk the bow. If a courtyard is empty and swept clean in the middle of a crowded city, do not stand in the center, even for a joke. If a map carved on a cistern shows a warp, memorize it. That warp is a warning, not art.
6.  **Bring analog time:** A wind-up watch. Phone clocks may skip a second forward/back in sag. You need to know how long you’ve actually been walking.

### 4. The Silence Will Get To You Before The Cold Does

There are no birds. No insects. No animal calls at all. No background you grew up with.

What you get instead: your breath, your boots squelching on moss, faint hissing of springs, ticking of dwarf birch, spore clouds rustling without wind, and a low pressure sigh when cloud lifts.

Most travelers report three stages:
1.  First hours: Peaceful. Beautiful.
2.  Day 1-2: Hyper-alert. You keep turning because you thought you heard something. You talk too loud to fill space.
3.  Day 3+: Either deep calm or deep anxiety. Some people can’t sleep without white noise. Some weep without knowing why.

**Prepare:**
*   Bring foam earplugs + sleep mask for nights. Paradoxically, you need to be able to create pure silence and pure dark to reset. The stars are so bright they cast shadows; the cities glow amber far off. Your brain will not think it’s night.
*   Do not bring bluetooth speakers. Do not play music on ridges. Sound does not carry right — it’s eaten by moss and thin air — so you’ll crank it, and everyone in a basin a mile away will hear a warped thump with no context. It’s rude and disorienting.
*   Journal. Voice-record quietly in your tent. Bring something small and tactile to do with hands in camp — whittle, knit, repair gear. You need a human-scale task.
*   Don’t bring pets, drones that buzz, or anything that mimics animal life. You would be introducing a profoundly alien signal into a plant-only world. The plants will cover a dead drone in moss in a season; the people will remember you as the one who brought noise.

### 5. The Cities Are Homes, Not Ruins

They look abandoned from a distance — lichen-walled, reed-thatched, shoulder-to-shoulder stone houses around a cistern green. They are not. They are continuously inhabited, inward-building cultures. The outer walls are ruins on purpose; they keep the moss as insulation.

**Culture protocol:**
*   Approach slowly on the paved pink-gravel road, in daylight. Stop at the outer wall and wait to be seen. Don’t climb the wall. Don’t step on the lichen — it can be 100+ years old and they use it to seal roofs.
*   Be quiet. Locals speak softly because shouting is pointless and wasteful. Match their volume. Remove sunglasses when speaking.
*   Offer, don’t pay. They have little use for money but great use for: good salt, sealed honey, small sharp knives, steel needles, willow-wood glue, waterproof thread, antibiotics, altitude medicine, reading glasses, tea bricks. Bring a few, leave them at the cistern edge if invited to trade. Do not offer plastic trinkets.
*   Ask for water, not directions. “May I fill at your cistern?” is polite and gives them a chance to tell you which trough is for visitors. Never dip your bottle directly into the main covered cistern. Use the run-off trough they point to.
*   Don’t photograph people without asking. Don’t sketch their warp-maps without permission — those are navigational survival charts.
*   If invited inside a house: duck, sit where pointed, keep boots at door on the stone (not moss). They will give you hot broth and fern-bread. Eat it. It’s calorie-dense and will save you.
*   Don’t build. Don’t stack new cairns inside the wall. Don’t carve. The stability of that place is its ethic: stone lasts because no one moves it.

Language: they are human/near-human; on most iterations they speak a slow, guttural descendant of whatever mountain language settled the plateau first. Learn 10 phrases: hello, thank you, water, safe path, I will be quiet, I am leaving at midmorning, I mean no harm to the moss.

### 6. Leave No Trace In A Way That Actually Matters Here

The plants are the fauna here. The moss is not decoration — it’s the forest, the prairie, the herd.

*   Walk on stone, pink gravel, or established footpaths when you can. If you must cross virgin moss-carpet, step on the dwarf willow hummocks, not the pure moss mat. Pure rust-green moss can be crushed irreversibly.
*   No campfires on moss. Use a stove on a stone slab. Reed is fuel for them, not you. Never cut live reed.
*   Bury waste on bare basalt cracks away from water, or pack it out. The creeks are their aqueduct sources.
*   Do not introduce seeds, soil, pets, or untreated gear. Brush and freeze your boots/tent before you go. One Earth earthworm or grass seed could outcompete a millennium of slow lichen.

### Packing Checklist (Ordinary Human, 7-Day Traverse)

**On body:** Merino base, fleece, hardshell, gaiters, boots, liner gloves, beanie, glacier goggles
**In pack:** Down jacket, hardshell pants, 3x socks/underwear, overmitts, buff, sun hat
**Sleep:** -10°C sleeping bag, insulated pad (R-value 5+), 4-season tent + stone anchors
**Breathe:** Diamox, pulse oximeter, O2 canister + conserver, N95s, sunscreen
**Drink/Eat:** Filter + SteriPen, 2x 1L bottles + 1L thermos, electrolytes, 3500 kcal/day (no-cook + stove fuel), tea/bouillon
**Navigate:** GPS + paper topo + compass + wind-up watch + flagging + notebook + pencil (pens freeze)
**Respect:** Trade gifts, repair kit, trowel, WAG bags, earplugs/sleep mask

Finally: plan your exit. Mark your entry point with GPS waypoint *and* a physical cairn below the sag-line, not on the ridge. Set a hard turn-back time every day. Tell the nearest basin-city when you expect to return — they will notice if you don’t, not because they watch you, but because any new absence on the moss is visible for miles.

If you do it right, you’ll leave having heard what a world sounds like when it has finished arguing with itself — just stone breathing, water learning to fall, plants doing all the work of animals in perfect, busy silence, and far apart, amber lights holding out against a sky that is sometimes a little too deep.

Walk soft. Breathe shallow. Speak low. Don’t try to fix the thin place. Learn where it sags, and walk around.
