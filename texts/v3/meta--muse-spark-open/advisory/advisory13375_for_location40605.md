This is one of those places that will try to kill you *quietly*, by pretending it isn't trying at all.

It looks stable. It is stable. That's the problem. For maybe 50-70 years it has been in perfect equilibrium — same air cycling, same temperature, same dust falling, same sound at the same tempo. No seasons, no decay, no growth. Just a held note.

You are not in equilibrium with it. You are wet, warm, salty, iron-blooded, noisy, vibrating at 60-100 beats per minute, exhaling water vapor with every breath. In that cylinder, you are not a visitor. You are a *contamination event* — and the only living thing in there has spent its entire evolution learning to eat contamination.

Don't go unless you have to. If you do, prepare like you're doing a short, hostile spacewalk, not a hike.

### 1. Understand What You're Actually Walking Into

Forget "garden." Your intuition should be: **abandoned lung.**

* **Up top:** 4,700m. That's Himalayan high camp. Air is ~55% of sea-level pressure. It's not just cold, it's *desiccating* — hyper-arid katabatic wind that strips water faster than desert heat. You'll get altitude sickness, nosebleeds, cracked lips, and dehydration in under an hour without prep, even before you go down the ladder.
* **Inside the cylinder:** It's not outside air. The description is very clear: *equilibrium engine, never exchanging with the outside.* Four bellows just beating the same volume of air back and forth for decades. That means:
  - Oxygen is low. CO/CO2 is probably high. It's full of warm iron, copper salts, sulfur, magnetite dust.
  - It is 100% non-breathable. You cannot "get used to the smell." If you breathe it unfiltered, you'll get metal fume fever at best, chemical pneumonitis at worst.
  - It's also *bone-dry.* There is no condensation because there's no humidity at all. Your eyes, sinuses, throat, and lungs will dry and crack within minutes. Any exposed mucus membrane is a wound waiting to happen.

* **The sound:** A four-beat industrial THUD-drag on loop plus a 50Hz transformer hum that you feel in your molars. This is not "loud music" loud. This is infrasonic, whole-body, bone-conduction loud. It will disorient you, spike your heart rate, make you nauseous, and after 20-30 minutes you will lose time perception completely. That place is designed to erase time. People in environments like that hallucinate, panic, or just... sit down and forget to leave.

* **The smog-fern:** This is the real hazard. Read its rules:
  1. It doesn't photosynthesize. It eats metal and sulfur out of dry air.
  2. It grows **toward vibration and noise**, not light.
  3. It has already covered every surface that *doesn't* move. The only thing stopping it is the pistons that never stop moving.

  You vibrate. You hum. Your phone vibrates, your watch ticks, your chest thrums, your jaw clicks when you swallow. You are the loudest small thing to enter that room in half a century. If you stay still and quiet long enough, you will feel it: the knee-high plumes by the pistons will lean toward you. Not fast. Slowly. Listening.

  It doesn't need water and there are *no animals* in there — not even mites. Think about what that means. Either everything that wanders in dies, or this thing outcompetes/out-poisons everything else. Do not touch it with bare skin. Do not inhale near it. Do not give it water.

### 2. How to Pack for an Ordinary Human Body

You are ordinary. You have no filters. Bring them with you.

**Breathing - sealed, not filtered:**
A dust mask or N95 is not enough. You need a **full-face, closed-circuit or supplied-air respirator.** At minimum: full-face P100 + organic vapor/ABEK cartridges + eye seal, PLUS a separate supplemental oxygen bottle you can switch to.

Ideal: A rebreather or SAR with a 30-45 minute bottle. You want to breathe *your* air, not its air, at all. Practice putting it on with gloves before you go. If your mask fogs from your own breath, you will panic in that dim amber light — bring anti-fog and don't exhale into the cylinder if you can avoid it.

**Ears - double protection:**
Foam earplugs (NRR 32+) *under* over-ear industrial muffs. The low-frequency thud will go through plugs alone. You need both. Test them. You should still hear the THUD as pressure in your chest, but not as a stabbing in your ears. Bring earplugs with a string so you don't drop them into the felt. Conserve your hearing — you need it to hear the ladder, your own breathing, and any change in the machine tempo.

**Eyes, nose, skin:**
- Sealed goggles if not full-face mask. The air is full of grit and metal salt nodules the size of glass beads. Dry wind above + dry dust inside = corneal abrasion in minutes.
- Saline nasal spray and artificial tears in a sealed pocket. Use *before* you go down and immediately after you come up. Don't spray them openly inside.
- Lip balm, heavy moisturizer applied beforehand.
- Full coverage suit: windproof, breathable shell up top for the outside; inside, you want smooth, non-porous fabric that won't snag filaments. Tyvek coveralls over your warm layers are perfect. Tape the cuffs. The fern is wire-wool — it will cling to fleece, wool, hair. You do not want to bring it home in your cuffs and have it try to colonize your warm, damp apartment ventilator.
- Thin nitrile gloves under work gloves for the rusted ladder. The ladder will be unstable, cold enough to burn, and coated in a thin layer of that felt. Don't climb with bare hands.

**Altitude & Cold:**
- Acclimatize if you can. Sleep at 2,500m+ the night before, hydrate aggressively 24h prior, no alcohol. Bring acetazolamide if you tolerate it, but know it makes you pee more — which dehydrates you faster up there.
- Layers: moisture-wicking base, insulating mid (synthetic, not down — down dies in that dry wind if you sweat), hard-shell windbreaker. The cylinder itself is described as "cellar-cold" — your breath shows. So you'll be sweating from the climb and wind, then freezing when you stop. Bring a hat and neck gaiter you can pull over your nose *outside*.
- Closed water system: hydration bladder with tube under your mask, or a sealed bottle you only drink from up top, *never* inside the cylinder. Do not spill. Do not pour water to "test" anything. You are carrying the one thing that biome has never had.

**Light, Time, Mind:**
- Headlamp with a red-light mode. Don't rely on those two sodium lamps — they're sand-blasted opaque and could die while you're down there, leaving you with only a tiny amber bulb and a circle of sky 4 meters up. Bring two lights, plus chemical glow sticks as backup that don't buzz or whine.
- **No buzzing electronics.** That transformer hum is the fern's sun. Your phone on vibrate, your smartwatch alarm, a portable speaker, a drone — all of that is a dinner bell. Airplane mode, vibrate off, alarms off. If you bring a camera, wrap it so it doesn't autofocus-hunt loudly.
- An anchor: analogue watch, a small vibrating timer set for 12 minutes. You will lose time. The place feels eternal because it *is* eternal. Decide before you go down: "I will be up the ladder at X time no matter what." Tell someone up top, or set a multiverse tether/return beacon if that's how you travel. Don't trust your brain to count in there.
- Earplugs in, you will feel isolated and watched. That's normal. The felt has no eyes, but it is all facing you because you are making noise. Expect anxiety, irrational dread. It's altitude + infrasound + sensory monotony. Name it: "This is the sound getting to me." Don't make decisions in there. Observe, then leave.

### 3. Entry & Exit Protocol

**Before you get to the lip:**
Rest. Check O2, mask seal, ear protection, lights. Eat something salty. Pee. Secure everything — nothing dangling that could fall and be swallowed by the carpet. Sit for 2 minutes and just listen to the wind so you have a baseline.

**Climbing down:**
Test every rung. Rust + weight + vibration = metal fatigue. Keep 3 points of contact. Don't kick off dust clouds — that dust is metal salts. Descend facing the ladder, slow.

Do not jump the last meter. The floor is not floor, it's felt over basalt. It may hide holes, cables, the buried catwalk.

**Inside — the 10-Minute Rule:**

Ordinary humans should not be in there more than 10-15 minutes at a stretch. Set your timer.

1. **Do not talk loudly.** Whisper, or better, use hand signals. Your voice is vibration.
2. **Do not touch the plumes.** You can touch clean basalt if you must steady yourself, but if you peel a clump, you create a wound. The organism will prioritize repairing it, which means growth toward the disturbance — you.
3. **Do not breathe out openly.** If you must cough, do it into your mask.
4. **Do not sit or kneel.** Your body heat + moisture + pulse will mark that spot. The fern on the control booth roof grew into antlers just from a transformer hum. Imagine what your 37°C heartbeat does.
5. **Keep moving, slightly.** Gentle, slow shifts. Don't stand perfectly still for minutes — that makes you a very clear, stationary vibration source to triangulate.
6. **Watch for change.** The most important line in the description: *Nothing here changes.* That means if you *do* see change — a frond twitching against the bellows rhythm, a nodule brightening, dust suddenly not settling — you're the cause. Leave.

**Climbing out:**
Same ladder, even slower. You're now slightly hypoxic, ears ringing, eyes dry. Don't rush. At the top, crawl away from the lip before you stand — the wind up there can knock you.

Once you're back in outside air, don't rip your mask off immediately. Let your eyes adjust. Breathe your bottle for another minute, then switch to outside air slowly. Drink water in small sips.

### 4. Aftercare

You were in a dry, metal-rich, spore-dense lung.

- Brush yourself off *outside*, away from the hole. Remove coveralls and bag them. Don't shake them in your tent/ship.
- Rinse nose, eyes with saline, shower if you can. Monitor for 24h: persistent cough, metallic taste, fever, tight chest = metal fume fever / pneumonitis. Get oxygen and medical help. It's not psychosomatic.
- Tinnitus is expected for a few hours. If ringing >24h, get checked.
- Decontaminate boots, tripod legs, ladder gloves. Those filaments survive by eating air; a warm, oily house with a humming fridge is paradise to them. Throw away anything porous you brought inside if you can.

And finally, the ethical prep: that place is perfect because it has been alone. It has no witness and no need for one. It doesn't want you. It doesn't hate you. It just *maintains.* Every human who goes there brings water, skin, breath, noise — stories — and the cylinder has to absorb that and return to stillness.

Leave nothing. Take nothing — not even a pretty glassy nodule. If you take it, you take a piece of its equilibrium, and you will keep coughing metal for a week to prove it.

Go as a ghost: sealed, quiet, brief. Listen to the chord, understand you are not part of it, climb back out, and let it go on roaring to itself in the dark.
