This place will not kill you quickly. It will kill you completely, and politely, and without noticing.

You are not going to visit an ocean. You are going to visit the bottom of a weight. Treat this file like a dive brief for a deep cave in space where the cave wants nothing from you, not even your body.

### 1. Understand What You're Actually Going To

Forget every idea of "swimming" or "diving."

**Pressure:** At 1-2 kilometers down you are under 100 to 200 atmospheres. That's 100 to 200 kilograms pressing on every square centimeter of you. A soft wetsuit, a scuba tank, even a technical rebreather means instant death — your lungs flatten like wet paper, nitrogen dissolves into your blood and makes you insane before you drown, your chest can't expand. There is no recreational gear for this place.

You have two honest options:

*   **Option A - Stay In The Bottle:** Go inside a vehicle. A submersible, even a one-person tourist sub like a Triton or a hard bathyscaphe. This is the correct choice for 99% of travelers. You remain at 1 atmosphere inside. You will feel safe until you remember the water outside could crush that bubble like a soda can if the hull is flawed.
*   **Option B - Wear The Deep On You:** An Atmospheric Diving Suit — a hard anthropomorphic exoskeleton like a Newtsuit or ADS 2000. Or if your multiverse tech allows it, an active pressure field/kinetic barrier. You stay at surface pressure inside while articulated joints let you walk. You will move slow. You will clank. You will *feel* enormous.

There is no Option C. Do not try to "acclimatize" with saturation diving to this depth as an ordinary human. High Pressure Nervous System Syndrome, oxygen toxicity, thermal collapse — you will seizure in the dark and never be found.

**Cold:** 0 to 1°C. Not "cold water" cold. Pre-ice cold. A salt-brine that steals heat faster than air by a factor of 25. Your fancy undersuit will fail in minutes if your suit heating loop fails. Hypothermia here doesn't make you shiver. It makes you stupid, then sleepy, then still. Assume any breach = lethality in under 15 minutes.

**Light:** There is none. Not dim. *None.* Your eyes will create false indigo swirls trying to compensate. Bioluminescence is real but it is not illumination — it's a match struck in a cathedral. It ruins your night vision more than it helps.

**Force:** The water looks still and is not. You will be hit by thermohaline rivers — current sheets hundreds of meters thick moving at 0.5 to 2 knots. Silently. No warning rumble. One moment you're hovering over the nodules, the next a wall of cold ooze has lifted around you and you are tumbling sideways with no reference for up.

Internalize this: The floor is ancient and finished. The water above it is young and violent and never stops. You are a speck between them.

### 2. How To Pack For The Floor That Has Everything Already

You can't bring much, because drag will kill you. Every extra thing is something for current to grab.

**Essential Systems - Triple Redundancy:**

1.  **Light Discipline.** Bring two primary lights and one emergency chemical/beacon-only light. But DO NOT run everything at full blast. You need:
    *   A wide, warm-white flood, diffuser-covered, on the dimmest setting you can tolerate. This is to see the nodules without blinding yourself with backscatter from marine snow.
    *   A tight, hand-held spot with a red filter for looking far. White light spooks the few animals and washes out the bacterial mats.
    *   And practice running DARK. Spend 10 full minutes per hour with all lights off. Let your eyes adjust, watch for dragonfish flickers. Your brain needs true darkness to stop hallucinating.

2.  **Thermal Core.** Active heating is non-negotiable. Hot-water shunt from your sub/Suit reactor, electric mesh undersuit with isolated batteries, argon inflation for insulation, and a passive survival bag clipped to your thigh. Two independent temperature readouts — one for suit exterior water temp, one for skin. If skin drops below 35°C, abort.

3.  **Anchoring & Movement.** You do not swim here. You crawl.
    *   Mag-locks or suction anchors for your boots/hull skids. The nodules are manganese/iron — weakly magnetic, perfectly smooth. Test your anchors before you trust them.
    *   A short, 20m hard tether, not line that tangles. You clip to a deadweight sled you drop, then leapfrog along it. Never free-drift unless you have thrusters powerful enough to fight a river. Fins are useless; vectored thrusters or walking is real locomotion.
    *   Weighted gloves/knee pads. You will be on hands and knees looking closely. The nodules click and shift. They are slick with biofilm. Gloves protect you from cuts that won't heal in cold salt.

4.  **Sensory Kit:**
    *   Low-frequency hydrophone turned DOWN. The currents have infrasound that will vibrate your sternum. You want to hear the one real sound: the occasional *tok* of nodules knocking together. That tells you a surge is coming.
    *   Sonar, but narrow-band and short range - 30m. Long-range sonar just returns a flat horizonless fuzz of nodule field forever. Useless and disorienting.
    *   CO2 / O2 / Humidity monitors with audible alarms INSIDE your helmet/vehicle. Your own breathing will kill you before the water does if your scrubber clogs with cold ooze.

5.  **Navigation That Doesn't Lie:**
    GPS doesn't work. Compasses are sluggish. There is no horizon, no hill, no feature larger than a fist.
    *   Drop inertial beacons every 200m - small glowing transponders that sit on the floor and chirp. Otherwise you will walk in a perfect circle for hours thinking you went straight.
    *   Do not trust your sense of direction. The currents induce vertigo. Keep a physical plumb indicator — a small weight on a string inside your cabin — to know true down when the water spins you.

Leave behind: knives larger than a stub, nets, sample bags bigger than a fist, any idea of taking a nodule home. They took millions of years to grow around a shark tooth. Taking one is not souvenir hunting, it's grave robbery to whatever built the cairns.

### 3. The Mind Is The Weak Hull

Everyone prepares for pressure and forgets this part. This place is psychologically corrosive in a very specific way.

**It is Too Empty and Too Full at Once.**
Your visual field will be: black water to 20 feet, then infinite nodules to infinity. No variation. Your brain *craves* pattern and will start seeing faces, roads, buildings in the tiling. Then it will invent loneliness so profound it feels like being buried. Astronauts call it the Breakaway Phenomenon. Here it's stronger because there isn't even stars.

Train for it before you go:
*   Spend an hour a day for a week in total darkness and total silence in a small room, doing nothing. No phone. Just breathing.
*   Practice task-loading drills: counting your breaths, naming each nodule type you see, narrating aloud to a recorder. Your voice inside your helmet is an anchor.
*   Set strict time boxes. Do not "explore until tired." Decide: "I will transit 400m to bearing 030, observe for 12 minutes, return." Timers save lives when eternity is beneath you.

**Silence With Weight.**
Sound is swallowed. Your heartbeat will sound like someone else's. You will hear suit creaks and think they are creatures. Accept that most of the time you will hear nothing but your own inhalation. Some travelers panic and hyperventilate, burning O2. Learn box breathing: 4 count in, hold 4, 4 out, hold 4.

**Feeling Small Correctly.**
This floor finished happening before your species had hands. The currents that roll over it are older than language. You will feel not fear but *irrelevance*. Don't fight it. Don't make grand speeches. Just nod to it. The travelers who do worst here are those who need the place to acknowledge them. It won't.

And there is something else: those little stacks.

When you find the first cairn — two flat stones balanced, already half fallen — your instinct will be awe, then intrusion anxiety. Good. They were made by something clever enough to rearrange its world but not clever enough to leave permanence. Do not rebuild them. Do not touch. Do not photograph with flash at close range. Hover 2 meters back, light on low, look, record by memory, leave.

If you stare long enough into the murk beyond your light, you may convince yourself something is staring back, guarding them. It's probably just a blind sleeper shark. Probably.

### 4. Being There: A Sequence

This is what a survivable visit feels like, minute by minute:

**Entry:** You drop from above through kilometer after kilometer of nothing. Light dies in layers. Blue -> indigo -> absolute black around 800m. Pressure builds as a humming in the hull/suit joints, not pain if your systems work. Temperature readout ticks down. 10°C... 4°C... 2°C... 1°C. Your heaters kick in with a thump.

**Contact:** You don't land, you arrive. Thrusters puffing to avoid stirring silt. The floodlight hits the bottom and for the first second your brain fails: it looks like a cobblestone street photographed too close. Nodules shoulder-to-shoulder to the edge of light. No sand. No mud visible until current lifts it. The sheer, mindless completeness of it is nauseating. No gaps. As if someone tiled the world.

**On Foot:** You set your weight down gently. Silt puffs — it hangs for minutes. Your boot finds a barrel-sized nodule, slips, settles into the divot between two. Walking is slow, deliberate, like crossing a ball-pit filled with greasy bowling balls that all weigh 40kg. Each step makes that soft *tok*. Currents hit — a slow push, then shear. You crouch automatically. Marine snow spirals sideways. You lose 5 meters of visibility, wait.

You turn all lights off. Black total. Thirty seconds later a pinprick 15 meters out: blue-green, lives for two seconds, dies. Another further out. A glass sponge filament waves alone. Your breathing is loud.

You see the cleared path — a pale strip where ooze has been swept aside, straight as intention. Knee-high stack beside it, leaning. You don't approach fast. You kneel. You understand: we are not the first to need a mark in a place with no marks.

You stay no more than 45 minutes on the floor your first time. Cold and task fixation will tell you you're fine. You're not.

**Exit:** Reeling in. Slow ascent is mandatory even in a hard suit/sub to let systems outgas and to watch for pressure anomalies. Do not blast upward. As you rise, the nodules shrink to a texture, then vanish. You re-enter nothing again for a very long time. Only near 500m does the faux-indigo return, and it feels like dawn even though it's just less-dark.

Back in your transit hab, you will be exhausted despite doing almost nothing physically. You'll be dehydrated, chilled to the bone, ears ringing from pressure and silence. Decompress your mind as well as your suit: warm fluids, dim room, no debrief for an hour. Write what you saw immediately, because much of it will feel like a dream within a day.

### 5. What Not To Do

*   Don't go alone. Even two vehicles/ suits with cross-linked telemetry is barely enough. Solo here is suicide that takes 8 hours.
*   Don't chase fauna. That pale shark is faster than you think and doesn't care. Follow nothing.
*   Don't assume permanence. Any mark you leave — footprints in ooze, light scorch on bacterial mat — will be gone in a tide. The place erases. Let it.
*   Don't bring hubris. People die 30m underwater all the time because they thought "I can handle it." This is 2000m. Handle is not a word that applies.

### Final Brief

You are an ordinary human with an extraordinary ticket. This plain does not need you, did not invite you, and will not remember you. Its value is exactly that: it is Earth-like without being for us. The oldest floor, the busiest water, the least life, and a hint that even here, someone once tried to say *"we were here"* with three stacked stones.

Go in hard. Go in warm. Go in humble. Keep your tether short, your light low, your stay brief.

And when you are floating there, surrounded by shoulder-to-shoulder stones that have touched for a million years while invisible rivers roar soundlessly over your head — turn everything off for one minute.

Listen to the press of that water on every inch of you.

Understand that pressure isn't trying to crush you. It's simply complete.

Then turn your light back on, find your beacon line, and go home while you still can.
