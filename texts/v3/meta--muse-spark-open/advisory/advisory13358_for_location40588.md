This is not a city you visit. It's a climate, an ecology, and an ocean trench that forgot to have an ocean in it. An ordinary baseline human can survive a short trip to the Low City, but only if you stop thinking like a tourist and start thinking like a diver going into a deep, hot, inhabited cave system the size of a continent.

Everything about it wants to grow over you. Prepare for that.

### 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

The Low City is not built *on* land. It *is* the land. Thousands of years of construction have compressed into geology. There is no ground, no horizon, no wilderness to reset you.

As an ordinary human, you have four fundamental incompatibilities:

**1. You need dryness.** The Low City is at ~100% humidity, 28-32C, with constant weeping, seepage, warm rain, and ankle-deep mineral water. You will never be dry.
**2. You need quiet.** It sits at ~85-100+ dB everywhere, 24 hours a day. Hydraulic roar below, human/market roar in the middle, insect/avian/drone chittering above. It's physically painful.
**3. You need space.** Human density is post-saturation. You will be touched by strangers, machines, and animals constantly. There is no privacy, no open view, no silence to think in.
**4. You need stillness.** The City enforces movement. If you stop moving, participating, trading, cleaning, flowing - the City literally grows over you. Bacterial film, calcite, silt, market stalls, other people's lives.

You are not visiting a place. You are inserting yourself into a turbulent fluid that is already at equilibrium.

### 2. Body Preparation: Before You Shift In

Do this for 3-5 days before you go. Don't go hungover, sleep-deprived, or sick. The City will punish that immediately.

**Heat & Pressure Acclimation:**
The air is sea-level thick, or thicker. Lowland tropical plus waste-heat. If you have any history of heat stroke, hypertension, asthma, heart issues - do not go without medical clearance. Your heart will be working hard from the moment you arrive.

Start heat acclimation: hot showers, saunas, walks in humid heat if you can. Start electrolyte loading 48 hours prior. Your sweat will not evaporate effectively there. You cool poorly.

**Skin & Lungs:**
Cut your nails very short. Moist skin plus micro-abrasions equals instant infection.
Get a dental check. A low-grade mouth infection will explode in that bacterial load.
If you shave, stop 2-3 days prior. You want intact skin, not micro-cuts.
Bring any inhaler you have, even if you rarely use it. The air tastes of ozone, hot metal, wet stone, aerosolized mineral, bacterial spores, and smoke. Asthmatics will trigger hard.

**Hearing:**
Get fitted for real hearing protection. Not foam earplugs from a pharmacy - you need:
- High-fidelity earplugs for walking around (like Loop Switch, Etymotic, Alpine MusicSafe - ~20dB reduction, keeps speech intelligible)
- Over-ear industrial muffs for sleeping or sheltering (3M Peltor X5 or similar, ~30dB)
Without this you will have tinnitus and temporary hearing loss within 6-12 hours. With it you will still have fatigue and headaches. Without it for 72+ hours you risk permanent damage.

Practice lip reading and hand signals. You will be shouting to be heard and still not be heard.

**Vaccines & Meds:**
You need, at minimum:
- Tetanus booster (rust, concrete, cuts are guaranteed)
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate or cipro as prescribed for traveler's use)
- Serious antifungal: terbinafine cream + clotrimazole, plus oral fluconazole if you can get it. Trench foot, crotch rot, and foot/skin fungus are not "maybe" - they are "when"
- Anti-diarrheal (loperamide) + oral rehydration salts (10+ packets)
- Antihistamines and decongestants
- Ear drops that dry water (alcohol/acetic acid) - swimmer's ear will be constant
- Water-resistant bandages, hydrocolloid blisters, antiseptic
- Any prescription meds for +50% longer than you plan to stay. You *will* get delayed.

Talk to a travel medicine doctor. Tell them "prolonged tropical slum + cave diving + industrial exposure." That gets closest.

### 3. What To Wear and Carry

You will not stay clean or dry. Your goal is to stay *functional* while wet.

**Clothing - The Wrong Idea:**
Do not bring cotton. Cotton kills here. It stays wet, stays heavy, breeds fungus, chafes you raw.
Do not bring anything "waterproof" like a raincoat unless it's breathable waders. Waterproof ponchos just trap sweat and you boil inside. Everything weeps anyway - water comes from above, below, and sideways.

**Clothing - The Right Idea:**
Think tropical caving + kitchen work + reef walking:

- **Feet:** This is life or death. You will be wading. You need closed-toe, drainable, grippy shoes. Best: neoprene dive booties with hard sole, or lightweight canyoneering shoes, or perforated trail runners. Not sandals, not boots. You need to drain *and* be able to kick off a rat or sharp rebar. Bring 3 pairs of merino wool or synthetic toe socks. Change twice a day. Bring foot powder with antifungal and zinc oxide. Goretex boots will fill with water and never drain and you will get immersion foot in a day.

- **Legs:** Synthetic quick-dry hiking pants with zip legs, or lightweight wetsuit pants if you run cold. Board shorts underneath. Everything should dry in <1 hour when hung.

- **Torso:** Synthetic wicking shirts (nylon fishing shirts are perfect). Long-sleeve with UPF to protect from ducted/piped sunburn and from constant rubbing. Bring a thin merino mid-layer for when transit wind or indoor condensate rain makes you hypothermic - yes, you can be sweating and shivering at once.

- **Hands:** Thin nitrile gloves for wading/handling railings. Your hands will be in slime.

- **Head/Eyes:** Brimmed hat that can get wet - nylon boonie, not baseball cap. It keeps condensate drips out of eyes and sun off neck. Sealed, anti-fog goggles or wraparound glasses. The City rains indoors. Filthy water drips off calcite and rust. You don't want it in your eyes. Bring a buff/neck gaiter to pull over nose/mouth in the worst bacterial pan zones or market smoke.

**Pack:**
No wheeled luggage. No big backpacks - you will not fit through passages and you'll be pickpocketed by humans and birds.

Bring:
- 20-30L dry bag backpack (roll-top, truly waterproof). Double-bag electronics in ziplocs + silica gel. Liner of bright color - you can spot your stuff in murk.
- Chest pouch / money belt under shirt for passport/portal beacon/decoder.
- Collapsible trekking pole - for testing water depth, probing silt, keeping balance on slimed ground. Also keeps people a little farther away.

**Critical Gear:**
1. **Water system:** DO NOT DRINK THE CITY'S WATER. Not the seep, not the pans, not the rain, not the tap if there is one. It is industrial runoff, overflow, bacterial filter soup. It is full of heavy metals, salts, and bioengineered film.
   Carry a 1L Nalgene + 2L hydration bladder + Sawyer Squeeze filter + chlorine dioxide tablets. Filter *and* chemically treat everything unless it's factory-sealed. Bring electrolytes - you will be drinking 5-7 liters a day and sweating salt constantly.

2. **Light:** Headlamp (warm white, 300+ lumens, IPX8 waterproof) + small waterproof flashlight. It never gets truly dark, but it gets *dim* - shaft-light that flickers, brown-outs. You need to see under walkways, in tunnels, and to signal.

3. **Sleep:** Ear plugs + muffs + blackout eye mask + inflatable pillow + ultra-light hammock or bivy sack. You will not be offered a quiet, dry, dark hotel room. The best rest is hammocking between rails on a mid-level where air moves. Never sleep on the floor - water rises, film grows, things crawl.

4. **Tech:** Your phone will die fast from humidity even if you don't drop it. Bring: cheap waterproof burner phone in a case, high-capacity power bank in a dry bag, short cables. A handheld air quality monitor if you have one. A personal locator beacon / multiverse return anchor with manual activation - set it to 48h auto-return, keep it on your body at all times. Do not rely on City networks - they exist but you don't speak the protocols.

### 4. On Arrival: How To Not Die in the First Six Hours

**First sensation:** You'll feel hit with a warm wet towel. Breathing is work. Sweat instantly. Sound is a wall. It's normal to panic. Stand still for 60 seconds. Breathe through your buff. Put in your earplugs. Put on your hat and glasses. Drink.

**Movement:**
You learned the rule: *Everything must keep moving, or it becomes part of the floor.*

That is literal survival advice for you.

- Don't set your bag down for more than a few minutes, especially low down. If you must, put it on your hammock, on a hook, on your back. The bacterial felt - those black, rust-red, yellow mats - will literally start to adhere to fabric in hours. Mineral drip will cement it.
- Don't stay still in a flow lane. Transit films - those moving roads of dark glass - have grooves. Step on, stay balanced, low center of gravity, knees bent like you're on a slow airport walkway. Step off like a surfboard: commit. If you fall, you will be trampled or taken somewhere else.
- Keep your feet moving when standing. Shuffle to prevent film suction if you're in shallow pan water.
- If you need to rest, lean, don't sit. Use vertical surfaces. Find a service ledge, not the main flow.

**Navigation:**
You will be lost immediately. There is no map at your scale. Streets become walls become ceilings.

- Rule 1: Follow water downhill and air uphill. Water weeps down; fresh air is ducted down from high mirror terraces. If air feels cooler and brighter, you're going up toward sky-shafts and exits. If water gets deeper and hotter, you're going down toward retention pans and old strata.
- Rule 2: Follow the fauna. Pigeons and swifts ride thermals off heat vanes - they go up. Rats go along dry pipe racks. Cats go where they can stay dry.
- Rule 3: Never go down more than one level without a way back up. The lowest strata are poured stone faults, unlit, flooded, and unpoliced. People live there, but they are not welcoming to clumsy baseline humans who need rescue.
- Mark your path with chalk on high walls, not low (low gets dissolved/drowned). Better: GPS breadcrumb on offline device + take photos at every junction. Transit films rewrite maps. Your memory is worthless in that density.

**Social:**
No one is in charge. No police. No tourist office. Everyone is busy maintaining the turbulence.

- Don't stand and gawk. You're blocking flow. Move to an eddy - a shrine alcove, stall front, bridge strut - to observe.
- Don't touch healing infrastructure. If you see black filament filling a crack, glowing repair dust, a shimmer moving against wind - hands off. You'll be bitten by maintenance systems or by locals who depend on it.
- Buy something small immediately. Water is currency. So is power charge and light. Carry small, sealed trade goods: sealed candies, tea bags, lighters, zip ties, cheap reading glasses, aspirin packets. Bargain loudly, quickly, smile. You become "participant" instead of "obstacle."
- Languages: You'll hear 6+ plus machine dialects. English or whatever Common you bring will be understood by someone within 50m. Shout keywords, show objects, point. Don't expect fluent help.
- Don't photograph people's hab-cells without asking. That's their home, bathroom, shop, temple. Ask with gesture.
- If offered food: only eat what is boiled/fried in front of you and piping hot, served on disposable leaf or plastic. Avoid raw pan-harvested anything. Your gut has no adaptation to the engineered biofilm ecosystem.

**Health in Real Time:**
- Check your feet every 4-6 hours. Dry them, powder them, change socks. If skin goes white, wrinkled, and stays that way - you have immersion foot. Get to higher, drier level, elevate feet, dry for 6+ hours or you risk disabling infection.
- Drink before you're thirsty, pee check: pale yellow good, dark yellow bad, no pee for 6h = emergency - drink ORS and find cooler shaft.
- If you get a cut, wash, disinfect, seal with hydrocolloid immediately. In that soup, any open wound is infected within hours.
- Watch for heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, stop sweating, confusion. Move to airshaft, douse with clean water, sip ORS, rest. If confusion worsens, hit your emergency return. Heat stroke kills baseline humans faster than anything else there.

### 5. Sleeping and Time

It never gets dark. It gets dim. Your circadian rhythm will break in 48 hours.

Force a schedule: 8 hours attempt, even broken. Use eye mask + double hearing protection. Hammock is better than ground. Find mid-level transit support beams where security drones ignore you - locals will show you the tolerated sleeping webs if you ask/buy politely.

Don't sleep near:
- Relief valves (deafening when they blow)
- Pan edges (water rises silently)
- Anything humming with field support (gravity anomalies mean dropped things fall sideways - you don't want to roll into that while groggy)

### 6. The Exit

You cannot walk out. The description is not poetic: it is a thousand kilometers in every direction. There is no edge you will reach on foot.

You leave the way you came: multiverse shift/beacon. Test it every 12 hours. Keep beacon dry, warm, and on your chest.

Set a hard abort criteria *before* you go and obey it:
- Fever over 38.5C / 101.5F
- Feet unable to bear weight after drying
- No urination for 8 hours
- Panic/confusion you can't talk yourself out of in 10 minutes
- Beacon malfunction

If you hit one, leave. The City will not help you. It has no cruelty and no mercy. It will simply laminate over where you fell and keep weeping mineral water over the bump for a century.

On body: quick-dry hat, goggles, buff, wicking long-sleeve, drainable shoes, toe socks, earplugs on cord, headlamp, chest beacon

In dry bag:
- [ ] 2x spare wicking shirts, spare pants/shorts, 3x socks, underwear
- [ ] Foot powder, antifungal, zinc, blister kit, antibiotics, ORS, meds
- [ ] Sawyer + tablets + electrolyte packets
- [ ] Power bank + cables + sealed phone + silica packs
- [ ] Eye mask, foam plugs + muffs, hammock, 2x dry bags/ziplocs
- [ ] Trekking pole, 6m paracord, carabiners, chalk
- [ ] Small tradables, headlamp batteries, nitrile gloves

And the most important piece of gear: a willingness to keep moving, keep drinking, keep protecting your ears and feet, and to leave when your body tells you to.

If you do that, you'll come back with a memory few humans have: the sight of a patch of real blue sky, impossibly far up a crooked shaft of a billion windows, with a flock - birds or drones, you'll never know - wheeling as one through borrowed sunlight, before a wall of moving faces slides shut and the warm rain starts again indoors.

You'll breathe shallow the whole time. That's normal. That's what Lung-low means.
