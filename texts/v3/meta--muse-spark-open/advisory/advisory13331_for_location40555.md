This is not a dangerous place. It's an *uncomfortable* place, which is more dangerous for an ordinary human traveler, because you won't notice you're in trouble until you are.

What you've described is not a valley or a temple or a ruin. It's a terrarium. A hothouse. A closed experiment running on a young mountain, pretending to be a crater.

Whatever made it is still running, and it doesn't care about you, but it also hasn't turned you away. Treat it like entering someone else's greenhouse while they're gone: quiet, reverent, gloved hands, don't touch the thermostat.

Here's how you prepare and what it will actually feel like to be inside.

### 1. Understand What You're Entering

From the outside it looks empty and dry. From the inside it is wet, hot, pressurized, and alive. This is your first and most important clue: **your eyes and instruments will disagree.** The bowl has a lid you can't see from below - that faint soap-film shimmer where the four distortions meet. It's a containment field that holds a thicker, wetter atmosphere in place like water in a cup.

That means:
- **Air is not the same inside as outside.** It's heavier, wetter, hotter. Wet-bulb temperatures in there will kill you faster than thirst.
- **Water does not obey you.** It flows *toward* things, not just down. It appears out of stone that hasn't seen rain.
- **Space and sound are partitioned.** The air has "rooms." Gravity is polite but negotiable.
- **It's young rock doing old things.** The basalt is fresh, sharp, still outgassing. The water cycle inside should have run out a long time ago and hasn't. Something is recirculating it.

The whole bowl is breathing. The pylon hums, the cube inhales, the pitchers open and close, the water fills and spills. You will feel that rhythm in your chest within ten minutes. It's not hostile. It's just *complete* without you.

### 2. Before You Climb: Body and Gear

You are an ordinary human. You do not have heat exchangers in your skin or gills. This place was not made for you, so you have to bring your own margins.

**The climb will try to cut you before the bowl tries to cook you.**

That lower slope you described - black glass, stair-stepped columns, edges that ring - is new basalt. It's obsidian-sharp in places. It has not been weathered soft.

You need:
- **Boots that can be cut and keep going:** Full-ankle, hard-sole mountaineering boots. Not hiking shoes. Not trail runners. Bring spare laces. Gaiters. The rock will slice soft fabric.
- **Gloves and skin coverage:** Lightweight, cut-resistant climbing gloves for handholds. Long sleeves, light color. The sun overhead is real sun, and you will be reflecting off black rock the whole way up. Sunburn + exertion + heat is a bad combination to start with.
- **Poles or a stick:** The columns form natural stairs but they are uneven and brittle. A slip on fresh basalt will open you to the bone.

**Inside, heat is your timer, not your enemy.**

It's a greenhouse at altitude. High, hot, humid. Not desert-hot where sweat saves you. Jungle-hot where sweat *doesn't*.

Pack like you're going to spend 2-3 hours at 95-105°F / 35-40°C at 95-100% humidity, with air that feels thick in your throat:

- **Not a heavy pack:** You will overheat carrying weight. Cache water at the lip if you can and keep your inside kit light.
- **Cooling, not just insulation:** A phase-change cooling vest or at minimum a neck wrap you can soak. It will be the only thing that feels cold in there.
- **Water you bring:** At least 2-3 liters of your own, plus 1L of electrolyte. Do not plan to drink the bowl's water. There is water everywhere but it's warm, full of dissolved rock, pitcher-plant enzymes, dragonfly larvae, and whatever that orange lichen is shedding. It beads on leaves and weeps from walls for a *reason* - it's distilled and recirculated endlessly. It will give you diarrhea or worse and dehydration will kill you faster in that humidity.
- **Filter + purification as backup only:** A Sawyer or similar + chemical tabs. If you *must* drink bowl water, take only the clear drip from vertical seeps, not the warm skin-water on the plates, not the pitcher pools. Even then, boil if you can.
- **Salt:** Real salt tablets or oral rehydration salts. You will sweat, it will not evaporate, and you won't realize how much salt you're losing until you get dizzy and ill-tempered.
- **A way to measure yourself:** A small thermometer/hygrometer and a cheap fingertip pulse oximeter / CO2 alarm if you have one. The air pools and is thicker than it should be. You want to know if CO2 is building up at foot level where the warm pans sit. If you get a headache, metallic taste, rapid breathing, or feel strangely short of breath while sitting still - get up higher, get to the dry plate in the center, and prepare to leave.
- **Earplugs:** Not a joke. The *pit... pit... pit...* of a thousand seeps is meditative for ten minutes and maddening after an hour. Your brain will start trying to make the drips into patterns. It interferes with sleep and thought. Bring foam plugs so you can create silence when you need it.

**Your senses will lie to you.**

- Bring a **compass, but expect it to be useless** near the three vibrating rods and the pylon. Bring a length of string with a small weight instead. Watch which way water actually wants to go. That is the truth of the bowl's gravity.
- Bring a **field recorder or phone in airplane mode.** Play back the dragonfly later. Inside, its wings beat loudly then abruptly mute as it crosses an invisible acoustic wall. The recording will help you believe it after. It also helps map the "rooms" in the air.
- Bring a **polarized lens / sunglasses.** The shallow mirror-pans double the overhead sun. Eye strain comes fast.
- **Light for night:** The orange lichen pulses after sunset and is not bioluminescence. It's some kind of distributed circuit. Don't use white headlamp at full blast on it. Bring a dim red light if you want to stay after dark without blinding yourself or disrupting whatever it's doing. Better yet, don't plan to sleep there.

What NOT to bring:
- Drones. Anything that flies. The lid will eat it or confuse it, and the acoustic rooms scramble propeller feedback. You will lose it and add trash to an experiment.
- Glass vials or open soil kits to "sample" aggressively. The black cube inhales dust, gnats, droplets. You do not want to find out what it does with a person who tries to pry up the lichen.

### 3. The Entry: Over The Lip

You'll know you're close when the bare black glass suddenly gives way to condensation on your boots.

Stop at the lip. This is the most important pause of your trip.

1. **Drink water before you go in.** Pre-hydrate. Eat something salty.
2. **Look back down the mountain.** Memorize the way out. From inside, the outside is hard to see correctly. Mark your exit with a cairn or bright cord *on the outside* of the lip, not inside - things inside drift toward the cube and the floating steps.
3. **Listen.** From just outside it's "empty." Step one foot over. You'll hear the humidity as a change in air pressure against your eardrums, like entering a steam room. Your shirt will stick to your back within 30 seconds.
4. **Step up and over, don't jump.** The vertical drop inside is 20-30 meters of wet, undercut wall. The plates at the bottom are tilted and puddled. Ankle breaks happen here.

The moment you are fully inside, three things happen at once: sound gets too clear, air gets too present, and your sweat stops working. Accept it.

### 4. Being Inside: How to Move and Not Get Hurt

Think of the bowl as 4-5 islands on a black, warm, wet floor.

- **Stay on rock when you can, not in water.** The water is everywhere thinly - ankle-deep marshes, skins over stone. It's bath-warm and hides tilts and clefts. Your feet will prune and blister fast. Bring dry wool socks in a sealed bag and change immediately if soaked. Foot rot in 24 hours is real.
- **Treat each terrace like a room with its own rules.** One has umbrella trees cupping water. One is empty with breathing moss that steams. One has pitcher plants the size of barrels. Do not walk between islands on autopilot. Stop at each edge, watch water for 20 seconds: which way does it actually want to go? Uphill for a handspan toward the breathing cube? Toward the lowest floating step? That's the local slope of the field. Walk perpendicular, not against it, or you'll be fighting gentle but constant tug all day.

**The Four Techs - Look, Don't Touch:**

1.  **The Waist-High White Pylon:** It hums in your teeth. Infrasound. It will make you nauseous if you stand near it for more than a minute or two, and give you a headache behind the eyes. Don't touch the seam that never condenses - it's actively repelling water at a field level and your fingers are mostly water. The vertical heat-haze lens above it is a time-slowed view. If you toss a leaf through, it comes out warmer and later. Don't put your hand through it to feel it. No one knows what the delay does to nerves. Watch it, note it, move on.

2.  **The Three Slender Rods:** They sweat on one side only and vibrate without wind. They are cardinal markers / anchors for whatever is holding the atmosphere in. They are the only truly vertical things in a place where gravity wobbles. Use them to reorient yourself if you feel dizzy. Don't pull them. Don't pluck them like chimes.

3.  **The Seven Floating Steps:** About a meter apart in an arc, indifferent to you. Completely stable when stepped on. Water under them is perfectly still even when it ripples elsewhere. This is your safest crossing if you need to go from one tilted plate to another. Step lightly in the center, don't jump, don't try to rock them. They aren't supporting you with force; they are ignoring gravity, and you are a guest. If you feel any tingling, step off.

4.  **The Breathing Heart-Cube:** Black, opening and closing like a slow lung, smelling of iron and distant rain. Cool air on exhale, long inhale that draws dust and gnats. This is the bowl's lung / vacuum / cleaner. **Give it space.** Don't crouch directly over its seam. Don't feed it things to test it. Let it inhale passively. The ferns shiver for a reason. Every closed loop needs a filter. You're standing inside someone's filter.

**Fauna:**

- **Mica-winged dragonflies:** Huge, beautiful, loud up close then silent. They're harmless but startling. Don't swat. Their wings are mica-thin and tear easily. If one lands, stay still.
- **Small white diver in pitcher pools:** It never surfaces on the same side. It's either very fast under water or passing through something. Watch but don't put your hands in barrel pitchers. The water inside is digestive. It's warm and will blister if kept against skin. If you slip and get it on you, rinse with your own water.
- **The *coo* that moves impossibly fast:** You will never find it. That's the point. It's either teleporting, ventriloquizing through the acoustic rooms, or there's many of them tightly synchronized. Don't chase it terrace to terrace. You will exhaust yourself and slip. Let it call. Answer once if you want, quietly, then let it be.

**The orange lichen:** Branching circuits that pulse after sunset, growing into the rock, a millimeter a day. Do not scrape it. It's either a sensor or wiring. Treat it as live. If you sit to rest, don't lean your back against a wall covered in it. Find bare black basalt.

### 5. Time, Heat, and Knowing When to Leave

This place wants you to stay. Not maliciously. It's just very good at being a perfect, warm, wet, enclosed world. Humans are bad at sensing wet-bulb.

**Hard rules:**

- **Set a timer before you enter: 90 minutes max for your first visit.** Even if you feel fine. Even if it's beautiful. Your sweat has stopped evaporating but your body hasn't told your brain yet. Heat stroke in humid air hits like a wall: confusion, nausea, goosebumps in heat, inability to sweat more. By then you are in serious danger and a long hot climb out is still ahead.

- **Check your socks, your face, your urine.** If your socks are soaked through in 20 minutes, that's normal for the *place*, not for you. If your face is red and dripping but you stop sweating, leave. If you haven't needed to pee in 3-4 hours despite drinking, leave.

- **Watch dust.** Stand on the dry plate in the center and look up. See the soap-film shimmer where the four fields meet? Watch how dust motes rise and hang. If they start to collect or spiral instead of hanging, pressure is shifting. That's your cue the bowl is "inhaling." Go toward the walls where water seeps, not the center.

- **Do not stay the night unless you have to.** At noon the distortion from the pylon is visible. After sunset the lichen does its work and humidity hits 100%. Sounds carry differently. The *coo* changes location faster. Your headlamp makes the shallow mirrors flash back at you. It's easy to get disoriented and step into a cleft.

**How to Leave:**

Leave the same way you came: up and over the lip. Don't look for a back door. There isn't one.

As you climb out, you'll feel the air thin and cool instantly, like walking out of a greenhouse into mountain wind. You'll be wet. You'll be chilled within minutes despite being hot a second before. Have a dry layer waiting *outside* the bowl, not inside. Put it on, drink your electrolytes, and sit with your back to the bowl for ten minutes before starting down the sharp glass again.

Don't look back expecting to see lush green. From outside it looks empty and dry again. Take a photo from inside if you want proof, but don't be surprised if exposure and focus hunt strangely through the lid.

### 6. After

You will carry the dripping with you for a day. *Pit... pit... pit...* Your own house will sound too dry.

You may feel irritable, wrung out, or strangely calm. Both are normal after high humidity + infrasound + mild time-dilation exposure. Drink, sleep somewhere dry and cool, eat salty soup.

Write down what you saw, in order, while it's fresh. The bowl is designed to make you remember it as more natural than it is - as "just a crater with plants." Don't let it. Note the things that were *too* polite: water that flowed uphill, sound that stopped mid-beat, steps that didn't know you were there.

And don't take souvenirs. Not water, not a leaf from the umbrella trees, not a chip of lichen-covered basalt. The bowl is a closed loop that kept itself for a very long time before you arrived. It noticed the leaf you tossed through the lens warmed up. It will notice if you take.

Come back another time if you have to. The mountain is young and still learning to be gentle. The bowl inside it already is.
