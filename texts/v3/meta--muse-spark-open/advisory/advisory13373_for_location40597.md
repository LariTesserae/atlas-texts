You don't pack for The Pocket like you pack for a city. You pack like you're going to be swallowed and politely not digested. Remember the first rule of the place: **It is already in equilibrium. You are not.** You are the disturbance. Your job is to become as little of a disturbance as possible, or it will correct you.

This is not a survival guide for a hostile dimension. Nothing there wants to kill you. It's a guide for visiting a place that has solved the problem of existence by deciding to stop happening - and does it so well it might convince your body to do the same.

### 1. Before You Go: Understand The Arrival

You won't portal in with a flash of light. That's not how The Pocket works.

The description is literal: *It is not a city you arrive at. It is a city you notice you are already inside.* Multiverse travelers report the same thing. One moment you're squeezing through a fissure, or stepping through a basement doorway in a world that has one, or just paying too much attention to the sound of a dripping faucet - and then the sound gets louder and doesn't stop, and the air gets cool and heavy, and you look up and the ceiling is too low and too close and wet.

You are already standing in ankle-deep water. Someone is looking at you from a slate doorway, not surprised.

**Preparation:**

*   **Set an anchor BEFORE you notice.** Don't rely on a device inside. The Pocket is thermodynamically closed. Batteries drain strangely, or rather, they don't - they just settle to half-charge and stay there. Clocks tick but don't keep time. If you use a tether, totem, thread, recall word, or temporal breadcrumb, set it *before* you go under. Make it physical and stupidly simple: a knotted cord around your wrist, a stone in your pocket from home that is *dry*. Something that cannot come into equilibrium with The Pocket. When you can no longer remember why you tied it, pull it.
*   **Accept the scale.** The most dangerous shock isn't claustrophobia, it's *comprehension*. In most cities you can't see the edges. Here you can lie down in the center and see every wall, every house, every lamp at once. Your brain wants to file it as "small room" and then panics because there are 60 houses and people living in it. Don't fight that vertigo. Let it happen. It's the bubble telling you: *this is all there is.* If you fight it you'll get nauseous and disoriented for hours.
*   **Decide how long you're staying before you enter.** Don't decide inside. Time there isn't frozen, but it is *indifferent*. The lamps burn without consuming, the water falls without rising. Your circadian rhythm will lose its reference in about 3-4 hours. Bring a sandglass or other non-electrical timer if you must, but better to decide: "I will stay for 20 sleeps of drops" or "I will leave after I have spoken to three people" and honor it. The Pocket's kindness is that it never pushes you out. That's also how it keeps you.

### 2. Your Body: The Real Problem

You are a warm, dry, loud, rotting thing entering a cool, wet, loud, non-rotting place. Your body is going to complain first.

**Cold That Isn't Cold:**
It's 55-60°F / 13-16°C, 100% humidity, always. Not enough to kill you, exactly enough to make you *never be warm or dry again* while you're there. This is trench-foot, hypothermia-by-attrition weather.

*   **Do NOT wear waterproof boots.** I know it's counterintuitive. If you wear sealed rubber boots, water will get in from the top when you step in a deeper runnel and then *never leave*. You'll be sloshing in your own sweat. Wear what they wear: nothing waterproof.
*   **Wear:** Thin, tight wool socks + open, strappado sandals or bare feet if your feet are tough. Or simple leather moccasins that are already soaked. You want water to flow *through* and out. Bring 3-4 pairs of thin merino wool socks and change them constantly. Keep the wet ones tied to your belt - they won't dry, but the act helps mentally. 
*   **Layers:** 2-3 thin wool layers, not one thick coat. No cotton. No down. Cotton kills here; it will soak and stay cold against your skin. Wool stays warm when wet. Bring a wool beanie even if you never wear beanies. The ceiling radiates cool onto your scalp when you stand. You'll feel it.
*   Your fingertips will prune in 20 minutes and stay pruned. Your skin will feel like a second, truer skin, as the description says. Moisturize heavily *before* you go with a lanolin-heavy balm. Bring more balm. Your lips will crack otherwise.

**Water:**
Do not drink the floor-sheet. It's not immediately poisonous, but it tastes of feet and stone and bacterial biofilm and 800 years of mineral equilibrium. It will give you cramps and diarrhea, which in this humidity will dehydrate you fast.

*   Bring your own water in a soft flask you can keep inside your shirt to keep it from getting as cold as the room. 
*   If you must drink local, drink only from the *pots set to catch ceiling-drip*. Those are drinking pots. Every house has one or two. Do not just take one - ask. Gesture: palm up, point to pot, then to your mouth. They understand. They will nod. Always use your own hands as a cup if offered - don't put your flask in their pot.
*   Bring high-calorie, non-perishable, non-fragrant food and eat it privately. Protein bars, pemmican, dense oatcakes, nuts. **Do not offer them food.** You have no idea what a gift of an apple would do here. There are no plants. No animals. The concept of surplus food is mythic. A gift would create obligation/distortion they can't resolve. Eat quickly, discreetly, and pack out every crumb. Nothing rots here, which means your crumbs will sit there forever as litter in their perfect system.

**Sound:**
This will break you before the cold does.

It's not loud like a concert. It's loud like being inside a drum that is being tapped by hundreds of fingers that will never find a rhythm. *tok. tok-tok. tok.* Each drop echoes off hard, curved, wet basalt until you can't tell source from reflection. The floor-sheet slaps. The walls sheet water silently. You will have to shout to be heard leaning 12 inches from someone's face.

*   **Bring soft silicone earplugs, but DO NOT wear them constantly.** If you block the sound completely, you are deaf and rude - you won't hear voices or footsteps in the narrow squeezes. Wear them for 20 minutes every 2 hours in a corner to give your auditory cortex a break. Otherwise, let your brain do what theirs did: learn to un-hear it. It takes about 6-8 hours for the pressure to replace the noise. Until then you will be irritable and think everyone is yelling at you. They are. They've all gone half-hoarse from living in a bathroom the size of a town.
*   Your own voice will be hoarse within a day. Bring throat lozenges with honey. Whispering doesn't work - it gets lost. Speak from the chest, low and slow.

**Light:**
Leave your 1000-lumen tactical flashlight at home. If you blast white light across The Pocket, you'll blind everyone, kill their dark adaptation, and reveal yourself as profoundly disrespectful.

*   The light is 0.7 dim. Weak tea amber from dozens of fat-stone lamps that sweat oil as fast as they burn. Each lamp makes a puddle of visibility an arm's length wide. Between them are rippling mirror-darknesses reflected in the floor-sheet.
*   Bring a single, dim, warm amber light. A salt lamp on lowest setting, or a candle lantern with amber glass. Keep it hooded, pointed down. Use it only to see your footing in the deepest shadows between houses. Never shine it in a window-hole. Never try to "help" by relighting a lamp. Their lamps never go out for a reason - relighting is hard labor in damp air and is ritually significant. Tending one's own lamp is tending one's own existence.
*   Bring a tiny UV-free headlamp with a red-light mode for emergencies only. Red preserves night vision and won't insult the amber.

**Air:**
It's so humid your breath doesn't plume, it just makes the air heavier. At certain times - their "inhalation" - the air thickens and you *will* see your breath hang too long.

*   If you have asthma, bring your inhaler and keep it warm/dry in a ziploc inside your shirt. The damp mineral air is heavy and the basalt dust is fine.
*   No fire beyond their lamps. Don't bring a stove. Don't smoke.

### 3. Your Mind: The Real Real Problem

The Pocket is psychologically infectious, and not in a horror way.

It is *peaceful* in a way that is dangerous for a traveler. Open worlds are entropic, chaotic, demanding. This place has finished demanding. It is loud and crowded and dim and wet and *done*. No wind, no weather, no seasons, no decay, no growth. Nothing to fix. The runnels go in a slow pointless circle. The people move slowly, economically, conserving a different kind of energy.

You will feel, after ~12 hours, a profound calm. The pressure of the drops becomes depth. The cool becomes normal. The fact that you can comprehend the entire world at once becomes comforting. You will understand why they never left. There is nowhere to go where equilibrium is so kind.

**This is the trap.**

For an ordinary human traveler, the risk isn't death. It's *acclimation*. People who stay too long stop wanting to check their anchor. They start tending a lamp. They find a gap between two slate houses and think, "I could fit a house there." The Pocket has room because it is dense, not because it is empty - and yet it makes you feel like there is a space *for you*.

**Preparation:**

*   **Claustrophobia:** If you are severely claustrophobic, do not go. The ceiling is low enough to feel on your scalp. The houses touch. You squeeze sideways through gaps. There is no visible outside, no sky. You must be able to sit with that and breathe. Practice: 10 minutes in a small, dark, tiled bathroom with the shower running cold and the lights off, just listening to drips.
*   **Bring a small, dry, *private* task.** Whittle, knot rope, write in a waterproof notebook with a pencil. Something that is *yours* and requires change and progress. The city has no progress. You need a reminder that you are a creature of change. Do not do this task to show off - do it in your corner.
*   **Do not try to map it perfectly.** You can. That's the problem. Travelers become obsessed with mapping the 60 houses, the runnels, the lamp positions, trying to find the pattern. There is no pattern, only packing. Let it be messy and dense.
*   **Do not try to "solve" the impossibility.** Don't measure the water level obsessively, don't try to prove the lamps are perpetual motion, don't sample the biofilm for lab analysis. The equilibrium is not a puzzle to crack; it's the local physics. Your instruments will lie or give up. Your attention is better spent on the people.

### 4. Etiquette: How Not to Be a Disturbance

There is no mayor, no guards, no square. Governance is water management and mutual shoulder-brushing.

*   **Move slowly and sideways.** They move economically. Don't rush, don't splash. When you displace floor-water, do it gently. Step, pause, let the little wave slap and settle. If you barrel through you'll soak someone's hide-curtain and dwelling.
*   **Touch lightly.** The pillars and walls are glossy from a thousand years of shoulders brushing past. You can touch them. It's okay. Don't carve, don't scrape the white calcite fans or black manganese blooms or travertine dams. That's their only decoration, their slow mineral art.
*   **Doors are hide curtains, windows are holes.** Don't knock - there's nothing to knock. Stand at the threshold where the floor-sheet is slightly worn, say a soft "hello" loudly, and wait to be waved in. Never enter uninvited. Never look too long into a window-hole before being invited - light is communal, but dwelling is private.
*   **Lamps:** Never touch another person's lamp. Tend your own if you brought one. If your amber lantern sputters, they will watch intently to see if you know how to coax it in damp air. That's a small test of competence.
*   **The Salamander Rule:** Fauna is religion. If you see the pale blind salamander paddling through the floor-sheet, or a cave shrimp flicking in a pool, STOP. Let it go where it's going. Do not pick it up, do not follow it eagerly, do not point and exclaim. Quietly watch. If a local sees you being still for it, they will respect you immensely. If a rat ever finds its way down the fissure, you are witnessing a once-in-a-generation event. Be solemn.
*   **Speak loudly without shouting, and listen.** They are hoarse, not deaf. Don't condescend. Don't ask "why don't you leave?" They have heard it from every traveler who thought they were rescuers. The answer is in the walls. Instead ask: "Who built which layer?" "Which runnel was chipped first?" "Whose hands smoothed this pillar?" They love those questions.
*   **No gifts that rot, grow, or shine.** No fruit, no seeds, no live things, no bright LEDs, no mirrors. Good gifts: a well-made, simple hand-tool (a good steel awl, a small hammerstone), a length of good wool felt, a story from an open-air place told briefly without nostalgia. Bad gifts: anything that implies they are deprived.

### 5. Leaving

This is harder than arriving because the fissure is somewhere in the dark ceiling where no ladder reaches. The story that the bubble breathes once a day is probably true in the way traveler stories are true: not literally, but usefully.

*   Watch the air. When condensing atmosphere thickens and your breath finally shows and hangs too long - that's the inhale. That's when the fissure is *open* in the conceptual sense. The air shifts. The tok-tok of drops seems to pause, just for a heartbeat.
*   That's when you pull your anchor. Don't wait for a physical hole to appear. Stand in the center, hold your dry stone from home, say your recall word out loud even though you have to shout it over the drops. Mean it.
*   You will notice you are no longer inside. You will be somewhere else, probably damp, with ringing ears and pruned fingers that take days to un-prune. You'll be hoarse for a week.
*   Don't look back immediately. The Pocket is very good at being noticed. If you look back too fast, you might notice you're still there.

**Final checklist to pack on your body:**

1. Wool, wool, wool - socks, base layers, beanie - no cotton
2. Open sandals / soft moccasins
3. Lanolin balm, throat lozenges, 2L soft water flask
4. Dense, odorless calories in ziplocs
5. Dim amber lantern + emergency red headlamp
6. Soft silicone earplugs
7. Dry anchor stone/cord in inner pocket
8. Small private hand-task
9. Waterproof pencil notebook

**Leave behind:** expectations of a city, need for progress, any urge to fix or save, your brightest light.

If you do it right, you'll leave with the sound still in your ears for days. *tok. tok-tok. tok.* And a small, unsettling understanding that equilibrium is not death. It's just a different kind of aliveness - one that has decided it has enough houses, enough water, enough light, and doesn't need you to add anything at all.

That's why you should visit briefly, respectfully, and then go.
