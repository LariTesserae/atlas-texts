This is not a dangerous place. It will not eat you, shoot at you, or freeze you solid five minutes after your portal closes.

It will just *weather* you. Patiently. The way it weathers everything.

An ordinary human can live in the High City indefinitely -- people do -- but an ordinary multiverse traveler who shows up unprepared will be miserable, sick, lost, and culturally offside within about six hours. Not because the City is hostile. Because it is *complete*. It has already solved every problem its environment poses, over centuries, and your solutions from elsewhere will be wrong.

Here's how to prepare.

### 1. Reset Your Expectations

Most travelers prepare for extremes - cold, heat, radiation, monsters. Prepare instead for **constancy.**

**Scale:** You cannot traverse the High City. Do not try to "see it." It's the size of the American Midwest lifted a mile straight up. The horizon doesn't end, it dissolves into haze and steam and more brick. Your brain will keep trying to find an edge or a downtown and fail. For your first trip, pick *one district* and treat it as the whole world. If you wander more than a kilometer from where you arrived without a guide or a marked water-path, you are lost.

**Time:** Nothing is fast here. Weather arrives at exactly ~3pm every day. Streets deepen a millimeter a year. People speak slowly, work steadily, repair endlessly. If you come in looking for adventure, you will be driven insane by 2 AM belt-hum of presses and cooing of ten million birds. Come in looking for *inhabitation.*

**Light & Sky:** This is high desert sun on wet stone. Brutal UV at noon, hard shadows, luminous shade under awnings. Bring sunglasses, even though it feels like an industrial northern city. At night it's not dark the way a wilderness is dark -- it's orange sodium, window light, low cloud reflecting the hum.

> Intuition check: Visiting is not like landing on an alien planet. It's like being dropped into Manchester or Łódź or Pittsburgh in 1928, except someone took away all the trees and turned the ground itself into a warm radiator and a spring.

### 2. Your Body In That Air

You will be breathing hot-wet limestone forever.

**The Humidity Cycle:**
Morning: 70-85% humidity, stone sweating.
~3:00 PM: Deposition Event. The air condenses. It doesn't rain. It *materializes* on you. Everything darkens two shades, railings weep, your shirt becomes unwringable in 7 minutes. Then sun returns and the entire continent steams. Visibility drops to half a block.
Evening: Warm, muggy, dripping.

That cycle is why locals have damp walls as a fact of life and keep papers in tin boxes. You, as a visitor from a climate-controlled universe, are extremely vulnerable to:

- **Trench foot / immersion foot:** Feet wet for days.
- **Fungal bloom:** Skin, ears, lungs. Groin, armpits, under breasts, between toes.
- **Respiratory irritation:** Fine brick dust + bird guano ammonia + mold spores = brutal on asthmatics and allergy sufferers. The morning scraping crews raise a pale paste that smells like cat piss and old concrete. That paste *is* the City's topsoil and it goes airborne easily.

**Medical Prep Before You Go:**
1. Update tetanus. Rust is everywhere and everything is sharp in a worn-down way.
2. Bring a serious antifungal - topical clotrimazole and tolnaftate powder, plus whatever your doctor recommends for prevention. You will use it daily whether you think you need it or not.
3. If you have any mold allergy, asthma, or chronic bronchitis, talk to your doctor. Honestly reconsider a long stay. At minimum bring antihistamines, steroid nasal spray, and a real supply of N95/KN95 masks. You'll wear one on the first morning when the crews scrape the guano.
4. Ear care: constant drip and steam means otitis externa [swimmer's ear]. Bring drying ear drops.
5. Sun protection: SPF 50, lip balm. The UV at a mile-up is no joke even though the air feels damp.
6. Get used to sleeping with earplugs *before* you go. The sound never stops.

### 3. Pack Like a Local (Who Owns Zip-Locks)

Forget tactical gear. Pack like a maintenance worker for an infinite bathhouse.

**What to Bring:**

*Footwear:* This is #1. The bedrock is persistently, weirdly warm -- 30-35C / 85-95F to the touch in places. Not burning, but enough that cheap glue-soled sneakers delaminate in a week and your feet will stew. And everything drains. You need:
- Closed-toe, quick-drain sandals OR perforated leather boots with breathable, non-waterproof membranes. No Gore-Tex boots. They will never dry.
- Merino wool socks, 5+ pairs, hung every night. And talc/antifungal powder.
- A cheap pair of indoor slip-ons/slides. You WILL take your shoes off inside. Every home has a little fired-clay draining rack by the door. Using it correctly earns immediate respect.

*Clothing:*
- Nothing cotton that you care about. Cotton stays wet for a day and then grows things. Synthetics or lightweight merino that dries in an hour.
- Long sleeves and pants even in heat. Protects from sun, from guano dust, from beetles, from abrasion on that mineral-shiny stone.
- A truly waterproof, breathable rain shell -- not for rain, but for the 3 PM Deposition. Locals just get wet and wait, but you don't have their tolerance yet. You need to be able to sit through the steam-off without being soaked to the bone.

*For Your Stuff:*
- Your electronics *will* die without help. Condensation beads on cold metal and subway tiles that were never near a subway. Bring:
  - Multiple large dry-bags + gallon Ziplocs + silica gel by the pound. Keep phone, passport, meds, papers in tin boxes / Pelican cases.
  - A small dry-box for sleep.
  - Assume any exposed steel will spot-rust overnight. Wipe down, oil your knife, don't bring heirloom anything.
- Power: The City is electrified - humming belt-driven lathes bolted straight to bedrock. Outlets exist, usually 220V-ish Euro-style depending on district, often jury-rigged. Bring a universal adapter and a power bank. Keep it dry.

*For Your Lungs/Nose:*
- N95s, at least a dozen. You'll need them in under-levels, during scraping, near Brickworks smudges.
- Simple saline nasal rinse bottle.
- Vicks or menthol to put under your nose if the ammonia-grout smell gets to you.

*For Sleep & Sanity:*
- High-quality foam earplugs + silicone over-ear. The chord is constant: presses + drip + cooing + flutter + hosing streets. It reverberates off wet stone and low cloud from a mile away. Earplugs won't silence it, but they turn it into what locals hear: background.
- Eye mask. Lights in alleys never really go out.
- Something green for your eyes. Seriously. Bring a tiny photo of a forest, a small jade plant in a sealed terrarium, a green scarf. There is NO green at distance. Only ochre dust, black-green slick, rust-felt, brick-blood. After 3-4 days many travelers get a weird low mood they can't name. It's plant-deprivation and sensory sameness.

**What NOT to Bring:**
- Seeds, seedlings, houseplants. The ecology is closed and mineral-based. Introducing foreign biology is disrespectful and, per local creed, pointless. Plus there is nowhere to put it; nothing leaves, soil doesn't exist. Customs [informal, but real] will confiscate or mock you.
- Tall tents, lightweight pop-ups, awnings not tied to stone. Wind at the Table edge will take them. Weight matters is literally law.
- Pets. Dogs are rare for a reason. Cats exist and are revered as semi-feral municipal employees. Do not bring your cat. Do not pet feral cats without invitation. Do not shoo birds. You will be stared at.
- Open food, especially starch/grain. You are entering the base of a food chain. Your crumbs feed bacterial felt which feeds woodlice the size of your thumb which... everything else. Store all food sealed, hanging. Expect moths the color of limestone to find it anyway.

### 4. Water, Food, and Don't Fight The Weep

**Water:** You are surrounded by it and should not drink most of it untreated.

It sweats from stone, beads on tiles, runs in every gutter-canyon. But it has run over guano-paste, bacterial felt, rust, brick dust, and ten thousand cisterns. Even taps are often captured condensation piped building-to-building.

Rule: Ask where a building's **formal water path** is. It's etched in deeds. There will be a bucket under a weep, a channel, a communal gutter. That is drinking/scooping water *after* boiling/filtering. If Mrs. Deluca upstairs empties her dehumidifier bucket into the communal gutter before breakfast and lectures you, thank her and learn from her channeling.

Bring a Grayl/Sawyer filter + UV pen or tablets. And a collapsible bucket. Imitate locals: you don't dam. You divert.

Dehydration is sneaky because the air is so wet you don't feel you're losing water. You are - warm rock, steam, walking on endless stone. Drink more than you think.

**Food:** Eat what locals eat. It's safe, starchy, mineral-heavy: boiled, tiled-oven baked, fried. All water used for cooking has been re-caught many times and thoroughly boiled. Bring electrolyte powder. Avoid raw street things that have sat in deposition steam unless you watched them cooked bolted-to-bedrock hot.

### 5. Fauna Etiquette: You Are Outnumbered

You heard them before you saw infrastructure for them. The sky furs.

- Birds nest in everything, not trees. Wires become thatch. Ledges, cisterns, wall cavities. Entire districts taking off at once looks like flicker, then sounds like shuffling paper. **Do not try to clear a ledge, dislodge a nest, or wave them off aggressively.** That ledge IS a nest. Legally and practically. Scoop around.
- Their droppings mixed with dust are scraped every dawn and sold to brickworks. Don't photograph crews like animals; nod and move. Don't step in fresh paste barefoot. Breathing it dry is the main histoplasmosis vector.
- Woodlice & beetles: The woodlice are blind, pale, thumb-sized, eating felt off stone. The gutters seem to move because black beetles click along by the thousands. They are not infestation. They ARE sanitation. Never poison, step deliberately only, don't kick. Walk *with* the gutter drainage cuts, not against their traffic.
- Moths: limestone-colored, only move in steam hour. If one is on you, brush gently.
- No real predators. Lean, wet cats try. Leave them alone to do their sacred, failed job.

If you are disgusted by bugs/birds, cut your trip to 48 hours. There's no relief, no park without them. Exposure therapy will work or you will flee.

### 6. Navigation & Culture

**Navigation:** Streets are worn in, corners rounded by shoulders, gutters canyon-deep. Maps age badly because nothing is demolished, only accreted -- a fallen wall becomes a bench/dam. What *doesn't* change is water. Learn to navigate by flow: water always knows where Down-Table and Out-To-Edge is. Follow it downhill to find bigger service levels; follow it uphill to find cisterns/towers.

Climb a water tower on day one and look. You will see neighborhoods exhaling at different rates, smudges that aren't weather. That dark smudge west *is* fifty square miles of kilns. Accept you cannot hold the whole city in mind.

Tell someone where you are. GPS struggles over that much homogeneous brick + moisture cloud. Paper map + noted gutter numbers > phone pin.

**Culture - Three Laws You Will Feel:**

1. *Water runs, you follow.* Don't mop and throw out. Bucket, channel, redirect. Empty your dehumidifier into the correct gutter or Mrs. Deluca will correct you in annoyed kindness. Building permits ARE water permits.
2. *Weight matters.* Don't build tall/light, tie to stone. As traveler, this means secure EVERYTHING before afternoon wind, don't leave laundry/corrugated junk untethered, and absolutely don't lean or climb temporary scaffolds near the Table edge. Edge cliffs are columnar basalt a kilometer straight down, with source-less waterfalls pouring out of aquifer. Updrafts catch scraps and birds -- and anything light you drop will be gone three districts away.
3. *Nothing leaves.* No landfill, no forest to absorb. Brick dust becomes new brick. Water caught-used-caught again. Sound reused as echo. So carry in, carry out doesn't apply -- *nothing* leaves, period. Pack out trash to your portal point or ask where the local brickworks temper pile or reclamation shop is. Don't toss biodegradable "because it'll rot." It won't. It'll be eaten by woodlice and you will be judged.

Generally: Be quiet, be patent, be heavy. Doors left open in winter because warm rock solves heating badly; close a door loudly and you've announced you don't live here. Keep papers in tin. Offer desiccants, dry matches, good earplugs as gifts -- far more valued than sweets.

Language drifts but is understandable; expect dialect where water-words outnumber green-words fifty to one. Learn: *weep, cut, cistern, temper, accretion, re-cut.*

**Sundays at the Edge:** Yes, go. People watch gulls catch scraps on updraft, argue that next gutter re-cut is in "fifty years" as it's been for three generations. It's pilgrimage and social club. Stand back from crumbling lip, keep hat secured, don't litter.

### 7. How To Leave Well

- Plan departure BEFORE 2:45 PM or AFTER 5 PM. Opening a portal/multiverse door during deposition means instant condensation *inside* your electronics/luggage.
- Dry everything obsessively before portal transit. Mold spores from the High City will happily colonize your home closet/backpack. Wash synthetics hot, sun-dry, bag with silica.
- Check shoes for hitchhiking woodlice/eggs/beetles. Customs both ways cares.
- Expect reverse shock: your first night home in silence will feel uncannily dead. No drip, no cooing chord through stone transmitted up through feet. Many travelers sleep poorly the first night back.

At 3 PM, when the whole enormous place glazes over, then steams quietly in the sun and smells exactly like fresh rain on hot brick, every bird for a thousand miles will go silent at once, listening to the water run. If you have prepared, you'll be standing under an awning in dry shoes, bucket correctly placed under your wall's weep, not fighting it -- and you'll feel precisely why people stay. Because the stone under your bed is warm.

Enjoy the High City. Tread lightly -- but weigh heavily.
