You do not visit this plateau as a tourist. You visit it as a trespasser who has been *allowed* to trespass — wet, cold, loud, and hopelessly human in a place that solved those problems by ceasing to be human at all.

If you are an ordinary, unmodified human traveler, you can go there and come back alive, but only if you prepare to be uncomfortable for every second you are there. This is not a hostile city. It is an *indifferent* city. It will not attack you. It will not rescue you. It will keep condensing, weeping, humming, and growing, and you will be in the way of all of it.

Here is how to survive it.

### 1. Understand What This Place Actually Is

Forget "mountain city" as in Tibet or the Andes. This is not a city *on* a mountain. This is a city that is *part of* a mountain that is being born.

**The deal:**

1.  **Too high, too cold, too wet at once:** At ~4,500-6,000m equivalent, the air is thin enough to make you stupid and clumsy. It should be a dry frozen desert. Instead, it's a dripping wet greenhouse because the whole plateau is a geothermal condenser. You will be hypoxic *and* soaked.
2.  **Nothing is natural anymore:** There is no soil, no trees, no birds. The "fauna" rating is not wildlife — it's infrastructure. The things flying, carpeting, and swarming are the city's power grid, immune system, repair crew, livestock, and pets all at once, half-grown, half-built.
3.  **Nothing is fast, except how fast you can get hurt:** The mountains rise millimeters per year. The city adds a terrace per decade. The swarms migrate over weeks. You, breathing hard, dripping, shivering, shouting over the choir, will be the fastest, most frantic, most breakable thing there.

If you go expecting beauty or adventure, you will panic. If you go expecting to witness a living factory that happens to look like a city in a low cloud, you will be okay.

### 2. Your Body Is The Problem

You cannot acclimatize to this place naturally in one jump. Do not try to raw-dog it.

**Non-negotiable physiological prep:**

**A. Oxygen:**
*   You need supplemental oxygen. Not "maybe" — you need it. The air is thin, cold, and water-saturated. Every breath costs you heat and water. At this pressure you will get altitude sickness in 2-4 hours, HAPE/HACE risk in 12-24 hours if you exert yourself.
*   Bring a closed-circuit or demand-valve system with a heated regulator. Cheap open-flow tourist bottles will freeze shut here. The fog will ice over valves in minutes. You want a mask that covers nose and mouth with an anti-ice membrane, not nasal cannulas.
*   Pre-breathe for at least 48 hours before you translate. Arrive already on O2. Set your flow to keep SpO2 >90% at rest. You will still feel drunk, headachey, and short of breath. That is normal. If you can't walk a straight line or your lips turn blue *with* oxygen, you leave. No debate.
*   Bring acetazolamide if your travel medic approves it, and dexamethasone as an emergency bail-out only. This is not medical advice — talk to a real multiverse travel physician. But do talk to one.

**B. Cold + Wet = Death Clock:**
This is not dry alpine cold. Dry cold you can layer for. This is -20C to -40C *and* 100% humidity with constant seep, drizzle, and condensation. You will be wet to the bone within 15 minutes, and wet kills 25x faster than dry cold.

*   You need a true waterproof-breathable hardshell system, not a puffy jacket. Think: sailing in a polar storm. Seam-taped, pit zips that actually seal, integrated hood, face gaiter.
*   Underneath: synthetic or wool only. No cotton anywhere on your body, including underwear and socks. Cotton kills here in under an hour.
*   Your boots must be waterproof, insulated to -40C, with aggressive traction for plate-glass ice. Bring gaiters that seal to your pants, and 2-3 pairs of vapor-barrier liner socks. You will be standing in 5-15cm of mineral seep-water in every street.
*   Gloves: System of thin liner + waterproof insulated mitten with wrist leashes. You will need dexterity to operate your mask and not touch anything, then immediate warmth.
*   You will still be cold. Accept a core temperature drop. Your goal is not to be comfortable. Your goal is to stay above hypothermia for 6-12 hours.

**C. Sensory Overload:**
*   **Sound:** The choir never stops. Clicks, chitters, honks, thrums, ice-song. In an enclosed alley it hits 80-90 dB continuous, with infra-bass you feel in your chest. This causes disorientation, anxiety, and inability to sleep within hours.
    Bring active noise-cancelling earplugs/headphones rated for cold. Not to block everything — you need to hear cracking ice and wind — but to take the edge off so you can think. Bring earplugs for sleep.
*   **Light:** There is almost no sun. Your eyes will strain. The city glows orange from heat veins, cold white from rime refracting, and sporadically from fauna. It's beautiful and deeply depressing. Bring a headlamp with RED light only. White light blinds you, angers the roosting thatch-fauna, and marks you as clueless. Bring amber clip-ons for your O2 mask so you can see your gauges.
*   **Smell/Taste:** Everything smells like wet stone, ozone, hot mineral, and faintly of methane/beer-yeast from the mantas' metabolism. The air tastes metallic. Bring menthol inhaler sticks if you are sensitive to nausea — the hypoxia will make it worse.

### 3. Your Kit

Pack like you are going to work on an oil rig that is also a beehive in a freezer car wash.

**Wear/Carry on body:**
1.  Heated O2 system + 2x spare batteries kept inside your core layers, against your skin
2.  Hardshell suit, insulated mid-layer, vapor barriers
3.  Ice-rated boots + crampons/microspikes that fit them
4.  Red headlamp + backup
5.  ANC headphones/earplugs
6.  Thermal flask with hot, salty, sugary liquid. You will not want to eat, but you will burn 4,000+ calories a day just staying warm. Electrolytes are critical.
7.  Translator/beacon with dead-man timer. If you pass out, it yanks you out.

**In a small waterproof pack:**
*   Chemical hand/foot/body warmers — dozens. Keep batteries and regulators warm with them.
*   Dry bag with complete change of base layers. If you fall into a drainage channel, you change *immediately* or you leave.
*   Traction aids, ice axe or collapsible pole — not for climbing, for testing ice thickness over drains.
*   Waterproof gloves, balaclava, goggles. Full goggles that seal. The wind-driven rime will sandblast your eyes.
*   Small thermos of isopropyl or de-icer for your mask regulator.
*   Simple first aid: blister care, ibuprofen, vaseline for exposed skin, altitude meds.

**Do NOT bring:**
*   Drones. The airspace belongs to the mantas and spheres. Your drone will be swarmed, mistaken for a rival, and either dismantled or ignored until its rotors ice up and it crashes onto thatch-fauna. That's rude.
*   White flashlights, laser pointers, loud speakers. Don't try to talk to the choir by shouting.
*   Food to share. Do not feed the fauna. They do not eat human food. They drink seep-water, filter spores off heat-exchangers, and graze on city-grown biofilms. Your protein bar will just get wet and contaminate a condenser intake.
*   Suction cups, pitons, anything to "climb" the city. The walls are alive and will try to heal around your gear.

### 4. How to Move and Behave

**Arrival:**
Tell your rig to place you on an outer terrace, not the core. The core is warmer but denser, wetter, louder, and the fauna is so thick you cannot move without brushing it. The outer terraces have wind, but you have space to breathe and calibrate. Arrive standing still. Do not arrive walking.

Give yourself 10 minutes to just stand: Check O2, check seals, check footing. Let the sound hit you. Let your eyes adjust. You will want to move fast because you are cold. Force yourself to move at 1/3 your normal speed. Every slip on transparent ice at altitude can break an ankle you cannot afford.

**Walking:**
*   Roofs are roads. Roads are roofs. There is no "sidewalk." Follow the heat. Dull orange glow under ice = actively heated, less iced, more stable. Dark, thick transparent armor = abandoned, possibly thin over a void.
*   Never step on the thatch. If a wall is carpeted in shivering feathered things opening and closing, that is not decoration. That is a radiator. Give it 0.5m clearance. If you brush it, they will scatter, you will lose heat, and repair filament will try to clean you off.
*   Drainage channels are sacred. They are knee-deep, fast, and full of slick writhing forms drinking. Do not wade in them unless the path forces you. If you must cross, step *over* on a pipe or narrow span, test with a pole.
*   Bridges: Look pencil-thin, but are carbon-lattice and stronger than anything you own. Still, cross one at a time, low center of gravity. The press-wind can make you stumble. Don't look down at first — the "below" is unlit young geology, a black drop that triggers vertigo even without fear of falling.
*   Alleys go *through* buildings. They are airlocks. Expect double doors/curtains of heavy plastic or bacterial membrane. Close one before opening the next. You're keeping heat in, not just for you.

**Gravity spots:**
You will feel them. A plaza where fog hangs weirdly long = light gravity. Your steps will be bouncy, your O2 mask will feel light. Nice, but don't linger — low-G plazas are often sorting or growing fields and fauna nests there. A heavy pit where your knees ache = high-G industrial. Leave immediately. Your lungs and heart are already stressed. Do not try to "experience" it for fun.

**The Inhabitants:**
There are no human locals to ask for directions. The city *is* the local.

*   The big translucent mantas are grazers. Harmless, slow, but huge. If one honks over you, freeze. It is echolocating through fog. It will pass. Don't wave your arms.
*   The marble spheres are builders. They click together into temporary bodies to fix a crack, then dissolve. If a swarm flows across your path, stop. Wait. It will flow around you if you are still. If you walk through it, it will try to incorporate your boot, then realize you're not basalt and leave, but you'll have lost time and footing.
*   The thatch/carpets are the most important. They are thermoregulators. Do not touch, poke, or photograph with flash. A thermal camera will show them as blazing white — they are burning calories to stay warm. Respect the heat budget.

General rule: Be predictable, slow, small, and boring. This city rewards slow condensation. Be fog.

### 5. How It Feels (So You Don't Panic)

You will feel claustrophobic even though you're outdoors. The cloud is at roof-height, the alleys are tunnels, the sound has no silence. You will feel profoundly alone — no human faces, no green, no sky — and simultaneously crowded, because every surface is alive.

You will get irrationally angry at the dripping. Drip, trickle, sheet-flow, refreeze, crack. It is the city's metronome. Your brain will try to find a pattern and fail. That's when the ANC headphones earn their weight.

You will be awed, then quickly exhausted. Hypoxia makes everything emotional. Bring a mantra: *Wet, slow, dim, loud — all normal. Wet, slow, dim, loud.* Say it when you feel overwhelmed.

You will want to stay "just a little longer" to see a swarm wheel or a geothermal vent breathe. Don't. Set a hard time limit *before* you go: 4-6 hours for first visit, max 12 if you are experienced and have a warm refuge identified. The cold debt accumulates invisibly.

### 6. Leaving

Have two exits. Primary translation on timer, secondary manual panic beacon on your chest, under your shell but over your mid-layer so you can hit it with mittens.

If you fall in water, if your O2 ices over, if you get vertigo and can't tell slope from void, if you develop a wet cough that won't stop — you leave. No photos are worth pulmonary edema on a plateau that heals its own stone but will not heal you.

When you get back, you will be soaked, deafened, and buzzing with thin-air headache for hours. Change into dry heat, drink something hot, sit in silence and darkness. Let your nervous system remember what quiet sounds like.

---

**Final intuition:** This is a womb-place. Not a womb for you — for mountains and for a kind of life that decided warmth is more important than sunlight, and patience is more important than speed. You are not invited to stay and live there. You are invited to witness that such a solution exists: a city that weeps without raining, glows without sun, and hums without ever needing to be quiet.

Go. Be respectful. Be waterproof. Be slow. Let it press on you, drip on you, and sing to you, and then come home and appreciate dry ground and a truly dark, quiet night.

And bring back nothing but condensation on your boots — even the water is part of its body.
