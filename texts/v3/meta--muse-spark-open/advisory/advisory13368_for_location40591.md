You are not preparing for a dive. You are preparing for a burial.

That is the first thing to understand about this place. People think "ocean" and imagine going *in*, and then later getting *out* — surface, shore, boat waiting. This ocean does not have an out. There is technically air and sky miles above you, but for every practical purpose you will never see it. You arrive *inside*. You leave *inside*. The water is not an obstacle between you and the location. The water *is* the location. You will live inside a cold, dark, heavy liquid for the entire duration of your visit, while standing on a continent-sized floor that used to be architecture.

If you go there as an ordinary human — soft, warm, air-breathing, surface-evolved — you need to compensate for the fact that absolutely nothing about this place wants you alive. It won't try to kill you, either. That's worse. It will just ignore you until you quietly stop.

Here is how you prepare.

### 1. Solve Pressure Before You Solve Anything Else

This is not snorkeling depth. This is not recreational scuba depth. The description — no directional light, uniform cold, weight as a sense — puts you hundreds to potentially thousands of meters down. That is deep aphotic zone pressure.

**An ordinary wetsuit and SCUBA tank = instant death.** Your lungs would collapse, nitrogen would dissolve into your blood until you went insane, oxygen would become toxic.

You have three viable options:

**a) A hard-shell solution:** Atmospheric diving suit (ADS) like a Newtsuit, or a small personal submersible. This is the safest for an ordinary traveler. You stay at 1 atmosphere inside, the machine takes the pressure. You will be clumsy, heavy, and slow — which is *good* here. This place punishes speed.

**b) A saturation solution:** Trimix rebreather, heated drysuit with hot-water umbilical, years of technical dive training. You will need staged decompression that takes longer than your actual visit. If you don't know what that means, you are not qualified for this. Do not learn it just for this place. Take the hard suit.

**c) A multiversal exemption:** If you have access to body-modification, pressure-nullification, or translated-biology tech that lets you breathe water at ambient pressure — bring it. But bring it *redundantly*. Bring two systems and an emergency ballast ascent bladder you can trigger with your teeth.

Whatever you choose: **Test it in normal heavy water first.** If you haven't spent 20 hours in your system in a dark, cold lake, you have no business spending 20 minutes on the Floor.

### 2. Solve Cold Second. This Cold Is Different.

It's not -20°C and windy. It's +2°C to +4°C, perfectly even, utterly soaking, and infinite. It has thermal mass on its side. You are a 37°C bag of salt water. It will take your heat through conduction 25x faster than air.

You will not shiver at first. You will just... get slow. Thoughts will get slow. You will decide you don't need to check your pressure gauge right now. You will decide to rest your forehead on that nice smooth brick for a minute. That is hypothermia. In this place, hypothermia doesn't kill you with drama. It makes you comfortable and then you sit down in the silt and never get up.

You need:

* **Active heating, not passive insulation.** A good drysuit with Thinsulate keeps you alive for an hour. A drysuit with an active hot-water or electrical heating loop keeps you alive for a day. In an ADS, you need internal climate control. Bring 3x the battery/heating you think you need.
* **No exposed skin. Ever.** Hood, heated gloves, heated socks. Your hands will be useless in 10 minutes without them. And you need your hands.
* **Caloric pre-load.** Eat like you're going on a polar expedition. Bring high-fat, high-sugar emergency food in a suit-accessible pouch. You are going to burn calories just existing.
* **A hard time limit.** Decide *before* you jump: "I leave after X hours no matter what I see." Write it on your arm. Cold makes you lie to yourself about how you're feeling.

### 3. Solve Light And Orientation

This is the psychological killer.

* There is no sun. The "bruised dusk" is diffuse, directionless, and weak. It will not cast a shadow, so your brain loses up/down/depth cues. You will get vertigo.
* Visibility is 10-20 meters at best, often less when you stir silt or when "weather" — slow thermal turbidity — rolls through. 
* There are no landmarks. The Floor is designed to be flat and repetitive for eternity. A terrace edge that runs for a hundred miles looks exactly like every other terrace edge. You *will* get lost, even with a compass.

**Bring your own light, but treat it like a sin.**

The plants here survive on almost nothing. You don't know if bright white floodlights will damage them, trigger spore release, or just be a waste of power. And in that suspended, particulate-filled water, a powerful light just blinds you with backscatter. It's like driving in fog with high beams.

Bring:

* **Two low-intensity, warm-toned dive lights** — 500-1000 lumens, wide flood, not spot. Use the lowest setting that lets you read the stone at arm's length. Bring a red-light mode for close work. It bothers the forest less and saves your dark adaptation.
* **A tether line.** This is non-negotiable. A physical, spool-out guide line connected to your entry point/beacon. The Floor has no hills to climb for perspective. Without a tether, you will swim for 20 minutes, turn around, and have no idea where you started. The forest all looks the same. The line is your only road home.
* **An inertial navigation system + dead reckoning computer.** GPS doesn't work under miles of water and stone. A compass works but local mineralization can skew it. Use an INS that tracks your movement from your beacon. Check it obsessively. Do not trust your instincts.
* **A stationary beacon:** Drop a weighted strobe/sonar pinger at your arrival point. Set it to low ping every 10 seconds. That is home. If you lose sight of it, follow the tether back.

Do not bring sonar mapping drones that scream. Sound carries brutally far in cold still water. A loud ping will just give you a panic-inducing echo of flatness for kilometers. If you use sonar, keep it passive or on the lowest chirp.

### 4. Solve Movement

You do not walk normally here. You do not swim normally here.

You are heavy under pressure, neutrally buoyant at best, and every surface is covered in a foot of algal fur, oily curtains, and flour-fine silt that has been settling for ten thousand years.

**If you kick hard, you will blind yourself for an hour.** The silt you kick up hangs. It does not settle quickly. One bad fin stroke and your visibility goes from 15 meters to 15 centimeters.

Protocols:

* **Learn the frog-kick and the pull-glide.** Fins pointed up, small motions, hands pulling you along just above the plants. Never walk *through* the dense hedges of wrist-thick kelp — go over them. They are anchored deep in mortar lines and will not move; you will get tangled.
* **Weight yourself to be *slightly* negative.** You want to be able to stand on the brick, not float away into the dusk. But not so heavy you crush the plants. Think moon-walk. One step, settle, look, next step.
* **Carry a short, blunt probe pole.** 1.5 meters, like a ski pole. Use it to feel ahead, part curtains, test if that "plaza" is actually a cistern full of gas-bladder kelp that will entangle you. Do not use a knife much — cutting these plants releases spores and gas that fouls your water and your suit.
* **Move slowly or you will die of heat loss.** Exercise feels warming for 5 minutes, then you dump heat into the water faster than you can make it. Slow is smooth, smooth is warm, warm is alive.
* **Bring line cutters, not a big knife.** If you get wrapped in a filamentous curtain, you need to snip, not slash. Two small shears, one on each wrist, accessible with cold fingers.

Expect travel times to be absurd. Covering one kilometer could take an hour. Plan for it.

### 5. Solve Air, Time, And Silence

If you're in an ADS/submersible, air is your least worry until it's your only worry. Carry scrubbers for 2x your planned time.

If you're on a rebreather, bring a bailout and never, ever hold your breath. Pressure barotrauma here is not forgiving.

More important is **time discipline:**

* Nothing sudden happens here. Your brain, evolved for savanna predators, will freak out at the absence of events and start inventing them. You will hear a distant *pat* and think something is coming. Nothing is coming. There are no animals. Nothing wants to eat you.
* The sound is your own breath, the groan of stone with temperature, the slough of algae. After an hour, that silence becomes oppressive. After three hours, it becomes hallucinatory. Bring a quiet audiobook or music you can play inside your helmet, just loud enough to remind your brain that time is passing. Otherwise you will lose track.
* Set hard alarms: 30 min - check heat, check tether, check gas. 60 min - check navigation, drink water (warm, from internal bladder), eat. 90 min - mandatory turn-around consideration.

### 6. Solve The Psychological Load

This is the part experienced travelers get wrong because they think they're tough.

This is not a scary ocean with leviathans. It is far worse: a **grief ocean.**

* It is vast beyond comprehension, but you can only see 15 meters of it at a time. Your brain can't model it, so it tries and fails constantly. That failure feels like awe, then vertigo, then depression.
* It is an endless city with no people. Every brick was placed by hand, precisely, with love, for forever — and forever came, and it is cold, dark, and covered in weeds. You will feel loneliness for people you never knew. You will feel the weight of what "ruin" really means when ruin has finished ruining and become stable.
* There is nothing to interact with. No fish. No eyeshine. The lushness is vegetable, slow, indifferent. It does not react to you. You can shout and only silt will answer. For most humans, total absence of animal life triggers deep unease — we are wired to know that if no *thing* lives there, maybe we shouldn't either.

Preparation:

* **Do not go alone if you can help it.** Even with comms, having one other light in the dusk anchors your sanity. If you must go alone, keep an open voice log. Talk. Describe the bricks.
* **Bring a totem.** A warm-colored object that is not stone, not green-black. A red mug. A child's toy. Something that reminds you that color and warmth exist elsewhere.
* **Accept before you go that you will not understand the Floor.** You will not map it. You will not find its center or its edge. You will not decipher the ghost-letters. You are a warm visitor seeing one square meter of infinity. If you arrive needing answers, the place will break you. Arrive needing only to witness.
* **Plan for post-travel decompression that isn't about nitrogen.** When you get back to dry, warm, noisy air, you will feel the cold in your bones for days. You will miss the quiet. You will dream of flat stone under flour-silt. This is normal. Tell someone where you went. Make them listen.

### Quick Checklist for the Ordinary Human

**Before Jump:**
[ ] Pressure system tested 20+ hrs in cold water
[ ] Active heating + 3x batteries
[ ] Dry food, warm water
[ ] Low flood lights (x2) + red mode
[ ] Tether reel + weighted pinger beacon
[ ] INS / nav computer
[ ] Heated hood/gloves/socks, no skin
[ ] Probe pole, twin line-cutters
[ ] Time limit written on arm
[ ] Buddy or voice logger

**On the Floor:**
1. Drop beacon, spool tether, ping check.
2. Kneel. Put your palm on a brick. Feel the algae fur and the stubborn right angle underneath. Understand where you are.
3. Move slow. Kick never. Test with pole.
4. Light low. Look close, not far. The beauty is not in vistas — there are no vistas — but in how a holdfast splits a brick over a century.
5. Every 30 minutes: Heat? Air? Tether? Still want to be here?
6. Leave before you want to. This place rewards the one who leaves early.

**Do NOT:**
* Don't bring a bright spotlight and blast the forest.
* Don't try to collect souvenirs by ripping kelp holdfasts out. You will tear the mortar and accomplish nothing. If you must take something, take loose silt, a spore sample in a sealed vial, a photograph. Leave the city alone.
* Don't stir silt. Don't touch things with bare hands.
* Don't chase a "weather" fog to see what causes it. You will get lost in it.
* Don't stay still too long. Stillness is heat loss.

If you do it right, you will return cold to your core, humbled, and utterly quiet inside. You'll have spent hours moving through a drowned eternity that was built by patient, primitive hands and then patiently reclaimed by patient, mindless green. It won't have noticed you. But you will have noticed it — and part of you will always be there, standing on that perfectly flat, perfectly endless Floor, under a light that is only a memory of light, listening to the forest breathe once.

Go warm, go tethered, go slow, and come back.
