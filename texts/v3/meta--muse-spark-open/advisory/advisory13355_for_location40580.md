You are not going underwater. You are going *into* water itself. Don't prepare like a diver going to look at a reef. Prepare like a mountaineer going to live in a high alpine village where the air happens to be liquid. This place is high, shallow, sunlit, and inhabited — and your human body is not built for it.

Here is how you prepare, as an ordinary, air-breathing, surface-evolved human:

### 1. Understand Where You're Going

Forget open ocean. This is a **drowned mesa in midair.**

40-50 meters down sounds like "not deep" until you live there. It is 5-6 atmospheres of pressure. The light is late-morning through clean water — not dark, not shafts of god-rays, but a slow, breathing dapple, like sunlight through leaves a long way up. The chop on the surface 140 feet above you, the swing of the kelp canopy itself, and all the suspended snow in the water make the light *pulse*. Very slow. Inhale, exhale.

The temperature is wrong for what you expect. It's not tropical warm, it's not abyssal freezing. It's that steady, endless cool that makes you feel more awake. Around 16-19°C / 60-66°F. You can live in it, but you will go hypothermic in hours without a skin if you just float there. The locals don't notice anymore.

The stone plateau is huge, ancient basalt, wind-smoothed before the world drowned and then frozen in time. It's not eroding. It's not growing. It's *held*. Everything built on it has had centuries to find its final, smooth, hand-oiled shape and stop. Nothing is new. Nothing is rushed. The current itself is not a current so much as a planetary breath — back… and forth… back… and forth. You will feel it move your hair even when you are holding onto a rope.

And on that stone sits the Forest-That-Is-The-World.

At 2.9/3 on the vegetation scale, plants stop being objects *in* a place and *become* the place. This one species — Mother Kelp — is the ground, the walls, the streets, the houses, the ceiling. House-thick trunks rise from the rock. Leaves fifty meters long braid overhead like a cathedral vault. Living rooms are literally living room-sized bladders of oxygen and sweet water, grown, not built. Terraces are not built onto the plant, they are cut *into* it, and the plant has healed around the wounds for generations.

You will be inside a living building the size of a town.

### 2. Your Body: Preparation Before You Step Through

If you arrive unprepared, you will die in the first three minutes — not from a monster, but from drowning, panic, or pressure.

**A. You must be able to be there and breathe.**
You will materialize *in* water, 40+ meters down. You cannot hold your breath there.

*   **Do not rely on a single scuba tank.** At 50m your air consumption is 5-6x surface rate. A normal AL80 will last you 15-20 minutes if you panic, 30 if you don't. You need a closed-circuit rebreather if you have training, or at minimum twin 100s with nitrox 28-30 and a pony bottle, and a plan to *stop using them*.
*   **The bladders are breathable but they are not hotels.** They filter oxygen the way leaves do — slowly. The air inside is humid, warm, smells like cut grass and iodine, and is perfectly breathable for hours to days *if you treat the bladder well*. You enter from *below* through a muscular sphincter. You float up into the air pocket inside. CO2 pools. You must not close the mouth all the way or you will suffocate yourself and wound the plant. You must learn to fan the air, to spit excess water out the lip, to let it breathe.
*   **Rehearse equalization obsessively.** Descending 50m without clearing is how you rupture an eardrum. The children there learn to read pressure in their ears like farmers read wind. You need to be able to equalize hands-free, gently, every meter, without thinking. Practice Frenzel. Bring decongestants, but do not depend on them. If you can't equalize, you don't go.

**B. Pressure will make you drunk and then kill you if you rush.**

At 40-50m on air, you will be nitrogen-narcosic. Stupid, happy, slow. On top of that, sound is 4x faster and everything is visually overwhelming. You will get enchanted and forget to check your SPG.

*   **Plan your narcosis:** Pre-decide hand signals, turn pressures, and exit routes. Write them on your slate. Do not make decisions at depth.
*   **You cannot bolt for the surface.** From 45m, a direct ascent is a decompression illness emergency. You will need staged decompression stops or a very, very slow creep up through the canopy as your computer dictates. The locals can go up and down because their bodies have lived there for generations in this balanced, shallow-high pressure and they never go to true surface fast. You are a visitor. Respect deco. Bring a computer and follow it like scripture.

**C. Thermal and Buoyancy.**

*   **Wear a 5mm wetsuit with hood, or a 7mm if you run cold.** Even though the water feels "cool, not cold," you will be in it for hours. You will shiver quietly and not notice your dexterity leaving.
*   **Weight for neutral buoyancy at 15m, not at the surface.** You need to be able to hover motionless in the middle of a trunk forest without kicking and tearing leaves. Bring 2-4 lbs more than tropical weighting, and be prepared to drop a pocket if you need to enter a bladder positively buoyant.
*   **Fins: big, soft, forgiving.** Not tech blades. You will be walking on ropes, pushing off trunks, not sprinting. Jetfins or freediving fins will slice nets and spook the grazers.

### 3. Gear: Less Metal, More Cloth

This tech level stopped at stone, shell, bone, and rope. Primitive doesn't mean stupid — it means every object has had a hundred years to be perfected. Your aluminum and plastic will be loud, corrosive, and rude.

**Bring:**

1.  **A good mask with low volume, and a backup mask.** Visibility is good, but you're going to be looking *up* through leaves for a long time. Neck strain is real.
2.  **A dive slate + pencil + permanent marker.** You will want to draw pictures for children.
3.  **A blunt-tip dive knife, worn inside your leg, not outside.** Not for defense. For entanglement. The nets between leaves are house-sized and slack. Monofilament ghosts would be worse. Cut yourself free, then tell the net-minder and help repair it.
4.  **Two cutting tools that don't look like weapons:** trauma shears and a small shell knife if you can find one. You'll be gifted one anyway.
5.  **Rope gloves.** Lightweight, grippy gardening gloves. Every surface is rope, kelp fiber thicker than your wrist, or living membrane. Bare hands will blister in an hour of moving along walkways.
6.  **A dim, warm, handheld light, and a chemical glow stick.** Not a 2000-lumen cannon. At midday you won't need light. At depth among the trunks it goes to green gloom. A harsh beam blinds the cuttle-birds, angers the kelp-oxen, and marks you as a tourist who cannot see. Use amber if possible.
7.  **An acoustic signaling device that is NOT a tank banger.** A small wooden clapper, a hollowed gourd, or even two smooth stones you can click. You will need to sing/tap constantly while moving (see Etiquette). A tank banger is too sharp and carries too far in this water.

**Do NOT bring:**
Harpoons, spearguns, loud sonar, DPV scooters, bright strobes, single-use plastic that can shred into the forest, or anything you wouldn't want to lose into an abyss that has no bottom.

### 4. How To Move

You do not swim *over* this town. You swim *through* its hallways.

*   The hamlet is sparse. Thirty households scattered through a plant-city you can swim across in ten minutes. That emptiness is intentional. Each bladder needs water between it to breathe. Don't cluster. Don't crowd a family's air pocket unless invited. Hover at the rope-rail outside and wait.
*   **Use the infrastructure.** Every rope walkway, ladder of knotted stipe, and bone walkway is load-bearing and ancient and *maintained*. Pull yourself hand-over-hand. It is faster and quieter than finning, and it tells everyone you know how to be there. Finning stirs snow and scares the plate-sized silver grazers — and if you scare the grazers, the forest overgrows, and you have just made work for everyone.
*   **Learn the tap-song before you arrive.** The rule is: *never move in silence.* The big, slow kelp-oxen deep in the trunks are blind and huge and chew old growth all day, exhaling bubble-pearls that people harvest for lime. They determine predator from person by vibration. Hum. Low. Through your regulator if you must, but better to take it out and vocalize if you can — *mmmmmmmmm* — or gently tap your clapper every few kicks. Locals sing while they swim. If you go silent, something big will come to look, and it will be your fault.
*   **Do not touch the bladders without asking.** The shrimp the length of your forearm that tend the membranes will let you know — they will posture. The wall is alive. Stroke it gently or not at all.

### 5. Etiquette: How To Be a Good Guest in a Place That Is Alive

This town is complete. It doesn't need you, which is why it might let you stay.

1.  **Never cut a main stipe.** You can harvest shoots from the farming troughs if offered. You can eat the edible blisters that grow on terrace lips. Cutting a structural column is like cutting a support pillar in someone's house while they're in it. The plant will bleed slow amber sap for days and everyone will know you did it.

2.  **Never close a bladder-mouth all the way.** You will want to, for warmth or privacy. Don't. Leave at least a hand-span open to the water. The plant must breathe, exchange gases, equalize pressure with the sighs and pops you hear. Closing it is smothering your host.

3.  **Do not swim over the edge. Especially at night.** In the day the edge is just dizzying — a wall of green falling away into perfect blue that gets darker until it isn't blue at all but black. At night, things from the true floor — two thousand, three thousand meters down — come *up* to look at the garden. They are attracted to light and to the sound of isolated kicking. Children are taught before they can speak: *The plateau ends, you stop.* Stay 20 meters back from the drop after sunset.

4.  **Bring gifts that are useful, not shiny.** The best gift you can bring an air-habitat is a new thing to breathe with it — woven kelp baskets for the cuttle-birds. They will love you forever if you help weave. Second best: shell polish, bone needles, good cordage, or stories drawn on waterproof paper. Worst: candy, batteries, or anything that implies they should be more like you.

5.  **Respect the loudness.** Newcomers expect silence in a slow place. This place is *deafening* in a low, living way. Trunks groan like old ships. Bladders sigh. Leaves creak acre by acre. Shrimp click, grazers scrape, beaks grind, hunters crack. Your brain will try to filter it as background. Don't. The people there *read* that soundscape. When the leaves go quiet, something is above them. When a blender-like grinding rises from below, a kelp-ox is close. Sit still at midday — when everyone pauses — and just listen until you can pick apart five layers.

### 6. How It Will Feel To Be There

Disorienting at first, then deeply right.

You will arrive and your ears will hurt and your mask will fog and you will be too buoyant or too heavy and everything will be green-black-amber and moving. Then someone — a woman mending net with bare feet looped in rope, a kid riding a kelp-ox's back — will see you flailing, swim over with absurd, effortless grace, tap twice on their chest, and offer you a hand. Take it. Let them tow you to the nearest bladder.

Inside, you will bob up into air that is somehow already 28°C and 100% humid, drip onto a membrane floor that gives under your knees like a drum, and hear human voices talking normally for the first time in your visit. Because water carries voices so well, everyone outside has been hearing you since you arrived — that distant, garbled "a dozen human voices answering a bladder's sigh." Now *you* are one of them.

Stay for that midday stillness at least once. The current slacks. The light goes straight down. The grazers hold position like silver leaves. The whole cathedral hangs without wind. You can see, in one long glance, the whole impossibility: ropes and nets and air-homes and laughing people *hanging in the sky* above an abyss, held by a plant that decided to be a world.

You will understand why they don't remember if the ocean rose or the world sank. It doesn't matter. The high place is still high.

### 7. Leaving In One Piece

Don't overstay your first trip. 60-90 minutes bottom time at that depth is plenty for an ordinary human, even with decompression.

*   Ascend through the canopy slowly, hand over hand, breathing your deco stops. Do not shoot up toward "boat-light" just because you see the surface shimmer. That shiver 50 meters above is still 5 atmospheres away.
*   Do a safety stop at 5m hanging *on* a leaf — it will be swaying gently. Watch the particulate snow rise past you like inverted snow.
*   Hydrate on the surface like you've hiked a desert — you have, you just couldn't sweat.
*   Wait 18-24 hours before any other depth or before jumping to another world that has pressure.

And when you're back on dry land that stays dry, you will notice two things: your ears will keep popping for an hour, and silence will feel *too* silent.

If you ever go back, learn the songs properly.
