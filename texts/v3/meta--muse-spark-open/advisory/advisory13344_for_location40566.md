You're going to a nursery. Not a mountain. A mountain nursery.

That changes everything about how you prepare. You are not preparing to *survive* a hostile peak. You are preparing to walk into a place that is *busy making itself* while you're standing on it, and does not know you are there, and will not pause for you.

An ordinary human can visit here and walk out fine. But you have to go in on its terms. This is not a hike. It's a visit to a construction site where the foreman left a hundred thousand years ago and left the machines on.

Here's what your intuition should be telling you:

### 1. Prepare to be Confused by Your Body

You're going *up*. High enough that the clouds are below you like a floor you could walk on. Your body knows what that means: it should be cold, it should be bright, it should be hard to breathe.

It will be none of those things, and that will mess with you.

*   **The Air is Warm and Heavy:** Think tropical greenhouse, not alpine summit. 25-28°C / 77-82°F, near 100% humidity, bathwater air. You will sweat profusely but it won't evaporate. Heat exhaustion is your #1 risk, not hypothermia. Your brain will keep telling you "you're high up, you should be cold" and you'll overdress and overexert and get sick.
    *   *Pack:* No down, no fleece, no hard alpine shell. Pack as if you're going to the Amazon at 4,000 meters. Ultra-breathable wicking base layers, ventilated hiking pants, a wide-brimmed hat that breathes. Bring electrolytes - a lot of them. Salts, magnesium, potassium. You will lose water even though water is everywhere.
    *   *Prepare:* Acclimatize as if for altitude anyway. Your ears will pop. You may get a headache, nausea, shortness of breath on exertion. The warmth masks hypoxia. Force yourself to breathe slowly, deeply. Don't sprint the rim. Sit for 20 minutes when you arrive and just breathe. Take acetazolamide if you use it for altitude normally. The fact that it's warm does NOT mean there's more oxygen.

*   **There is No Shadow, No Sun, No Time:** The light comes from everywhere - that low, glowing, amber-gray haze 20 meters up like a ceiling. It's bright enough to see perfectly, dim enough to feel like perpetual dusk. No shadows. No sun position. Your circadian system will short-circuit. Photos will look flat and wrong.
    *   *Pack:* A headlamp anyway, but on low red mode. Not to see, but to create a shadow you can control. It helps with depth perception on the black glass. Polarized sunglasses to cut the glare off wet basalt, even though it seems dim. A watch with an altimeter and compass - your phone GPS may drift near the anchors. Bring an analog timepiece. You'll need to *decide* when to leave, because the sky will never tell you.
    *   *Prepare:* You will get eye fatigue and spatial vertigo. Practice walking and judging distance without shadows before you go. Close one eye test - everything will look closer than it is on featureless wet rock.

### 2. Prepare for Ground That Is Not Ground Yet

This is the most important safety briefing.

Normal mountains are old. They have soil, scree, broken rock, predictable friction. This place has none of that. It's embryonic basalt, black glass still cooling from *being assembled*, not erupted. It's beautiful and treacherous in ways a normal trail never is.

*   **It is Slick and Sharp and Hot at Once:** Basalt glass + sheet-flow water = ice-rink friction. Those water braids are millimeters deep, fast, and cover everything. One moment the surface is cool to the touch, the next your palm hisses where interior heat bleeds through a new fold. There are ridges that are razor-sharp because no wind has ever dulled them.
    *   *Pack:* NOT hiking boots with hard vibram soles. They will hydroplane on wet glass. You want soft, sticky-approach shoes or canyoning shoes with felt/siped rubber - think fishing wading boots or climbing approach shoes that grip wet rock. Gaiters to keep gravel/glass shards out. Thick-soled, but flexible. Bring heavy-duty gloves - the kind you'd use for handling slate - for when you inevitably catch yourself on a ridge. Knee pads aren't overkill. Trekking poles with rubber tips, not carbide, so you don't chip the new stone. You don't want to be the first erosion.
    *   *Move:* Shuffle. Short steps. Keep your center of gravity low. Test every fold before weighting it. Do not jump between terraces - that basalt is growing a millimeter an hour. The terrace you land on may be a fresh fold, hollow underneath and hotter. Listen: rock sighing and shifting is normal here. If it sighs *under you*, freeze.

*   **Water Has Its Own Rules:** Rivers don't exist yet, so water hasn't learned to flow downhill. Near the white-ribbed buildings/anchors, it flows *uphill* in perfect arcs. You can't trust gradient. A shallow braid can suddenly deepen into a trench being actively pulled into existence by a field. You will get soaked to the knees no matter what.
    *   *Pack:* Waterproof your feet by giving up: quick-dry everything. No waterproof membranes - they'll just trap sweat. Neoprene socks are perfect here. Dry bags inside your pack for electronics, food, first aid. Bring your own drinking water - 2-3 liters - even though water is everywhere. The water here tastes of iron and ozone for a reason. It's mineral-heavy condensate being wrung out of a construction field, full of basalt fines and probably not safe without serious filtering. If you must drink it: filter *and* purify. Sawyer Squeeze + Aquatabs. Don't drink the glowing water near the structures. That's not water.
    *   *Prepare:* Learn to read field-affected water. If water is flowing in a too-perfect curve, or suddenly clear and uphill, or beads and hovers off a surface, you are in an active anchor field. Back away slowly. Do not try to dam, divert, or cup water near those frames. You are interfering with an instruction.

### 3. Prepare for Life That Is Too Much and Too Fast

This place should be barren. Bare rock, a few mosses, nothing else. Instead it's deafening. The warmth + constant water + no winter has created a hyper-productive, no-equilibrium bloom. Everything here breeds, eats, dies, and is eaten at 3x speed.

This is not a serene alpine meadow. It's a tidal pool at low tide in a greenhouse.

*   **Everything is Predatory, Nothing Cares About You (Mostly):** The dragonfly-things with forearm-long smoked-glass wings, the mantis predators that snap like twigs, the clouds of moths that live an hour, the tadpole-things with too many eyes - they are not interested in humans. You are too big, too salty, too warm in the wrong way. But you are *in the way*. You will be landed on, investigated, drunk from. Those dragonflies will drink condensation off your sleeve. Let them.
    *   *Pack:* Head net and lightweight long sleeves. Not for mosquitoes - for moth clouds and amber beetle rivers that will march straight over your boots. You do not want a surprised mantis-predator on your forearm because you swatted. Skin coverage is kindness here. Permethrin-treat your clothes before you go. Bring antihistamines and hydrocortisone - bites/stings from unknown pioneer arthropods will happen. A small squeeze bottle of white vinegar for stings.
    *   *Do NOT Pack:* Insect repellent with DEET in heavy concentration. It will sheet off in the humidity and contaminate the newborn water braids, and might kill the glasswort. Use picaridin sparingly if you must. Better to be covered.
    *   *Move:* Slowly through water. 90% of the life is in the water braids. Every step you take crushes dozens of those tadpole-things and beetles. That's unavoidable, but don't stomp and splash for fun. Watch where you put hands. The moss cushions that sigh - they're alive and farming colonies underneath. Don't sit on them. Sit on bare black rock that's already wet.

*   **The Big Something Overhead:** You'll hear it before you feel it. A low thrumming sigh as it displaces the haze, a sudden cold rain for ten seconds that hammers on your hood, a wet slap against the lone doorway. You will not see it clearly. Don't try to.
    *   *Prepare:* This is not your predator, but it's big enough to knock you over with pressure change. When you hear the thrumming build, crouch, lower your profile, secure your hat and pack. Don't run for the doorway for shelter - the animals *avoid* that doorway for a reason. And don't shine a light up into the haze to see it. You don't want to be noticed as interesting. Let it pass. It lives in the construction field. You don't.

### 4. Prepare for the Buildings

There are five or six. Hamlet-scale. White ribs humming. Disc half-sunk. Doorway to nowhere. They are not ruins. They are not buildings. They are *anchors* - they tell matter how to behave.

Treat them like you would treat a live industrial transformer in a shallow pool.

*   **Rules:**
    1.  Do not touch the matte white surfaces. Water hovers a millimeter off them. So will the oils on your skin, but the field won't like your capacitance. Observers report nausea, metallic taste, and vertigo within 2-3 meters. That's your cue to step back. The hum will get louder in your molars, not your ears.
    2.  Do not enter or circle the doorway. The air on the other side is 10°C colder and smells of deep ocean. It's not an illusion. It's a differential. Things that go through don't come back the same, if they come back. All the local fauna gives it a wide berth. Copy them.
    3.  Do not leave anything on or inside them, and do not take etchings/rubble. The basalt is growing *around* one of them - the disc. It's being incorporated. The etchings that stop mid-line are not decoration; they are unfinished instructions. Your boot print filling with water is a better offering than any souvenir.
    4.  Do not sleep within 15 meters of any structure. Fields pulse at night, or what passes for night when the haze dims a fraction. Camp/shelter on the rim, not in the bowl's center near the blister.

*   *Pack:* An EMF meter or even a cheap AM radio if you're a multiverse traveler who likes diagnostics. They will crackle and whine near the anchors - useful confirmation you're too close. A dosimeter if you habitually carry one; unlikely to be ionizing, but unknown fields are unknown. Bring earplugs - not for silence, but to damp the hum if you need to rest nearby.

### 5. What Your Visit Will Actually Feel Like (So You Don't Panic)

*   **Loud, Wet, Warm, and Lonely.** The sound is constant: drip, whir, click, snap, splash. No birdsong to organize it. It's arrhythmic. You'll feel overstimulated within an hour. That's normal. Bring foam earplugs for breaks.
*   **You Will Feel Like an Intruder and a Giant.** Everything here is knee-high or smaller except the unseen thing overhead. The trees are stunted to your knee. You could count them all. You'll feel clumsy and huge and responsible.
*   **Time Will Drag and Race.** The stone grows while you watch - a millimeter an hour - and the insects live and die while you have lunch. You'll see a rain that lasts ten seconds and then vanishes. You'll feel like you've been there for days when it's been 90 minutes because nothing casts a shadow to mark time.
*   **You Will Want to Fix It or Finish It.** You'll see the half-etched geometric table and want to complete the line. You'll see water flowing uphill and want to correct it. Don't. This mountain is trying to become. Your job is to witness the first trickle taking its first path, not to give it a better one.

### Minimal Pack List for an Ordinary Human (2-4 hour visit)

**Wear:** Quick-dry long pants + long-sleeve shirt, billed cap + head net, sticky canyoning shoes + neoprene socks + gaiters, light gloves on cord.

**Pack:**
- 3L water + electrolyte powder/salt tabs
- Filter + purification tabs
- Headlamp (red mode) + polarized sunglasses
- Trekking poles (rubber tips)
- Dry bags, small towel, spare socks in dry bag
- First aid: blister care, antihistamine, hydrocortisone, vinegar, altitude meds if needed, electrolyte sachets
- Foam earplugs + watch
- Light tarp/bivy (no tent stakes - nowhere to stake, use rock weights) if you plan to stay >3 hours. You won't need insulation, you need rain/condensation protection.
- Notebook + pencil (pens clog in humidity), camera in waterproof case
- Emergency beacon/PLB - haze may block satellite, but rim may be clearer

**Leave at Home:** Heavy boots, down jacket, DEET bombs, hammers, chisels, drones (the field will eat them), expectations of a summit photo.

### How to Leave

Walk backward for the last ten steps. Not for ritual. So you can see the bowl once more without turning your back on that doorway. Check your footprints - they're already full of water, already erasing.

You came high - higher than clouds - and found warmth where there should be frost. You stood before erosion begins. You heard a place breathe, click, and grow.

Don't try to hold it in your head perfectly - that's the mountain's job, and it's still learning. Let it be small, unfinished, loud, and alive without you.

And when your ears pop on the way down, remember: you were never meant to be warm up there. That warmth was for the glasswort and the dragonflies and the stone that hasn't decided what it is yet.

You just got to borrow it for an hour.
