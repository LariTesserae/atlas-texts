You do not visit the High Water the way you visit an ocean. You visit it the way you visit a desert, a clean room, and a high-altitude glacier that someone forgot to put a sky over.

Everything about it is telling your senses the wrong thing, and your body will try to obey the wrong signals until you get hurt. Prepare for that first.

### 1. What Your Body Will Think Is Happening (And Why It's Wrong)

**You will think you are in shallow, warm air.**
The water is optically pure. No particles, no plankton, no marine snow. Visibility is 30-50 kilometers horizontally. Sunlight arrives in hard, undistorted columns. Your primate brain evolved to interpret that kind of clarity as *air*. You will have no depth perception. You will think a volcanic cone 12 kilometers away is a rock you can touch, and a manta 2 kilometers long is a small ray 20 meters away until it keeps getting bigger.

This is the primary killer. Vertigo, dissociation, motion sickness, and lethal misjudgments of distance. Pilots over salt flats get it. Divers here get it a hundred times worse because you can *breathe* and still not feel submerged.

**You will think you are safe from pressure because you can see the surface.**
You are not. The Table is kilometers deep, and the Mills rise 2km and *still don't break the surface*. You are under serious pressure the entire time. The water is brutally clear but it is still water - cold, heavy, and patient.

**You will think the silence is safe.**
It isn't. There is no silence here. There are two sounds that never stop:
1. The Edge: a planet-sized waterfall pouring off the Table in every direction. Even in the center, 1,000km from the lip, it's a low, thrumming thunder you feel in your molars and sternum. It's bone conduction, not eardrum sound. It will give you headaches, nausea, disrupted sleep, and a false sense of urgency for the first 48 hours until your brain edits it out.
2. The Mills: each one has its own whine/clang/hiss. They sound *intentional* and therefore comforting compared to the Edge roar. Don't let that comfort trick you into staying near one.

Long exposure without dampening causes organ resonance, tinnitus, and fatigue that feels like being mildly concussed. You will not notice it happening.

### 2. You Cannot Swim Here. Understand That.

An ordinary human free-swimming in a 3.8°C freshwater ocean with continental fetch and Edge-driven currents will be dead in under an hour, even with a good wetsuit.

The High Water is not swimmable in any recreational sense:

*   **Temperature:** It's held at exactly 3.8-4.0°C everywhere, forever. That's just above maximum density for freshwater. It's not "cold," it's *heat-stealing*. Without active heating, you lose dexterity in 3 minutes, consciousness in 15-30, even in a thick drysuit, because the water never stratifies and never warms. There is no warm layer to duck into.
*   **Current:** On the scale of the Table, water always falls *outward*. In the center it's a gentle drift, 0.1-0.3 knots, easy to miss until you realize your navigation says you've moved 10km sideways while staring at what you thought was the same rock. Within ~50-100km of the Edge, it becomes a true pull. Swimming against it is impossible. Boats with engines struggle. Everything not anchored goes over eventually. The animals let it take them. You cannot.
*   **Distance:** Density 0.1. This means you can swim for *days* and see nothing change. The relief below is continent-scale basalt desert. No sand beaches, no reference points, no shelter. If your propulsion dies in open water between Mills, you will die of exposure and existential dread long before anyone finds you. Which brings us to:

**There is no rescue here.** There are no people. The Mills are maintained by stupid crab-drones that will ignore your distress call and may try to scrape barnacles off your hull while you sink. The animals are abundant and indifferent. You are on your own.

### 3. Preparation Checklist for a Baseline Human

Do not go as a skin diver. Go as a submersible pilot or not at all.

#### A. The Shell

You need a **pressurized, heated envelope.** Three tiers, pick one:

1.  **Best: Small 1-2 person submersible.** Hard hull, viewport with distance-ranging overlay, active sonar, autopilot, life support for 2 weeks. Think deep-sea research sub, not a tourist bubble. Heated interior is mandatory. This protects you from cold, pressure, sound, and sharp rock. You will still *see* everything through viewport but you won't *feel* the water trying to kill you.
2.  **Acceptable: Atmospheric diving suit / hard suit.** Heated, with thrusters. Like a space suit for water. You get to be "outside" without dying. Much more tiring psychologically.
3.  **Bare minimum for experts only: Heated drysuit with full-face rebreather, augmented with a diver propulsion vehicle (DPV), bone-conduction dampeners, and a tether to a surface habitat.** You will be cold, loud, and scared the entire time. You must be a technical cold-water diver with cave/wreck overhead training. Even then, limit exposure to 2-3 hours.

Whatever you wear, it must be:
*   **Cut-proof.** The seafloor is not seafloor. It's fresh pillow basalt, volcanic glass, blade-sharp graben faults and caldera rims with zero erosion. No silt to dull it. If you touch down, you will slice gloves, suits, and hull coatings. Reinforced knees, gloves, and belly pan are non-negotiable.
*   **Actively heated.** Not insulated. Heated. Chemical warmers are useless at this scale. Electric heating loops fed by a big battery is standard. Bring two batteries.
*   **Pressure-rated to at least 100 atmospheres 1000m, ideally more.** You won't be at the bottom everywhere, but you will be tempted to go look at that beautiful sharp cone, and that cone is 4km down.

#### B. Sound Protection

This is not optional. You need:

*   **Bone dampeners:** The crews that work the Edge-rim Mills wear them. They are collar/neck units that disrupt low-frequency conduction through your skeleton. Without them, the Edge roar literally vibrates your lungs and gut. Ask any supplier for "industrial infrasound attenuation for hydro work." Wear it turned on before you arrive.
*   **Active noise-canceling ear protection** inside your helmet/sub even when it seems quiet. The roar will keep you from sleeping and from hearing your own equipment alarms. You need to create artificial silence.

Bring earplugs for when the electronics fail. The animals' clicks are the only thing that can cut through the roar - they are shockingly loud up close and will startle you into dropping things.

#### C. Navigation - Because Your Eyes Are Liars

Leave your human depth perception at home.

*   **LIDAR / Sonar with synthetic horizon.** You need instrument distance at all times. Your eyes will tell you a Mill tower is close. Sonar will tell you it's 14km away. Trust sonar.
*   **Inertial navigation + dead reckoning.** No GPS satellites here unless you brought them. No magnetic anomalies to map. The Table is featureless at continental scale. If you lose track of which direction is outward, you will drift to the Edge without ever seeing it coming. The current is too slow to notice without instruments.
*   **Mark your path.** Drop disposable pingers. The water clarity makes you think you can just turn around and go back. You can't. Everything looks the same. Even your own bubbles - if you have them - vanish instantly in the huge water.

**Rule: Never approach the Edge closer than 50km in anything without at least 10 knots of spare thrust and a fully charged battery.** That pull is non-linear. At 30km it's noticeable. At 10km it's a river. At 2km it's a wall falling away into cloud. Nothing that goes over comes back. Satellites show planet-sized cyclones underneath. You don't want to meet them.

Use the Mills as lighthouses. They are spaced 100-300km apart, all identical in function but unique in form, and they are the *only* reliable landmarks. Map them. Name them. Always know vector to nearest two.

#### D. Mind Preparation - The Hardest Part

This place is psychologically hostile to humans in a very specific way:

1.  **Agoraphobia + Claustrophobia at once:** You are suspended in illuminated nothingness between an infinitely detailed black glass floor and an infinitely distant blue surface. You are both exposed and trapped. People report panic attacks not from fear, but from *scale rejection* - the brain cannot hold "single ocean on a hemisphere-sized mountain that pours forever" as a real place, so it decides *you* are not real.
2.  **Time Dilation of Emptiness:** Dynamic 0.4 means nothing changes. No weather, no seasons, no decay, no sediment. A basalt shard looks like it broke yesterday. A dead Benthos stands upright for decades. Your brain uses change to mark time. Without it, hours feel like minutes and days feel like hours. You will check your clock and be shocked. Bring strict routines: eat, sleep, log at fixed intervals, or you'll drift.
3.  **Machine Loneliness:** The Mills are profoundly uncanny. They are clearly made for humans, clearly human-scale industrial, and clearly empty. Doors open for no one. Welders weld nothing. They hum in unison. Humans are pattern-makers, and your mind will *invent* operators, ghosts, gods to explain them. Don't. They are thermostats. That's all. Treat them as geography, not as invitations. Do not moor to one for more than a few hours - frost forms on the rock near you because your habitat's shade/presence disrupts their careful thermal halo. Do not try to turn one off to see what happens. The system compensates, but the drones may see *you* as debris to be cleaned.
4.  **Sound-Induced Euphoria/Dread:** After about 72 hours, the unending thunder stops sounding like noise and starts sounding like silence - a presence so constant your auditory cortex subtracts it. This is when travelers do stupid things: taking off helmets to "hear the real quiet," swimming toward the Edge because "it's so peaceful." It's not. It's just your brain fatiguing. Keep your dampeners on.

**Exercises before you go:**
*   Spend 8 hours in a sensory isolation tank, then 8 hours in a whiteout snowfield VR with no markers. If either makes you weep or rage, double your training.
*   Practice instrument-only navigation until you're bored.
*   Record your voice. Bring music, audiobooks. You will need human-scale sound that is not thunder or whine.

#### E. Biology - What to Do About the Animals

They are not monsters. They are livestock grazing a pasture made of stone and waste heat.

*   Mantas: 30-100m wingspan, slow, filter chemosynthetic bacteria. Harmless if you don't startle them. Keep 200m distance. Their wake can tumble a diver.
*   Knife-fish: Eyeless, echolocating packs. Curious. They will ping you incessantly - your sonar will light up. Annoying, not dangerous, but they bite if you smell like food. Don't.
*   Benthos: Oil-rig-sized, jointed grazers. Slow, crackling, utterly indifferent. Do not shelter under them. Their legs chip basalt and they step without looking. A dead one standing is a useful landmark - mark it.

None rot in the human sense. Bacteria here eat rock and Mills' chemicals, not meat. If you get cut and bleed, your blood will drift as perfect red ribbons for an improbable distance, not cloud and disperse. If you die, your body will drift, uncorrupted, for a very long time. Think about that and bring a trauma kit, antibiotics, and clotting agents. Wounds don't get infected the usual way, but they don't heal warmly either. Cold slows everything.

Bring all your own food. You cannot eat anything here. You cannot drink the ocean directly for long - it's fresh but mineral-leached and near-freezing, and drinking it drops your core temp catastrophically.

### 4. How to Be There Well

1.  Arrive high and mid-Table, not at the rim. Give yourself days to acclimate to the light and sound before you move.
2.  Spend your first day motionless over a shallow basalt ridge, just learning distance. Pick a rock. Guess distance. Check sonar. Repeat until humility sets in.
3.  Move between Mills like island-hopping. Each Mill is a tiny warm oasis where life clusters. Linger in its lee, watch the shimmer, watch the animals come to graze heat. That's the real show.
4.  Keep lights low when not needed. The sun is already unnaturally bright for depth. Your strobes will reflect off volcanic glass like mirrors and blind you.
5.  Log everything by instrument, not memory. The place *wants* to be forgotten because it never changes. Your camera will show identical frame after identical frame with wildly different coordinates.
6.  Never free-fall. No matter how clear and inviting the water column looks. You are not falling through air. You are descending through a column that will squeeze and chill you incrementally.

When you leave, ascend slowly. Even in freshwater, decompression matters. Look up through that impossible clear water one last time - at the sun made hard by altitude, shining through kilometers of water that should be black but isn't - and understand what you're seeing: a planet that decided to be young forever, and built a machine to enforce that decision.

Don't try to bring a piece of the black glass home as a souvenir. It will cut you, it's legally meaningless, and weight limits aside - the High Water does not like accumulation. It likes equilibrium.

Leave no silt.
