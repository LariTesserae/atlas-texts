You do not prepare for this place like you prepare for a desert or a cave. You prepare for it like you prepare for being inside someone else's machine while it's running.

This is not a valley. A valley wants sky. This wants equilibrium. It's a sealed, small, sharp, warm lung of rock about the size of a stadium floor and a low cathedral ceiling — you could walk the wall in an hour — and everything inside it has agreed, a long time ago, to stay exactly as it is forever. The temperature holds at 31-34C. The lights buzz. The motors run on nothing. The beetles open. The beetles close. The mantas circle. The air is so dry it will drink you.

An ordinary human is a bag of warm saltwater with holes in it. That's the entire problem. This place has no use for that, and it will not make an exception.

If you go anyway — and you can, briefly — here's how to do it and come back mostly intact.

### 1. Understand What Kind of Hostile This Is

It's not going to collapse on you. It's not going to bite you. It's not going to get hot or cold or dark.

It's going to do something worse: it's going to ignore you perfectly while taking your water.

Think of standing inside the back of an old refrigerator left running in a closet. It's dim amber light, low and close. It's warm from the floor up, not the sky down. It's loud in a way that becomes part of your body — not deafening, just *occupying* — motor hum, ticking metal, chitin on salt, air ionizing. It's crowded, claustrophobicly crowded, but not with people. With thousands of dry-world animals per cubic meter that have no eyes and no interest in you, layered on the walls like breathing texture. Needleflies hanging in strata. Fist-sized salt-crusted beetles shoulder to shoulder venting heat. Rope-thick blind millipedes underfoot. Three-meter translucent mantas swimming the air in a slow gyre, filtering dust, pulsing with the generators.

And none of it stops. Nothing has changed here in a very long time. You will scratch a mark in the white alkali frost and watch the electrostatic wind fill it back in within ten minutes. Not healed. Reset. The chamber's whole rule is:

**Charge In = Heat + Light + Sound Out, Forever.**

No water in that equation. You are the only wet thing. The air wants to correct that.

That means three things for you psychologically:

1. **You will be irrelevant.** Not threatened, not welcomed. Your presence does not register as meaningful. That is deeply unsettling to a primate brain that expects landscape to react.

2. **You will be entrained.** The flicker-cycle, the wingbeat, the pumpjack nodding forever, the buzz — your pulse will try to sync to it within 15-20 minutes. It feels warm and lulling and like belonging. It is the first sign you have stayed too long.

3. **You will not be able to navigate by leaving traces.** Footprints, scratched arrows, dropped breadcrumbs — erased. Sound pools and repeats thirty seconds later from a different corner. A dropped screw falls sideways. Gravity stutters over thin halite. It's a small room and you can still get disoriented in it.

You are not there to explore. You are there to witness a perfect, dry, buzzing stasis for a few minutes and leave.

### 2. Who Should Not Go At All

Don't go if you have: asthma/reactive airway, severe claustrophobia, epilepsy/photosensitivity, tinnitus that ruins you, or a tendency to panic when you can't find the exit — because there is no exit. There is only rock. Your way in must be your way out, and it has to work through 100+ meters of basalt and a soup of electromagnetic noise. If your jump/translation method needs sky, GPS, radio beacons, or ambient humidity to lock, test it first with an unmanned probe. Many fail in here.

Don't go alone on your first time if you can help it. Not because anything will grab you — but because no one will come looking if you faint from dehydration on that salt flat. The place erases evidence.

### 3. What To Wear: Seal The Bag

You need to cover every membrane and slow every leak.

**Skin — full coverage, light and tight:**
- Long sleeves, long pants, light color. Not heavy Tyvek — you'll cook — but woven, tight-weave, breathable sun-desert clothing. Think nomad, not hazmat.
- Thin nitrile or sun gloves. The salt hexes are knives, the alkali frost is caustic, and the dust gets into every micro-crack in your hands.
- High, thick-soled boots with rubber that insulates. The floor radiates heat up. The hex edges will slice soft soles. Ankle support is mandatory — those halite sheets over hollows ring like a drum and can flex or crack. Use a collapsible trekking pole to tap-test before you step on the clear/white drum areas.
- Wide-brim or cap to keep the sodium light and dust out of your hair/scalp. Static will lift your hair straight up anyway — bring a lip balm/conditioner to reduce spark snap.
- Knee pads if you plan to crouch to look at the salt fauna. You will want to crouch.

**Mouth, nose, eyes — non-negotiable:**
- **Respirator, not a dust mask.** A half-face P100 with nuisance-ozone cartridges. The air tastes of hot iron, alkali, and ozone. That frost you walk on — precipitated salts, fine as moth wings — becomes airborne with every step. It will shred your sinuses and lungs. For an ordinary traveler without custom gear: at minimum a well-fitted N95/P100 + sealed goggles. You will still cough later. With a respirator you won't.
- **Sealed goggles, not sunglasses.** Ski or lab style, anti-fog. The air is so dry your tear film evaporates. You will be blinking constantly. Bring preservative-free eye drops and saline nasal spray. Use them before you feel you need them.
- **Ear protection.** Low-level, constant. Foam plugs + low-profile earmuffs, or good active noise-cancelling earbuds under ear defenders if you still need to hear anomalies. Without it you'll get a headache in 10 minutes and auditory afterimages for hours. Keep one ear slightly open if you're solo so you can hear the halite crack underfoot.

**Sweat management:**
- Your sweat will not bead. It vanishes into the air before it can cool you. You will dehydrate without realizing you're sweating. Pre-hydrate hard the day before — electrolytes, not just water.
- Bring a **closed hydration bladder with a hose** inside your shirt. No open bottles, no cups. Open water films over with dust in seconds and evaporates. You will sip constantly in tiny amounts.
- Electrolyte tablets/salts. Plain water alone will leave you cramping. Alternate.
- Salty snacks in sealed wrappers for after, not during — you don't want crumbs in your respirator.
- Lip balm with SPF/wax, applied thickly before entry and reapplied. Your lips *will* crack within minutes otherwise. It's the fastest tell that the void-dry is working.

No exposed lotion, no perfume, no insect repellent. It will just become dust-food.

### 4. What To Bring

Think: **closed, analog, dim, grounded.**

**Essentials:**
- **Water for two:** the water you'll drink, and the water you'll use to wash your eyes/face on exit. At least 2 liters per 30 minutes of intended stay, even if you won't drink it all. You need the margin. Add a small 250ml squeeze bottle of clean rinse water just for decontamination.
- Headlamp with **red/amber low mode.** The chamber is dimly lit by its own amber filaments and sodium lamps. Your eyes will adapt. A blinding white headlamp will just reflect off dust and panic the needlefly strata. Use red to look at gauges and foot placement without disrupting the mantas' gyre.
- Analog watch with a timer + a separate loud pocket timer. Not your phone. Set a hard exit alarm — 25 minutes for a first visit, 45 minutes max for anyone. When it goes off, you leave even if you feel fine. Especially if you feel fine and *buzzy and at peace*. That's entrainment.
- Small notebook + pencil, not pen. Ink clogs in this dust/heat. Write down entry time, bearing, how you feel.
- Trekking pole / probe for halite testing.
- Grounding strap on your wrist clipped to your pack frame if you're sensitive to shocks — static pools here. You'll get zapped touching junction boxes, railings, even beetles. It's not dangerous, just startling, and a spark near a dusty junction box is rude.
- Small roll of bright paracord or climbing tape. Not to mark the rock — it will be covered or ignored — but to keep a physical tether back to your entry point if you wander. In a 300m bowl you think you won't need it. You will when every wall looks the same and sound lies.

**Do NOT bring:**
- Anything you intend to build, install, or leave running. This place does not want your contribution. Motors you bring will be ignored. Structures will be dusted over. It's not hostile to building, just utterly indifferent, which feels worse.
- Open food, open water, wet towels, humidifiers. They will be consumed instantly and make you a target for every filter-feeder.
- Drones with light props. The air is thick, warm, and full of mantas and needleflies. You'll just foul a wing and lose it.
- Loud tools, speakers, or the idea that you need to "test" the anomaly by yelling, banging, or touching the pumpjack while it's nodding. The sound catches and repeats for 30 seconds. You will just make it harder to think.

### 5. How To Be Inside

**First two minutes:** Stand still at your entry point. Let your boots stop crunching. Breathe through the respirator. Let your eyes adjust to the low amber. Listen to the buzz separate into layers: electrical hum, mechanical tick, chitin rasp, ionized air hiss. Notice the heat coming up through your soles. Drink.

**Move slowly.** The floor is fractured hexes and knife edges hidden by frost. Shuffle more than stride. Tap drum-halite. Give the rope millipedes right of way — step over, not on. They part for nothing and you will not win.

**Don't touch the built things.** The pumpjack nodding on nothing, the conveyor carrying crushed salt from nowhere to nowhere, the junction boxes puffing sparks — they are not puzzles. They are part of the equilibrium. Touching them gets you a static bite and no answers. Look, don't operate.

**Don't touch the big fauna.** The mantas are filtering, not hunting. If you stand under the gyre they will drift over you and you can see the lights through them. Don't reach up. Don't throw dust to watch them feed. Just let their shadow pass over you. If you swat needleflies you'll just create a cloud that gets in your goggles.

**Expect weird pockets:**
- Over the thinnest halite, your inner ear will say *tilt*. Your dropped pole will fall a little sideways then correct. Pause, breathe, plant your feet wide. It's brief.
- In one corner, usually downwind of the pumpjack, your own footsteps will come back 5, 7, 30 seconds later, perfectly preserved. It's not an echo as you know it — it's the dry cold air holding sound. Do not chase it. It will make you dizzy.
- Veins of fungus in cracks glow faint warm-white. Sparse glass-russet stalks with black bristles shiver when generators surge. They are the only "plants." Don't pick them. They are combing charge out of the air and there are almost none.

**Track yourself:** Every 5 minutes: sip, check watch, check lips/nose for bleeding, wiggle fingers. Check your tether. Say out loud, muffled through respirator, what time it is. If your voice sounds delayed or doubled, you're in a sound-pool — step out of it.

When the filament flicker and the wingbeat and your heart start to feel like the same thing — **that's your cue to leave.** It's pleasant. Don't trust it.

### 6. Leaving and Aftercare

Don't expect a dramatic exit. The room doesn't close behind you. It just continues being exactly as loud and warm and full as it was before you came.

As soon as you're out:

1. **Rinse.** Eyes with eye drops, nose with saline spray, mouth with water. Blow your nose — it will be black with alkali and iron. That's normal. Wipe skin with damp cloth, then moisturize heavily. Your sweat glands have been working overtime with no visible sweat.
2. **Drink slowly** with electrolytes. Don't chug. You'll cramp.
3. **Sit in silence.** You will have a ringing/buzz afterimage for 30-90 minutes even with ear protection. It's phantom. Give it quiet.
4. **Decontaminate gear.** Tap boots, shake clothes outside, wipe respirator filters, wash goggles. That fine white frost gets *everywhere* and it is mildly caustic. Don't bring it into your bedding or next jump.
5. **Log it.** Write down how long you stayed, how you felt at 5/10/15 minutes, where the weird gravity and sound pockets were. Next time — if there is a next time — your log is more useful than any mark you left, because the room kept nothing.

You will cough lightly for a day. Your lips will peel even with balm. You'll smell hot mineral in your nose when there is nothing there. That's the price.

### 7. Intuition: What It Feels Like

If you're an ordinary human who has hiked deserts and crept through caves, this will feel like neither.

It feels like being shrunk and placed inside an old radio that's been left on inside a jewelry box lined with living velvet. It's too warm, too close, too even. The light never moves. The sound never changes. The animals never stop doing exactly what they are doing.

You will feel the urge to fix it — to oil the pumpjack, to sweep the salt, to shoo the beetles, to turn off a light. Resist that. The equilibrium is the point. It was holding this note long before you arrived and will hold it long after your little pocket of water is gone.

Go to stand there, to feel small and dry and buzzing and thoroughly out-of-place in a place that is perfectly in place. Go to learn what a world that needs no sky looks like.

Take nothing. Leave nothing — it wouldn't let you anyway. Keep your water sealed, your time short, and your mouth covered.

And set that timer where you can feel it vibrate through your bones. You will want to ignore it.
