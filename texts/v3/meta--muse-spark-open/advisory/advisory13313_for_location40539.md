This is a High Unfinished Place. That’s what travelers call locations like the Valley — worlds that are still in the loading screen, where physics was turned on before the level design was finished.

Do not treat it as a ruined city or a post-apocalypse or a beautiful haunted valley. It is not dead. It is not abandoned. It is *incomplete.* You are not visiting a place that used to be normal. You are visiting a place that hasn’t decided to be normal yet.

If you go without preparing, it will kill you slowly and politely, with bad air and bad distances and bad assumptions.

Here’s how to visit and come back.

### 1. THE AIR: Prepare Your Body For A Place That Doesn’t Want You

The Valley feels high the way a deep pool feels deep — instantly, in your chest.

**What it’s like:**
Not Everest-high, panicked-gaspring high. More like Denver x 2, held there forever. High enough that stairs wind you. High enough that sleep is shallow, dreams are vivid and mean, hangovers last twice as long, cuts heal slowly. Everyone breathes audibly there. You will hear the workers wheezing faintly as they walk. You will hear yourself.
It’s also *damp-cold* — not dry mountain cold. Think late October in England or Seattle, 45-50°F / 7-10°C, 90% humidity, forever. No winter to kill germs, no summer to dry you out. Everything steams and drips. You will never be fully warm or fully dry.

**Preparation:**

1. **Acclimate BEFORE you jump.** Spend 3-5 days at moderate altitude if you can. If you are jumping in directly, bring supplemental oxygen for the first 48 hours. Not a full tank — a pulse-dose hiking concentrator or canned O2 boosters. Locals have generations of red blood cells you don’t. You will get altitude headaches within hours without it.
2. **Water discipline.** The whole valley sweats but it has almost no drinkable standing water. The condensation runoff is black with basalt dust, coal soot, oil, lichen acids, and iron oxide. The 6-minute rains are clean-ish but collect off tarped roofs and through moss mats that are biologically active. Bring a 1L+ wide-mouth bottle and a filter that handles biologicals + particulates + chemicals — a Grayl or LifeStraw Mission type, not just iodine. Drink *more* than you think. Thin air dehydrates you fast even when you’re clammy.
3. **Clothing: Warm/Wet/Industrial.** Forget down. It will wet out. Forget cotton. You need what the workers wear:
   - Waterproof shell top/bottom with pit zips. Gore-tex, not ponchos. You need to move through ferns that dump liters on you when brushed.
   - Synthetic or wool midlayers. Two sets, so one can dry while you wear the other.
   - Steel-shank waterproof boots with serious grip. The moss is a 2-foot thick sponge. It hides rebar, rusted plates, warm pipes. The new rock is razor-sharp when fresh and greasy when wet. Your ankles will roll constantly on empty boulevards that camber weirdly. Ankle support is non-negotiable.
   - Wool socks x5, glove liners + waterproof overmitts, beanie that covers ears. The chill is steady-state; no sun to warm your back.
4. **Light & Nutrition:** There is no sun. Ever. The cloud deck is a fluorescent ceiling office light. Your circadian rhythm will die on day two. Bring vitamin D chews, electrolyte salts, and calorie-dense food you can eat cold — nuts, cured meat, oat bars. Workers’ food is starchy stew and moss-bread. It’s nutritious but your gut biome will need a week to tolerate the spores. Don’t live off it at first.

### 2. GEAR FOR A CITY THAT IS HALF FACTORY, HALF SWAMP

You are visiting an open-air industrial cathedral covered in living carpet.

**Sound:** The Valley never shuts up: 60-cycle generator thrum so low it lives in your teeth, punch-presses echoing 3 seconds late off cliffs, steam hissing, bells from herds, drip-drip-drip everywhere. It’s 70-80 dB constantly. You will get listening fatigue and then you’ll start missing actual warnings — pressure valves blowing, trams squealing, predators moving.

Bring high-fidelity earplugs / musicians’ dampeners (like Loop Switch or Eargasm). Not foam total blockers. Wear them on long transits between districts, take them out in factories and intersections. Sleep with foam plugs + eye mask. Otherwise you won’t sleep more than 90 minutes at a stretch.

**Visibility:** Sodium-gray, shadowless, flat light. Distance is impossible to judge. A wall that looks 200 meters away is a mile away. A herd that looks close is tiny juveniles far off. Buildings loom out of mist with no contrast. Bring a warm-tint headlamp (3000K yellow) even for daytime. White/blue LEDs wash out completely. Red light preserves your night adaptation which you actually need at noon.

Leave your laser rangefinder. It will lie to you. Bring a mechanical click-wheel distance measure and/or a 50m paracord. Chalk is holy. So is flagging tape.

**Rust & Spore:** Everything metal sweats and is being eaten by purple moss. Your gear will rust overnight. Wipe down knives, guns, cameras with light oil before sleep. Bring anti-fog wipes for optics. That moss sporulates when trains vibrate near it. If you have asthma, severe allergies, or immune issues, you **must** bring N95s or a half-face respirator with P100 organic vapor filters. Not for COVID — for fungal blooms the size of satellite dishes that puff when touched. Healthy people get a dry cough and green-black snot after 3-4 days. That’s normal. Wheezing is not. Leave if you wheeze.

### 3. NAVIGATION WHEN SPACE IS SOFT

This is the critical part. Forget maps.

In the Valley, gravity and distance are negotiated, not given. Workers call it “soft curing” or “uncreased.” You will experience:

* Echoes returning too early/late
* Rain falling sideways in a perfect square
* Trams passing through each other on intersecting tracks
* A stair where dropped things hover
* A room bigger inside than outside. Birds avoid these spots but otherwise fly straight through them. That’s your tell.

**Ordinary humans are deeply unsettled by this.** Our brains use consistency to make reality feel real. Here, it flexes a millimeter and your limbic system screams *WRONG*. You get nausea, déjà vu, sudden anxiety, a sense you’re being watched by the walls. Do not intellectualize it. Expect it.

**Rules:**

1. **Never navigate alone.** Buddy system always. If you must solo, leave a trailing line — literally. 20m of bright paracord tied to a fixed railing outside the anomaly. Locals do this with twine and paint.
2. **Two-Touch Rule:** In breakdown zones, only trust surfaces you can touch twice. If a handrail feels solid on grab 1 and grab 2, you can lean on it. If a floor feels flat under two steps, step again. Don’t trust your eyes.
3. **Don’t fix, don’t test, don’t play.** New travelers obsessively throw rocks into gravity patches, put sticks through ghost rails, stick hands into overlapping space to “see what happens.” This is how you get lost. The birds know: go *around*. Give every soft patch 2-3 meters berth.
4. **Mark and note, don’t memorize.** Distances stretch. That warehouse corner where you saw yourself really did show you the back of your head — you’re not hallucinating. Note time, place, symptom in a waterproof field book: “11:42 Pike Warehouse NW corner, interior larger, vertigo, exited same door.” Locals respect travelers who write it down calmly. It validates them.
5. **Compass/GPS/Phones:** Magnetics are skewed by miles of iron mills and extruding basalt. GPS has no satellites here to lock. Your phone will work as camera/flashlight/journal only. Don’t rely on electronic altimeter/barometer — the air pressure is strange under the fixed cloud deck. Learn to read the factory steam whistles that blow the hour. They are the only true clock.

If you become seriously disoriented — double vision, tinnitus spike, feeling of falling while standing still — sit down, back against a real brick wall, eyes on your boots, breathe, count 60 drips. It passes. Then retrace *exactly*. Don’t push through.

### 4. THE LIVING CITY: Animals & Workers

You are a guest in a working town that happens to share its streets with megafauna.

**Animals:**

* **Grazers (six-legged, woolly cows):** They are semi-domesticated but not tame. They weigh 600-800 kg, move in slow herds, chew moss off active machinery. They do not care about you. Never walk between mother and calf. Never touch the bells — workers tune them to track age/health. If a herd blocks tracks or road, wait. Honking does nothing. They move on geological time.
* **Glass-wing birds:** Beautiful soot-colored flocks that live on refinery heat. When they lift off together, they sound like TV static. Don’t stand under vents when a valve blows — they panic and will blind you with wings.
* **Water-antler deer:** Gorgeous, timid. Their antlers literally catch rain. Let them. Don’t approach to photograph. Predators hunt near aqueducts because prey comes to drink.
* **Feathered long-limb predators:** Think man-sized secretary bird meets lynx. Intelligent eyes. They watch. They learn which humans feed them. *Do not feed them.* Children can because they’ve made a pact over years. You have not. Keep 15m distance. Carry a solid walking stick/trail pole. Tap it hard on railings if one follows you — sound deters them. If one vocalizes, back away slowly, facing it. Never run into an empty lot — that’s their hunting ground.

Meat is unsafe unless cooked by locals you trust. Moss/fungi can look edible but many are biofilters heavy with metals. Ask: “Is this lot food?” Workers distinguish growth by color: jade = insulation only, viridian = edible after boiling, bruised-purple = never eat.

**People:**

Population density is shockingly low for industrial sprawl. You can walk ten minutes in a steel district and see no one, then find a five-person family eating lunch on a gantry like it’s normal.

They are reserved, tired, kind, proud. They know their city is half-empty and their mountains are unfinished and their physics is tender. They are not embarrassed. They are not cultists. They will think you’re odd for being amazed.

Etiquette:

* Don’t call it empty, abandoned, post-anything, or creepy. Say “spacious” or “patient.”
* Don’t complain endlessly about glitches. To them it’s weather. Say “the stair’s hanging today” like you’d say “it’s raining.” They’ll nod and show you around.
* Accept tea/stew when offered. Eat what they serve. Ask about tram schedules, furnace times. Compliment the moss insulation on their homes — it’s hard work to cultivate.
* Don’t take pictures of children or of the glowing extrusion seams up high without permission. The upper crags are sacred/dangerous. Stone births are watched, not harvested.
* Worksite rule: If it’s hot, humming, or hissing, assume it’s active even if abandoned-looking. That switchyard with half a mile of ferns? A tram can scream through at 40km/h with 30 seconds warning. Stay off rails, stay off catwalks unless a worker waves you up.

### 5. YOUR MIND: The Real Danger

Physical survival is easy. Psychological survival is hard.

The Valley is built to unmake human time. Everything is slow, vast, loud, patient. There are boulevards for crowds that don’t exist. No shadows means no time-of-day cue. Echoes return wrong. You will feel simultaneously exposed and claustrophobic — small under flat cliffs and huge inside empty plazas. 

Most travelers get **Uncreasing Sickness** on Day 2-3: a heavy, mournful calm, difficulty remembering why they came, urge to sit and watch moss grow, conviction that nothing matters because nothing ever finishes. It’s not depression — it’s entrainment to the place’s speed. The Valley thinks in decades. You beat at 80 bpm.

Countermeasures:

* **Keep a strict ritual.** Wake, log, eat, walk, talk, sleep at fixed intervals announced by whistles. Set alarms. Don’t drift.
* **Bring a tether object from crisp reality:** a favorite song (played on speaker, not earbuds — hear it bounce right), a smell (coffee beans, pine oil), photos of loved ones, a small game to play with buddies each evening. Something utterly mundane that asserts completed physics.
* **Limit solo staring.** The vertical mile-high flat faces and hanging cloud will entrance you. Stare >3 minutes and you lose depth cues permanently for hours. Use the 20-20-20 rule. Focus on close things: hands, mug, notebook.
* **Talk daily.** Even if just to journal audio. Language keeps your brain folded correctly.

Plan to stay 4-7 days max first trip. Longer stays require workers adopting you into rhythm. Most newcomers who stay 3 weeks without integration decide never to leave — and then fade quietly, taking the slowest job in the quietest mill, growing moss on their boots.

### QUICK PACK LIST

**Non-negotiable:** Waterproof shell, insulating mids, waterproof boots, 5x wool socks, headlamp (warm tint) + spare batteries, 50m cord + chalk + flagging tape, 1L filtered bottle + electrolytes, high-fi earplugs + foam sleep plugs, respirator/N95, oximeter, trail poles/walking stick, waterproof field book, vitamin D, oil cloth for metal gear.

**Strongly recommended:** Small oxygen canister/concentrator for first nights, povidone/bandages (cuts from new rock infect fast), antihistamines, antifungal cream, thermos (hot tea is currency), lightweight tarp/bivy (don’t rely on buildings finishing), gifts: quality gloves, pencils, tea, chocolate to trade with workers.

### LEAVING

Don’t try to climb the extrusion walls to “see the birth.” You will not be able to breathe, you will get lost in seams that weren’t there yesterday, and rescue is impossible — they don’t do rescues there, they wait for you to walk back or not.

Leave the way you came. On departing, brush off all seeds/spores meticulously. Quarantine boots/jackets for 24h before bringing to another world. The moss is eager. It grew on cooling stone within weeks. It will love your apartment’s damp corners.

And when you get home, where shadows snap sharp and echoes land right and gravity never apologizes, you will hear the Valley thrum for a few nights in your bones. That’s normal. Drink water. Walk at human speed again. Remember that you visited a place that is still learning how to be a place — and let it continue without you.
