For the Build Under Water — the drowned continent that isn't a continent — that sea-sized factory under the seafloor.

This is not a dive. This is not Atlantis. This is not a ruin. Do not prepare for any of those.

Prepare to go inside an industrial planet that happens to be filled with cold water and left the lights on.

### 0. Reframe What This Place Is

Your instincts will be wrong here in predictable ways, so fix them before you go:

**It is not hostile. It is indifferent.** Nothing here wants to kill you. No curse, no entity, no malevolence. It's an empty machine the size of a country running its daytime shift forever. That makes it more dangerous, not less. It will not notice you drowning, freezing, or getting lost.

**It is not deep-sea.** Pressure should kill you, light should be gone, temperature should be near-freezing. None of that is true. Pressure is weirdly forgiving here, light carries too far, water stays at a workable 8-12C / 46-54F everywhere. Do not trust your dive training. Your depth gauge will read nonsense. Your no-deco tables are useless. Think of it as a flooded building the size of Europe, not an ocean.

**It is not abandoned.** It is *empty*. Abandoned means things stopped. Empty means things kept going without people. Pumps pump. Conveyors run empty. Gates cycle. Drones scrape. Alarms go off for no one. The hazards are all active industrial hazards, not decay hazards.

**It is not wilderness.** Every surface is poured, welded, bolted, tiled. Life is a thin trespass on it. You are not exploring nature. You are trespassing inside infrastructure that is still on.

If you can hold that in your head, you'll come back.

### 1. What Will Actually Hurt You

In order of how fast it gets travelers:

**1. Cold.** This is your primary killer. The water is consistently, relentlessly cool — late-October lake water. Not instantly lethal, but it seeps. It steals heat through any suit, through any hull, through gloves and hoods after an hour. Metal sweats. You shiver. Then you get stupid. Then you get clumsy. Then you can't operate your clips and valves.

You will be wet for hours, maybe days. Hypothermia here is slow, quiet, and dignity-killing.

**2. Getting Lost / Losing Scale.** This is what almost gets everyone else. The Build is so vast that your brain can't process distance. A vault three kilometers across with one crane in it looks like a swimming pool until you try to swim to the crane and it takes you 40 minutes. A corridor a mile wide looks like a hallway. Pillars recede to a blue haze exactly like the open sea does — so you have no sense of wall vs. open water.

Everything looks the same: white lamps on black trusses, clean concrete terraces, endless grating. Landmarks don't exist unless you make them. People don't get eaten here. They just swim off toward a light that never gets closer until their air and heat run out.

**3. Moving Water and Entanglement.** The Build breathes water. Cathedralsized ballast pumps kick on without warning — a *BOOM* you feel in your molars, then a river appears where there was slack water. Overflow rims pour skyscraper-height waterfalls *underwater*, constantly, white and roaring. They dissolve before they surface but the current they make will tumble you down terraces.

And everything is snaggy: rebar whiskers, empty conveyor chains rattling on sprockets, cable runs, grating, formwork ties, unfinished scaffolding with sharp, fresh, unrusted edges. Monofilament kelp that looks thin will foul a propeller or ankle in seconds. This place will cut and snag you, not crush you.

**4. Barotrauma by Inattention / Air.** Most of the volume is fully flooded. No air to breathe. Control islands and lamp housings and trapped pockets under inverted floors have air, but it's stale, damp, unbreathable machine air that will have CO2 pooling and algae film on everything. Don't trust any air you didn't bring.

Gulls nest up there somehow. Don't take that as a sign it's safe to breathe.

**5. Fauna.** Nothing here is anomalous or magic. But the ecology has adapted to *indoors*. Herring and smelt schools move like weather fronts between pillars and will black out your visibility for minutes. Cod the size of dogs sit in pipe mouths and don't move until you bump them. Basket-sized crabs walk *upside down* on gantry undersides and will drop on you. Eels in conduits. Cuttlefish at inspection lights that stare back.

And the whales — or the things that used to be whales — that learned to navigate corridors. You will hear them first. Their song is broken up and amplified by concrete and travels for kilometers, so loud your ribs vibrate a kilometer away. They are not aggressive, but a 20-meter animal making a turn in a service tunnel the size of a canyon does not see you and does not need to. Keep clear of high-water transit lanes. If the water suddenly goes quiet and all the small fish vanish, look up and get flat against a structure.

None of this wants you. All of it can kill you accidentally.

**6. Sound.** The Build is LOUD. Low, physical loud. Expansion joints groaning, generators thudding in a heartbeat you feel in your teeth, metal contracting, alarms muffled and distant, waterfalls roaring without cease. You have to shout to be heard next to a spillway, and then the water flattens your voice. Long exposure grinds you down. It also hides things — you won't hear a pump start behind you over the pump in front of you. Bring ear protection that still lets you hear alarms.

### 2. How to Pack Your Body

You are an ordinary human. Your body obeys ordinary rules here, which is lucky. Prepare it like a cold-water industrial diver, not a tourist.

**Thermal is everything.**
- Dry suit, not wetsuit, if you have any choice. With proper undergarments: heavy wool or synthetic base + insulated mid-layer. Double-seal wrists and neck. The water *seeps*.
- Dry hood, 7mm gloves minimum, or better yet dry gloves with liners. You need your fingers for the first 6 hours, not just the first 30 minutes.
- Bring chemical heat packs, sealed. Put them in your gloves, boots, kidney belt. They are single-use and useless anywhere else — here they buy you an extra hour of clear thought.
- Your face will be exposed even with a mask. Expect lip and nose numbness. Practice operating all your gear by touch with numb hands before you go.

**Breathing.**
- Full closed-circuit rebreather with redundant open-circuit bailout is ideal. Ordinary SCUBA works, but you will burn through gas fast if you panic in current, and there are no dive shops down there.
- At least 2x the gas you think you need for the time you plan. Rule of thirds is not conservative enough here — use halves. Half in, half out, half for the thing you didn't plan.
- Full-face mask with comms if you can. It keeps your face warmer, lets you breathe through your nose, and lets you talk to a buddy without shouting over the roar. A standard half-mask *will* fog and leak from temperature differential; bring anti-fog and a spare.
- CO2 monitor if you're in any trapped air pocket for more than a few minutes. Don't trust the bird.

**Food, Water, Waste.**
- You will dehydrate fast breathing dry gas in cold water and not feeling thirsty. Insulated hydration bladder inside your suit with warm, sweet drink. Not cold water.
- High-fat, high-sugar solid food you can eat with gloves: wax-wrapped cheese, chocolate, pemmican bars. No crumbs that foul your mask.
- Pee valve for your dry suit or adult diapers. You will be out long enough for this to matter, and holding it makes you cold faster.

### 3. How to Pack Your Kit

Think caver + sailor + factory maintenance tech.

**Navigation — your #1 job:**
- **Physical tether + reels.** Bring at least one primary cave line reel (400m+) and two jump spools. Run line from your entry point the entire time. No matter how clear and bright and "I can see my entry" it looks — the haze eats distance. If you don't run line, you *will* not find your way back. Mark the line with directional arrows every 10m.
- Compass does work here (it's just steel and concrete), but steel masses pull it. Trust it for general trend, not precision. Bring a mechanical compass plus a solid-state one.
- Rangefinder / laser measure. Your eyes lie. A laser that tells you that pillar is 800m away, not 80m, will save your life.
- Sonar pinger / fishfinder, small handheld. Sound travels forever here. A single ping off a distant wall a minute after you drop a wrench is real — use active sonar to map voids. Don't rely on vision.
- Multiverse anchor/beacon that works fully submerged, potted in epoxy, with acoustic and radio modes. Test it at the threshold before you commit. Place one at your entry, one at your first terrace control island. If your recall is line-of-sight, you're in trouble — get a breadcrumb system.

**Light:**
- You don't need light to see — the Build provides noon-white industrial floodlight everywhere, banks of floodlamps, amber sodium over drydocks, caged fluorescents in every stairwell. It's workmanlike and endless. But bring your own anyway:
- Two primary canister lights (flood + spot) + two backup handhelds. Not for illumination, for signaling. Strobe mode to mark your line, your exit, your buddy.
- Chem lights / cyalumes by the dozen. Clip them to your line every 20m on the way back. They make the void human-readable.
- No need for UV or red-light preservation. This place has no natural dark to respect.

**Tools:**
- Trauma shears + line cutter (two, different places on your body) for kelp / old monofilament / tag lines.
- Small pry bar / spud bar. Sluice gates move. Grating shifts. You may need to lift or wedge.
- Waterproof slate + grease pencil + permanent marker. Write on everything. Label your line, label air pockets "DO NOT BREATHE - CO2" or "EXIT ->"
- Dry bags inside dry bags. Everything that must stay dry *will* get damp from condensation. That weird detail — rain falling *up* from the lowlands to the underside of the ceiling kilometers above — means mist and dripping everywhere near thermal boundaries. Your spare clothes, your batteries, your meds: double-bagged with silica gel.

**Protection:**
- Helmet. Not a dive accessory, a real caving / hard-hat with chinstrap. You will bump fresh concrete corners, rebar whiskers, low gantries you misjudged, and fauna. A head cut in cold water ends trips.
- Knee and elbow pads (hard-shell) for grating and concrete. Barnacles grow in neat lines on bolt heads — they are razor blades.
- Ear plugs / filtered ear protection that still lets comms through. Tinnitus after a day next to a spillway is guaranteed otherwise.
- Gloves that can handle concrete abrasion. Kelp slime makes everything slick.

**Buddy + Comms:**
- Do not go alone. The density is near-void — a single boulevard of columns with nothing moving for minutes. If you black out from cold or snag, no one will find you and no drone will pause its scraping to help.
- Hardwired buddy comms through full-face masks, or at least waterproof FRS/GMRS with throat mics. Acoustics carry weirdly; shouting doesn't.
- Pre-agreed signals: light signals, line pulls (one pull = attention, two = go, three = emergency), touch contact drills for low-vis fish schools.

### 4. How to Move

**Entry:** From the top. You will descend long after you think you should have hit bottom. Light does not fail. Pressure does not spike lethally. Trust your instruments over your gut, but keep watching for narcosis-like fixation — not from nitrogen, from scale vertigo.

Descend slowly along a structure, not blue-water. Keep a hand or eye on concrete. The moment you are in open water between districts, you are in a desert. Speed means nothing without reference.

**On terraces:** Move like you’re on scaffolding in a storm. Every terrace edge is an active waterfall to the next level down, even deep underwater — white, churning, full of foam that never surfaces. They are loud enough to knock you off balance. Stay back from overflow rims, clip in if there’s a rail, and cross spillways one at a time.

Expect everything to vibrate. Grating hums under your fins/boots. Handrails thrum. That’s normal — expansion joints and distant turbines. If a new vibration starts — a low boom rolling for miles — that’s a big pump cycling. Pause. Watch for new current. Don’t be in a narrow turbine gallery when it does.

**Navigation habits:**
- Never leave a control island or pillar without knowing the next visible structure you’re swimming to. Leapfrog. Island -> piling -> gantry -> island. Never strike out toward haze.
- Look back every 50 kicks. The view behind you is different and you need to memorize both directions. Take photos of your line junctions.
- Mark everything: line, chem lights, cairns of bolts where you can. The maintenance drones will eventually clean them, but not today.

**Life around you:**
- Watch fish behavior the way you’d watch birds. If a school turns in unison, something cast a shadow. If cod all face one way out of pipe mouths, current is coming that way. If the shrimp vanish from a girder, a cuttlefish or crab is near.
- Don’t touch biofilm deliberately. It’s not poisonous, just slick and everywhere. If you scrape it, it grows back in weeks — but scraping it kicks up a cloud that kills visibility for ten minutes.
- Give whales 100m+ vertical clearance. Feel their passage in your molars and stay flat. Don’t chase for a photo.

### 5. How to Stay Human

The psychological hit here is specific: **agoraphobia and claustrophobia at the same time.** Infinite bright empty water inside a concrete maze that has its own weather.

You will see clouds form *inside* a maintenance tunnel big enough to hold weather — mist condensing where cold runoff meets heat-bleed from a power manifold. Your brain will scream *this should not be indoors*. You will hear a wrench ping for a full minute as it bounces off something you can't see, because sound has nothing to stop it. You will see a plaza the size of a county with one crab clicking across it and nothing else. You will feel both exposed and boxed in.

Plan for it:
- Bring a small, human ritual object: a thermos of real tea to drink on a control island with rubber matting still non-slip under slime. Sit, look out at white lamps receding to blue, and just listen to the hum. Let the scale be boring for five minutes.
- Timebox your dive. Decide *before* you go: "I will be back at entry in 90 minutes" and obey the alarm even if that boulevard of lights is so beautiful you want to see one more truss. The Build rewards curiosity with more empty.
- Talk. Even to yourself. The factories never stop rattling on empty sprockets. Your voice anchors you.
- Have an abort trigger list written down: shivering you can’t stop, confusion reading gauges, any suit leak you can't warm, any current you didn't expect. When one triggers, you leave. No debate.

Hypothermia makes you feel warm and brave. That's when people strike out for "just a bit further." Don't.

### 6. How to Leave

**You need an exit that does not require you to be smart at the end.** You will be cold, task-loaded, and spatially stupid.

- Your line is your exit. Follow it hand-over-hand if visibility goes. Don't reel it until you're out.
- Your beacon is your exit. If you use multiverse stepping, set it to trigger on dead-man or timed recall, not just manual. Cold fingers fumble.
- Leave breadcrumbs you can see from distance: strobes at terrace edges, reflective tape on railings.
- Log your entry heading, depth-by-structure (e.g., "Terrace 3, yellow gantry crane, east face"), and gas used on the way in so you know what "out" should cost.

When you're back on top — where the seafloor should be but is just poured concrete becoming ocean — don't stand up fast. You’ve been cold and horizontal. Warm slowly, hydrate warmly, eat. Your ears will ring for an hour. That's the Build leaving you.

You’ll remember a few images forever: the clean knapped face of a blasted channel glittering under 20 meters of water, wood grain left in concrete by formwork just stripped, a single kelp individual stretching like a prisoner toward an amber sodium lamp, a boulevard of lighted columns with nothing moving between them, and that endless hum.

Everything is humming. Everything is flowing. Everything is holding. You were just a visitor. Keep it that way — touch nothing you don't need to, take nothing but pictures and line back in, and let the drones keep scraping.

**Checklist to copy:**

Dry suit + thermals + hood + dry gloves + heat packs
Rebreather + bailout + 2x gas
Full-face mask + comms + spare half-mask
Helmet + pads + cutters x2 + pry bar
Primary reel + 2 jump spools + line arrows + chem lights
Compass + rangefinder + handheld sonar
2 can lights + 2 backups + strobe
Anchor beacons x2, tested wet
Slate, markers, camera, drybags + silica
Warm drink + dense food + pee solution
Buddy, hard comms, pre-agreed signals, 90-min timer

If you can check every box, you’re ordinary enough to go. If you can’t, you’re not ready — the Build will still be there. It was built to outlast you.
