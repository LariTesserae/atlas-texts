This is not a hike. This is not urban exploration. This is visiting a building that got so big it forgot it was a building.

You do not prepare for the Construct like you prepare for a mountain or a city. You prepare like you're going to be a small, soft, mammalian object left on a very high, very bright, very patient piece of industrial equipment that is still running.

If you rush any part of this, it will hurt you. If you move slowly, it will probably let you leave.

### 1. Your Body Will Think You Are Dying

The air up there on the Upper Tier is at about ~3,000-4,000m equivalent. Not Everest-deadly, but thin enough that every system you have will notice.

**For two weeks before you go:**
* Do cardio. Not to get strong, but to teach your heart to work a little harder without panicking.
* Sleep with your mouth closed if you can. Practice nasal breathing. You will need it.
* Start hydrating like it's a job. The Construct sweats, you will too, but the water never stays. That fine snag-rain and condensation cycle will soak your clothes and leave you dehydrated at the same time. Bring more water than you think and then double it.
* Get acetazolamide if your transit method allows it. Ibuprofen, electrolytes. No alcohol for 48 hours before jump.

**What to bring for your body:**
* **Altitude lungs:** A pulse oximeter. A rescue inhaler even if you don't have asthma. The dust here is not soil dust - it's concrete powder, aluminum oxide, powdered sealant. It's alkaline and sharp. An N95 or better is non-negotiable for the first 6 hours until you learn which levels are dusty and which are damp.
* **Clothing for exposure, not warmth:** Pale, bone-colored construction reflects light like snow. You will get sunburned in your nostrils and under your chin. You need:
  - High-altitude glacier glasses or welding-grade clip-ons. Regular sunglasses are not enough. The light is hard, precise, and *bounces* from every white surface and pipe. Snow blindness is a real risk here, even though there is no snow.
  - SPF 50+ zinc that doesn't sweat off. Reapply every 90 minutes. Cover your neck, ears, hands.
  - Layers for 1.4 Cool: That means a climate that is cool, but never cold enough to kill you, never warm enough to comfort you. Think 10-14C / 50-57F at noon, dropping to near 4C / 39F when cloud catches. With wind and thin sun, you will feel colder. Bring a windproof shell, a breathable mid-layer, and nothing cotton. You will go from beaded-condensation damp at 07:00 to dry-bright at 12:00 and back. You want to peel and add, not shiver or stew.
  - Hard-sole, steel-toe, high-ankle boots with excellent grip on wet concrete and oil-slick metal. Grates are still sharp. Scuppers are ankle-breakers. You will be walking on floors that are also ceilings that are also roads.

* **Knees and patience:** Freight platforms take 20 minutes to cross one terrace. Stairs go nowhere. Corridors loop. This place punishes fast movement. If you try to power-walk it like a city, you will get altitude sick, lost, and exhausted in an hour. Plan to cover 1/3 of the distance you think you can.

### 2. You Will Be Seen By The Light

This is the most dangerous thing about the Construct for an ordinary human. It's not the height. It's the brightness that has no warmth.

You are used to light coming from *a* sun. Here light comes from everywhere - the low, close sun, the pale concrete, the aluminum, the sodium lamps left on forever in empty bays, and eventually, the light that has started to pool like water in corners.

**Preparation:**
* Bring a wide-brim hat with a dark underside. Baseball caps make the glare worse by bouncing light up.
* Bring a Buff/gaiter to pull up over your nose. Again, sunburn from below.
* Bring eye drops. The air is dry when it's bright, damp when it's dim. Your corneas will hate both.
* Bring a sleep mask that actually blocks light. And earplugs + a white noise app. The Construct holds light after dusk and is quiet in a way that makes your brain invent sound. You will not sleep well the first night without help. Poor sleep + altitude = terrible decisions.
* Do NOT bring a 10,000-lumen tactical flashlight to "see better." You will just add to the pool. Bring a warm, dim, directional headlamp with red-light mode. Use it to check shadows, not to flood rooms.

**Rule:** If you see light behaving like a liquid - collecting in a corner, humming, not moving when you pass your hand through it - do not touch it, do not stare into it, do not try to disperse it. Walk around it. Mark it on your map and tell someone else where it is. It's not for you. The locals leave it alone for a reason.

### 3. Gravity Is A Suggestion Near The Middle

This is where ordinary humans get hurt because they trust their reflexes.

On the outer terraces and upper escarpments, physics mostly works. Dropped things drop. Shadows fall away from the sun. Four left turns brings you back.

As you move toward the center of the valley - toward the deepest, most built-over part - that stops being true.

* A bolt can fall, pause, and drift to stick to a wall.
* Your shadow can point toward the sun.
* A corridor loop can gain you a level without stairs.
* Perspective inverts: the farther gantry crane looks closer.

**How to prepare for negotiable physics:**

1.  **Never go to the center on your first trip.** Stay on the rim, the Upper Tier, the outer terraces. The wrongness gets stronger the deeper and more central you go. Treat the center like a deep cave dive. You need experience first.
2.  **Bring a gravity kit:** Not fancy. A small bag of steel bolts/nuts, a plumb line on a string, a cheap laser level, and a few glow sticks. Before you trust a floor, a plaza, or a catwalk, toss a bolt. Watch where it goes. Watch your shadow vs. the sun vs. your plumb. If any two disagree, you are in a soft spot. Back up. Mark it.
3.  **Tether yourself.** A climber's harness and a 10m dynamic tether clipped to rail, pipe, rebar loop. Not because you'll fall off the Construct - there's always another floor - but because "down" might change and you might step onto what looks like floor and become wall. People here move carefully, slowly, with a hand trailing on a rail. Copy them. Sudden moves tear things.
4.  **Navigation must be physical:** GPS will drift. Compasses will spin. Phone altimeters will lie. Bring:
    * Paper map you draw yourself as you go
    * Chalk or painter's tape to mark left turns
    * A spool of mason's line or paracord for backtracking in service voids
    * Count stairs, count presses, count lamp posts. Repetition is a hazard - identical gantries stepping into haze will hypnotize you into perspective inversion. Stop looking into the distance. Look at your feet, then at the next 20 meters.

If you find yourself one level above where you started after four lefts, do not try to "solve" it by going faster. Sit. Drink water. Breathe. Re-trace with your line.

### 4. Water Is Not Water

There is no river. No lake. No tap you should trust.

The damp arrives sideways as cloud, runs six meters in a clean square gutter, disappears down a scupper, reappears as a ceiling sheen three levels down. It looks clean because the concrete is clean. It is not.

Every horizontal surface is intermittently: beading wet, sheeting, dry, beading again. Algae in the sags. Mosquito larvae in the cisterns. Rust, cold iron, wet concrete smell. Cooling basins are industrial.

**Bring** 4-5 liters carrying capacity, electrolyte salts, and a filter that handles chemicals/heavy metals, not just microbes. Better yet, bring all your water in. Use local water only for washing, and even then, not on open cuts. That concrete dust in water will do awful things to your gut.

Your boots will get wet in the morning cycle no matter what. Bring dry socks in a ziplock. Trench foot at altitude is miserable.

### 5. It's Quiet, But It's Not Empty

The dominant sounds are ticks, taps, coos, your own breathing. Then a massive press stamps ONCE an hour and the sound takes forever to fade. Freight elevators sigh through your teeth. This quiet makes humans anxious. You will want to fill it with music or talking. Don't, at least not with both ears covered.

You need to hear:
* The loose cladding tapping = wind picking up on an exposed cantilever. Get off it.
* The pigeons going silent = cat or something bigger in the pipe chase. Or air pressure change before a snag-cloud.
* The ticking accelerating = condensation cycle starting. Find cover if you don't want to be damp for hours.
* The bees in the vent stack getting loud = swarm. Leave vertically, not horizontally - vents move air sideways.

Wildlife is urban abundance perfected. It's not afraid of you.
* **Do not corner feral cats, martens, or rat-things with extra joints.** They live in walls. Let them.
* **Starlings in mesh undersides:** Don't walk under a roost that is visibly sagging. That mesh is old.
* **Moths the size of your hand:** Harmless. They will land on your light. Let them.
* **Scuttling in service voids:** Do not go looking. Fauna that adapts to building, not wilderness, has no fear response you recognize. If you must enter a void, make noise, use red light, go with back to wall, and never put hands where you can't see.

And the hanging gardens, hanging workshops, hanging cages that brush your shoulders - those are homes. You are sideways-walking through someone's interval. Move sideways respectfully. Don't touch laundry, prayer strings, bird nets. A nod is better than a hello.

### 6. Psychological Preparation: The Patience Tax

Everything here is young in construction but old in intention. Fresh pour, sharp lip, red plastic caps on rebar. Yet the *idea* of it is ancient. Young terrain, old intentions. That contradiction messes with your sense of time.

Decay is slow. Paint peels in year-long curls. Rust creeps. You will feel like nothing is happening, then realize you've been staring at repeating cranes for 15 minutes and you're dizzy.

* **Bring a task with a slow rhythm to match the place:** Sketch, knit, mend, whittle. Something for your hands when the press stamps and you wait.
* **Expect loneliness without solitude:** Dense to saturation, but quiet. You'll see laundry and nests and cages and never see who lives there. You'll feel watched by pigeons and concrete.
* **Set a hard turn-back time and obey it.** The golden hour when the sun cuts horizontally and every edge turns to gold is beautiful and disorienting. You will want to stay for it. Plan to be back at a known, physics-stable terrace before it starts.
* **Do not test the wrongness for fun.** Don't drop things repeatedly in soft spots to see what happens. Don't step into pooled light. Don't take four lefts on purpose to see if you gain a level. The locals move carefully as if sudden movements might tear something. Believe them.

### Final Pack Checklist For An Ordinary Human:

* Glacier goggles, zinc, hat with dark underside, no cotton layers
* N95s, pulse oximeter, electrolytes, altitude meds, 5L water
* Boots, harness + tether, dry socks, windproof
* Red headlamp, paper + chalk + cord, bolts + plumb, glow sticks
* Sleep mask, earplugs
* Filter, socks, snacks that are salty not sugary

### How It Will Feel When You're There:

You will stand on the Upper Tier and the light will have an edge. The air will be cool and thin and your breath will be a little fast until you slow it on purpose. The terraces will fall away in impossible, sharp steps toward haze, and you will think *I can walk that* and then you will try to move through one pipe-thick interval and realize you have to turn sideways while sparks rain down from Level 41 onto Level 40 and a swift screams past without making a sound.

You will hear drip, tick, hush. You will see green only where dust collected in an expansion joint and allowed one brave grass blade. You will feel exposed the whole time, even in shadow, because every shadow is precise and every wall is still curing.

If you honor the thin air, the bright light, and the negotiable gravity - if you move slowly, test down with a bolt, and leave pooled light alone - it will let you wander for an evening and leave with gold edges in your memory.

Go slow. Stay peripheral. Breathe. Come back up before dark.
