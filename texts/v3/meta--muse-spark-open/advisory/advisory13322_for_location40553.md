This is not a dive. This is not a high-altitude lake. This is not Atlantis.

This is a place where the physics are wrong for you, and the culture has adapted to physics that should kill you. You are an ordinary human. You breathe air, you move quickly, you make noise, you need solid ground and a horizon. None of that will work here.

If you go, you will arrive already under. Prepare for that.

### 1. Understand What You're Actually Entering

**You are inside a perched ocean.**

Think less "ocean" and more "warm, mineral-thick lens of water the size of a country, balanced on top of a brand-new mountain range 2-4km up." There is no surface above you to breach for air. There is no shore to swim to. There are only the *edges*, kilometers away, where the whole ocean pours off the plateau in endless white falls. You do not want to drift to the edges.

The water column you'll be living in is shallow by ocean standards - most human activity is between 5 and 40 meters deep - but there is no air above it. It's water all the way up until it isn't, and where it stops, there is only thin, cold nothing and then death by falling.

Other things that will break your brain:

*   **Warm everywhere:** 28-30°C, bathwater warm. You will not get cold. You will overheat if you exert yourself. You will dehydrate fast, even while submerged, because you sweat and you don't notice.
*   **Light without a sun:** A diffuse olive-gold disc through jade. No shadows, no glare, no true day/night. Below 2-3 meters of depth it's perpetual late afternoon, dim teal and amber. Your eyes will strain. Your circadian rhythm will break within 48 hours.
*   **Water that isn't water:** It's stratified, silty, full of dissolved iron and carbonate. Sheets of warmer/colder water crawl past each other over weeks. Curtains of suspended silt hang vertically for days. The water itself will feel slightly viscous, almost broth-like. It will blur your vision at arm's length, it will clog filters, and it will irritate your eyes, nose, and throat if you try to open them without protection.
*   **Sound is dead:** This water eats sound. No echoes, no sonar bloom. A hammer on rock is a soft *thunk*. A voice is a hum you only hear within 2 meters. You will feel unnervingly deaf and isolated. Locals communicate by rope tugs, hand gestures, and close-range humming. Shouting will just waste air.
*   **The ground is alive and unfinished:** Basalt domes are still cooling and cracking *underwater*. Vents open without warning and belch 40-50°C chemically aggressive water and clouds of steam that dissolve before they rise. Tufa terraces grow an inch a year and snap like stale meringue. Bubbles the size of your house rise with terrifying slowness and pop silently somewhere above. Nothing is stable for long.

> **Rule for your brain:** This is not a reef to explore. This is a flooded construction site, overgrown with a buoyant jungle, where a town has been knotted together to keep from falling off the world.

### 2. Your Body is the Wrong Tool

You cannot do what the locals do.

They hold their breath for 10-15 minutes, moving with dream-slowness to conserve oxygen. They "wear" the water, with modified skin and blood. They breathe from communal "lungs" - inverted, living leaf-bells that slowly collect oxygen from the forest over a week. That air is communal, sacred, and barely enough for them.

**You will drown in 3 minutes without help. Plan accordingly.**

#### Preparation Protocol for an Ordinary Human:

**A. Do NOT attempt breath-holding tourism.**
You are not adapted. You will hyperventilate, panic, stir silt, tear living ropes, and die. Even if a local kindly offers you their family lung, you do not have the social right to drain a week's worth of their air in one panicked gulp.

**B. Bring your own air, and make it closed-loop.**
Open-circuit SCUBA is a terrible idea here:
1. Bubbles will foul the visibility for everyone for hours.
2. Bubbles will disturb spore clouds, tear filamentous algae, and be seen as incredibly rude/destructive.
3. You have nowhere to surface and swap tanks easily.

What works:
*   **A rebreather with long-duration scrubbers.** Preferably an electronic CCR set to a low work rate. You need 4-6 hours minimum per charge, because resupply is impossible.
*   **Full-face mask or helmet.** Not just for air - for eye and skin protection from mineral-rich, spore-filled water. The oily spore clouds that hang like smoke will irritate lungs, eyes, and mucous membranes. Rust-red iron bacteria will stain everything. You want a seal.
*   **Ballast, not fins.** You do not swim here. You drift. You will be positively buoyant in warm fresh-ish water, especially with air on your back. Bring a soft weight belt/harness with *ditchable* stone weights - ideally locally-collected tufa in net bags, just like locals do. This lets you adjust buoyancy without bringing foreign metal. Fins are nearly useless in the dense lattice of kelp-pillars and ropes; they just kick up silt that hangs for a day. Bring soft, gloved hands and ankle toe-loops to hook onto ropes.

**C. Thermal and skin protection.**
You will not be cold. You will be waterlogged. After 6-8 hours of continuous immersion in 30°C mineral water, your skin will macerate, your feet will soften dangerously, and small cuts will infect fast. Bring:
*   A thin, full-body dive skin / 1mm wetsuit - not for warmth but for abrasion. The kelp-wood is rough, the basalt is glass-sharp, the tufa will slice you.
*   Petroleum jelly or zinc balm for friction points, ears, and lips.
*   Freshwater to drink. Yes, drink while submerged. You will sweat heavily. Dehydration and hyponatremia are real risks even though you're surrounded by water. Carry a hydration bladder with a bite valve inside your mask.
*   Ear equalization will be constant but shallow. Practice gentle, frequent Frenzel equalization. The water layers have different density - your ears will pop oddly even when you're not changing depth.

**D. Light and senses.**
Your flashlight is a weapon here. A bright white dive light in that teal murk will blind locals, scatter off silt into whiteout, and ruin your own dark adaptation.
*   Bring a **dim, warm amber light** - a jar of glow-algae is ideal if you can trade for one, but a covered, low-lumen amber headlamp works. Think candlelight, not searchlight.
*   Bring clear, low-magnification optics. Turbidity means you won't see more than 10-30 meters anyway. Binoculars are pointless. A magnifying lens for close work is better.
*   Bring earplugs for surface? No. Bring patience for hearing *nothing*. The forest creaks, oxygen pearls off leaves one bubble at a time, basalt ticks as it cools. That's it. Many travelers panic from sensory deprivation before they panic from air loss.

### 3. How to Move, How to Behave

The local physics is: **What is still, grows. What moves fast, breaks.**

This is not a metaphor. It's engineering.

The entire city is grown, not built. Platforms are living kelp-wood, ropes are living kelp cord as thick as your waist, houses are hollowed living domes. If you thrash, you will snap a load-bearing rope that took 20 years to grow. If you kick, you'll shatter a white tufa terrace that someone's ancestral knife was going to be knapped from.

**Required movement training before you go:**
1.  **Slow down by 80%.** Practice Tai Chi underwater. Every gesture should take 3x longer than you think it needs to. To hurry is to waste air and to signal disrespect.
2.  **No free-swimming.** Always keep one hand on a rope, vine, or pillar. Ladders are ladders of knotted vine. Streets are levels, spaced vertically. You move hand-over-hand along rope runs, towed by toe-loops. If a rope brushes your arm when you arrive, that is deliberate - grab it. It is an invitation and an anchor line.
3.  **Don't stir the settling world.** Don't stand on mud basins - they are half-formed calderas that exhale. Don't touch vertical silt curtains. Don't wave away a spore cloud. That "smoke" is next year's forest. Hold still and let it pass.
4.  **Ballast discipline.** Locals dive to the stone-yards at ~30m by carrying rocks in net bags, then drop them to rise. Never drop a stone randomly - place it on designated drop terraces. A falling rock through the levels will tear leaf-roofs.
5.  **Air etiquette is life and death.** Never approach a family's breathing bell without being invited. Never breathe from it without the slow ritual: they will gesture for you to duck your head up into the trembling silver dome, take 3-4 calm breaths, taste the green iron, and duck back down. Do not take more. Do not touch the bell wall. Do not exhale bubbles up into it - exhale sideways, slowly, away from it. If you brought a rebreather, you politely demonstrate you don't need theirs. They will love you for it.

**Communication:** Learn 5 rope signals before you go. A long pull = stop/wait. Two short tugs = come. Three = danger/vent opening. Touching foreheads gently in water = thanks. Humming very close, mouth almost to ear, is how you speak. No shouting.

### 4. Dangers That Will Actually Kill You

This place feels womb-like and safe because it's warm and green. It isn't.

*   **Exhaustion & CO2:** The biggest killer of visitors. Warm water + overexertion + poor buoyancy = CO2 buildup you don't notice until you black out. Your rebreather alarms are useless if your mind is slowed by heat. Set a vibrating timer every 20 minutes: check PPO2, check buoyancy, drink, *hold still*.
*   **Vents and new rock:** Anchor deep, but not *too* deep. New basalt cones can vent 50°C, highly alkaline, sulfurous water without warning. White and hot cracking seen in the distance means somewhere new is rising. If you feel a sudden warm updraft, see lace-like fresh tufa precipitating like snow, or hear a louder ticking/cracking, move laterally along contour lines, not up or down. Do not touch fresh basalt. It will be glass-sharp and can still be chemically burning hot even underwater.
*   **Entanglement:** With fauna 1.2/rare, nothing will bite you. The forest will. Those veils of green hair algae re-anchor instantly. Filamentous kelp will wrap your gauge hoses while you hover. Carry two blunt-tipped, locally-made tufa knives (buy them there, don't bring steel if you can avoid it) and know how to cut *slowly*. Panic-yanking will just tighten the wrap.
*   **The Edge:** You cannot see the fall until you are in it. The water gets subtly clearer, faster, colder. The kelp forest thins. The light gets slightly brighter with no canopy. If you notice a gentle, endless outward pull and no more mooring ropes, you are too far. Swim/drift inward immediately. Do not try to look over the edge.
*   **Infection:** Every cut in warm, iron-rich, life-thick broth will infect. Disinfect immediately out of water. Better yet, don't get cut. Gloves always on.

### 5. Packing List for the Ordinary Human

**On your body on arrival:**
- CCR rebreather, full-face helmet with comms mic (close-range hum mic, not radio - radio is useless)
- Soft ballast harness + 6kg extra mesh bag for local stones
- 1mm full dive skin + gloves + hood
- Dim amber headlamp + backup glow stick (amber/red)
- Two blunt tufa/obsidian knives
- Hydration bladder with fresh water + electrolyte salts

**In a tethered dry-bag:**
- Spare scrubber, O2 sensors, seal kit
- Petroleum jelly, ear drops (alcohol/vinegar mix), broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment, waterproof bandages
- Collapsible mesh basket for gifting/trading (locals value cordage, cured leaf patches, not coins)
- A small, living offering: a cutting of fast-growing freshwater plant from your home world, presented to a weaver. This is worth more than money.

**Do NOT bring:**
- Metal tanks that clang (acoustic vandalism)
- Bright strobes, lasers
- Fins, scooters, or anything that creates current
- Sonar, fish-finders (there are no fish, and you'll just scream into the quiet)
- Expectations of speed, privacy, or horizon

### 6. Coming Home

You will leave by letting go. Most insertion points retrieve you by desaturation float - you will be pulled slowly upward along a fixed rope until the water thins, lightens, and then suddenly you are cold and dry and breathing thin mountain air at 3km with no water to hold you.

Expect vertigo. Expect grief. After days of slow, green, humming, womb-warm suspension - where everyone moved slowly and shared air - the old world will feel too loud, too fast, too dry, and too empty.

Be still for a day after return. Drink broth, not water. Let your ears and eyes relearn air. Don't tell people "it was beautiful." Tell them "it was slow, and I had to learn to be still enough not to break it."

And if someone gestures for you to put your head up into a trembling silver bell of air - say yes. Breathe slowly. Taste the iron. That is someone giving you their week.
