High above where a mountain ought to be cold, there is a summer that never breaks.

You feel it first in your lungs — the air is thin on paper but it doesn't act thin. It is hot, wet, heavy, pressing on the skin like a greenhouse left sealed at noon. 3,500, maybe 4,000 meters up, where snow should be permanent, the atmosphere hangs instead in a slow, condensing haze. It's not quite fog and not quite rain; the air itself sweats. Water beads on every surface that hasn't been warmed quite enough - on stone, on leaves, on your forearms - and gathers until it falls.

This is the Mesa.

Not a peak, but a country cut off from the world below. A lifted plateau, a broken table of ancient basalt and sandstone ten, fifteen kilometers across, its edges falling away in sheer, weathered cliffs that drop straight into a sea of low clouds. From a distance you would think it an island, but there is no ocean. Only the cloud sea, white and level far below, making the fall absolute. Up here, the world is open and flat in a way high places rarely are - you can see weather coming from a valley away. A regional, town-and-wilderness scale, big enough to get lost in, small enough that every cliff defines it.

It shouldn't be hot. It shouldn't be wet. But the Mesa holds its own climate.

### The Weeping Stone

The rock here is old. Erosion reads as 1.6 - not raw, not crumbling, but *established*. This place has been like this for a very long time, long enough to find its shape and keep it.

The basalt has weathered into broad, stepped terraces, rounded shoulders, deep bowls. Water has been working it lazily for millennia, not carving canyons in violent floods but seeping, dissolving, polishing. Every horizontal surface holds water. Shallow tarns the size of houses sit in stone cups with no clear inlet or outlet. Rills braid down every gentle slope in sheets ankle-deep and clear as glass. The cliff edges weep constantly, not from rivers but from the stone itself - the plateau exhales groundwater in dark stains that become curtains of drips, then threads, then a fine, endless mist that falls down the outer walls and never reaches the bottom.

You hear it everywhere. A 1.5/3 loud. Not a roar, but a thickness of sound: water ticking on broad leaves, trickling over stone lips, plinking into pools, the low thrum of insects layered over it, the sudden crack and whistle of birds. Because the air is still, the sounds carry across the open flat for a mile.

Where the stone is flat, it is rarely bare. It furs over within seasons if exposed. Black lichen, then emerald crust-moss, then mats that hold soil, then soil that holds everything else.

### The Lush That Should Not Be

Vegetation is 2.2 - lush to the point of obstruction, but not jungle chaos. This is a high, hot, wet garden that grows *dense*. 1.5 dense - many presences per volume, near-touching, layered.

Canopy trees exist but they are short and wide, wind-trained into umbrella crowns ten meters up. Under them is not shadow but green light, filtered normal-day bright. The real forest is vertical: bromeliads the size of bathtubs clamped to limbs, each holding its own pond; vines thick as your thigh that hang straight down and root again where they touch; ferns that grow not from the ground but in floating drifts between trunks, their root-balls wrapped in perpetual mist.

Everything is epiphyte on epiphyte. Flowers grow on leaves. Leaves grow on other leaves. The ground, where you can find it, is a sponge of peat and living roots half a meter thick that bounces when you walk and squelches water with every step. The air just above it is busy - butterflies the size of your hand moving slow in the heat, dragonflies with glass wings as long as knives, beetles varnished black-green humming like bad wiring.

Fauna is 2.1 - abundant, and unafraid. This plateau has been isolated so long that nothing here knows to run. Small deer-like things with damp, moss-colored pelts pick through fern seas. Capybara-big rodents wallow in the tarns and watch you. Canopy above rustles constantly with monkeys that are not monkeys - long-limbed, hairless except for algae-green down on their backs, whistling to each other in chords. And birds. Flocks that rise like thrown cloth, so many and so loud they drown the water for a moment.

Nothing is in a hurry. Dynamic 0.7 - slow. Plants grow fast in the heat and wet, but cycles turn patiently. A fallen tree becomes a hill of flowers over twenty years and stays that hill for a hundred. Water levels rise and fall across seasons, not hours. Even the animals move with a deliberation, as if conserving something.

### The Quiet Machines

Built 0.8 / Tech 2.7. This is the strange heart of it, the 2.1 weirdness that makes the impossible climate hold.

There are almost no buildings. What there is is scattered, half-swallowed.

A pylon stands in a tarn, thirty meters tall, leaning two degrees, its surface not metal and not stone but something matte that sheds water without ever being wet. No seams. The forest has grown up and through it - a tree has germinated in a socket halfway up and now crowns it with leaves. It hums, very low, felt more in the sternum than the ear. Around its base, the water is warm and never stagnates, and lilies bloom twice their normal size with faint light pulsing under their pads.

A bridge spans a shallow gorge with no road on either end - a single arch of pale material that flexes almost imperceptibly under your weight, like cartilage. The rain that hits it turns instantly to vapor without sizzling.

Lines in the ground - straight, geometric, now softened by erosion but still too perfect - that from above you realize are the faint outline of a grid kilometers wide. Drainage? Foundation? The plateau's flatness may not be entirely natural.

These are not ruins in the human sense. They are not dead. They are still working, but slowly, patiently, with no need for operators. They hold the air thick and hot this high up. They pull water up from cloud level and let it weep out through stone. They keep the Mesa in its own state, weathered but in balance. Current physics can't fully describe what they do; you can only see the results - that normal light falls warm and gold through a humid haze that should be freezing, that sound carries too far, that gravity feels just slightly kinder at the cliff edges, as if something is still holding the rim from crumbling.

No one lives here now to claim them. Perhaps no one ever did in the way we mean living. At the edge of one tarn you find a low wall, waist-high, made of the same seamless material, now hosting a complete garden on its top - moss, orchids, ants. Inside the shallow hollow it once enclosed, rainwater has become a perfect circle of sky.

You sit there. The heat presses. Water beads on your skin and falls. Somewhere far across the open Mesa, one of the pylons exhales a slow, almost-inaudible change in pitch, and a fresh bank of condensing atmosphere begins to form over the western terraces, drifting inward at walking speed. The birds go quiet for a breath, then louder. The forest drips.

It has been doing this for a very long time, and will be doing it long after you leave.
