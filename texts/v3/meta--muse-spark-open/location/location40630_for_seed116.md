There is a mountain, but you will never see it from the outside.

Not really — not the way you'd expect. From a distance, it is almost nothing. The oldest kind of mountain, at the end of its life. It was once jagged, once tectonic and violent, and then time simply sat on it. Ten million years of rain and wind and ice leaned on its shoulders until its peaks gave up. Now it is a broad, bald dome against the sky, extreme in a different way — not sharp, but *high*. Impossibly high. A whale-back of pale stone lifted into the warm upper air where clouds have to part around it. The summit plateau is near-flat, scoured smooth as a river stone the size of a county. Nothing grows there but lichen-crust and wind. The air is thin and bright and terribly quiet.

And then, near what would have been the center of the old caldera, there is a seam. A perfect circle, thirty feet across, where the rock stops and tile begins.

That's the place.

### The Cistern

It is not a lake. It is not a cave. It is a room that someone built inside the sky.

The Old Crown Reservoir — that's what the maintenance stencils call it, in letters six feet tall and rust-bled down the inner wall — was poured, bolted, riveted and sealed entirely out of made things. There is no natural substrate here. The floor is not rock but interlocking ceramic hexes, each the size of a tabletop, glazed a faded institutional green and worn at the edges until they are soft and dished like molars. The walls are riveted boiler-plate and poured concrete, curved inward to form a perfect cylinder, perhaps forty feet across and sixty feet deep, capped at the top with a domed lid of glass and steel girder. And it is full.

Totally, deeply, actively full of water.

When you dog the hatch and drop down the service ladder, you don't enter a room *with* water in it. You enter *into* water. The reservoir breathes. It was built to catch monsoon and melt and atmospheric condensation at this impossible height and hold it for the cities far below in the foothills — a mountain-top canteen. The inlet pipes still work. Far above, three storm mouths the size of doorways gulp the warm rain when it comes, and the outlet at the bottom still sighs and throttles and sends a measured column down through the heart of the mountain in a black pipe you can hear thrumming. It's industrial — not sleek, but heavy, honest industry: big wheels, chain drives, brass pressure gauges gone verdigris-green, an electric pump house in its own little cage on a gantry that has been chattering to itself for centuries without anyone to oil it. Someone left the system on.

So the water never stills. It is warm — body-warm, bath-warm, 27 or 28 degrees Celsius held by captured sun through the dome and by the sheer thermal mass of all that liquid stone-high in the sky — and it moves in slow, visible thermals. Cold rain falls through the dome vents and sinks in smoky plumes. Warm water rises from the dark bottom. The whole cylinder turns over like a soup pot on low simmer.

Light comes down normally, impossibly normally, through the dome. Normal daylight at 4,000 meters is fierce and clean, and here it is filtered first through glass thick as a thumb, then through two atmospheres of suspended green. Shafts stripe the water, full of particulate, and they swing as clouds pass overhead. You can tell time by how blue or gold the suspended motes become.

Everything in here is ancient in the built sense. The erosion parameter is not about rock — it's about made things that have lived so long they've become geology. The concrete has pitted. The rivets have swelled and wept rust stalactites. The ceramic tiles on the windward side have been scoured paper-thin by a slow gyre that has spun for a thousand wet seasons. Handrails have been worn to a bright polish and then worn past polish back to roughness by countless hands, and then by no hands at all, just water. Corners that were square are now round. Edges that were sharp are now lips. The whole industrial interior has settled into its final form.

### A Hanging Lushness

And because it is warm and lit and left alone, it is choked with life in the sparsest possible way.

The rule here is: nothing touches natural ground, because there isn't any. So life learned to grow on the made.

From the underside of the gantry and the pump-house catwalk and the lower rungs of every ladder, vast curtains of vegetation hang down *into* the water instead of up toward the sun. They are not quite algae, not quite fern, not quite mangrove. Thick, rubbery fronds, dark emerald and rust-red, anchored by root-mats that have chemically welded themselves to painted steel. Some send pneumatophores straight up to gulp air. Others flower underwater, opening waxy white bells that fill the water with pollen that drifts like snow. Lianas of a kind of industrial ivy — leaves like overlapping solar panels — have found the hairline cracks between concrete pours and pried them wider, drinking condensation.

It's lush, obscenely lush for such a high place, but sparse. That's the trick of the scale. For all its depth, this is an intimate room. A pocket-ecosystem. A terrarium the size of a chapel. You can see every inhabitant at once if the water clears. There is only one curtain-forest on the north wall, one mat of floating heart-leaves near the inflow, one colony of luminous shelf-fungus blooming on the pump housing. Much space between presences. Long stretches of empty, clean, humming water where motes spiral alone.

Which makes the fauna feel abundant even though there are only dozens, not thousands.

They came with the rain or the pipes or on bird feet. Translucent shrimp the length of your hand graze the tile-floor like cattle, clicking. Small, blind white fish with whiskers school in perfect toruses around the central outflow current, never bumping the wall. Flat, hand-sized newts with external gills like red ferns cling to the vertical boiler-plate and pulse. Above the waterline — because there is still an eighteen-inch lens of trapped humid air under the dome, warm as a mouth — dragonflies the size of swifts hawk and rattle their wings against the glass with a sound like cellophane. Swallows that nest on the outer mountain know the vent grates and dart straight down through the rain shafts to snatch dragonflies mid-dive, piercing the surface like spears and emerging soaked and screaming.

Everything is loud. The pump never stops its low, gut-shaking industrial thud-thud-thud. Water jets hiss where the inlets calibrate. The metal dome pops and ticks in the sun. And underneath it all, biology: the shrimp ticking on tile, the frogs — fat, moss-backed frogs that live on the floating leaves and sing with a deep, amplified *bonk* that rings in the cylinder like a bell — the whine of insects, the splash of a swallow, the groan of stressed steel.

The weirdness is quiet but insistent, as warm water often is. Gravity feels slightly wrong in here. Because the water is so warm and so still at its edges, surface tension becomes architecture. Beetles walk not just on the top surface under the dome but on vertical meniscuses that cling to the walls. Pollen balls roll uphill along invisible current seams. If you hold perfectly still on the gantry with your legs dangling in, tiny fish will come and clean the dead skin from your ankles, unafraid, as if you were just another piece of industrial structure left here to soften and be colonized. And sometimes — when the outlet valve cycles — the pressure in the whole room drops a fraction and every plant pearl-strings with oxygen bubbles at once, and the entire submerged forest exhales silver.

You are not supposed to be able to stay long. Your lungs still want that thin summit air that leaks in through the vents, brackish with rust and chlorophyll. The warm water wants to make you sleepy. The pump wants to keep working whether you're here or not.

If the dome ever cracked, it would all fall at once — sixty feet of warm, living water dropping straight through the ancient, flattened mountain like a poured thought.

But it hasn't cracked yet. It has been here long enough to learn how to hold itself together.
