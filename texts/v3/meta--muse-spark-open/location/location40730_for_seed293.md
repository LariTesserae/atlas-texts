You are not looking at a forest that has a city in it. You are looking at a city that decided to become a forest and then kept going until there was no distinction at all.

This is the Basin. Or one of them — there are others like it scattered across the low country, but never close enough to see each other.

The land here is the oldest kind of flat. Not prairie flat, not drained flat. Ancient-flat. The continent finished arguing with itself a long time ago and settled, sank, and smoothed. If you walk for a day in any direction you will not gain twenty meters of elevation. The bedrock — a soft, pitted limestone the color of old bone — lies right under the skin of the world, worn down by so many wet seasons that it has no edges left. Everything is bowl and saucer, shallow cup and gentle lip. Water has nowhere to go, so it stays.

It stays on the surface, it stays in the air, it stays in the rock itself.

The basin you are standing in is maybe two hundred meters across, rim to rim. A perfect, shallow dish eroded into the limestone, its lip barely higher than the surrounding plain. From above it would look like a thumbprint. From inside, it feels like a room. The sky makes a lid. The far rim is always visible, a low green wreath cutting the horizon.

And the whole dish is full.

Not full of trees. Full *with* tree, singular. One living mass that has taken the basin as its pot.

It is difficult to know where the plant starts. The roots are the floor. They braid and mat across the entire depression in a dense, springy peat that weeps if you step hard. Broad buttress leaves — each one the size of a door, waxy, dark jade — angle up to catch light and gutter rain along their central veins into waiting cisterns. From those cisterns rise trunks, but the word is wrong. They are columns, buttresses, living walls. A dozen massive growths, each as thick as a house, rise from the mat and then flare and join overhead in a vaulted canopy that never quite closes. It is architecture that grew itself after being told what shape to want.

Because this was built.

Long before this plant-mass was this big, someone seeded it with instructions. Not cut, not planted — programmed. In the sap you can still see it working: faint lines of amber light crawling up the fibers like tracer dyes, like data. The lattice is nanoscale, then suddenly bigger than biology should allow. Filaments of engineered cellulose lace the air between branches, humming faintly, condensing water straight out of the warm air. Petals open with a soft hydraulic sigh and display not pollen but fine, dark screens of spore-pixels that rewrite themselves when you look away. The building is still building. A stairwell of living wood spirals up the central column, its handrail sweating cool water, its steps adjusting their height for your stride as you approach.

This is a town. It just happens to be made of one thing.

Homes are not built *in* the plant. They are grown as swellings on its sides — smooth, hollow gourds the size of rooms, with translucent membranes for walls that you can tune opaque with a touch. Inside one, you see the grain of the interior lit from within. Someone has left their laundry hanging on a tendril that is slowly, thoughtfully drying it with warm air. Walkways are bridges of interwoven vine, as wide as streets, with a slight give. On one, an old woman has set up a stall and is selling cuttings that chatter. The settlement is sparse by design. For all its density of life, it is not crowded. There are maybe thirty or forty inhabitants in this entire basin-room, and the plant leaves great, empty halls between them — resonant chambers of root-floor and leaf-ceiling where nothing is stored and no one lives, where the air just hangs, humid and buzzing, and the sound of dripping carries forever.

Because it is always dripping.

Water is everywhere, but never in a river, never in a lake. The limestone sweats it. The leaves distill it. The air itself, warm — a late-summer warm that never breaks — holds so much moisture that breath is visible for a moment after you exhale, even in the heat. It condenses on every cool surface. It beads on the undersides of the great leaves and falls in a constant, irregular pitter, plip, splash onto the root-mat below, where it gathers in amber-tinted pools that reflect the canopy. You can hear the basin breathing: water moving up through xylem with a faint, high hiss, then falling again. Somewhere a large leaf has cupped too much and tips all at once with a rush and a slap. Somewhere a pipe of living wood is flushing itself clean. The stones of the rim itself weep, dark streaks running down the limestone even days after rain.

Nothing rots here. Or rather, nothing is *allowed* to stay rotted for long. The turbulence is constant.

This place is loud and restless and alive in a way that overwhelms stillness. The fauna does not hide. It *is* the weather.

Beetles the size of your thumb, iridescent green-black, drill through the air in straight lines and strike the membranes with a soft *thunk*, then right themselves and keep going. Flocks of needle-birds — not birds, but too quick and too feathered to be insects — move as a single sheet from one side of the vault to the other, turning on a thought, their wings making a dry-paper rustle that rises to a roar and then cuts out entirely. In the pools, things croak and click and bellow: throat-sacs inflating, joints popping. Vine-monkeys, pale and long-limbed, swing through the middle stories chattering and rewiring filament bundles as they go, tending the city-plant the way electricians tend a grid, the way gardeners tend a vine. Everything is in use. Everything is growing, being pruned, being rebuilt.

And sometimes, quietly, weirdly, the rules slip.

You will see a patch of moss on a column produce a perfect, slowly rotating cube of water that does not fall. You will watch a seed drift upward instead of down, hesitate, then remember gravity and drop. The tech in the plant is not just engineering — it is persuasion. It asks physics for favors, and physics, warm and wet and old and tired here at the end of the landscape's life, often says yes. Light lingers too long in the hollows. A hallway will be longer coming back than going. The plant hums, and the hum resolves for half a second into a chord that sounds exactly like a name you have not heard since childhood.

No one finds this alarming. When you live in a house that listens, you learn to live with strange acoustics.

At noon — which is a soft, even, normal light here, filtered through a hundred layers of leaf and further softened by mist — the whole basin sighs. The great leaves angle together, sluicing stored rainwater down their backs in a sudden, warm flash-flood that lasts twenty seconds and drenches everything. The inhabitants tilt their faces up to it. The root-mat drinks, swells, exhales perfume. The pools overflow into each other and become, for a minute, one shallow, trembling mirror.

Then it passes. The dripping resumes. A child runs laughing across a vine-street that tightens under her feet so she will not fall. A monkey-thing scolds from above. A wall of the city-plant shivers and extrudes a new bud the size of a fist, already veined with light, already hollow, already waiting for someone to move in.
