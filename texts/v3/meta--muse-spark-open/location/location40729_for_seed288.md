It is a basin, not a valley. A valley implies it was cut to lead somewhere. This place leads nowhere; it is where the world settled.

You are below the surface. Not in a cave — there is still sky above you, a long, uneven oval of it, pale and distant — but the ground fell away here a long time ago and never came back up. A sink the size of a small country, so wide you can’t hold its edges in one glance, so shallow in its slope that you don’t feel you are descending until you realize the horizon has become a rim. The walls are low and impossibly old, chewed by wind until they are no more than long, exhausted dunes of stone, their strata exposed like the rings of a very old tree, then smoothed until the strata blur. There are no sharp edges left anywhere. Every boulder is an egg. Every cliff is a slump. The erosion here is finished. Nothing is falling anymore. The land has achieved its final shape and is simply waiting.

The air is cool all the time. A high-desert cool, a cellar cool. It has the thin, clear chill of deep shade even at noon, because the light never fully arrives. The sun has to cross the narrow mouth of the sky to look down here, and by the time it does it is weak and slanting. Most of the day is a long blue dusk. The basin floor is in a permanent late-afternoon, dim enough that colors flatten and distance becomes unreliable. Mist doesn’t gather here — there is no water for mist — but dust hangs always, a fine mineral haze that softens everything further.

And there is no water. Not in any way you would recognize.

No river, no lake, no spring, no seep. The ground is dry salt and dry silt and dry stone powdered down to flour. If you kneel and crush it, it leaves no damp on your fingers, only a cold alkaline sting. In the lowest part of the basin the salts have dried into a perfect, unbroken white plain, cracked into polygons as regular as tiles, each one cupped slightly upward like a dry palm. The wind moves salt crystals across it in thin, hissing sheets. You could walk for a day and hear only your boots grinding on halite.

This should be a desert. In any honest world, this should be bare.

Instead it is lush.

Not green — the dimness and the dust leach the color — but dense and soft and overwhelming. From the rim it looks like a low cloud has fallen and caught on the basin floor. Up close, you see it is a forest without trunks.

The vegetation here has divorced itself from water. It does not root for it because there is none to find. It drinks the air.

They are tillandsias and lichens and mosses grown monstrous and patient. Great bearded curtains of gray-green air-moss hang from nothing, anchored to the wind itself, or to the faintest irregularity in the stone. Pillars of staghorn felt, as tall as a man and as wide as a house, rise from the salt pan without roots, their surfaces furred and porous, condensing the cool night’s trace humidity directly onto their skin. On the ancient slopes, carpets of liverwort as thick as mattresses roll for miles, springy underfoot, exhaling a damp, earthy breath that belies the aridity all around them. Everything is epiphytic on everything else. Leaves grow upon leaves. The dominant plants are not trees but colonies — vast, slow-accumulating mounds of leaf and filament and spore-body that have been fixing dust and dew for ten thousand years without moving an inch. You can push your hand into one and feel how cool and almost-moist it is inside, like the inside of a lung.

They are spaced strangely. The density of the basin is sparse, in the way a cathedral is sparse. There is a great deal between things. Between one moss-pillar and the next there may be a hundred yards of bare, polished salt. Between one liverwort field and the next, a stretch of clean stone where nothing has yet decided to grow. The emptiness is part of the architecture. You are never crowded by the green; you are presented with it.

And then you realize the green is not the city. The city is something else.

At first you think the pillars *are* buildings. Then you see the buildings behind the pillars.

They rise from the salt — towers and domes and ridges and terraces, all the color of the basin itself, all made of the basin itself, but with intention. A settlement the size of a province, low and spread and complex, following no grid but with unmistakable streets and avenues and plazas left empty between. No metal glints anywhere. No glass. No cut stone either, not really. The edges are too rounded, too seamless, too… grown.

This place was built without tools. It is urban without technology. It is a hive.

The builders are still here. You hear them before you see them.

The sound is deafening.

It is not a roar. A roar would imply violence, a peak. This is a plane of sound, a solid, a weather. The basin is filled from rim to rim with the stridulation of millions of throats and wings and legs. Crickets the length of your forearm, black crickets with backs like polished basalt, sit on every exposed salt tile and saw. Cicadas with translucent wings as wide as your chest hang in shrouds from the air-plants and click in a single, phased chorus that makes your teeth ache. Beneath that, a deeper thrum: beetles rolling salt balls larger than boulders, their chitin humming against the ground. The sound is so loud it ceases to be sound and becomes pressure. It vibrates the moss. It blurs your vision if you stand still. You have to open your mouth to equalize.

The fauna is abundant beyond any census. Everything moves slowly, as everything here does, but there is so much of it that slowness doesn’t matter. Pale moths as big as blankets drift between the pillars like ash, never flapping quickly. Blind white lizards with frilled necks lap salt without needing to drink. Things like armadillos, but soft-backed and covered in symbiotic moss, bulldoze lanes through the liverwort at a pace you could set a watch to — if you had a watch, which you don’t, which nothing here does. And the architects themselves: low, broad, ant-like but the size of dogs or ponies, the color of dust, moving in endless, unhurried processions along the salt roads, each carrying a pellet of chewed salt and spore and excrement in its mandibles, adding it to the wall of a dome that will be finished in three hundred years. No queen is visible. No central plan. The city simply accretes, as the moss accretes, grain by patient grain.

There are no ruins. Though the land is ancient, nothing here is ruined. Things are either growing or being maintained. A dome that collapses — and they do, very slowly, under their own weight — is immediately softened by liverwort and then re-assimilated, its salts carried off again. Change does happen: a moss-field will, over a decade, creep a foot closer to another. A tower will gain an inch. The song will shift its pitch as one generation of crickets dies and the next, tuned ever so slightly differently, takes over. But you have to stay a long time to notice. The dynamic here is the dynamic of breathing out.

The rules of the place are simple, once you feel them:

1. Water is not allowed to pool. Life must take it from the air or not at all, and so only patient surfaces survive.
2. Nothing is wasted, but nothing is hurried. Salt is eaten and excreted as architecture.
3. Sound is substance. The animals do not call to each other across the basin; they fill the basin with a single shared note so that no one is ever alone, and the plants lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the vibration.

At night — which is not much darker than day, just a deeper blue — the towers give off a faint warmth, the metabolic heat of millions of bodies inside them, and the air-mosses glow faintly at their tips where they release spores like sparks, and the chorus never stops. You lie in a hollow of liverwort that is cool and dry and soft as any bed, and the ground hums under your back, and the salt plain reflects the dim starlight like a dead sea, and you understand that you are in a city that has been growing itself for longer than any human history could measure, in a bowl where no rain has ever fallen, tended by things that have no hands.
