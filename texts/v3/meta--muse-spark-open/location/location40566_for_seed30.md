This is not a mountain yet.

It is trying to become one.

You have come up high — high enough that the air thins and cools, but it doesn't. It stays warm here, a low, bathwater warmth that clings to the skin, even as your ears pop with altitude. The plateau breathes out heat like a living body. Below you, clouds lie flat and white as a sea; above you, there is no summit, only more sky the color of old pewter. Dim light filters down through a veil that never quite becomes rain and never quite clears.

This whole place is small enough to hold in your head. You could walk its rim in an hour.

And it is still being made.

### The Unfinished Stone

The ground under your boots is wrong for how old mountains should feel. There is no sand, no soil, no talus slope of broken ages. The rock here is embryonic — raw geology, still soft at the edges.

It came up all at once, and hasn't decided what it is.

In the center of the hollow — a shallow bowl maybe three hundred meters across, like a palm cupped to the sky — a blister of basalt is still cooling. Not from volcanism; from *assembly*. Black glass ridges rise in sharp, clean folds, as if the earth was pinched together by great fingers. They are too perfect, too unweathered. No wind has had time to blunt them. Water beads on them and hisses faintly where the stone is still too hot inside, though the surface is cool to the touch.

There are no riverbeds because no river has existed long enough to carve one. Instead, water simply *appears*. It seeps straight out of the air, condensing on every vertical face, weeping from the weeping rocks themselves. It gathers in sheets on the glassy stone and runs in braids, finding no channel, splitting and rejoining over bare rock, making a lacework of shallow, fast, clear water over black.

Erosion hasn't started here. You are standing before erosion begins. Every trickle is the first trickle to ever take that path.

The air tastes of iron and ozone and hot mineral water, like the moment after lightning hits a lake.

### Light Without Source

The light is dim, though it should be searing at this height. The sun is up there somewhere, but you can't see it. The sky is not cloudy so much as *thick* — a low, luminous haze that hangs twenty meters overhead, perfectly even, perfectly still. It glows faintly amber-gray, like light through paper lanterns. It gives no shadows.

And yet the water glows a little from within. And the structures do.

Scattered around the bowl's lip are five — maybe six — buildings. Or what were buildings. Or what will be.

They are small, hamlet-scale, each no larger than a chapel or a greenhouse, set far apart from each other with much empty space between. They are not ruined. They are incomplete, or interrupted. One is a frame of matte white ribs, open to the air, humming. One is a low disc half-sunk into the rising basalt, as if the stone grew up around it while it slept. One is just a doorway standing alone on bare rock, opening onto nothing, its threshold dry while rain that isn't rain condenses everywhere else.

They are not made for weather or for gravity quite. Their surfaces repel water; droplets hover a millimeter off them before sliding away. Their angles seem to correct themselves when you aren't looking directly at them.

No roads connect them. No footprints lead between them, except yours, which fill with water as soon as you lift your boot. Faint marks in the stone — scorch lines, geometric etchings the size of kitchen tables that abruptly stop mid-line — suggest someone intended a much larger complex here, then left the tools running.

Because the tech did not leave.

### The Sound of Abundance

It is loud here. Too loud for so little life, at first.

Then you learn to hear it.

The sparse vegetation — and it is sparse — consists of only three or four kinds of pioneer that have managed to grip the raw rock. Glasswort-like red stalks that root directly in flowing water, with no soil at all. Tight cushions of black-green moss that swell and sigh as they soak. A single species of stunted tree, no taller than your knee, with waxy leaves turned edge-on to catch condensation, spaced so far apart you could count them all without losing track.

None of them should support what lives here.

But the air *teems*.

Everything flies, or leaps. Things like dragonflies the length of your forearm, wings of smoked glass humming so fast they blur into halos, patrolling the water braids. Things like translucent shrimp that launch themselves from pool to pool with a sharp *tick*. Clouds of needle-thin white moths that live for an hour and die in the water, becoming food the next minute.

On the warm stone, colonies of amber beetles run in glittering rivers, farming the moss, herded by larger, mantis-shaped predators that stand perfectly still until they strike — *snap* — loud as a twig breaking.

In the shallowest sheets of water, tadpole-things with too many eyes dart and swirl, so numerous the water seems to vibrate.

And higher, in the thick light itself, something large moves slowly, unseen. You hear the displacement — a low thrumming sigh as it passes overhead, condensing a sudden cold rain for ten seconds that patters violently on your hood, on the rock, on the humming ribs of the unfinished building, then stops. Something glances with a wet, membranous sound against the lone doorway and is gone.

There are no birds. Nothing sings. Everything clicks, thrums, whirs, splashes, and drips. The whole bowl is a percussion of water on stone and chitin on chitin. Active, restless, predator-prey, birth-death-birth happening at triple speed because the world here is warm and wet and new and has no equilibrium yet.

### The Rules Here

This place does not obey erosion, so it obeys something else.

If you stand still long enough beside one of the white-ribbed frames, you notice the water near it doesn't flow downhill. It flows *toward* it, uphill over a slight rise, in a perfect convex arc, feeding a trench that was never cut. The stone under that building is growing faster — a visible creep, a millimeter an hour, the basalt folding itself into terraces that were not there this morning.

The haze overhead is not weather. It is exhaust, or exhalation, or a construction field. It holds heat in and light out. It makes water from nothing.

The animals avoid the doorway. Even the dragonfly-things veer around it, though there is nothing there. If you step close, the air on the other side is colder by ten degrees and smells of deep ocean, though you stand kilometers above any ocean.

Someone — or something — started a mountain here. Not by pushing tectonic plates, but by seeding it. By placing anchors that tell matter how to behave, and then letting physics do what it wouldn't normally do. The project was left on. The anchors are still talking to the rock, to the water, to the sky.

The mountain is listening. The life came to listen too, opportunistic and abundant, finding a warm, wet, predator-rich cradle with no winter and no history.

You crouch by a braid of newborn water, cup warm stone that hasn't learned to be cold, and watch a swarm of glass-winged hunters drink from the condensation on your sleeve. Behind you, the rock sighs and shifts, a fraction louder than the insects, as another fold rises, embryonic and steaming, making more world for them to fill.
