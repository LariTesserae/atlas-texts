The mountain was made before it could be a mountain.

You are high up — not at a peak, but on a high place that was leveled to be high. A table cut out of the sky. The air is thin and cool enough that your breath is visible even in the brightness, even at midday, even though the sun is hard and white overhead and lays itself flat across everything without shadow to hide in. The light is not warm. It is clear, high-altitude, post-storm light, the kind that makes water shine like a blade.

There is no soil here. There never was, or if there was it was scraped off and set aside generations ago.

Everything is built.

In every direction to the horizon — which is startlingly close and startlingly far, the way it is on a height — the ground is not ground. It is floor. It is roof. It is cistern and channel and stair and wall. The entire valley, if you can call it a valley, is a construction: a nest of basins packed together until there is no wasted space.

From above it would look like honeycomb, or like the cracked mud of a dry lake inverted in stone — thousands of interlocking cells. From inside it, standing in it, it is a labyrinth with no walls higher than your chest. Low, thick walls of unmortared stone, dark basalt and paler tuff, sharp-edged and freshly split. The erosion here is young; the quarry faces are still crisp. The corners have not been rounded by wind. The stone still shows the feather-marks where it was wedged and broken by hand, by fire and cold water, by wooden mauls and stone hammers. Primitive work, but thorough. Total. Every available surface has been shaped. Every slope has been stepped into a terrace. Every terrace is a shallow pan.

And every pan holds water.

This is a wet place at a dry height. Water should not be here, not like this, but it is. It weeps. It falls sideways. The wind that comes over the level edge of this built plateau is heavy with condensation — it hits the cool stone and beads instantly, runs down every vertical face in bright trickles. The walls sweat. The floors sweat. In the early morning the whole complex is veiled in a low fog that is not a cloud come down but water being born out of the air itself between the stones, collecting.

There are no springs being tapped. There are no rivers diverted. The builders learned to make the place itself catch the sky. The bright, cool air is wrung like a cloth.

You hear it before you understand what you are seeing: a faint, endless ticking and trickling. Not a river's noise. A thousand small noises refusing to make a loud one. Drip from a lip of rock into a pool. The thin sheet-flow over a weir no wider than your hand. The soft *plok* of condensation falling from an overhang. The stone here is engineered not to shed water but to slow it, to hold it. Each cell — each shallow terraced basin, some ten paces across, some no larger than a sleeping mat — is slightly dished. Each has a rim notched at one corner, a thumb-wide spillway that feeds the next lower cell. The pattern is dense, saturated, almost textile. There is no room between presences: wall becomes channel becomes basin becomes wall, without interval, a continuous packed instrument for holding and moving water by inches.

The water is clear and dark. It shows the stone bottom. It is cold enough to ache. In some basins it is only a skin, a millimeter bright on the dark rock, trembling when the wind touches it. In others it is knee-deep, thigh-deep, still. Every basin is full to its notch, or almost full, or just beginning to fill again after draining. The light hits it and doubles.

Nothing grows here.

That is the first unnatural thing you register after the wet, because wet at this scale should mean green. It does not. No algae films the pools. No lichen softens the walls. No moss in the notches. No reeds. The stone is bare, mineral-clean, scrubbed by hands as well as by water. On the windward edge where the fog is thickest, you can see faint rust-red stains where iron bacteria might want to live, but they don't. Or they are not allowed to. Some of the basin floors have been swept with stiff brooms; you can see the arc-marks. Some have been scoured with sand until they are pale. The water itself seems empty — no wriggling larvae, no midges, no birds come to drink. The air holds no insects, no calls. Animal life has not been invited, or has not found a way up, or left.

This is a mineral world kept in that state on purpose.

And yet it is not dead. The rules here are simple and maintained by slow, patient labor:

1. Stone catches water.
2. Water does not stay.
3. Nothing stops the water.

You watch a basin near your feet reach its notch. The surface tension bulges, holds for a long second, then breaks. A thread of water slips into the cut and runs — silently, almost too slowly to see — down a shallow groove cut in the top of the wall, to the next basin. That basin, already full, shivers and spills in turn. A chain reaction moves outward, not fast, not dramatic. A slow dynamic; the whole plateau breathes water in pulses that take hours to cross from the high edge to the low edge, where at last the water gathers in deeper, chest-high reservoirs as large as houses, dark and still and open to the bright sky.

There are tools left behind for this breathing. Not machines. Levers of wood, bleached and swollen. Flat stones for damming a notch. Hoes with basalt heads for scraping silt — though there is almost no silt; the young rock has not yet made much. Long-handled scoops for lifting water from one level to another when the wind shifts and a section dries too quickly. Everything is hand-made, hand-mended, stone-on-stone. No metal worth naming. No wheels. The work is not to build anymore — building is finished; the plateau is full — but to tend. To keep the notches clear. To keep the basins empty of life.

You find footprints in a thin film of water: bare human feet, or feet wrapped in wet leather, going from wall to wall. Not many. Whoever tends this place does so mostly alone, or in a quiet line, moving as slowly as the water moves. Perhaps they come only after rains. Perhaps they live below and climb only to keep the mountain's instrument tuned. In the bright, cool quiet, you cannot tell if the place was abandoned yesterday or if it has been kept empty on purpose for a thousand years.

On the far side, the built valley simply ends. There is no parapet. The outermost wall is the lip of the world. Beyond it the mountain — the real, unmade mountain — falls away in a young, sharp cliff, freshly exposed, still angry with fracture planes that catch the sun. Far below you can see clouds snagged on the lower slopes. The water that finally leaves the system does not pour in a waterfall. It leaks, it weeps out through a hundred small mouths in that outer wall, and becomes mist again before it can fall.

The light stays bright. The stone stays cool. The water keeps moving, a hand's breadth at a time, through a town-sized bowl made entirely of hands, with no voice to claim it.
