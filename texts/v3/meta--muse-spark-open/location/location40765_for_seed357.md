This is not a forest that grew over ruins.

This is a forest that was grown *as* ruins, as city, as mountain.

---

You are high up — high enough that the air should be thin and cold, but it isn't. It's hot here, a lowland kind of hot that has been lifted intact into the sky. The elevation is wrong. The plateau you stand on is young rock, black and sharp and barely weathered, a shield volcano that thrust itself up too fast and hasn't learned how to be a mountain yet. It rose like a blister. The cliffs at its edges are fresh fractures, ninety-degree drops where you can see the cooling bubbles still frozen in the basalt, unsoftened by time. No scree slopes, no rounded shoulders. Just sheer, new geometry with water weeping endlessly out of it.

Because the water is everywhere. Not as rivers, not quite. At this height the clouds don't pass over — they snag and condense. The air itself sweats. You can see it happening: the heat shoves vapor up off the hot rock, up off the breathing leaves, and then the altitude wrings it back out again in a constant, warm drizzle that never quite stops. It isn't rain the way lowlands understand rain. It's a thickening of the air until it drips. Water beads on every surface, runs in sheets down the black rock, collects in every cupped leaf, overflows in threads and falls again.

And the leaves are everything.

There is no substrate that isn't plant. This is what `total` means: the rock is there, yes, young and sharp underneath, but you rarely touch it with your feet. The vegetation hasn't covered the built environment — the vegetation *is* the built environment.

Long before you arrived, something grew this place — not built it, grew it. And without tools, without tech, without even animals to tend it, it kept growing according to its first instructions.

You walk on causeways that are living trunks, braided together into roads as wide as rivers. They are not fallen logs; they are intentional, grafted while green, their bark fused into a single, ridged floor that still pushes out leaves from its sides. The railings are living roots that reared up and hardened into balusters. The walls are hedges that were convinced to become walls — ficus and banyan and something with no name, their aerial roots descending like pillars, thickening, lignifying into buttresses and arches and vaults.

It is a city in the way a coral reef is a city. Every surface is structure, and every structure is alive.

But it is sparse. That is the strange, quieting truth of its scale. For how totally the plant fills every inch of volume — hanging, stacking, vaulting, crisscrossing — there is an immense emptiness between presences. The architecture is monumental and infrequent. One enormous, hollow basilica of woven trunks stands a half-mile away from the next. Between them is not emptiness, but a fierce, turbulent thicket of ungoverned growth — vines as thick as your waist, leaves big as doors, constantly birthing and strangling each other. The built things are islands in a sea that is also made of the same plant-flesh, but wilder.

There are no roads connecting them anymore. Or rather, the roads are being unmade as fast as they are made. This is a turbulent place. You can hear it. You can hear it before you understand it.

The sound here is loud — 1.6 loud, which is to say you have to raise your voice not because of wind or water, but because of growth. The forest creaks. It is not a metaphor. Wood under tension pops like gunfire. Vines tightening around a column groan. Somewhere to your left, a fig cathedral that took decades to weave hallows out and collapses under its own water weight with a roar of snapping timber and splashing cisterns, while to your right, in the same minute, a new arch is throwing itself across a gap at visible speed — you can see the pale, wet tips of the shoots nosing forward inches per hour, seeking, finding, fusing. Creation and destruction are not seasons here. They are weather.

And because there is no fauna — nothing with eyes, nothing with wings, nothing to eat the leaves or carry the pollen — the plants have invented strange solutions. That's the weirdness, the 2.0 strangeness that hums under the heat.

They move air themselves. Huge, pitcher-like flowers the size of houses inhale with a slow sigh and exhale warm, cinnamon-scented mist to push their spores. The canopy is full of those lungs, opening and closing arrhythmically, so the whole valley seems to breathe out of sync. Some leaves have become translucent and filled with water, acting as lenses, focusing the normal, diffuse highland light into bright spots that scorch competing growth beneath them. Other plants have learned to be briefly carnivorous not of meat — there is none — but of each other, sending out pale, root-like filaments that dissolve a neighbor's bark on contact.

Everything drips, steams, and competes. Water gathered in leaf-basins the size of pools suddenly tips when the leaf shifts, sending warm waterfalls down three stories of woven balcony and stair. Mist condenses on the underside of every vast leaf and falls as a second rain. The black, young rock underneath is always slick, always steaming, weeping from a thousand vesicles.

You stand on a bridge that was grown to be a bridge. Its planks are living branches, its joints are grafts. Below you, thirty feet down, a courtyard that was grown to be a courtyard is being erased — its floor heaving as a new trunk erupts through it from below, splitting the living flagstones. The air is hot, wet, loud with cracks and sighs and splashes. No birds call. No insects drone. The loudness is entirely vegetable and hydrological and geological.

The mountain is still young. Its edges are still knives. Its city is still unfinished. It will never be finished.

It builds itself, unbuilds itself, overgrows itself, drowns itself and drinks itself, high in the hot clouds, in the constant post-rain that never ends.
