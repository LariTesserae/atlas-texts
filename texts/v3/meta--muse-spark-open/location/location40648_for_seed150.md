It is not a forest that has a village in it. It is a village that *is* a forest — or what a forest becomes when it tries to be a village in a place too high and too cold for either thing to exist comfortably.

You find it in a bowl. That is the first rule of its scale.

High up where the true mountains begin, the rock is still young and angry about being exposed. This cirque was lifted or scooped or snapped open recently, geologically speaking — last week in the planet’s memory. The walls are too sharp. Black basalt and grey shale in fresh, unsoftened slabs, unweathered, with edges you could cut your hand on, stepped in frost-shattered terraces that still match each other like broken teeth. Scree hasn't had time to make gentle slopes yet; it lies in new, steep cones beneath the cliffs. Nothing is rounded. Nothing is forgiving. And inside those walls, about the size of a hamlet, a cathedral, a single great hall — you could walk its circumference before your hands got cold — there is a hollow that holds everything else.

Because the plants got here first, and they left no room for anything else.

It is not trees. There are no trees this high, not in vertical trunks. In the thin, dim air under the permanent cloud cap, trees would starve for light and snap in the wind. What grows here is lower, denser, older in strategy: moss, liverwort, lichen the size of blankets, cushion plants, alpine dwarf willow and birch compressed until they forgot how to be individual shrubs. They grew into each other. Over each other. Through each other.

And then they kept going.

From the rim it looks like a dark green-black knot of wool has been pressed into the stone bowl. Closer, you see it has structure. Streets that are not streets, but growth seams — narrow clefts where two massive colonies of frost-moss meet and never quite fused, leaving a passage exactly shoulder-wide, floored with springy, frozen peat. Walls that are not walls, but the vertical sides of living hummocks, ten or twelve feet tall, made of thousands of interwoven stems, each stem glazed with ice. Roofs that are not roofs, but canopies where the cushion plants have leaned together overhead and sealed the gap with their own leaves, stitching themselves shut against the cold and the wind, leaving translucent domes that let in a weak, greenish light.

It is urban. Unmistakably. There are neighborhoods, though no one built them. On the windward side, the mass is low and tight, aerodynamic, a dense mat of lichen-tile shingles overlapping like scales, weeping constantly. On the leeward side where snow piles deepest, the plant-city throws up towers — not proud, but desperate for the dim sun. Hollow columns of braided willow-root, as thick as houses, with the willow's tiny leaves crowded on the sunward face and the interior left empty, a warmish chamber lined with soft white pith and down. Between them, plazas open: shallow depressions where the living floor pulls back to expose a stone basin. And there are bridges, thresholds, archways, all without toolmarks, because the material grew into that shape.

No one planted this. No one prunes it. The instruction is in the cold and the wet and the need.

The second rule is that water is everywhere, but rarely as water.

At 1.5 kilometers and colder than freezing should allow for wetness, the air itself is tired. The cloud that caps the cirque never really lifts — a low, luminous, pearl-grey lid that makes noon look like dusk and dusk look like deep water. Moisture condenses on every surface. It beads on the moss-hairs like glass, runs down the basalt walls in black weeping streaks that never dry, freezes overnight into lace, melts by mid-day into drip.

You hear it before you see it, in the mostly-quiet.

Drip. *Tock.* From a leaf-tip to a leaf-tip to a frozen puddle.
A sigh as a snow-load shifts on the outer moss-roof and slides.
The faint, constant creak of living wood and living peat expanding and contracting, growing even now, a millimeter at a time. The colony is active — terribly, slowly active. In summer, if you could call these few weeks of less-cold that, the cushions swell visibly with stored melt. Shoots extend. A new alley closes as two hummocks kiss and graft. An old doorway strangles shut while a new one splits open across the way. No inhabitants needed; the city renovates itself for better light and better drainage.

Inside the plant houses, it is warmer by degrees, not much — enough that your breath does not immediately plume. The walls respire. They hold the day's meager heat and the ground's faint warmth. Touch the interior pith and it gives like cold flesh. It is damp, always. Water is near the surface here, pressed up from the young rock, a perched water table that has nowhere to drain. It wells in the plazas as shallow, black mirrors rimmed with star-shaped liverworts. It sweats from the ceilings in cold dew that tastes of stone and tannin. Where it finds shadow, it becomes clear ice, thickening in pillars from floor to ceiling that the moss slowly grows around and incorporates as windows.

Light never gets strong. The cloud diffuses it, the mass of leaves filters it again, and by the time it reaches the alleys it is submarine — a dim emerald dusk, even at midday. There are pale things that like it: ghost lichens that glow faintly with their own chemistry, not bright enough to read by, just enough to mark an edge. Snow light reflected off the cirque walls comes in horizontally through gaps, blue and clean, and for an hour in late afternoon the whole interior glitters as every bead of condensation catches it.

There are almost no animals. A few endure. A white mite the size of a pinhead moving in herds through the moss-hair, farming fungus. An ice-crawler insect, wingless, blind, that tunnels through the pith walls and leaves behind neat round holes that later become cupboards for stored rainwater. Sometimes a ptarmigan blunders in over the rim, stares at the impossible green knot in the white-grey bowl, pecks once, and leaves. The city does not need visitors. Its ecology is closed: plant feeding on plant, dead layer feeding live layer, water cycling from cloud to leaf to ice to cloud again.

But it feels inhabited anyway. The alleys are too deliberate. The chambers are too well-sized for bodies, with raised sleeping platforms of woven root that fit a curled human shape exactly, though humans have never lived here. The thresholds are low, as if expecting you to bow. In one place, near the center where all the slow sap-ways converge, the different species have made something like a square — a perfect circle of cropped moss, soft as carpet, surrounded by pillared willow trunks whose branches have linked overhead to form a dome with a single oculus open to the weeping sky. Rain falls straight through without touching wood. The water collects in a cistern of living peat that contracts around it, holding it. It is a well, a council fire, an altar. It does nothing but be there, and be maintained by roots that tighten and loosen with the seasons.

If you stay too long — and the cold cautions against staying long — you notice the weirdness, which is not loud. It is the sense that the place remembers the shape of shelter even though it has never seen shelter. That the willows learned to make doors faster after you pushed through one. That the passage you came in by is now a little narrower, as if the city, packed to bursting and with no room left to spare, is still trying to accommodate you by making itself more dense, more intricate, more itself.

Outside, beyond the rim, the young mountains go on, sharp and lifeless and cold. Inside the bowl, the green-black mass steams faintly in the dim air, condensing its own weather, dripping, creaking, growing.

It has no streets on a map because it is too small for maps. It has no builders because it needs none.
