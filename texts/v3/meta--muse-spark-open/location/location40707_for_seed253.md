This is not a forest that grew in water.

This is a place that *was built* and then was grown over, and then was drowned, and then kept growing anyway — until there was no distinction left between those three events.

### The Mountain That Is Not A Mountain

You are on the slope of something too big and too regular to be geology.

Far below you — far below light, far below warm currents — an ancient range rises from the abyssal plain. It should not be here. Everything around it has long since been ground down to silt and salt-flat by time. The sea floor for a thousand miles in every direction is primordial and featureless, a cold desert of fine grey mud settled into its final form.

But this interrupts it.

A ziggurat. A terraced mountain. A stepped pyramid the size of a province, built in tiers so vast each one holds its own weather. The stone, if it ever was stone, is gone. What remains is the form: level after level after level climbing beyond the reach of sight, its edges softened not by wind but by age — corners rounded to smooth, broad curves, stairways worn to shallow ramps, vertical faces undercut into gentle overhangs. It is the oldest stable state a built thing can achieve: no longer crumbling, no longer sharp, just *resolved*.

The elevation is extreme. From its drowned foothills to its apex is more than four vertical kilometers, and you are somewhere in the middle tiers, clinging to a wall that falls away beneath you into black and rises above you into black. You cannot see the top. You cannot see the bottom. You can only feel the presence of both as a pressure in the water.

And it is all built. There is no bedrock here. Every surface is worked. You can run your hand along what looks like a cliff and feel the faint, regular seams where blocks larger than houses were laid, now fused together by accretion and time. Vast plazas, avenues wide enough for rivers, colonnades where each column is thicker than a redwood. All of it still legible as architecture, despite being worn to the smoothness of river stones.

There is no technology here. No metal. No light. No tool-marks remaining. Whatever people or things made this did not build with machines, or if they did, the machines are long since dissolved. What is left is purely biological in its maintenance.

Because everything is covered.

### The Living Substrate

You cannot find a patch of bare built-surface larger than your palm.

The vegetation here did not colonize the city. It *became* the city. In the cold, dark water, plants do not behave as you know them.

They are kelp, and mangrove, and hanging moss, and root-mat, and liverwort, and something with no name — all at once. From every horizontal terrace, forests of black-green kelp rise fifty, a hundred meters tall, their stipes as thick as trunks, their fronds broad and tattered and endlessly swaying despite the stillness. From every vertical wall, mats of vegetation hang like tapestries, like curtains, like living thatch — dense, interwoven root-felt meters thick, dripping with smaller growths. Vines as thick as your body lash tiers together. Bacterial sheets glow faintly and then go dark. The built stone is only the armature now; the plant mass is the actual material of the place.

It fills the space completely. Water moves *through* leaves, not around them. You swim — or drift, slowly, in the cold — through tunnels and halls and streets that are not carved from rock but grown from interlaced canopy. An avenue becomes a cathedral of kelp trunks. A doorway becomes a sphincter of living root that has learned the shape of an arch and kept it. A plaza becomes a meadow of slow, waving frond-grass that hides the flagstones entirely.

It is sparse, despite its totality. That is the strangeness. For all that every surface is alive, there is immense emptiness between presences. One colossal kelp holds its territory alone for a hundred meters. One hanging garden drapes a wall while the next wall over is bare except for a thin green film. The forest does not crowd itself. It spaces itself like elders in a huge, quiet room, each allowing the others distance. You can see for a long way through the cold, clear water between trunks, down long, dark, plant-lined boulevards where nothing moves.

Until something does.

### The Inhabitants and The Loud Dark

The water is cold — not freezing, but a deep, bone-held coolness that never changes. 4 degrees, 5 degrees, forever. It does not vary with depth or season or day, because there is no day. Light barely reaches here. From far, impossibly far above, a faint, bruised indigo filters down through kilometers of water and kelp canopy, just enough to make shadows, never enough to see color. Most illumination is briefly bioluminescent, and incidental.

You would think this would be silent.

It is brutally loud.

Because the fauna are abundant.

They are everywhere, but like the plants, they are spaced, solitary, huge, and slow. Pale blind fish the size of canoes glide between kelp trunks, mouths open, filtering. Crustaceans the size of houses cling to the hanging mats and pick at them with delicate, endless motions, their joints creaking like old wood. Squid as long as avenues drift overhead, trailing veils. And everywhere, the filterers and singers: barnacles grown to the size of drums encrusting every ledge, opening and closing with a resonant *thunk* that carries through water for miles; anemones the size of doors pulsing shut; something like a whale, but flatter, and with fronds of its own, moaning continuously in the dark so that the water itself vibrates.

The sound is not chaos. Nothing hunts frantically here. Nothing blooms and dies quickly. This is a frozen equilibrium, a system that found its balance a million years ago and then stopped. The same loud chorus — creaks, booms, clicks, the low organ drone of the great flat swimmers, the endless rustle of kelp fronds stirring without current — repeats without variation. You can listen for an hour and realize you are hearing a loop so long it feels like permanence.

The water never warms. The plants never grow faster. The animals never migrate. The silt never quite settles, never quite clears. The mountain never erodes further. It is a post-flood world that has finished flooding.

You rest on the lip of a terrace, your back against a living wall that was once a temple or a granary or a house, and you look out over the next tier down. Below you, the kelp forest continues, tier after tier after tier, fading into indigo darkness. Above you, the same. The water presses cold on all sides. A pale shape passes far out in the void, singing.

Nothing here was made for you. Nothing here knows you are here. It was built, and then it was grown, and then it was drowned, and now it simply *is* — a vast, dark, cold, loud, living architecture holding perfectly still at the bottom of the sea.
