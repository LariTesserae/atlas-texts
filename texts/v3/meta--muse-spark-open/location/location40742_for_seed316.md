This is not a valley. Valleys have sides.

This is what is left when a continent has finished happening.

### The Flattened Sea

Imagine land that has been rained on, blown over, flooded and sun-baked for so long that it has given up on shape. Elevation  is a courtesy here. You can walk for days and gain or lose no more than the height of your ankle. The old shielding mountains were chewed down to a roughness in the distance, then to a stain on the horizon, then to nothing. The bedrock itself lies exposed — not sharp, not jagged, but ancient. It has been tumbled by time the way a stone in a river is tumbled until it becomes a perfect, inexplicable sphere.

Here the rock is sandstone and shale and basalt that has forgotten it was ever magma. It is soft to the touch. It sighs. It is worn into continent-wide ripples, broad scallops hundreds of meters across and only a meter deep, like the thumbprint of a god who pressed down and never lifted. Between the ripples are pans, and every pan holds water, because water has nowhere to go.

There are no rivers. Rivers require a downhill. Instead there are sheets.

A warm, dim light hangs over everything without ever seeming to come from anywhere. The sky is not cloudy so much as it is *thick* — a low, luminous haze the color of wet linen, pearl-white and amber at once, high enough that you cannot see a sun but bright enough that shadows still try to exist and fail. It's dusk that never was evening. The air is warm on your skin, bath-warm, sweat-warm, and it is absolutely saturated. Water condenses on everything without rain. It beads on the stone, runs down weeping cliffs that are only two feet high and go on for miles, gathers in pools that reflect the sky perfectly until a wind touches them and they skin over again.

Vegetation should not be able to live here and mostly it doesn't bother. This is too wet, too flat, too old. But sparse life has learned to be stubborn in the way lichen is stubborn. Black-green mats cling to the windward side of stone undulations, more mineral than plant. In the shallowest pans, single reeds stand like sentinels, one every twenty meters, perfectly spaced as if planted. They are not planted. They are bone-pale, nearly translucent, with a single puff of seed at the top that never disperses despite the constant wind. In deeper water, fleshy, low rosettes float, their leaves slick and pimpled to catch condensation. Nothing tall grows. Tall would be noticed by the sky.

Because the sky notices things here.

---

The first thing you hear before you see anything is the sound, and it never stops.

It is deafening, but not loud in the way an explosion is loud. It is the loudness of a million quiet things happening at once in a room with no walls to contain them. Water moving sideways across stone in a million braided sheets makes a hiss like static the size of a continent. The stone itself, so old it has become porous as sponge, exhales when you step on it — a low *whoomph* of trapped air sighing out of a million tiny mouths. The wind, which comes from everywhere because there is nothing to stop it or channel it, carries with it the clicking of the reed-seeds against each other, a dry rattle that should be delicate but multiplied by infinity becomes a roar.

And underneath it all is a deeper sound that is not sound. A grinding, a subsonic churning. The plain rearranges itself while you watch.

This is a turbulent place. Not weather-turbulent, though the weather is ceaseless — but *land*-turbulent.

The water does not sit still. In minutes, a pan the size of a lake will drain sideways into nothing, its surface shivering and then vanishing to expose glistening mud that cracks and heals before your eyes. A low stone ridge a half mile long will swell up an inch, like a breath held, and then subside somewhere else. The ripples in the bedrock migrate. Slowly. Visibly. You can stand and watch a scallop of ground flow past your feet like cold honey, carrying its little puddle and its reeds with it. Nothing is anchored.

The horizon shivers. Distances lie.

You think you see a cairn far off — one of the built things — and you walk toward it, and you do not get closer. You walk for an hour and it is still exactly as far. Then you blink and it is behind you.

The scattered built things are not villages. No one lives here long enough to build a village. They are remains, or attempts, or warnings, left by people who passed through when the plain was slightly less awake. A single lintel of dark basalt standing without walls. A circle of stones that are all perfectly upright despite having no foundation — they hover a finger's width above the soupy ground, and have for centuries, humming faintly. A stairwell cut into the soft rock that descends three steps and then ends in solid stone, as if the builder forgot which way was down. All of them are worn to roundness, the ancient erosion working on human work faster than on the earth itself. You find handprints pressed into rock that is harder than steel, preserved perfectly, but the hand has six fingers.

No tools. No metal rusting. Even if you brought a machine it would not work. Compasses spin lazily and point at you. Fire burns sideways. Your own memory of what straight is starts to fray after a few hours; you catch yourself thinking that the sky is below you and the stone is a sky you are falling into.

This is the weirdness, which is not decoration but rule. Reality here is thin from being stretched so wide and for so long. Scale has broken it. When a plain is large enough to generate its own weather, it eventually generates its own physics, and this one has decided that fixity is inefficient.

Things duplicate. You will see your own footprints ahead of you. You will hear a second set of footsteps walking beside you that stop half a second after you do. The warm pools reflect not the sky but a different sky — a night sky crowded with too many moons, or a clear blue noon that has never existed here — and if you touch the water, your hand is cold for an hour afterward.

It is packed.

Not with plants, not with animals — those are rare. You might see one thing living move in a day: a pale amphibian the size of your forearm pulling itself across a sheet of water with too many joints in its legs, its eyes filmed white, utterly silent. It does not flee. It has nowhere to flee to.

No, what packs this place is presence. The air is crowded. The water is crowded. Every cubic foot of haze holds droplets, midges of light, spores that never land, echoes that haven't been made yet. There is no empty space. To inhale is to drink. To stand still is to feel the plain pressing in from all sides, not hostile, but *attentive*, the way a crowd presses when it is watching something happen in its middle. You are in the middle.

And still — it is wet. Always wet. Your clothes never dry. Your skin puckers. Water wells up from your footprints after you leave them. It weeps from the low rocks in constant, clear threads that taste of iron and time. It gathers on the underside of the stone overhangs that shouldn't exist and falls upward. It is warm water, body-temperature, and it makes the whole vast, dim, roaring flat feel like the inside of something breathing.

At night — if you can call it night when the light only dims from pearl to pewter — the scattered reeds glow faintly at their tips, not enough to see by, just enough to tell you where they are. The sound deepens. The stone flows a little faster. New pans open with a soft sigh and catch the sky.

You understand, standing there with water to your ankles and infinity in every direction, that erosion is not destruction. It is peace. This land has been working its whole long life to become this flat, this smooth, this perfectly unresolved. It is finished becoming itself.

And now it has nothing to do but move, and listen to itself move, forever.
