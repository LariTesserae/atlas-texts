It is not a forest that grew. It is a forest that arrived.

You come down into it, and that's the first wrongness - you have to come *down*. The surface up top is a flat, sun-baked pan of cracked salt and ironstone, hot to the touch and utterly empty. No shrubs, no dust devils even. Just a pale, high sky and a ground that rings like a kiln shelf when you step on it. Then the ground opens.

Not a cave mouth. A seam.

A long, raw rip in the world, maybe ten miles long and a mile across at its widest, where the crust has yawned apart so recently the edges haven't learned how to be cliffs yet. The rock is still sharp. There are no talus slopes softening the drop, no riverbed smoothing the bottom. The walls are stair-stepped basalt and halite, sheer and fresh-broken, with hanging plates of stone still half-attached, humming with tension. You can see the crystals where they snapped. This place is embryonic - erosion hasn't really started here yet. It is pre-formation geology, a room that was made last week in geologic time and hasn't been furnished.

And light gets in anyway. The fissure overhead is wide enough that noon falls straight to the bottom, undiffused and brutal, a white-gold column that makes the dust in the air visible as a slow swirl. That's normal light, desert light, but it has nowhere to go except down. It paints the far wall in hard shadow and glare, and it should be killing everything below.

Instead, everything below is alive.

### The Dry Jungle

There is no water here. Not a trickle, not a seep, not a damp stone. The air is warm - not burning, but womb-warm, breath-warm, about 34 Celsius in the shade if there were any shade that wasn't alive. When you climb down the maintenance ropes the locals have left, your sweat evaporates before it can bead. Your lips crack on the way down.

The vegetation doesn't care.

It fills the rift from wall to wall to ceiling in a dense, interwoven mass that makes the word "forest" inadequate. This is not trees in ground. The plant *is* the ground. The plant *is* the architecture. Up top, at the rim, it starts as a thin crust of black-green lichen-like sheets that have glued themselves to the salt rock and are prying it apart with slow, chemical fingers. A few meters down, they thicken into leathery ribbons, then into rope-fern trunks as thick as your torso, then into something that has no name outside this hole.

They are dry plants. That is the only way to think of them. They are not succulents in the earthly sense; they have no water to store. Their leaves - if you can call them leaves - are not green but a dusty olive-grey, an amber, a rust-red, all of them matte and waxy to the point of looking like cut paper or insect wing. They rustle with a sound like a library of dry parchment being shuffled. When you touch one, it's warm, stiff, and vibrates faintly. They are full of resin, not sap. Oil, wax, aerogel flesh. Some are translucent, lit from within by the light they harvest. Some have evolved to drink directly from the air: vast, feathered nets that comb the warm updraft for the merest fleeting humidity, condensing it on internal cold-traps and then *sealing it* in wax bladders where nothing can evaporate. Others don't bother with water at all. They are lithovores, their roots dissolving halite and basalt and pulling metals straight into their tissues, exhaling fine, fragrant powders of salt and rust.

They grow with absurd speed and violence. This is not a patient jungle. You can watch it happen. A vine as thick as your wrist darts out a meter in an hour, blindly questing, curling around anything - other vines, rock, the discarded woven ladder of the inhabitants - and then hardens into wood-like cabling. Puffs the size of melons swell and burst soundlessly, releasing clouds of fibrous seeds that float like ash and immediately snag and sprout on whatever they land on, even on each other's leaves. The whole mass creaks and sighs and pops as it expands against itself in the heat. The rift is turbulent, hyperactive - a construction site where the building materials build themselves. A clearing made this morning will be a thicket by evening. A trunk that was there yesterday will have been strangled and hollowed and re-inhabited as a trellis by tonight.

You do not walk on soil. You walk on plant. A springy, layered mat ten or twenty meters deep, made of living and dead layers of the same material, all interwoven. It gives under your feet like a thatched roof. Beneath it, somewhere, is the true stone bottom of the fissure, but no one has seen it in months. The jungle mat has lifted itself off the floor and become its own geography: hills and hollows and tunnels and pillars, all still growing, all breathing warm, cinnamon-and-ozone air.

And everywhere, the light filters through. Not dappled like a normal canopy, but shattered. Because the leaves are dry and thin and半-translucent, the whole interior glows - a diffuse, amber cathedral light. Sunshafts come down through holes that open and close as fronds move.

### The Inhabitants

It should be silent, this dry tinderbox. It is not. It is deafening.

The sound hits you halfway down the cliff, before you even reach the canopy. A physical pressure of noise that makes your ribs buzz.

The fauna here is overwhelming not in size but in number and insistence. The plants are not alone. Every surface seethes.

They are not birds. They are not mammals. They are things that have evolved to live without drinking. Beetle-things the size of your hand with mirror elytra that flash as they swarm; kite-things like dried leaves with too many wings that ride the thermal updraft in screaming flocks; long, many-legged fiber-worms that chew through the waxy wood and leave behind perfect round tunnels that immediately become homes for someone else; bulb-throated singers the size of fists that hang from vines and inflate and deflate, each one a single piercing note, and there are millions of them making a chorus that is less like a song and more like a faulty electrical grid heard from inside.

Everything clicks, rasps, saws, drums. The plants themselves are noisy - their seed pods snapping open with a crack like a rifle shot, their trunks groaning as tension builds, their dead leaves shattering. The insects amplify it, mimicking it, riding it. You have to shout to hear yourself think, and even then your voice is swallowed.

Nothing large lives here, or rather, nothing large *stays* large. Anything bigger than a dog would be entangled in minutes. Instead bulk is replaced by density: a hundred thousand small bodies where you would expect one deer. They crawl over you the moment you stop moving. They are not aggressive, just incurious and busy. They will use your shoulder as a road, your hair as nesting material. If you stand still, tiny weavers will begin to stitch your shirt to the nearest frond with a white, hot resin that hardens instantly.

This is a system running far too fast. Birth and death happen on the scale of hours. You see a flowering stalk shoot up, unfurl a corona of dry, papery petals that smell of hot iron, get swarmed, pollinated, devoured, collapsed, and already sprouting fungal-analogues from its corpse, all between lunch and the time the light starts to slant.

The rules are strange here. Evolution normally leans on water as a solvent, as a mediator. Here, in its absence, other chemistries have taken over. Sap is a low-volatility silicone oil. Genetics - if you cut a stem, you don't find rings, but nested, folded sheets of information-bearing fiber, like a book that grows its own pages. Cut a vine, and the two ends will seek each other for hours afterward, blindly waving. Some of the plants remember touch; run your hand along a certain feather-fern and all its leaflets fold shut in sequence, then stay shut for days as if sulking. Some of the animals are not clearly animal - there are things that photosynthesize on their backs and hunt with their mouths. The line between plant and fauna blurs when everything is made of the same dry, waxed stuff and everything is trying to be everything else at once.

### The Marks

People live here, which is somehow the strangest thing of all.

Not many. The rift is too unstable, too loud, too hungry to support a town. But there are scattered marks of habitation, always temporary, always half-swallowed.

You see them as you pick your way along the springy mat: a platform woven from living vine and left to be reabsorbed, its fire-pit still there - but they don't burn wood here, wood is too valuable and too reluctant to burn; they burn the salt. Small huts like baskets, turned upside down and anchored with stone weights, their walls made of the harvested amber leaf-plates, overlapping like shingles. Ladders braided from fiber rope that go nowhere now because the wall they were tied to has moved. Carvings on the basalt where someone tried to cut a foothold - the plant has already grown over them, softening the edges with green-grey lips.

Their tech is primitive in the truest sense: hand tools. A knife of sharpened basalt hafted with resin. A loom made from two poles. A sling. Clay pots - but the clay is ground plant-fiber mixed with salt dust. They harvest the oil-sap and let it harden into a glassy, amber-like material they can flake into blades. They wear masks of woven fiber against the seed-fluff, and earplugs of wax - you have to, or the sound will bleed your hearing in a day.

They do not try to clear the jungle. You cannot clear something that grows faster than you can cut. Instead they garden turbulence itself - they guide a vine here, prune a tunnel there, knowing it will be gone by next week. Their villages move. Yesterday's floor is tomorrow's ceiling as the mat buckles and lifts. They speak loudly and in short sentences, and they never stay still for long, because stillness means being incorporated.

When you finally stop and let the heat and the light and the roar settle around you, you understand the place not as a valley but as a body. A long, open wound in the earth that is trying to scab over with living paper, furiously, chaotically, stitching itself shut with dry fiber and chitin and noise. It is raw geology becoming biology without bothering to wait for rain. It is a desert that decided to be a rainforest anyway, using wax and hunger and hurry instead of water.

And it is still growing, still creaking wider, still roaring, right under your feet, right against your ears, asking - without asking - to be helped or to be left alone, you can't tell which, because you can't hear your own thoughts anymore.
