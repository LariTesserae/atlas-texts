You come down into it without ever climbing.

That's the first thing wrong with this place. The land was low to begin with — a coastal hollow, a river basin so wide and shallow you could mistake its rim for the horizon — and yet to enter it you descend. Not steeply. The air just gets thicker, cooler, dimmer, and the flat ground layers itself under your feet like old pages settling. This valley has been here a long time.

It is settled in itself. Nothing here is raw or broken. Water has had ten thousand years to teach every stone how to carry it. The basalt shoulders are rounded to black pillows. The low ridges are not hills but the slow meanders of a river that no longer needs a riverbed; it has braided itself into a lace of a hundred shallow throats, oxbow scars turned to peat, sandbars grown over with sighing grass. Everything is weathered soft, to a polish. Even the cliffs to the east - if you can call those three-meter lips cliffs - overhang in smooth, scooped hollows where centuries of seep have lapped them away from underneath.

And it weeps. Constantly.

The water table lives right at the skin of the world here. You never have to dig for it. It finds you. Every rock face sweats clear beads that gather and run without rain. Every footfall in the moss releases a cold welling around your boot, as if the ground were saturated sponge over dark glass. The air itself is heavy with it - not fog exactly, but a breathable condensation that beads on your arms, on the hair of your neck, on the leaves until they tip and pour. Small, nameless channels carry it everywhere, brown-black water moving absolutely silently between banks of ferns, and then suddenly out loud where it drops an inch, a finger's width over a root dam, and redoubles.

Because this place is loud.

In full daylight it would be quiet - a marsh rustle - but there is no full daylight here. The sky is always that hour before true night, a dim, even pewter-blue with no visible sun, as if the light has to filter through deep water to get here. And in the dim, sound takes over. Water ticks and patters on a million leaves. The peat sighs and pops as gas moves under it. The ferns - huge, lush, frond over frond, rack upon rack of green living lattice so dense you cannot see ten meters in any direction without green interrupting - shiver when a drop hits them and shiver again a second later when nothing has hit them.

Nothing here was built. There are no walls, no ruins, no cut stumps. No straight lines ever. If people lived here they left no mark, or the basin erased it perfectly. The only architecture is erosion's own: root-arches groined like cathedral vaults holding back the streamlets, moss pillars where mineral water has laid down travertine around a stem one micron at a time for centuries.

And yet, everything is engineered.

You notice it first in the water. A side-channel splits around a hummock, rejoins, and for a half-second the flow upstream of the split runs *uphill* to meet itself coming down. Just a hand's breadth of impossibility, then it remembers which way is down and corrects with a little sucking chuckle. The ferns flicker. If you stare at a clump of sphagnum long enough you see the pattern in it stutter - copy, paste, flawed repeat - like the texture resolution gave up.

The technology here, if you can call it that, is not *on* the landscape. It *is* the landscape, and it has moved past needing tools. Post-physical. The basin is running.

The cool air hangs motionless and then rearranges. Condensation gathers in midair too fast, drawing itself into brief, trembling geometries - lattices, tori, long strings of dewdrop writing that hang for three heartbeats, dripping light that isn't there, and then collapse into normal rain with a soft, too-loud splash. When they collapse, the local gravity hiccups. Pollen hangs suspended. Your inner ear pops. To the west, across the open flat that goes on for miles - this whole valley system is kilometers wide, town-sized and valley-sized at once, everything visible in its dim, close-haloed way and nothing distant - you can see the same fern hill twice. Once where it stands, and again, paler, thirty meters south, at a five-degree offset, as if the space was folded and pinned badly. Sometimes dragonflies, rare as comets here - animal life is thinned to almost nothing, the plants took all the budget - cut through that doubled space and vanish in the seam, reappearing a minute later flying backwards.

It is not hostile. It is busy. Active in a way an old marsh shouldn't be. Photosynthesis ticking even in the low light. Peat growing at the edge of hearing. Water sorting isotopes. The ferns exhaling oxygen in great, silent pulses you feel as pressure changes against your forehead. The weeping rocks renewing their beads. And beneath/behind/through all of it, that other process - compaction, rendering, error-correction - humming. Not mechanical. More like a throat singing, many throats: the sound water makes when it remembers being cloud and being ice at the same time, amplified by wet stone bowls until your teeth ache.

If you stay still and breathe the cool, iron-clean air, you can learn the rules by feel:

1. Water wants to be connected to itself. Distance annoys it.
2. Green wants volume. It stacks until physics protests, then stacks a little more.
3. Light is rationed.
4. Places remember being other places, and occasionally trade.

There are no paths. Where you step, the sphagnum records a brief, glowing afterprint of your weight in faint amber, a lattice of nanoscale light that fades slowly, measuring you. Then the water wells up to erase it, grain by grain, bead by bead.

Behind you, your footprints fill, darken, and lie still - until one, without any new cause, briefly overflows its edges and spills uphill towards your heel.
