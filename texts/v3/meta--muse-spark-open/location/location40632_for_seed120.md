It is not a valley. It is not a mountain. It is a room at the top of the world that thinks it is a mountain.

You have to climb to get to it — three days up the white spine of the thing, where the air gets thin and then, impossibly, hot. The higher you go the hotter it gets, which is wrong. The stone under your hands should be cold and scoured by wind. Instead it sweats. The peak sweats. By the time you pull yourself over the last lip, your clothes are stuck to you, your breath is coming in wet gasps, and the sun is too large and too dim at the same time, like a coal seen through gauze.

There is no summit. There is a mouth.

From above it looked like a crater. From the rim you see it is not. It is built. Perfectly, totally built — a pocket, a hidden cell, a single feature sunk into the raw, unfinished rock of the new mountain. The mountain itself is wrong. It is too steep, too sharp, its angles too fresh, like it was extruded yesterday and hasn't learned how to be stone yet. The geology is embryonic. There are no scree slopes, no softened shoulders, no riverbeds. Just planes of dark basalt that meet at knife-edges, still warm from being made, still faintly soft at the corners if you stare too long, as if gravity hasn't finished deciding how heavy they should be.

And into that raw peak, someone has sunk a cylinder.

Perhaps twenty feet across. Perhaps smaller when you blink. A perfect throat of fitted stone going straight down. Not cut — *woven*. Every inch of it is made. There is no natural substrate left. The walls are courses of black, heat-glazed brick, each one thumb-printed on its inner face by the maker, and between the bricks, instead of mortar, there is a living caulk of moss and root and vine, packed so dense there is no room for air. It is lush beyond reason. In this heat, in this dim, it should be dead. Instead it thrives in the wet.

Because the wall weeps.

Water here does not fall. It arrives. It condenses out of the hot, thick air in beads on every brick, swells, and runs. It seeps sideways out of the stone itself. The bricks are porous and they cry constantly, a slow, loud weeping that fills the cylinder with sound. Drip, trickle, patter, splash — a whole weather system contained in a well. The sound is amplified by the packed closeness. It is deafeningly intimate, like putting your ear to a throat and hearing it swallow. And underneath that is a lower sound: the mountain humming as it continues to grow, a deep tectonic creak, as if new rock is still being pushed up from below even now, millimeter by millimeter, lifting this little room higher while you stand in it.

You descend by a ladder that is also a root.

It is not made of metal — the people who made this place never had more than stone hammers and fire-hardened wood and patient hands. The ladder is a mangrove-like thing they coaxed to grow in that impossible spiral down the wall, its rungs thick, knotted limbs that renew themselves. You have to trust it. The vegetation is everywhere and it is crowded. There is no bare wall. Ferns the size of your forearm jostle with flowering vines that open and close with audible pops in the hot dark. Broad, waxy leaves press against your shoulders as you climb down. Hanging moss curtains your face. The air is heavy with green rot and ozone and the smell of hot stone. Everything is saturated, occupied. Even the puddle at the bottom has no open water — it is a mat of duckweed and lily pads overlapping like scales, so dense you could think it was floor until your foot breaks it and warm water closes over your ankle.

At the bottom, reality gives up.

This is why the room was built. To hold it.

The light here is dim — not shadow, but a thickness. The opening far above is a pale, diffuse circle that should throw a shaft of light straight down. It doesn't. The light bends. It slides down the wet walls and pools in corners, it collects like oil in the leaves. Some of the bricks glow faintly from within, not with reflected light but with remembered light, and that glow drips upward. If you hold your hand out, your shadow falls in two directions. If you stay still too long, your shadow detaches and continues to fidget.

Gravity is also crowded here. In a pocket this small, packed this tight, there is no room for one gravity to operate cleanly. So there are several, weak and bickering. Water runs down, but steam rises sideways. A vine tendril grows toward the wall, then rethinks and corkscrews toward your warmth. Loose pebbles placed on the water's surface orbit each other slowly before deciding to fall. The air itself has layers — hot on top of hot, but one layer is humid and one is dry, and they refuse to mix, forming a visible, shivering membrane at chest height that you have to duck through, feeling it pop softly on your skin like a soap film.

It is active. Frantically, biologically active. Because nothing can leave, everything happens here. The vines bloom, fruit, rot, and re-sprout in the time it takes you to catch your breath. Condensation forms, falls, is drunk by roots, is transpired out again as mist in a five-minute cycle. The bricks themselves are still curing, still hardening, still settling — you can hear them tick. The whole pocket breathes. Inhale: the walls contract a fraction, the water rises, the leaves press in. Exhale: the walls sigh outward, the air pressure drops and your ears pop, the weeping intensifies.

There is almost no animal life. The system is too loud, too hot, too full of plant to leave room for anything that moves quickly. But there is one. You hear it before you see it — a rare, infrequent presence.

In the thicket of the wall, something primitive has made a nest of woven root-fiber and fired clay shards, exactly as the humans did but smaller. A bird? An insect? It is neither. It is pale, translucent, the size of a cupped hand, with too many joints, and it tends the vines with stone tools of its own — a flake of obsidian no bigger than a fingernail, which it uses to trim the growth and keep the weeping channels clear. It works ceaselessly, pausing only to look at you with eyes that reflect the wrong light, the light that falls up.

It does not mind you. You are just another condensation event. Another temporary pocket of water and heat that has fallen into this place where a mountain is still being born, where a room has been built to contain a piece of broken physics and keep it wet and fed and dim.

If you climb back out — and the ladder will let you, if the vines haven't grown over the rungs while you weren't looking — the mountain will be an inch taller. The air will be a little hotter. The weeping will be a little louder.
