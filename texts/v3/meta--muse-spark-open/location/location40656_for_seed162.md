It is not a mountain. It is a stack of unfinished worlds on top of a mountain.

You are high up where warm air should not be. The elevation is continental - not a peak but a plateau the size of a country lifted two miles into the sky, its edges still sharp because it has not had time to be softened. This is young rock. The world here was recently poured.

Below you, or what should be below you, the lowlands are not visible. There is a sea of cloud that never clears, a dim amber haze that catches the low sun and diffuses it until there is no sun at all, just a direction where the light is slightly more amber. The light never gets brighter than late dusk. It is always that hour before stormlight, even on clear days, because the air itself is thick.

And it is warm. Unreasonably warm for this height. The stone holds heat. If you put your palm to the basalt you feel the thrum of it, not hot enough to burn, hot enough to make the air shiver. The plateau breathes.

### The Ground

The terrain has not learned erosion yet. There are no meandering river valleys, no smooth hills. Everything is right-angled and cathedral-sharp where the earth cracked as it cooled and lifted. Vast mesas stand like black glass anvils, their sides fluted with columnar joints still crisp as fresh-cut honeycomb. Between them are chasms a mile deep and only a hundred feet across, so straight they look quarried, their bottoms lost in the dim.

This is not dry, but it is not wet either. There are no lakes, no permanent rivers. Water here is an event.

It arrives as condensation. As the warm stone exhales against the cool high air, it sweats. Every dawn, without rain, droplets the size of pearls bead on every exposed surface and run. They gather in the flutes of the basalt, in the lips of leaves, in shallow pans of stone that have never been scoured by a river. By mid-afternoon they are gone, evaporated back into the warm haze. Pockets of water appear where there should be none - a hanging bog suspended halfway up a cliff face, fed only by fog; a clear pool in a hollow the size of a room, full of darting things, that will be dry dust in three days and then full again after a night of heavy mist.

Where water lingers, everything explodes.

### The Loom

Because despite the damp-not-wet, it is lush. Obscenely, impossibly lush for rock that is still cooling.

Nothing is flat. The vegetation does not grow *on* the plateau; it *is* a second plateau built atop the stone. Layer upon layer, dense to the point of suffocation. From a distance the green looks solid, but up close it is all air and structure.

The first layer is a black-green crust that seals the hot basalt - a lichen-moss that feels like damp felt and pulses faintly warm. From that, stalk-things rise as thick as redwoods but not trees: they are hollow, ribbed, venting steam from pores along their trunks. Their leaves are not leaves but vast fins, veined like cooling fins on an engine, radiating heat and collecting condensation at the same time. Water beads on their undersides and drips constantly in the quiet, a metronome.

Above them, a second canopy braids itself together - vines with the tensile strength of cable, figs that swell and burst with hot vapor, epiphytes that hang like wet laundry, each one a garden for smaller things. The light is dim already, filtered through steam and fin-leaves, so everything glows on its own a little: the fungi, the nectar, the eyes.

And there are no structures. No ruins, no roads, no cleared fields. No sign any hand ever made anything. And yet -

Everything is industrial.

You hear it first in the quiet. The place is mostly quiet - the big sound is absent. No wind at this scale, or wind so vast you cannot hear it as wind. Instead, underneath, there is the sound of work.

The hollow trunk-things are not trees. They are distilleries, refineries, forges. You can stand next to one and hear the wet hiss as it draws mineral water up from the rock and fractionates it, venting oxygen in one sigh and sulfur in another. The fins tick as they cool. The figs are pressure vessels; when they ripen they do not fall, they discharge with a soft *thump*, releasing a cloud of spores and hot, petrochemical-scented steam that smells like rain on a foundry floor.

The ground itself is a factory floor. Ants the size of your fist, and things that are not ants, run in dense, silent braids carrying pellets of basalt dust to mounds that smoke. The mounds are kilns, alive and breathing, sintering dust into hard ceramic. The pellets cool and are carried again to build something else, then dismantled.

There are no builders. There is only building. Production without product. The forest manufactures itself, endlessly, turbulently, with no architect. A tree will assemble itself in a night, roar with heat and steam, and then be taken apart by beetles before noon, its materials already being reformed into a different shape a mile away. Species do not evolve here; they are prototyped.

This is why it is dense. Not just with leaves. With presences. Every cubic foot of air is occupied. Moths with wingspans like blankets, so dusted with metallic scales they clink when they fly. Things that look like dragonflies but have bodies of articulated glass, hovering and extruding fine copper wire from their abdomens and leaving it tangled in branches. Slugs that leave trails of warm, quick-setting resin. The fauna is abundant beyond counting, and almost all of it is at work.

You watch a clearing where nothing stands for an hour except steam. Then the ground bulges. A scaffold of living vine throws itself up, hissing, in minutes. On it, dozens of different animals - furred, scaled, chitinous, things with no name - swarm without fighting, adding parts, secreting, welding with bio-arc flashes. By the time you realize it has a shape, it is a tower of interlocked lungs and leaves, humming. By the time you blink, a fissure opens in the basalt beneath it and drops it a thousand feet into darkness with hardly a sound. The swarm disperses. Another scaffold is already rising fifty feet away.

No one is in charge. Creation and destruction are the same process here. It has the energy of a bazaar the size of a continent, all stalls being built and burned at once, all in near-silence.

### The Weirdness

The dimness is not just lack of light. It is wrong light.

At this height, the air should be thin. It isn't. It is heavy and slightly bent. Gravity is negotiable between the mesas. You can see it in the waterfalls that do exist - the brief ones after a condensation storm, when sheets of water pour off a mesa edge for an hour. Halfway down, some of the water stops falling and rises again as mist, or hangs in perfect, trembling spheres that reflect the amber haze. Birds - if they are birds - fly in straight lines and then repeat the same straight line a minute later, offset by three feet, like afterimages that have mass.

Time pools. In the deepest chasms where the warm rock is closest, sound lags. You hear your own careful footstep on moss three seconds after you make it, from behind you. The industrial forest does not seem to mind. A flowering engine will bloom, be pollinated by its own future self arriving early, and set seed before it opened.

If you stay still long enough, you notice the rules are not holding the landscape together as much as habit is. The plateau remembers what a plateau should do, and the jungle remembers what a jungle should do, and the factory remembers what a factory should do, and they are all remembering incorrectly together. There is no contradiction, just a heavy, beautiful, continuous error.

At night - though night is only a deeper amber - the whole vast high country cools just enough for the condensation to become rainless rain. A fine drizzle condenses out of nothing, out of the warm breath of the stone meeting the cooling air, and beads on every fin, every wire, every wing. The forest drinks it and exhales steam. The quiet deepens.

And in that quiet, in the dim, you can hear the continent working. Not building anything. Just working, endlessly, turbulently, lush and hot and damp atop its sharp young throne in the sky, too busy becoming to ever be finished.
