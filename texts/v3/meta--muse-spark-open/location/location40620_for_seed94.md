The High Sponge.

That is the only name that fits it, though calling it a plateau is like calling an ocean a lake. It is high. Not high like a peak, not high like a range you climb and descend. High like a new ground floor for the world was laid down, miles up, and left to weather.

The air up here is thin enough to make your lungs work for every breath, and cool — a late-spring cool that never leaves. Not freezing. Just cold enough that sweat never quite forms, that when you exhale the cloud hangs for a second too long before it is taken. The sun is normal here, a clean white-yellow sun at a normal distance, but the light has to come through a lot of air getting up here, and then through the breath of the plateau itself. So everything is bright without being harsh. No gloom, no alien dimness. Day is day. Cloud-shadow moves across the ground like a slow animal.

And the ground.

The plateau was lifted a long time ago. You can tell. This is not young, broken, jutting rock. This is old uplift, settled into itself. Its edges are not cliffs so much as vast stairsteps, each step a mile wide, each riser softened by a million seasons of water. The basalt underneath shows through only where it has to — black, column-jointed, lichen-welded — and everywhere else it is buried under itself, under the deep living mat it has grown.

Because nothing is bare. That is the first law here: no room.

From a distance, with your eyes unfocused by scale, it looks like flat tundra moss. Then you get closer, and closer still — you walk for a day and the horizon does not change because the horizon is thirty miles away and covered in the same thing — and you realize the shag has dimension.

There is no bare ground. No rock, no soil, no standing water that is not immediately rimmed, roofed, and drinking. The vegetation is not a forest in the way lowlands understand forest, with trunks and clearings. It is a packing. A saturation.

At the base: peat twelve feet deep that quakes when you step on it, black-brown and wet as a wrung sponge, made of a thousand generations of itself, still cold inside. Growing out of that peat is a thicket of dwarfed, twisted alpine birch and stone-pine, but stunted and dense as coral, their branches interlaced until you cannot tell one tree from the next. And growing on those branches is moss, and growing on that moss is moss, and growing on that moss is a flowering felt of liverworts and tiny white orchids no bigger than a fingernail, and threading through all of it are vines as thin as thread that carry water up and down like veins.

It is cool and it is wet. That is the second law.

It should be dry up here. High places are dry. But the plateau makes its own weather. The air hits the escarpments on all sides and is forced up and wrings itself out, and by the time it reaches the top it is cloud. Low cloud, two hundred feet up, that never quite decides to be cloud or fog. It condenses on every leaf. The rocks weep constantly. Not streams — though there are those, blackwater rivers a quarter-mile wide that move so slowly you only know they move because the flowers on them drift — but actual seeping rock. The basalt sweats. Every exposed face has a film of water running down it into the peat, and the peat squeezes it back out when you press it with your hand. You can hear it if you hold still, which this place forces you to do.

Sound does not carry here. That is the strangest thing for a place so busy.

You would think lush and packed and full of animals would be loud. It is not. The moss and the fog and the peat eat it. This is a vast, active quiet. You hear close things very clearly: the plink of water from a birch leaf to a bromeliad cup two feet below. The sigh of peat settling. Your own boots with a soft, wet squeak that is gone instantly.

And further away, muffled by distance and mass, the industry.

Because there is nothing built here. Nothing made. No walls, no roads, no towers, no cut stumps, no hearths. No straight line that was put there by a hand. And yet the place is industrial.

Not metaphorically. Functionally.

In the beginning you think the clicking is insects. A whole hillside of beetles rubbing their legs. Then you see one of the hillocks that dots the plateau every few hundred yards — perfect domes, twenty feet across, covered in the same dense felt as everything else — shiver and exhale a puff of warm steam into the cool air. The click deepens into a thud. Thud. Thud. Thud. Ten seconds between thuds.

You peel back the moss, carefully, because everything here is so intergrown that pulling one thing pulls five others, and you find it is not rock under there. It is not earth. It is a chamber, a bellows-lung as big as a house, made of root-fiber and resin and a kind of chitinous bark that has grown into a piston. Inside it, mineral water rich with dissolved iron and manganese from the basalt below is being drawn up through osmotic roots and heated — not by fire, there is no fire here, the whole place is too wet to burn — by slow geothermal heat and by the composting rot of the peat itself, which smolders coldly at 90 degrees year round. The chamber boils it, concentrates it, and then — thud — hammers it.

The hammer is a nodule of magnetite grown like a stalactite, swinging on a tendon of living fiber. Every thud it strikes an anvil of layered silica that another part of the organism has secreted. Flakes of native copper and black iron peel off and are caught in a mesh of fungal threads to be carried away, atom by atom, to be plated onto leaves, onto buds, onto the air-roots that need stiffening.

The whole plateau does this. Millions of those domes, those hearts, those forges. Some make copper leaf. Some make thread-thin wires of electrum that hum when the summer thunderstorms roll across the top — the plateau is large enough to make its own lightning, daily at 3 p.m., a grid of strikes that walk from one end to the other for an hour and are caught, stored, bled slowly through miles of vine-wire that glow faintly at night without ever sparking a fire because everything is wet. Some domes are not forges at all but kilns, where lime is baked from shell deposits left when this was a seafloor, before it was lifted.

No one built them. They grew. They evolved. The trees and mosses and fungi and colonial animals here have divided labor the way a factory town divides labor, only without a town. The peat is the coal seam. The weeping rocks are the ore conveyor. The fog is the cooling system.

And the fauna — because there is fauna everywhere, if you know how to look for density — are the workers, and the managers, and the product.

There are no large predators that crash through brush, because there is no brush to crash through, only packing. Instead there are hares the size of mice that tunnel through moss-highways, and pine-coiling weasels that swim through peat, and birds that don't so much fly as barge through the thick air on short, loud wings for ten feet before landing again. Everything is abundant and small and busy. You see a patch of lichen shiver and it is a thousand mites moving ore dust. You think a flower is a flower and it opens to show you it is a nest of glass-winged bees, each carrying a droplet of water to a forge-dome.

The weirdness is quiet, too. At 1.2 out of 3, it is not that physics are broken here, just bent toward purpose. Water runs uphill sometimes, but only because a root siphon is pulling it. The magnetic stones make your compass spin slowly, but only because the ground beneath you is being slowly electroplated. You find a pool that is perfectly rectangular, not because it was dug, but because four buttress roots grew toward each other at right angles over a century to dam it for their forge's quenching needs.

If you stay long enough — and the cool makes you want to keep moving, even though moving is hard — you start to see the plateau breathing. Not with lungs. With industry. In the morning, condensation gathers. By midday, the forges are thudding. By afternoon, the storm comes and charges the vine-grid, and all the copper leaves turn their faces up at once, not to the sun, but to the charge, and for a moment the entire continent-wide mat glints like a circuit board under the fog.

Then the rain comes, cold and straight down, and everything hisses briefly, and goes quiet again. The water runs down weeping rocks. The peat drinks. The thudding resumes, a little slower.

There was never anyone here to build anything. There never needed to be.
