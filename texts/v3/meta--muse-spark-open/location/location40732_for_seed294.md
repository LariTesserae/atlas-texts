You are under it.

Not under as in beneath a surface you can see above you — there is no surface here. There is only water, column after column of it, black-blue and cold enough to steal the heat from your blood before you feel the bite. This is a high place that has never seen air.

A seamount rises out of abyssal darkness, a blind mountain whose peak stopped a long mile below where any wave ever broke. On land it would be alpine, thin-aired and wind-scoured. Here, it is a plateau in the deep, and because light gives up long before it reaches this depth, the dimness is total and even. Not black. Dim, like late dusk held permanently in liquid glass. Light filters down as a rumor, a vertical pressure more than a color, just enough to turn the cold into a visible thing: motes of silt, snow-slow marine fall, drifting down forever without ever landing.

Nothing greens here. The temperature has edited plants out. No kelp, no algae mat —- at 0.4 it is too cold for anything that needs sun, and anyway, there is no sun left to use. This is a mineral world.

And everything here is built.

That is the first wrongness you register, then realize it isn't wrongness at all. There is no rock showing through. No basalt, no natural slope. The entire mountain — its terraces, its cols, its wide, shallow valleys that extend past seeing — is construction. Not poured concrete, not girders, not a city drowned. Nothing with tool-marks. Technology reads zero.

It is architecture without architects.

Generation upon generation of blind builder-fauna have made their world out of themselves. Tube worms the length of old trees, colonial mat-builders, filter-casters that excrete calcium lace like frost. All dead now, or rather, all *used* — their bodies are the brick. You swim over avenues of fused chimney-tubes, smooth and white-grey as old bone, flared at the mouth where a thousand mouths once fed. You pass ramparts made of honeycombed chambers, each cell large enough to crawl inside, now empty and whistling faintly as current passes through. Low domes cling to slopes, perforated like lung tissue. Spires that were once active chimneys for hot vent-water stand blunted and capped, turned into cairns.

It is all deeply, impossibly aged. Erosion at 2.8 does not mean crumbling — it means finished. This reef-mountain has been standing long enough for water itself to sand it down to its final shape. Edges are round. Corners do not exist. Every wall leans into the curve it was always going to become. Passages between structures have been widened and polished by millennia of slow current until they are throats, troughs, glides. Sediment has filled what was sharp; sponge-stone has grown over what was seam. You could run your hand along a kilometer of wall and never feel a break. It feels primordial, not ruined but resolved — a place that has settled into the last stable configuration it will ever have.

It is open country. From any rise — and there are many gentle rises, stepped terraces that fall away toward the dark edges of the plateau — you can see a long way through the cold clarity. A few hundred meters, sometimes half a kilometer before the blue eats distance. That is enough to understand scale: valley-sized, town-sized, but made of no town. Sparse, scattered. Structures do not crowd. They stand apart with water and silence between them, respectful distances. A field of low domes like skulls half-buried. A lone archway, thirty feet high, freestanding, serving no purpose now except to frame empty water beyond it. A boulevard of parallel tube-ranges running along contour lines for longer than you can follow, before curving out of sight.

The builders are gone or dormant. The inhabitants remain.

Fauna is abundant, but not dense. Abundance here doesn't mean noise or packing. It means wherever you look, something is moving, alone.

Pale things, most of them. Unpigmented from living in dusk. Some big as doors, slow and neutrally buoyant, trailing veils that barely move. Long filter kites that flex once a minute to hold position. Swarms — suddenly — of smaller things like darts of glass, hundreds strong, that appear from inside a chamber-wall, pour through an arch in a single decision, and vanish into another wall without a sound. Bottom-skimmers on hair-fine cilia that comb the mineral dust. Hangers, anchored by one filament to the lip of a spire, swaying. None of them make much noise. The water carries sound too well here, so evolution has opted for quiet. What you hear is mostly your own passage if you have one, and otherwise:

The creak of the mountain breathing. A very low sigh through those lung-chambers as pressure shifts.

The faint chime when current plucks the rim of an empty tube just right.

The soft clack of closing shells, far off, irregular.

And under and over everything — the turbulence. 

This is not a still ruin. For all its age, for all its polished quiet, this place is in storm-state. Dynamic 3.0 in a cold, dim ocean does not look like thrashing waves. It looks like contradictory, hyperactive flow.

Currents come down off the high ground and split and collide. Cold falls meet slightly-less-cold rises, and where they meet, vortices spin up out of nowhere — horizontal tornadoes ten feet across that live for thirty seconds and tear a cloud of silt and dartfauna into a spiral before collapsing. Snowfall reverses and goes up. A whole field of veil-creatures will suddenly fold and dive together as a pressure front rolls through like invisible weather. A tube-field will start to shudder as water is forced through every bore at once, humming, before going slack.

Things build and unbuild in fast-forward. A swarm will coat a dome in a living plaster in an hour, then strip it clean the next. A wandering colony of shell-cutters is gnawing a new passage through the base of a rampart while you watch, white mineral dust puffing behind them like saws in stone, working with mindless bazaar-energy. Behind them, cleaners already sort the dust.

No plan. Anthill energy. Endlessly busy, endlessly unsettled. Solaris-like in the sense that the entire plateau seems to be a single restless organism trying out forms and discarding them between tides that never stop changing direction.

There is a little weirdness to it, too, the 0.7 that keeps you from naming it reef or hive. Sometimes the water itself seems structured — you swim through a lens of colder and warmer interleave and the dim light refracts into a faint, moving grid for a few seconds, as if the ocean here remembers lattice. Sometimes a building pulses without occupant, a slow peristalsis along its length, though nothing alive is attached to it. Sometimes a school of darts stops mid-water, all facing the same impossible angle, as if listening to something tilted out of your geometry.

But if you stay — hover, get cold, let your eyes adjust until dim becomes normal — the weirdness folds back into the ordinary logic of a made place with no makers left to explain it. An entire mountain made of homes. Deeply old, worn smooth as river stones, spread wide under miles of black water, empty enough to feel holy, busy enough to never be still, quiet enough to hear the planet turn through its own deep sea.
