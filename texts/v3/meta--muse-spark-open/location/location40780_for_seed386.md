It is a high place that should not be hot.

Three thousand meters up and more — a lifted world, a plateau severed from the lowlands — and the air should thin and cool, should turn to stone and scree and alpine wind. It hasn't. The heat stays. Not lowland heat, not sweltering jungle-heat, but a high, bright, close heat that presses down from a white sky and rises back up from the rock itself. You feel it on the crown of your head and on the soles of your feet at the same time.

This is the summit of a massif that never finished becoming a mountain. Long ago it was thrust up in one great, stubborn block, basalt and red sandstone cooked and compressed, and then the weather went to work on it and learned patience. This is not a sharp young range. Its cliffs are immense - a thousand feet of vertical weep-stone, black-red and stepped - but they are weathered into softness, draped. The top is not a peak but a broad, undulating table, kilometers across, cut through by shallow canyons and hanging basins. A valley the size of a township, lifted into the clouds. Open enough that you can see weather coming from an hour away, and closed enough that everything that happens here stays here.

Water is the law of this place.

It is everywhere without ever becoming a lake. The air itself wrings itself out. Clouds come in low from the amazon below, hit the hot wall of the massif, are forced up and cool just enough to condense, and then they snag on the plateau and sit there, a cold breath on hot skin. The water precipitates not so much as rain but as continuous exhalation. You walk through suspended water. Your shoulders are damp within minutes. Every leaf drips. Every rock weeps.

The plateau cannot hold water, so it lets it go slowly. There is no soil to speak of - just a thin black peat over stone - but the vegetation has solved it by becoming soil itself. Mats of bromeliads and mosses a meter deep, spongy and saturated, red-green and ochre, hold the water like a living aquifer. You step and the whole mat sighs and releases a sheet of clear water that runs for a while and sinks again. Springs erupt mid-face on the cliffs for no visible reason, pouring from solid rock in cold bright arcs that fall six hundred feet and atomize before they hit bottom, feeding nothing and everything. In the shallow basins, the water braids into tea-colored marshes, ankle-deep, warm as blood, laced with white cotton-grass and carnivorous things — pitcher plants as big as your forearm, sundews glittering with glue.

And it is lush with a ferocity that belies the stone. This should be a desert of rock and wind. Instead it is a suspended jungle. Dwarf cloud-forest, but nothing stunted about its appetite.

Trees here do not grow tall — the wind and the thinness of the peat forbid it — so they grow *dense*. They grow into each other. Twisted tepui-oaks and wax-laurels, their bark black and slick with algae, their branches loaded with epiphytes until each branch looks like a garden in itself. Orchids, tanks, ferns, threads of lichen hanging like wet hair. Beneath them, a second canopy at knee height: giant terrestrial bromeliads with saw-toothed tanks that hold liters of trapped rainwater, each a pond complete with tadpoles and beetle larvae. The air between is not empty; it is filled with suspended roots, with vines as thick as your wrist that never bothered to find the ground, with sheets of moss bridging tree to tree. Light comes down normal and white, filtered through the high thin cloud, and turns green as it filters through three layers of leaves. Everything condenses, drips, steams.

It is dense and it is inhabited. Not crowded — saturated.

Insects first: clouds of stingless bees moving like smoke from flower to flower, wasps the size of thumbs working pulp, dragonflies with wingspans as wide as your hand patrolling the marsh channels. Frogs everywhere — you hear none of them now, in the heat of the day, but you see them: tiny gold and cobalt poison frogs on the bromeliad leaves, larger bellied tree-frogs asleep inside the pitchers. Birds move in bursts — swifts that nest behind the waterfall veils, screaming once and gone, iridescent hummingbird-moths working the high orchids, a hawk-like caracara that walks instead of flies, flipping bromeliads for grubs.

And in the warm marsh water, it is *active*. Things hunt in complete silence. A freshwater crab the color of rust slides under peat. A snake the thickness of a rope, banded black-yellow-black, waits with just its nostrils above the waterline. Tadpoles scatter. The whole system turns over quickly — bloom, eat, die, rot, bloom again — the heat forcing everything through its cycle faster than a temperate mind expects. You can see decay happening in real time: a fallen flower browning and sinking into the moss in an afternoon, already colonized.

There is nothing built here. Not a wall, not a cairn, not a cut path. No human has lived here; the cliffs see to that. The stone is unmarked except by water.

And yet - the industry.

You hear it before you understand it. A low, deep thrum that is not wind. A sighing of pressure released, a churning.

The plateau itself is a factory without walls.

On the eastern third, the hot rock comes close to the surface. You know it by the change in the plants: here the mosses give way to sulfur-yellow crusts, to reeds that tolerate near-boiling. Here the water does not just weep, it *exhales*. Pools of milky water, two or three meters across, sit perfectly still and perfectly round, their edges beaded with silica like lace. Every few minutes one shudders and empties itself with a great, soft *whoompf* — not a geyser that shoots, but a bellows-breath that lifts a tower of steam twenty feet into the air and lets it collapse back down as scalding rain. All day, on and off, the basin breathes. 

Further on, natural cisterns have formed in the peat, black and tannic, stepped one above the other like refining vats. Each overflows its lip into the next through a narrow gut where the water runs fast and hot, polishing the stone. Algae in each vat is a different color — rust-red in the hottest, viridian in the cooling one, a dense black-green in the last — cultivating itself, thickening, harvesting sunlight. In the outlet streams, mats of bacteria extrude threads of raw fiber, pale and strong, that tangle and collect on snags like industrial output. Insects farm it, cut it, carry it away in industrious lines.

And the sound — the *industrial* sound — is the sound of distillation. Condensers by the thousands: the huge leaves of the mountain gunnera, each three feet across, cupped to catch the cloud. They funnel water through grooved stems that whistle faintly as water rushes through, narrowed and pressured, spitting into the bromeliad tanks below. The tanks themselves click and tick as heat expands their trapped air, little pressure valves releasing. The whole marsh, at noon, purrs and ticks and hisses like a refinery at low idle. Not loud. Mostly quiet. You have to stand still inside it to hear how much work is being done.

It is unusual, but only just enough to make you look twice. The water running *uphill* for a moment on a slick of microbial oil before finding its level. The stones that are warm to the touch even underwater. The way the steam, in the late afternoon when the cloud finally lifts a little, forms at waist height for a moment into perfect, slow-rolling torus rings before breaking, as if the air itself were being machined.

The whole valley is established. It has been doing this for a very long time. The cliffs are settled in their drip, the pools in their pulse, the trees in their intergrowth. It is not a pristine wilderness waiting to be used. It is already in full production, in complete balance — a self-running engine of water and heat and green, making more of itself, spilling its surplus over the cliff edges in white threads to the rainforest far, far below, asking nothing, building nothing, needing no hand.
