This is not a valley that flooded. This is a mountain that drowned.

You are high up — very high — and still deep underwater.

Long after the ocean rose, or perhaps before it ever fell, a range was born here fast and sharp. Young rock. You can tell by looking. Nothing has been softened. The seafloor thrust upward in a single, cool shrug and never had time to weather. Black basalt and pale, almost white anorthosite, split in clean planes. No rolling slopes. Only knife-ridges, step-faults like a staircase for giants, chimneys and needles that end in points so fine they look like they could puncture the water itself. It is a mountaintop, but you would never know it from contour, because there is no air to give it away.

The sea is here too. All of it. Cold, clear to an impossible distance, and utterly still.

It does not move. That is the first wrong thing you notice. There is no current at this depth, no sway of particulate, no slow drift of cold on cold. The water column above you is kilometers thick — a weight that should be crushing — and it hangs perfectly motionless, like glass poured over the world and left to set. Light from far above comes down bent and thinned to almost nothing, a deep indigo dusk that never brightens or dims. It is always just before night here. It will always be just before night here.

Perched on that sharp, fresh summit is a town.

Not ruins. Not wreckage. Occupied, maintained, intact — but sparse the way a monastery is sparse, or a research station. A hamlet-sized city, small enough you could walk its bounds in half an hour, dense with *intention* rather than people. Its boundaries are abrupt: a sheer drop on all sides where the young cliffs fall away into the dark, and beyond that, nothing. The water holds the edge.

The construction is urban in the truest sense: layered, planned, interlocked. Terraces cut directly into the living stone, not built atop it. Streets are narrow canyons between basalt walls, stairwells that switch back on themselves, plazas that are really just wide landings. Everything follows the mountain's own geometry. There was no attempt to flatten the peak; they let the peak dictate the plan. The buildings themselves are low, heavy, windowless on their lower floors — pressure architecture — made of the same mountain stone fused smooth, and of a second material that was not quarried here at all: a matte, dark metal-ceramic that drinks the little light there is and gives nothing back, warm to the touch despite the cool water.

And then there is the third material, which is not a material.

Between certain walls, across certain avenues, over the central well — the water itself has been taught to behave differently. You see it as faint seams in the dark, like the surface of a soap bubble seen edge-on. Right angles in the ocean. Flat planes of stillness meeting stillness. The town is held inside a lattice of these planes. Inside the lattice, pressure is negotiable. Gravity is slightly less. The water does not press; it *holds*. It has been convinced to act as structure, as roof, as wall, as air. You can swim through a street and then, crossing an invisible threshold, find yourself standing on dry stone with water arching over you like a vaulted ceiling, not dripping, not flowing, simply present — a liquid sky that never ripples. In other places you are fully submerged and breathing water that breathes for you, oxygen parsed out by fields you cannot see.

This is post-modern technology so far beyond scaffolding and steel that it stops looking like technology at all. It looks like weather that decided to be architecture. No joints, no rivets. Fields maintain edges so sharp you could cut your hand on a shadow. The whole town hums with the kind of power that doesn't need to be generated anymore, just *permitted*.

There is no green here. No kelp forest draping the ridges, no algae slick on stone. The rock is bare, scrubbed clean. In the dark and cold and perfect stillness, nothing photosynthesizes. Even the engineered fields keep it out — a sterility not hostile, just absolute. What grows here, if you can call it growth, is mineral: thin black crusts of manganese that feather out from warm seams in the stone in fractal frosts, and pale, slow crystals that gather on the undersides of ledges like salt. They are not alive. They are just accretion, patient and mindless.

Animals are rare to the point of event. Once in a long while a thing passes high above the town — a pale, elongated shape with no discernible front or back, perhaps ten meters long, moving without undulation, just translating through the still water like a thought. It does not hunt. It does not seem to see the town. The locals, few as they are, do not watch it either. Once, you might see a shoal of tiny, translucent somethings flicker between buildings, each no larger than an eyelash, holding formation with impossible precision before vanishing between two planes of structured water.

People are here, but sparsely. You see evidence more than inhabitants: a light left on in a cut-stone room behind a water-wall, amber and steady. A set of tools laid out on a terrace — long, forked instruments that never rust — left for hours untouched. A figure in a dark, close-fitting suit walking slowly down a submerged stair, not swimming, not floating, boots finding purchase where physics says they shouldn't. No hurry. No sound to carry their passage.

Because there is almost no sound. The mountain makes none; it's too young to settle and too locked to quake. The water makes none; it does not move. The city makes none; its machines operate without vibration. What you hear is mostly yourself: your own blood, your own breath if you're breathing anything at all, and underneath that a very faint, high tone at the edge of hearing — not a machine, but the lattice itself, maintaining its impossible truce with the ocean. It never wavers. It never glitches.

That is the rule of this place: nothing changes.

Erosion does not happen here because the water has been forbidden to erode. Time does not really pass here, not in the way it does elsewhere. Metal does not pit. Stone does not round. A scratch you left yesterday will be there in a thousand years, exactly as deep, exactly as bright. Even the light never shifts. It is equilibrium made into law, a pocket of frozen stasis balanced on a needle of fresh rock under the weight of an ocean that forgot how to flow.

If you stayed, you would not age quite right. Your thoughts would become very clear and very slow, like the water.

The mountain holds. The town holds. The sea holds its breath — and never exhales.
