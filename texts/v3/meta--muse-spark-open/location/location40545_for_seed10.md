This place shouldn't exist at this altitude. But it does.

You are on a high country that has forgotten how to be a mountain.

### The High Fen

Elevation here is not a peak. It is a lift — a vast, ancient tableland heaved up two kilometers into the cold air a long time ago and then left alone for so long that it has resigned itself to flatness. This is an erosion rating of 3.0/3: not jagged, not alpine, not dramatic in the young-mountain way. This is primordial old. The wind and water have had epochs to work. The basalt and gneiss have been sanded down to a peneplain, a skin of exposed bedrock so old it has polished itself smooth as a river stone the size of a county. You can walk for a half a day and gain only ten meters, and then lose it in a shallow bowl a kilometer wide. It is high, and it is level, and because it is level, it cannot drain.

So it stays wet.

Water is at 2.4 — not an ocean, not quite a swamp, but a post-rain that never ends. The air itself is wet. At this temperature, just above freezing — 0.5/3, a steady, lung-biting cold that never quite commits to winter — the atmosphere condenses on everything. The low clouds don't pass over; they snag. They drag their bellies across the plateau and wring themselves out. Water weeps directly out of the rock. The ancient stone sweats. Every shallow declivity is a mirror mere, black water a foot deep over peat over stone, with no banks, just a gradual darkening. You hear trickling everywhere even when it isn't raining, because the plateau is leaking from underneath. Springs well up without pressure, cold enough to ache your teeth, clear as glass, then spread into braids that have no channel to follow and so go nowhere, just sheeting a few millimeters deep over moss for miles.

And the vegetation. 2.7/3. This is not a forest on top of land. The plants *are* the land.

There is no soil here, not really — the old rock wouldn't allow it. So the plants learned to be soil. A continuous, living mattress covers the stone: sphagnum and liverwort and frost-lichen and black-green peat mosses a meter thick, so saturated that stepping on it releases a slow well of brown water around your boot that takes minutes to subside. From that mattress, the architects grow.

They are sparse — density is only 0.6 — which makes them more immense. Not a jungle press but a scattered cathedral grove on an open scale, regional, valley-wide. Every few hundred meters, a single organism erupts. Call them Crowntrees, though they are not trees. Each one is a column of braided, living fiber as thick as a house, thirty, forty meters tall, leafless, made of layer after layer of vascular sheath that peels in wet curtains. At the top they splay into a shallow bowl, a rosette of fronds the diameter of a cul-de-sac, which catches cloud and rain and holds it. They are cisterns. They are islands.

From the rim of each Crown, a skirt of aerial roots hangs down like rigging, thirty meters of black root-hair dripping constantly. The roots never quite reach the peat-mat below — they sway a few meters above it, weeping. And between Crowns, nothing but the breathing peat and the meres and the cold distance. You can see three, four of them at once against the dim light, receding into haze. There is a lot of sky here. Too much. The openness is unsettling.

Because there is nothing built here. 0.5/3. No roads, no walls, no ruins, not even a cairn. No hand has stacked these stones. And yet —

### The Industry Without Builders

Tech is 1.8/3. Industrial. And you hear it before you understand it.

Each Crowntree is loud. Sound is a 1.6 — not deafening, but insistently loud, a low industrial thrum that carries for kilometers over flat water.

The bowls at the top work. In the dim light — 1.4/3, a perpetual overcast dawn, sun a brighter smear behind cloud-ice — the Crowns photosynthesize weakly, but mostly they *process*. They pull the cold, mineral-rich seep water up through the peat, up through their trunk-fibers, and boil it. Not with heat — with pressure and chemistry. Inside the trunk you can hear it: a churning, a thudding peristalsis like a factory press, like a heart the size of a locomotive. The trunks pulse. Steam — not warm, just a shade warmer than the air — vents in rhythmic sighs from pores along the trunk, condensing instantly into the dripping root-beards. The whole plateau sighs and hisses and clanks.

And the water that collects in the top bowl is expelled. Not gently. Every seven or eight minutes, a Crowntree fires. The bowl contracts all at once and a column of processed water, hot now, acrid with ozone and iron, shoots twenty meters into the cold air and falls as scalding rain. You hear the *WHUFF* and then the hammering patter. Around the base of each Crown, the peat is scalded into a black ring where nothing soft grows, only a crust of rust-red mineral bacteria that crackles as it precipitates.

The Crowns are factories with no design. They are not built. They evolved to be industrial, or industry evolved to be them. They manufacture nothing but weather. They take in cold seep and put out cloud. The plateau breathes through them, engine-like, electrified in a biological way: on dim days you see Saint Elmo's fire crawling over the wet fronds, blue-white discharge snapping between root-tips without ever striking ground.

You think at first the sound is just the Crowns. Then you realize the fauna is louder.

Fauna 2.4 — abundant — but also sparse like everything else. Not insects swarming. Megafauna, but not many species. Mostly one thing, in countless numbers.

They are called Shroud-Grazers by the part of your brain that needs a name. They are translucent, low-slung, the size of elk but with no head to speak of, just a forward-facing filter-mouth as wide as their bodies. Six stilt legs that punch through peat to stone. Their backs are covered in that same rust-red bacterial felt. They wade slowly through the shallow meres, vacuuming peat-moss, water, bacteria, everything, and excreting a fine, steaming silt behind them that smells of iron and pulp-mill.

They are drawn to the Crowntrees. They need the heat. Between firings, they crowd at that scalded ring — ten, twenty of them around a single Crown, otherwise solitary for kilometers — shoulder to shoulder, steaming in the cold, waiting. When the Crown fires, they tilt their backs to catch the hot rain and shudder. The bacteria on their backs bloom visibly, fizzing.

And then the dynamic hits. 2.8/3. Turbulent. Hyperactive. This place is never still.

Because the weirdness is high — 2.7/3, reality breakdown — and the plateau cannot hold its shape.

The Crowns do not stay put. Not quite. Over the course of an hour you will swear the one to the north was west before. The distance between them changes when you blink. The peat-mat itself ripples, slowly, like a waterbed, even though there is stone beneath. Sometimes a mere drains straight down through a hole that wasn't there, revealing for a few seconds perfect, dry stone thirty meters down that reflects no light, before water rushes back.

The Shroud-Grazers flicker. A herd will be there, and then only their legs, walking without bodies, and then bodies without legs, still filtering. Their hot-rain baptism sometimes falls upward, returning to the bowl that fired it. Sometimes a Crown fires and the water hangs in the air, a perfect glass cylinder, for too long, before deciding to fall sideways.

The industrial thud of the Crowns stutters. A trunk will double its beat, then beat out of phase with itself, producing a pressure wave you feel in your molars. The electrified discharge between rootlets occasionally writes things — not words, but diagrams, schematics for machines that would not work in this cold, in this air pressure — and then erases them.

The rule here is that cold + wet + ancient + high should equal barren. Tundra. Lichen and wind. Instead, because the plant became the land, and the land became a factory without a builder, and the animals became its maintenance crew, the plateau has become a turbulent, loud, dim wetland in the sky that manufactures its own weather and then forgets how physics should handle it.

You stand on living sponge over stone older than continents, in ankle-deep cold water that has just been inside a plant-engine, under dim cloud that was just exhaled by that same plant, watching a scattered herd of translucent grazers jostle for scalding rain that is currently falling diagonally, listening to the endless *THUMP... THUMP... HISSSS... WHUFF* of the Crowns across an open flat that extends past sight, breathing air that tastes of iron and ozone.

Nothing was ever built here. Everything is working.

And if you stay until the light dims further — which it will, because the Crowns are making more cloud than the sky can hold — you will see the root-beards of the nearest Crown finally brush the peat. And take hold.
