The High Arbor — The City That Grew Itself Over

It is not built *on* the mountain. It is what the mountain became after it stopped being a mountain.

Elevation here is 2.5 on the old maps, but that doesn't mean peaks. It means height without steepness. You are on the roof of the continent, where an ancient range has been grinding itself down for so long it has forgotten how to be jagged. This is ancient erosion — 2.6/3 — end-of-life topography. The hills are vast, balding domes of gneiss and basalt polished to a dark, lichenous sheen, separated by U-shaped troughs a kilometer wide where glaciers used to sit like gods. Nothing is sharp anymore. Everything has settled into its final form: rounded shoulders, shallow bowls, long slow valleys that run for days. You can see weather coming from three counties away because there is nothing to stop it.

And everything is cold. Not freezing — cool, 1.0/3, a permanent early October. The air is thin and clean and it makes steel ring louder. Your breath smokes in the morning, but by noon you can work in shirtsleeves if you keep moving. The stone holds the cold like a memory.

Water is scarce the way it is scarce at altitude — damp, not wet. 1.0/3. There are no lakes up here, no oceans. Instead there is condensation. Every night the cool air wrings itself out against the plant mass and by dawn every leaf, every cable, every rivet is beaded with cold dew that drips, gathers, runs. Intermittent rain comes sideways without clouds seeming to form — a ten-minute hammering that vanishes. Riverine threads cut through the troughs — not powerful rivers, but persistent ones, brown-green, braided through roots, disappearing underground into root-caverns and reappearing two valleys over, surprised to still exist. Pockets of water hang suspended in the canopy itself, great axillary cisterns between leaves the size of billboards, sloshing quietly. You never die of thirst here, but you never swim either.

Because vegetation is total. 3.0/3.

This is not a forest covering a city. This is a forest that *is* the city, or a city that learned to be a forest to survive its own age.

One species — or maybe one organism — did it. The Arbor. No trunk, no canopy in the traditional sense, but a colonial, architectural plant that decided verticality was inefficient and spread laterally across the entire high plateau. Its leaves are structural: waxy, veined with lignin as hard as timber, some translucent green-gold and lit from within, some opaque and black-green and thick as a mattress. Its stems are load-bearing, as thick as subway tunnels, hollow, ribbed. Its aerial roots are streets, bridges, buttresses, hanging down a hundred feet then curving back up. It grows in layers, hundreds of feet deep, and we live inside the layers like insects that learned agriculture.

The built environment — urban, 2.4/3 — isn't imposed on this. It's negotiated with it.

You walk on the upper lamina — a leaf the size of a city block, slightly springy, its central vein raised like a median strip, beaded with living streetlamps that are actually phototropic nodules. Warehouses are grown, then reinforced: walls made of trained root-weave, sealed with resin, roofed with another leaf that was coaxed flat and kept alive. Apartment blocks are stacked in the intervessel gaps where three massive stems intersect, leaving triangular courtyards open to the pale sky. Staircases are spiral figs of hardened vine around old industrial rebar. Elevators run inside hollow petioles.

And everywhere — industrial, 1.9/3. This is not a quaint tree-village. This is electrified, mechanized, scaled production, but all of it adapted to living substrate.

The factories are loud. 2.3/3 loud.

On the windward dome where the trough wind funnels, a line of wind turbines turns — but their towers are living stalks that sway and correct. In the lower troughs, hydro-mills sit astride those shy brown rivers, their turbines choked seasonally with fallen leaf-matter that workers have to rake with pitchforks the size of oars. Everywhere there are sawmills — but they saw plant that was already grown to be sawn, coppicing buttress-roots on a thirty-year rotation. The cut surfaces weep amber sap for weeks and smell like turpentine and honey. Forges glow orange deep in root chambers, where the living wood has been charmed or scorched into fire-resistance, hammering out brackets, rails, loom parts, turbine gearboxes. The Arbor doesn't mind the smoke; it drinks it.

Sound carries for miles in the cool, open air. You hear it before you see it: the clack of automated looms weaving fiber stripped from epiphyte beards, the hiss of resin-boilers, the whine of cable-cars that run along tensioned vines from dome to dome, the low thrum of the district generators — huge steam hearts burning pressed leaf-pellets — and underneath it all, the biological creak. Because this place is active. 1.6/3 active.

The Arbor is growing while you live in it. While the shift whistle blows, a tendril as thick as your waist inches forward across an alley, blindly seeking a new anchor, and a crew will have to divert it with hot iron by evening. Leaves unfurl with an audible pop overhead, sudden as umbrellas. Flowers — rarely, at this altitude — bloom the size of houses, a deep bruised purple, for a single day, filling the valley with a peppery, metallic perfume and then collapsing into a sodden, steaming heap that the sanitation crews shovel into composters before it ferments. Water moves. Sap rises. Conveyors move. People move.

Scale is open. 2.4/3 — not infinite, not claustrophobic. You can stand on the edge of the Lamina District at dusk and see the whole arrangement: three great vegetated domes, each cradling a town of fifteen thousand, connected by vine-bridges that sag and bounce over the river-troughs, and beyond them, the pale, rolling, plant-smothered highlands fading into blue haze. You can walk from one end to the other in a day if you know the shortcuts through the pith-tunnels. You can also get lost for a week in the understory where the light doesn't reach and the old city — the first city, the one that tried to build in stone — lies entombed, its brick facades slowly being digested by polite, patient roots.

Density is sparse. 1.2/3. For all that sprawl, there aren't many people. Or rather, there is too much space between presences. The factories run with skeleton crews; automation does the rest. The apartments are half-empty; the Arbor keeps making more rooms than children are born to fill them. You can ride a cable-car alone for twenty minutes and see no one but the conductor and the crows' nests tangled in the cables. On night shift, a machinist might turn off her lathe and realize the only other living motion in a hall built for sixty is the slow pulse of the wall itself.

Fauna is rare. 0.9/3. Almost nothing else survived the Arbor's totality. A few species adapted: lichen-cats — small, gray, six-limbed things that glide between leaves and steal heat from vents. Condensate moths the size of dinner plates that drink from the hanging pools and die if you touch their wings. The stilt-herons that stalk the braided rivers, impossibly tall and silent, that perhaps are not birds at all but something the Arbor extruded to tend its waterways. Mostly though, it's just humans and plant and machine. The absence of animals is part of the quiet loneliness between the loudness.

Light is normal. 2.4/3 — not dim, not blinding. At this elevation, the sun is frank and white at noon, and the leaves filter it into a shifting, cathedral green. At night, the industrial amber of sodium lamps and forge-glow mixes with the Arbor's own bioluminescence — soft, steady strings of foxfire along the veins, brightening when stepped on. Shadows are crisp and then suddenly interrupted because the ground beneath you moved an inch while you weren't paying attention.

And then there is the weirdness. 2.6/3. Reality breakdown.

The Arbor is old enough and big enough that it has started to argue with physics.

Gravity is negotiable in the deep layers. Sap doesn't always fall down; sometimes it falls up and collects on the underside of a leaf in a trembling, inverted pond where workers have learned to bail it upwards.

Distance stutters. Walk the same root-bridge three times and on the third time you arrive before you left; your other self is still mid-stride behind you, frowning, and then is gone when you blink. The foremen mark these spots with yellow paint — "echo joints" — and tell apprentices not to linger.

Time pools where the plant flowers. Inside the shadow of a newly opened bloom, clocks run twice as fast; you age an hour having lunch. Outside, your soup is still warm.

And sometimes the Arbor remembers other places. A doorway cut for ventilation will open onto a different valley on a different dome, with different weather, though you only stepped through three feet of pith. A warehouse wall will briefly be transparent, and you will see the ancient, pre-plant mountain as it was — bare, granite, snow-capped — overlapping the present like a double exposure, before it resolves back to green.

No one is particularly alarmed by this anymore. The industrial logic absorbed it. Maintenance crews carry plumb lines that are actually living tendrils that point to true down, regardless of local weirdness. Schedules include ten minutes of "drift." Children grow up knowing not to chase a ball that rolls uphill into a root-knot, because it won't come back the same size.

Rules of the world here:

1. Tend the plant, or it tends you. Neglect a district and its walls will seal shut, digesting what's inside for nutrients.
2. Keep the fires contained but not extinguished. The Arbor needs ash.
3. If you hear your name called from an empty pith-tunnel, answer with someone else's name.
4. Never dam a river completely. Let some water escape, even if you need it. Moving water keeps the echo joints from widening.

At dusk, the shift ends with a single, enormous steam whistle that rattles dew off leaves for a mile. The Arbor shivers. Thousands of sparrows that shouldn't exist lift from somewhere — or maybe they are leaves themselves, mimicking birds — and wheel once in the cool air before vanishing. The forges bank low. The living streetlamps brighten. Somewhere deep in the third dome, a new leaf the size of a cathedral is still unfolding, loud as canvas in wind, pushing against the carefully laid brick of a human street, and no one has yet decided whether to cut it or let it become the new third avenue.

It smells like ozone and crushed green and coal smoke. It smells damp.
