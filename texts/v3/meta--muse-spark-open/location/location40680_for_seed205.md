It is high.

Not high like a mountain. High like a table left up in the sky after the rest of the world was taken away.

The Plateau — because there is only one — goes on until weather forgets where it came from. You can stand at its edge where the stone drops away in old, soft cliffs, not jagged but worn, rounded by time the way knuckles are rounded, and see nothing but air and haze and the faint, darker smudge of its opposite edge a continent away. That is the scale of it. It makes its own wind. Clouds are born in its interior and die before they can escape. Everything below is theoretical.

On top, it is hot. A high, thin, brutal heat. The sun at noon is white and straight-down, normal light but relentless, pressing the stone until it shivers with mirage at the edges of sight. The air is dry in your lungs and then, suddenly, not. Because this place is not wet, but it is damp.

There is no sea here. There are no lakes that you can draw on a map. The water comes up, not down.

The Plateau is covered — no, made — of plants. Not a forest. A forest has a floor. This has no floor. Vegetation is the substrate. Everything else exists on it or inside it.

They are not trees as you know trees. They are Holdings.

Each Holding rises a quarter mile apart from the next, and each one is a world. At the base, a trunk wider than a village, bark not bark but layered plates of cork and living leather, cool to the touch, fissured with vertical gardens. Higher up, it flares into a vast, inverted cone of interlocking leaves, each leaf the size of a sail, each leaf the size of a house roof, thick and waxy and scooped to catch the sky. The whole structure is a single organism, a bromeliad-god, a green cathedral. From a distance the Plateau looks sparse, almost empty — these huge, solitary crowns standing apart like islands in air, with wide savannas of golden, waist-high air-grass and lichen-crusted stone between them.

Up close, you understand there is nothing sparse about it.

In the spaces between the Holdings, the stone breathes. In the morning, before the heat becomes a weight, condensation blooms. All that trapped plant respiration from the night, all that heat-driven exhalation, cools against the high stone and beads. It pearls on the undersides of leaves, it frets the air-grass with diamonds, it runs in sudden, temporary rivers that are not rivers — a sheet of silver a finger deep that moves for an hour across a plain, reflecting sky, and then is gone, sucked back into root and crack and throat. The whole plateau drinks from the air. If you put your hand out, it will be wet in minutes, though it hasn't rained.

It does rain. Violently, locally, absurdly. A Holding will gather a storm over itself alone, a black puff born of its own heat, and dump it all in ten minutes of hammering drops on leaves as big as drums, a private monsoon, while a mile away the stone stays dry and hot and buzzing.

For buzzing, for sound — you cannot understand it until you are inside.

It is deafening. Not loud. Deafening as a rule of physics.

The Holdings are hives, not hives like bees — hives like sound. Everything lives here, overwhelmingly, impossibly. The leaves hum with beetles armored like lacquered seeds, each the length of your forearm, sawing their legs. Ribbons of wasps the color of heat-haze pass in rivers through the air. In the air-grass, things click and chirp and rattle with a metronomic frenzy that never pauses. Above, the crown-birds — not birds, membranes stretched between elongated fingers, translucent crimson — wheel and shriek in colonies of thousands, and when they turn together the air itself screams. And the singers in the trunk-fissures, the throat-frogs that have no water but have learned to keep it in sacs in their backs, inflate and call in basso-profundo booms that you feel in your molars.

You cannot speak and be heard more than three feet away. People here have given up trying. They flash mirrors, tug ropes, beat on the resonant leaves like gongs.

The people are scattered. That is the other sparse thing. You might walk three days between smoke traces. They do not build cities; the Holdings will not allow it. A Holding is territory and house and well and farm and god, and it defends its damp.

So the people — small, dark from sun, lacquered with plant oils — carve. That is their tech. Primitive, perfect. Hand-axes of fitted chitin, knives knapped from the black glass of the plateau's old volcano seams, rope braided from air-grass fiber that never rots. They do not cut the Holding. You cannot cut the Holding with these tools even if you tried for a lifetime; its skin heals over a scratch in hours. Instead they tend, they train. They coax a leaf to droop lower, to form a cistern. They scrape a hollow in a bark plate and line it with wax, and live there, a family to a fissure, smoky fire kept tiny in a clay cup. Between Holdings they have thrown suspension paths — not bridges, but thick living vines encouraged over generations to reach, to meet, to thicken, swaying a hundred feet above the stone. To cross one is to feel the whole plateau sway with you, and to hear the chorus break for a second around you, as every insect watches.

Erosion here is not destruction. It is settlement. Everything looks long-agreed-upon. The stone between Holdings is pitted into honeycombs where condensation pools have sat for ten thousand years, each pit polished smooth. The Holdings themselves are weathered, bearded with epiphytes, with curtains of silver moss that never see rain but drink fog, with orchids whose roots hang twenty feet in the air like nerves. Things grow on things that grow on things. A fallen leaf — when one finally falls, every thirty or forty years — does not rot. It becomes land, a damp, sagging plain itself, colonized instantly by grass and beetles and a new, fast, opportunistic vine that will die when the leaf finally crumbles to sweet, peppery dust.

And the strange. The weirdness you feel when the heat goes still.

At midday, when the sun is directly overhead and the sound reaches a peak that almost becomes silence, the Holdings transpire together. It is visible. A shimmer rises off every leaf-sail at once, a clear exhalation, and the air between them thickens. The light refracts. For a moment the sparse giants seem to shift, to be connected by arches of damp light, a vaulted ceiling made of evaporated water. You can see the weather being made. And sometimes, if you are standing on the stone with bare feet, you feel the plateau expand — a slow, hot inhalation — as the roots deep in the high stone swell with that stolen moisture, lifting the crust a hair's breadth before settling again. The old ones say the Plateau is breathing. The old ones do not mean it as a metaphor. They leave shallow bowls of water out at these times, not to collect, but to offer.

At night, it cools fast. The damp deepens to true wet. Every surface drips. The chorus changes key, lower, slower, but never stops. The sky is impossibly clear and close at this elevation, stars like cold sparks. The Holdings close their outer leaves a fraction, like sleeping hands, conserving what they caught. In the fissures, small fires glow like fireflies, impossibly far apart, and you realize how vast and alive and loud and solitary this place is — a continent-sized garden hanging in the hot, high air, drinking its own breath, roaring with life so dense it seems empty by contrast, waiting for its one rain cloud to be born tomorrow directly overhead.
