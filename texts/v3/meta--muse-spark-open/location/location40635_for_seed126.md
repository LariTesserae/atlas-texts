There is no horizon here, or rather there is only horizon.

You are standing - or whatever passes for standing - low down in it. Not on a hill, not in a valley, because this place has forgotten how to have either. Elevation is a memory. For a thousand miles in every direction the world is low. Near-flat. The kind of flat that is not young, not a plain scoured clean, but the flat that comes at the end of things when everything that could be worn down has been worn down. The world has settled into its final form.

And everything is made.

There is no bedrock under your feet. There is no soil. At some point, a long time ago, the planet - or whatever size this thing is, because it behaves like a planet but feels larger, continuous - was covered. Paved, grown, printed, assembled. A continental crust of construction. Not a city in the way cities are supposed to be, with streets and towers. Older than that. The Builders are gone, or they became the material, and what they left behind has had eons to soften.

Under the brutal, bright light, you can see the strata.

The lowest layer, at your feet, is warm to the touch. It has the color of fired ceramic, of sun-bleached bone, a matte off-white that goes down meters through fused layers. It is pitted. Wind has worried at it for geological ages with nothing but dust to work with. The pits are smooth on their windward sides, sharp on their leeward. Each one holds a little shadow. Between them run fissures, not cracks from earthquakes, but seams. The joints where plates the size of countries were laid down. They have opened by a hand's breadth, a body's width, and from them a dry heat exhales. Warm, always warm. Not hot enough to burn. Hot enough to make the air shimmer if it had water in it. It doesn't.

The air is thin, bright, completely clear. The sky is not blue. It is white-bright, a high glare without a visible sun, as if the atmosphere itself is luminous, or as if the entire vault of the world above is a light. You don't look up for long. The light has weight. It flattens everything, bleaches color, turns every edge into a hard line. There are no clouds. There cannot be. There is no water to make them.

What water there is here is a rumor.

Very rarely, in the deepest seams where the heat catches and condenses something else, you find it. Not pools. Beads. A line of black glass beads sweating out of a vertical wall, hanging there without falling. They are not water. They are colder than everything around them and they do not evaporate. If you touch one your finger comes away dry but smelling faintly of iron and ozone. Brine that never was brine. The sparse vegetation knows it.

Because there is vegetation. Barely.

It is not green. Nothing here can afford green. From the seams comes a sparse, dense kind of life - sparse across the vastness, dense where it finds a foothold. Every hundred meters, sometimes every kilometer, a seam erupts into a thicket. They call them wire-gardens though no one is left to call them anything. Thin stalks of what looks like spun basalt and copper wire, branching fractally, ending in flat, black, dusty leaves that track the bright sky. No chlorophyll. They drink the light directly and the chemical ghosts sweating from the seams. They grow pressed together, near-touching, a layered mat of filaments that rustle without wind. Each stalk is etched with microscopic script, or it is script. Growth and construction are the same thing here. If you break a leaf - and you shouldn't - it bleeds a slow, amber resin that hardens in seconds into something indistinguishable from the ground.

The thickets are loud with stillness. That's where the fauna are, when they are at all.

Rare. You can walk for a day and see nothing move but heat. Then a shiver in a wire-garden. A presence the size of a hand, the shape of a folded kite made of translucent film and dust, detaches itself from the stalks and glides low across the white. It does not flap. It tilts its surfaces to ride the thermal updrafts from the seams. It lands on another thicket and becomes invisible again, folding itself into leaf-shape. There are others - slow things like amber slugs that melt their way through the ceramic, leaving a polished trail that the wind will erase over a century; things like dandelion seeds the size of fists that drift for years on electrostatic charge, never landing. Nothing hunts visibly. The predation here is slow, chemical, a matter of stealing light and trace moisture from a neighbor filament by filament.

And yet the place is active.

Not bustling. Not chaotic. Active the way a desert is active if you watch it for ten thousand years. The heat cycles. By day - if day is the word for when the light is at its most absolute - the plates expand. You can hear it if you hold your breath and press your palm to the ground. A deep, almost subsonic tick. *Tik... tik... tik...* The seams narrow by a fraction. The wire-gardens tighten. At dusk - when the bright sky thins just enough to show a deeper white behind it - the plates contract. A long sigh runs for miles along a seam, dust lifting in a perfectly straight line that can run to the horizon and keep going. The dust itself is strange. It is not sand. It is the world grinding itself down: fine ceramic powder, flecks of dead leaf, skin-cells of kite-things, all charged, all levitating for hours before settling.

There are larger movements, too. Because the scale is too large for stillness.

Far off, where the white meets the white, the air sometimes darkens in a perfect circle hundreds of kilometers across. A weather system that has nothing to do with water. A pressure differential born from the heat of the deep plates. It moves with impossible slowness across the flat, a lens of slightly bent light and electrostatic haze. When it passes over, the wire-gardens all turn at once, leaves angling, and the floating kite-things rise in a silent spiral into the haze and vanish for days. The shadow it casts is cool, almost cold, and in its wake the seams weep those black beads a little more freely. Then it is gone, and the bright returns, absolute.

You understand, standing low in this vastness, that the weirdness is not dramatic. It's structural.

The ground hums faintly even when nothing moves. If you dig - and the surface gives way in papery layers - each layer is different: one is honeycombed with dead light, a lattice that still holds photons trapped like insects in amber; the next is solid and inscribed with patterns too large for a human mind to see as writing, only as topography; the next is soft and warm and gives slightly under pressure, like a lung. Gravity is slightly wrong. A dropped stone falls a little too slowly, and dust falls a little too evenly, as if the world is still, in some residual way, holding itself up.

This is a made place that has outlived making. A sealed environment that sealed itself so well it forgot what it was sealing out. A warm, bright, silent, ancient machine the size of a continent that has become a kind of nature again - eroded, settled, patient - growing its own sparse fur of wire and glass in the cracks of its own body.

It does not need you. It does not notice you. The light presses down, the heat radiates up through your feet, the seams tick, and far away another circle of weather is being born out of nothing, to drift for a month across an endless, low, white, constructed plain.
