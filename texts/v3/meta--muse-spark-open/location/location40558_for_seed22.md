The City After the Canopy

You are standing on low ground, but not flat ground. This is young land, newly made. The elevation barely changes for kilometers in any direction — a vast, warm basin at maybe a hundred meters above sea level — but it is not smoothed by age. Everything is sharp. The earth here hasn't had time to be worn down.

The underlying plate is fresh basalt, still warm to the touch in places, fractured into geometric terraces and sudden, clean-walled fissures as if the ground had cooled and cracked last week. Which, in geological terms, it did. The city was not built *on* this terrain. The city *is* this terrain. Or rather, it was grown, poured, extruded and then left to settle.

Because of that, the streets are not laid flat. They follow the fault-steps. A single avenue might run for two kilometers dead straight, then drop three meters in a sheer, moss-softened stair, then continue. The buildings do the same — they do not have foundations dug into earth, they are part of the basalt, budding out of it. Young, sharp, unfinished-looking, like crystals that haven't decided what shape they want to be yet.

### Light and Water

There is very little direct sun. Not because it's night, but because the light itself is dim, filtered down to a perpetual late-afternoon amber. The sky is not overcast — it's *occupied*.

Above the city, holding station kilometers up, is the Canopy. It isn't a roof. It's a lattice of tech so far beyond modern that your eyes slide off it. From below it looks like dark glass veins, like the craquelure on old pottery scaled up to the size of a weather system, hanging motionless in the humid air. It doesn't block the light so much as drink it, then re-emit it slowly, dimly. The sun is a brighter smear behind it. The light that reaches you is diffuse, warm, and constant. No hard shadows. 6PM forever.

And it's damp. Not wet. There is no river through the city center, no sea. The water here lives in the air. It condenses on everything. On the underside of the Canopy, on the vertical faces of the basalt drops, on the leaves. Once an hour or so, the Canopy cycles — you can feel a pressure change in your ears — and condensation falls. Not as rain. As a sudden, brief, lukewarm mist that beads on your skin and vanishes again. The fissures between terraces are where water collects, in long, narrow, perfectly still canals that reflect the dim amber like mercury. Pockets of water in an otherwise dry immensity. You could walk for twenty minutes and not see more than a damp wall, then turn a corner and find a cistern-lake the size of a city block, black and silent, with lily pads the size of doors floating on it.

The air is warm. 27, maybe 29 Celsius. Warm enough that the stone holds heat, that the mist doesn't chill you, that everything wants to grow.

### What Grows and What Lives

And grow it does.

This is a lush place, impossibly lush for how little standing water there is. The plants don't need soil. The basalt itself is porous and they root directly into it. The city is empty, and so the vegetation has taken the vacancy.

Every vertical surface is furred. Not with moss exactly — with something that behaves like moss, but grows in hanging curtains, deep emerald and rust-red, with leaves like folded paper that open and close when the mist falls. From the sharp edges of the terraces, trees grow — fast, thin, desperate trees with pale bark and enormous, translucent leaves that angle themselves not toward the sun, but toward the Canopy, as if praying to it. Their roots are white and exposed, draping down the three-meter drops like tangled cable.

Between them, vines as thick as your thigh strangle the abandoned infrastructure. And there are flowers everywhere, but they don't look grown. They look manufactured — too symmetrical, blooming with a soft internal light, opening with an audible *pop* in the warm air.

You would expect a lush, warm, damp place to be dense with undergrowth, claustrophobic. It isn't. This is what is so strange about the scale. The city is vast — regional, open, you can see three, four kilometers down a boulevard before it curves with the basin — and it is *empty*. Density: near-void.

The avenues were clearly built for millions. Six-lane, eight-lane corridors of seamless dark stone, with transit grooves still humming faintly. Housing stacks rise twelve, fifteen stories, their balconies all facing outward, their windows dark. But there is no one in them. Or rather, there is almost no one. Vast space between presences. You could stand on an intersection and shout and the sound would travel blocks before it found anything to stop it, except the leaves. A whole urban valley built for a civilization, and left hollow. Not ruined — maintained. The edges are still sharp. The lights still work, dimly. The vines are the only residents on most blocks.

But empty of people does not mean empty of life. The fauna is abundant, deafeningly so. Just not human fauna.

### Sound

You noticed it before you saw anything: the sound is physical.

It is not the city. The city's tech hums — a low, bone-conducted thrum from the Canopy and from the basalt itself, as if the ground is a speaker. But layered on top of that is biology dialed to maximum volume.

Every leaf-curtain vibrates with insects. Not flies or mosquitoes — things like dragonflies the length of your forearm, with wings of etched glass that beat so fast they blur and scream. Thousands of them. Choruses of throat-sac frogs the size of dogs live in the cistern-lakes, inflating necks like pink balloons and releasing a sound like tearing metal. In the canopy of the pale trees, colonies of something like parrots but with too many joints in their wings wheel and shriek, their calls echoing off the sheer terrace walls and amplifying.

It never stops. Day and night are the same dim amber, and the sound never stops. It is a warm, wet, biological roar that makes conversation impossible more than a meter apart. You feel it in your chest. The city was built for sound at this scale — the avenues act as sound channels, carrying the howl of one district into the next.

And underneath, if you hold your breath, the other sound: the Canopy breathing. A slow creak, like ice on a lake, as it shifts its weight in the air.

### The Rules Here

This place operates on a few simple, strange laws:

**1. Nothing erodes, everything accretes.** This is young land. Wind and rain haven't learned how to wear it down yet. Instead, things build. Dust becomes leaf becomes vine becomes soil in weeks. If you leave a bag on a terrace ledge, in a month it will have a root through it.

**2. Light is rationed.** The Canopy decides how much reaches you. Sometimes, for no reason you can discern, a patch of city a block wide will brighten — the veins above clearing — and the plants in that circle will turn, rapidly, phototropically, creaking as they grow an inch before your eyes. Then it dims again.

**3. Space is not respected.** The buildings are bigger on the inside. You can tell by the windows. A housing stack that from the outside should be thirty meters deep casts a shadow as if it were three hundred. The doors are spaced wrong. Some streets, if you walk them, take longer returning than going. The post-modern engineering didn't quite finish integrating with Euclidean space. The city is urban, but it is hollow in a way that suggests *folded* emptiness. Most of the volume isn't here.

**4. The residents left, but left the automation on.** Transit grooves still warm themselves. Mist still falls on schedule. Somewhere above, the Canopy still thinks it's tending a population of millions that simply isn't there anymore, so it tends the forest instead.

You walk down the center of an avenue wide enough to land aircraft on. To your left, a sheer basalt drop with a waterfall of white roots. To your right, an empty tram, its doors open, its seats filled with soft red flowers that open and close. Overhead, the endless lattice. Ahead, the avenue vanishes into warm haze and green. Behind you, your footsteps are already being softened by moss.

Nothing is coming to stop you. There is too much space for anyone to bother.
