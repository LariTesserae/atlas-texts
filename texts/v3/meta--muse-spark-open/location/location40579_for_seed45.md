It is night at the top of the world, and it is warm.

You shouldn't be warm here. The air is so thin it barely holds itself together — an extreme elevation, not a peak but a newborn plateau that has punched up through the lower atmosphere and kept going. From its edge you can see the curvature of the dark below you, a haze of distant lowlands you will never walk down to. The scale is too large to hold in one thought and too empty to give you reference: a raw tableland the size of a country that has only just been made, stretching out of sight in a tangle of ridges and basins, town-to-valley-to-range, with nothing to interrupt it for kilometers at a time.

This place has not been weathered yet. That's the first wrongness you feel under your boots.

Erosion is something that hasn't happened. There are no softened slopes, no riverbeds, no dunes shaped by long wind. Everywhere the geology is embryonic, just-coming-into-being. The ground is warm basalt still exhaling heat, and salt, and glass. Black clinker fields crack underfoot with a fresh, sugary fracture; they have never been stepped on before you. Vast slabs have tilted up like broken teeth where the uplift came too fast, their undersides still showing the slick, ropy flow of the melt they were last week. Needle ridges rise impossibly thin and sharp — hoodoos and fins and spires that have never been blunted — some of them forty, fifty meters high and only a meter thick at the base, humming faintly when the wind finds their resonant frequency. Between them are flawless sinks and bowls where the crust sagged while still plastic, their interiors glazed smooth as foundry slag.

There is no water here. Not dry like a desert - dry like a place where water has never been. The air contains no humidity at all. Your mouth dries in minutes. Your sweat evaporates before it beads. The salt flats that stretch between the basalt fields are not the beds of dead seas - they are sublimation pans, minerals that sweated directly out of cooling rock, kilometers of hexagonal salt tiles the color of bone and ash, each tile curling slightly upward at the edges, ticking as it cools. No lichen. No moss in cracks. Vegetation is a concept that does not apply. Not even the hardiest scrub or fungal mat has arrived. It is pre-life mineral, a world waiting for its first spore that has not yet invented one. At most, in the deep shadow of the salt polygons, you see a faint discoloration - a rust-red or sulfur-yellow efflorescence that could be chemistry, not biology, precipitating in fractal ferns where hot gas breathed out once and left its stain.

And yet it is warm. 30, 35 degrees in the dark. The heat doesn't come from the sun. The sky is a deep, unbroken darkness - not quite black, a very dark plum and indigo, star-pierced but starlight is not enough to see by. There is no moon here, or it is permanently occluded by a high veil of fine ash still hung from the uplift itself. The light you navigate by is low, diffuse, and from below: the ground itself. Every major fissure glows. A dull, cherry-red seam-light leaks from the still-cooling joints of the plateau, enough to throw long double shadows behind every spire. It pulses very faintly, not flickering like fire but breathing. In places where a fresh slab has just cracked open in the last hours, the glow is brighter, orange-white, and you can feel the heat roll off it across tens of meters. The darkness is therefore not cold-dark, but kiln-dark. Warm dark. The kind of dark you could sleep in without shelter.

The structures are what make you stop.

Scattered across this raw expanse — sparse enough that you might walk an hour between them — are things that look built.

They aren't.

Or, they are, but not by anyone with tools. Erosion hasn't learned to make ruins yet, but the dynamics of this place already counterfeit them. Turbulence is the law here. This plateau is hyperactive, Solaris-like in its restless creation. You can feel it through your soles: a low, endless tremor as new material upwells. It is mostly quiet - the sound parameter of this world is a hush so complete your ears ring - but that quiet is punctuated without warning by geology happening in real time. A distant CRACK like a rifle shot as a salt tile the size of a house shatters from thermal stress. A deep, subsonic sigh as a lava tube collapses a kilometer away and the air pressure in your ears pops. A sudden, chaotic gust as a spire outgasses, venting hot, metallic-smelling wind that smells of iron and ozone, then stops.

In that turbulence, the wind and the cooling and the gas have made architecture.

There are hollow cairns of welded scoria that stand like beehive huts, with perfect round doorways where gas bubbles burst. There are walls - long, low, straight walls of basalt columns that cooled into hexagonal palisades and then tipped together, forming corridors that run dead straight for a hundred meters and end in nothing. There are towers that are actually fumarole chimneys, meters wide at the base, narrowing as they rose, their sides striated like hand-laid brick where mineral-rich steam deposited layer after layer of white halite and yellow sulfur. From a distance they look inhabited. Up close you see they are empty, still warm to the touch, whispering hot air from their empty tops. They are not ruins; they are previews of ruins. Nature practicing what it will do later when there are cities to ruin.

Every so often you find something even less plausible: a small, deliberate-looking stack of flat salt slabs, waist-high, balanced with impossible precision. Not wind-made. Or not only wind-made. Someone? Something? was here between eruptions and marked a place with the only material at hand, then left. There is no technology. No metal, no plastic, no cut edge, no fire pit, no footprint preserved in dust that never settles. Whatever made those stacks did not stay, did not need tools, or did not need to remain physical. The weirdness of this place is not overt - no floating rocks, no impossible sky - but a low, insistent 0.6/3 wrong: the sense that the rules are being improvised. Warmth at a height where there should be frost. Darkness that radiates heat. A landscape that builds houses before it builds soil. A silence that is somehow crowded with potential.

Nothing lives. No insects tick in the warm dark. No birds ride the thin thermals. No animal has had time to evolve or courage to migrate up here. If you stay still long enough, the only movement is mineral: a glitter as a salt crystal grows visibly at the lip of a vent, extending a fragile, fern-like arm into the air, then snapping under its own weight. A basalt flake exfoliating with a soft *ting*. The very slow, very real creep of the horizon as the plateau inflates another few centimeters while you watch, enough to make your inner ear lurch.

This is not a desert. A desert is an ending - life stripped away, water remembered. This is a beginning that hasn't decided what it wants to be yet. A dry, hot, high, dark womb of stone, open and empty and violently busy making itself, scattered with the accidental temples it made while learning how to be a mountain.
