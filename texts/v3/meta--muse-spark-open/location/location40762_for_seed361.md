On the top of the world, but not the way you expect.

This is not a peak. Peaks are young.

This is what is left after peaks have given up.

### The Table

You climb for two days through thinning air and thinning trees — warm air, improbably warm for this height — and the mountain doesn't get steeper. It gets *tired*. The granite and basalt have been here so long they've forgotten how to be sharp. Everything is round-shouldered, buffed down to a peneplain, a high savanna of ancient stone that was once a range of teeth and is now a broad, breathless table sloping almost imperceptibly toward the sky.

The wind has had millions of years to work it. It has. There are no cliffs, no scree slopes, only long, low swells of stone like the backs of buried animals, polished by rain that rarely comes. The rock itself is the color of old bone and rust, pitted with honeycomb weathering, lichen-crusted in black-green rosettes that have grown outward for centuries, a foot across, uninterrupted. Under normal light — clear, high-altitude sunlight that doesn't feel thin at all, but thick and golden and close — the stone glows warm to the touch at noon. You can feel the stored heat in your palms hours after sunset.

It is empty. That is its character. A sparse place. The sky is huge here, the density of everything low — one twisted ironwood every hundred meters, one boulder every field-length, one hawk's shadow crossing every hour. The vegetation that does exist is disproportionately lush for such emptiness, almost defiant about it. From a distance the table looks barren. Up close, each tree is an oasis unto itself: wide, low, umbrella-crowned, its leaves glossy and overfull, dripping with epiphytes, with orchids and bromeliads stacked five deep on every branch, roots dangling in air waiting for moisture. Each tree is a jungle in miniature, isolated by acres of sun-baked stone.

There is no river here. There can't be. Water doesn't stay long enough to become a river.

Water comes sideways.

### The Pocket

You almost miss it because erosion has been so kind to it. One of those long swells dips just slightly more than the others, and the lichen gives way to moss that shouldn't be there — a sudden, vivid seam of emerald in the bone-colored stone.

Then the ground opens.

Not a chasm. A room. A hidden scale, an intimacy made by collapse long after the mountains stopped growing. An old lava bubble or a sinkhole where the ancient table sagged and fell inward, maybe fifty meters across and twenty deep, its walls stepped and softened until they are not walls at all but steep, moss-staircased bowls leading down.

Inside, the climate changes.

Because this high table is warm — 25, 28 degrees even when the wind moves — but the stone cools fast after dusk. And when the warm air of the day hits the cold stone of the pocket at night and at dawn, it weeps. Condensation. The whole bowl sweats. Every moss frond, every hanging vine bead with water that was never rain. At 06:00 the pocket drips with a thousand unsourced streams. At noon, intermittent rain — not weather, but the table breathing — falls upward from evaporation and then falls again as a brief, hard shower that lasts four minutes and vanishes. There are no lakes, only pockets: shallow tinajas carved into the softened rock, dark as tea, holding tadpoles and water-beetles and a week’s worth of dew. Temporary, then gone, then back again.

And because of that, the vegetation inside is different. Lush becomes *lush*. Ferns the size of doors unroll from every ledge. The ironwoods on the table send daughters down here that grow twice as tall in half the space, their trunks braided with strangler figs, their canopy closing low over the bowl like a roof. Flowers open here that never open on the dry top — huge, waxy, white trumpets that exist only to catch condensation and funnel it to their roots. The smell is loam and warm stone and rot and bloom all at once, heavy and enclosed.

You have to climb down carefully. The stones are slick.

That’s when you hear it.

### The Inhabitants and The Leftover Machines

You heard it from the rim as a low roar, and thought it was wind.

It’s not wind. It’s wings.

Fauna here is overwhelming, and that is strange because there is so little room for it. But the pocket concentrates life the way a desert waterhole does. Density is low on the table; inside, it is a market, a city, a riot. Everything that lives high and hungry has found this damp room and claimed a ledge.

Wasps like polished amber, big as your thumb, working the bromeliads. Flies in iridescent clouds that rise and fall as one body. Beetles that click audibly as they walk. And above them — swallows, swifts, flycatchers by the hundreds, nesting in the pitted walls where the soft rock has been excavated into perfect spheres, darting, shrieking, feeding, never still. At dusk the bats come to replace them, funneling out of a fissure on the north wall in a ribbon that takes twelve minutes to pass. The sound never stops. Even at midnight, the crickets in the moss take over, a wall of sound so loud you have to raise your voice. Active — the whole bowl is active, predator and prey in full daylight chase, dragonflies hawking wasps, spiders the size of saucers tending webs strung between fern fronds, everything eating, being eaten, breeding, molting, singing.

And scattered through all of it — almost hidden by it — are the structures.

You thought at first they were rock. They are not rock.

Small, sparse, faint: three or four low cairns on the rim that are too regular to be natural, made of stone that is not the stone of the table — darker, glassier, without lichen. One half-buried arch in the bottom of the pocket, now serving as a trellis for figs, its surface warm even in shade, faintly vibrating if you press your hand to it. No doors, no windows, no ruins of a town. Not inhabited. Left.

The tech is beyond you. Not ruined — *settled*. One tinaja in the center never empties, even after weeks without rain. Its water holds itself in a slightly domed lens above the rock lip, as if surface tension were a suggestion it has learned to ignore. Dust never settles on the arch; insects avoid a perfect cylinder of air two meters above the deepest pool, where the dragonflies swerve around nothing. At night, very faintly, the walls of the pocket give off a residual glow — not light, exactly, but a cancellation of shadow, as if the stone remembers being liquid and is still dreaming about it.

Whatever built them engineered the pocket to *stay*, long after engineering should have eroded away. They smoothed the table, then kept this one damp room from smoothing further. A conservation project? A cistern? A nursery? The swallows nest in the perfect spheres because the spheres are perfect. The moss grows thickest where condensation drips most regularly off an invisible field. The whole micro-ecology is balanced on a maintenance system no one maintains.

Weirdness here is not dramatic. It is quiet and domestic. Gravity is 1% lighter immediately above the pool — you feel it as a slight lift in your step before you notice your hair rising. Sounds echo half a second too long inside the bowl, as if the space is larger than its walls. The white trumpet flowers orient not to the sun, but to the arch.

The rules inside the pocket are slightly edited.

Outside, the world is ancient, flattened, and drying toward stillness. Inside, it is held at its peak — warm, damp, loud, lush, forever late-summer, forever mid-morning after a rain that never fell.

You can stay a long time on the rim, watching. The table is silent and sparse and holds the heat of the sun like a memory. The pocket beneath you hums and drips and shrieks with life, an intimate, hidden engine that the mountain has carried on its back since mountains forgot how to be mountains.

If you throw a small stone in, it takes just a fraction too long to hit the water.
