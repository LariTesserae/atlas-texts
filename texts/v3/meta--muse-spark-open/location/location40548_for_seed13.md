It is a high place that has not yet learned how to be old.

You come up to it not by climbing but by emerging — the lowlands fall away behind you in a single long step of basalt, and then you are on the Table. 2,800 meters, maybe 3,000; high enough that the air thins just enough to make color a little cleaner, high enough that breathing feels like a decision. The sky is still normal, still blue and depthless at noon, with a sun the right size and a right amount of cloud — high, flat-bottomed, moving so slowly you can convince yourself they are not moving at all. The light is even and honest. Nothing amber about it, nothing dim. It shows everything.

What it shows is sharpness. This whole country is new.

The plateau was lifted or poured or cleft yesterday, geologically speaking, and erosion has not had time to argue with it. The ravines are cut like V's with a chisel, their walls still stair-stepped from the cooling of the stone. The ridges are knife-lines, not yet softened into hills. Even the scree slopes look fresh, as if the rocks fell and then decided to stay exactly there. In the distance you can see the caldera lip — not a crater anymore but a city wall, a perfect dark ring against the sky, unbreached except where the river has found one joint and slipped through.

That is where the water is.

It is not a wet place. There is no sea, no marsh, no constant rain. It is damp the way high stone is damp: condensation in the mornings so heavy it runs, rain that comes every third day at the same hour for twenty minutes and then stops as if a tap were closed, pockets where the world remembers water. The river is the main pocket — and it is more like a long lake with a current inside it, threading the center of the valley from the north notch to the south notch. Along its banks the ground is black and holds a footprint. Away from it, the stone dries to grey within hours. But even on the dry stone there are places where water lives without a source: hollows in the basalt where last week's rain is still held, perfectly still, perfectly clear, reflecting the sky without a ripple. Cisterns in the lee of every boulder. The city was placed to drink from these.

And the city is everywhere and nowhere, which is the first strange thing you notice.

It is urban without being dense. That sounds like a contradiction until you see it. From the caldera rim the valley reads as a town the size of a small nation — terraces, causeways, plazas, walls, towers, homes — organized, continuous, obviously planned. A single human intent has been laid over ten kilometers of plateau. But it is sparse the way a constellation is sparse: thousands of points of presence with a great deal of deliberately kept emptiness between them. There are entire blocks where there is only wind-polished stone and lichen, left empty on purpose. Between houses there are gaps wide enough to farm. Between districts there are avenues a hundred meters wide with nothing in them except the occasional shrine-marker.

No one crowds. No one needs to. The basalt gives all the room anyone could want, and the builders honored it.

The building itself is primitive only in the sense that it understands its materials. No smelted steel, no poured concrete, no hum of machines. Everything here is quarried, split, stacked, lashed, woven. The stone is the native stone — dark, fine-grained, breaking into straight lines. The wood is the low, hardy timber that grows along the river: black-bark pine and a kind of stunted, iron-heart larch that resists rot. Roofs are slate shingles pinned with wooden pegs. Ropes are braided lichen-fiber and gut. Tools are hand-tools, you can see the marks — the slightly uneven course of a wall where two different masons met, the adze-scallops on a doorframe. And yet the effect is not crude. It is precise, layered, urban in the deep sense: aqueducts that carry the river's overflow on trestles of stacked stone to cisterns on the high terraces; causeways that bridge the sharp young ravines without cutting into them; a library whose walls are just thousands of slate tablets slotted into basalt shelves, open to the air.

Who built it could have built higher or denser, and chose not to.

That is partly because of the green, and partly because of the quiet, and partly because of the rule that holds this place together.

Despite the cool — and it is cool, always early-autumn cool, exhalations visible at dawn, frost lacing the shadow-sides of the ravines until mid-morning — the valley is lush in its own sparse way. Not jungle lush. Highland lush. Wherever water lingers, life crowds extravagantly to it. Mosses make brilliant emerald mattresses over every seep. Ferns unfurl chest-high in the river bends. The pines along the water are short but impossibly dense with needles, and under them the ground is soft with fallen growth. Even on the dry terraces, lichen blooms in orange and sulfur-yellow circles a meter across, and in the gaps between houses people have coaxed gardens of tuber and bitter herb that grow low and thick, leaves waxy to hold what damp there is.

And the animals honor the same pattern. It is abundant — shockingly so for how quiet it is. At first you think the valley is empty, because the sound is at 0.8, a hush that seems intentional. No wind howling over the rim. No traffic, no engines, no chatter from the town. Just your boots on stone, your breath. Then you stop walking and the abundance shows itself.

The sky is full of them: white highland swifts that turn without calling, and larger, slow-winged gliders — almost vultures, but with rust-red chests — circling the caldera thermals in utter silence. On the stone, rock hares dart and freeze; their ears are tipped with frost even at noon. In the river, fish hold position, dozens visible through the still water, not schooling so much as station-keeping, each in its own exact place. Along the aqueducts, marmot-thick creatures with stone-grey coats sit upright on walls and watch you pass without alarm. Insects are everywhere but do not drone; beetles like polished basalt beads move across lichen, and moths the size of your palm cling to slate roofs, opening and closing with the light.

Everything is here. Nothing is loud about being here.

Which brings you to the last thing, the one you feel before you can name it.

This place does not change.

Not in the sense that it is preserved, or conservative, or well-maintained. In the sense that dynamics have been set to zero.

The young, sharp erosion you saw — that V-cut ravine with the fresh scree — has not deepened by a grain since anyone can remember, and no one remembers it being otherwise. The river runs but never floods and never dries. The gardens produce without exhausting the soil. The fish hold their places, and if one is taken for supper, by evening there is another fish holding that same place, the same size. The frost melts at the same hour and forms at the same hour. The twenty-minute rain comes, and the stones dry. The swifts circle. The moss stays exactly as thick.

You can act inside it — you can quarry stone, stack a new wall, carve a new tablet, plant a new row — and your action will remain, but the world will accommodate it as if it had always been there. You can walk from one end of the city to the other and you will not find a ruin, because nothing ruins. You will not find a construction site, because nothing is unfinished. It is all finished and all beginning at once. Time passes for you, but not for the valley; or rather, the valley has passed through time already and arrived at equilibrium.

The people here — because there are people, sparse as their houses, moving quietly between cisterns and markets with baskets of tubers, with hand-tools over their shoulders — do not find this eerie. They live inside the never-changing the way fish live in water. They speak quietly, not out of reverence but because there is no need to be loud to be heard. They build low and with space around everything because why would you crowd a moment that will never end?

At dusk the light goes amber for exactly six minutes — normal light, remember, just at a lower angle — and the whole plateau, city and river and sharp new stone, catches it and holds it. The moths open. The gliders settle to their ledges. The water in every hollow and cistern goes perfectly still.

And then, without fading, without night truly falling, it holds.
