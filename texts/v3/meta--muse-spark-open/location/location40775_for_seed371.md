It is not a valley so much as a room that the mountain forgot to finish.

You find it high — impossibly high for how small it is. The main ridge goes on for miles above and below you, a knife-backed spine of young stone at around 3,000 meters, still sharp from the uplift, still deciding what shape it wants to be. Glaciers have only just left it, and they left no softness behind. Everything is edge and joint and fresh fracture, pale grey limestone and darker basalt where it was forced up still hot. And then, on the north side where the sun never quite clears the arete, the rock folds in. Not a cirque, not grand enough for that name. A bite taken out. A hanging pocket. A bowl perhaps sixty meters across and half that deep, with walls that go up vertically on three sides and fall away sheer on the fourth.

From the lip you could think you could throw a stone clear across it. You would be wrong; the air tricks you. It is cathedral-small, hamlet-small, enclosed-small — the kind of scale where a voice comes back to you too quickly.

There is no sky in it. Not really.

The light never gets in. The eastern wall is a sheer curtain of new rock, unweathered, laced with vertical cracks that still hold the cold. The western wall leans over just enough to cap it. Even at noon in late summer the pocket sits in a deep blue dusk, a permanent pre-dawn. What light there is is indirect, bounced off clouds below you and snowfields above you, filtered through the almost-constant mist that condenses on the walls. It is not night-dark. It is the darkness of deep shade, of being at the bottom of a well. Your eyes adjust and then they don't — you see everything, but as if through smoked glass.

And yet it is choked with green.

That is the first impossibility that resolves itself when you stand there and breathe. The air is cool — 5 degrees, maybe 8 if the wind drops — cool enough that your breath makes small brief ghosts, cool enough that you want your jacket zipped. And it is damp, not wet. There is no lake here, no tarn, no stream that holds year-round. The water is intermittent, frugal, almost implied. In the morning, after the clouds push up from the valley two thousand meters below, everything drips. Condensation beads on every leaf and trickles into the rock joints. In the afternoon it may rain — a short, cold, sideways rain that hisses off the warmest stones and vanishes. By evening the walls weep directly. The basalt sweats. You can hear it ticking into the moss.

And the moss is everywhere. The vegetation has taken this absence of light and this abundance of cold damp and run absolutely wild with it. There are no trees; nothing could root in this thin, young soil. Instead there is a dense, layered, near-touching carpet that makes the whole pocket feel stuffed, upholstered. Alpine moss in cushions thick as mattresses, liverworts black-green and slick, crust lichens that look like dried paint and then flush neon after rain, dwarf willow no taller than your ankle but spreading in tight, interwoven mats, star-flowered saxifrage, moss campion, dense tufts of tufted hair-grass that shiver even when you think there is no wind. They do not grow *on* the rock so much as they *are* the floor, the walls halfway up, the bedding under the talus. Everything is covered in two or three layers. It is lush in the way a sponge is lush — saturated color, so dense you cannot see where one species ends and another begins. The green is almost insulting in this austere place. It shouldn't thrive in this darkness. It does, because nothing eats it.

There are no animals here. Not really. No birds nest on these too-fresh cliffs, no marmots whistle. Higher than the insects will bother, too enclosed for wind-borne raptors to hunt, too cold and too dark for anything with eyes to linger. You notice the silence that creates — and then how that silence is filled by everything else. No buzzing, no rustling of small feet. No decay from animal life, so the plant matter just keeps layering on itself, wet and slow.

Because the place is not still. It is young — geologically a blink — and it is terribly, energetically unstable. This is not the quiet of an old forest. This is turbulent ground. While you watch, things happen.

The rock is always moving. Fresh erosion means sharp, unsmoothed scree that has not yet found its angle of repose. Every few minutes something gives: a pebble skitters, then a handful of grit, then a slab the size of a door calves off the east wall with a clean, gunshot crack and slides in a brief avalanche that smears green and buries it, only for the green to be back, visibly resettling, in days. The mats of vegetation themselves creep and slump under their own water weight, whole sections sighing downhill an inch at a time, tearing, re-rooting. Water finds a new joint, freezes overnight, and splits a new edge by morning. Wind comes over the arete in falls and eddies, not as weather but as a physical presence — cold, damp, loud — turning over in the bowl, lifting mist into vortices, rattling the grass tufts like fingers on a washboard.

It is loud. Not just wind-loud. Industrially loud, though there is nothing built here at all. 

That is the strangest part until you understand it. There is not a wall, not a ruin, not a bolt or brick or cut timber anywhere in the pocket. Human hands have not shaped this place. But it *sounds* like a works. A factory floor with no factory. The wind through the fluted, sharp-cut chimneys of the west wall roars and then keens with a turbine's whine. Water, when it comes, pours down five or six chutes at once and hits bare rock with the clatter of a stamping mill. Stones grind. The whole small amphitheatre acts as a drum, and far, far below — so far that light and weather separate you — the actual industrial world is working: the valley railway, the cement plant, the freight line that threads the pass. On a clear night you can see its orange glow like a forge reflected off low cloud. But even when you cannot see it, you can hear it, carried up two thousand meters of thin, cold air and focused by the bowl. A low thrum of engines, a distant shriek of brakes, a hammering that might be a press or might be rockfall from the next ridge. The pocket amplifies it until it seems to come from the rock itself. The mountain is manufacturing itself.

The rules here are simple and non-negotiable:

Stay low if the wind is up; it will try to peel you off the lip.
Do not trust any surface that looks solid; the green hides moving talus and water-oiled seams.
Do not expect stasis. The map you make in the morning will be wrong by a few stones by evening.
And do not wait for silence. There is no animal quiet to return to. Only the pause between the rock's breaths, between the factory sounds the wind borrows from below.

If you stay long enough — and few do, because it is cold and dark and there is nowhere to sit that will stay dry for an hour — you start to see how coherent it is. A tiny, dense, hyperactive engine of plant and stone and weather, running in a dark room at the top of the world, with no need for animals or sun or builders. It makes and unmakes itself constantly, loud in its work, damp to the touch, green-black in the gloom, sharp as broken glass and soft as moss wherever you put your hand.
