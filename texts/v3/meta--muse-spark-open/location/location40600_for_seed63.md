It is a high place that holds heat after dark.

A tableland lifted out of the true desert below — not a mountain with a peak, but a mesa with a flat top, a single room cut from the world by wind. You can walk its rim in less than an hour if you could walk at all. On three sides the basalt drops clean away into black air, into nothing you can see. On the fourth, a low saddle of broken scoria connects it to the larger massif, but the wind there is constant and wrong, scouring the pass to polished glass. This is a place with edges. A bounded place. A cathedral floor without a cathedral.

The day is gone, but the heat remains. The rock itself is the heater.

The whole mesa is dark stone laid down in old flows — black-red basalt, pocked, vesicular, light enough to feel hollow under your hand. It is old lava, established, settled into its own shape. No sharp cones, no fresh ash. Long weather has chewed it into low waves and shallow bowls, into potholes the size of a house's foundation and ridges no higher than your knee. The wind has rounded every corner. Salt has done the rest. In the hollows, white crusts have bloomed — not snow, not ice, but evaporite, mineral salts left behind by water that hasn't been here in ten thousand years. They crack under pressure into hexagons, into scales. They taste of iron and battery acid on the air. The air itself is thin and absolutely dry. There is no water here. No spring, no seep, no damp line under a stone. The humidity is zero in a way that makes your nostrils stick together when you breathe. Your sweat vanishes before it beads.

There is nothing green. Not a blade, not a lichen, not even rot. The sun by day bakes any spore to sterility. What little organic discoloration exists is mineral — ochre films of iron oxide, blue-green copper stains in cracks where the basalt sweats metal but never moisture.

And yet the mesa is not empty. It is *covered*.

At first in the darkness you think the rock is textured. Then you realize the texture is breathing.

Everything is fauna. Layer on layer of it, packed with no gap larger than a knuckle. A living pavement. They are not things that need water, not as you understand it.

They are the size of a cupped hand, most of them. Flat, kite-shaped, the color of cooling lava when folded — deep maroon-black — and when they shift, flashing a pale, translucent underside the color of salt. They have no eyes you can see. No legs, really — just a fringe of fine, hair-like cilia along their vanes that grip the rock and grip each other. They lie shingled like fallen leaves, like scales on a fish too big to comprehend, three and four deep in places. The top ones are dry and leathery. The ones beneath are moist with oils, not water.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They metabolize the rock, or the air, or the heat itself — you can see a faint rippling of waste-heat above them, although the night is dark. The stones beneath where they cluster are bleached a little, etched, as if something is slowly licking the minerals out.

It is almost quiet. For the number of creatures here — thousands per square meter, millions on the mesa — it should be a chittering, rustling hell. It isn't.

Mostly there is wind over the rim, and the faint ticking of cooling basalt contracting, a sound like a house settling. And underneath that, the fauna moves. Not all at once. In patches. A shiver runs across a basin as one shifts and a hundred adjust to cover it. A soft, dry *papery* sound, like turning the pages of a very old book very slowly. Then stillness again.

That is the dynamic. Watch longer and you see the predation, because packed life cannot be peaceful.

There are other things among the kites — longer, centipede-thin hunters the color of salt-crust, nearly invisible when they flatten themselves into the white pans. They do not chase. They wait, then suddenly throw a barbed proboscis two body-lengths out, harpooning a kite at its edge and reeling it in. The surrounding kites don't flee. They simply flow closed over the gap, sealing the wound in the carpet. Elsewhere, a kite will suddenly convulse and turn over, its pale side up, and the ones around it will lift their fringes in unison and begin to dissolve it from the edges in, chemistry doing the work of teeth. No sound at all for that.

The strangeness is not that they eat each other. It's that they seem to be one decision distributed across many bodies. The ripples never overlap. A hunting strike in the north basin always coincides with a stillness in the south basin, as if attention itself is a limited resource being passed around.

And the scattered built things —

You almost miss them because the fauna covers them too. Four, maybe five low structures, none taller than a cairn. Dry-stack basalt, no mortar, circular as windbreaks. The kind of thing a shepherd would build for a night, or a pilgrim to mark a vigil, long abandoned. Their interiors are swept almost clean — the fauna do not like the angle of the stacked stone, or the wind eddy inside. Inside one, the salt crust is thicker, and someone has scratched thin lines into it: tally marks, or constellations, or a map of this exact mesa with its rim. The lines are re-healing as salt re-crystallizes, slowly erasing themselves. No metal. No plastic. No wire. Nothing here was ever made to last.

Above, there is no moon tonight. The stars are hard and high because the air is thin and dustless, but their light does almost nothing. The real light is low and diffuse, trapped heat leaking from the rock as infrared, the faint dull-maroon glow you perceive more on your skin than your eyes — the memory of the sun that hammered this stone all day and that the stone now returns, slowly, all night, to the dark air and to all those flat, dark bodies pressed against it, drinking warmth.

If you stand on the rim and hold your breath, you can feel the whole mesa inhale once every few minutes — not a sound, a pressure change, as millions of cilia beat together to pull the thin, hot, mineral air down into the living mat, then release it.
