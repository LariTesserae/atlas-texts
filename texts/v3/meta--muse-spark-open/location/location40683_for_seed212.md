This is not a forest. A forest has ground underneath it.

Here, there is no underneath.

You are standing — if standing is the word — in a low basin that was not here a year ago. The land is young. You can tell by the edges. Everything is too sharp to be old.

In the last upheaval, the earth shrugged and cracked into terraces and blades, low basalt-black ribs of stone still steaming faintly in the warm air, still without the softness that rain gives to old rock. They jut at wrong angles out of the green. They have not yet learned to be hills. Some are only a few meters high, a knife-edge of fresh cinder that you could cut your hand on, and then the next step drops you twenty meters down a sheer face into another district.

And every surface that can hold life has been taken.

### The City That Grew Itself

Vegetation is not *on* things here. It *is* things.

On that 0.7 scale of water — not desert, not drowned — there is just enough. Morning condensation wrings itself out of the warm air and runs in beads down every leaf. Occasional rain comes hard and vertical and then is gone, leaving pockets: a cistern cupped in a bromeliad the size of a house, a slow riverine cut at the bottom of the basin that appears for a week, vanishes into root, reappears two streets over. The damp is not in the air so much as it is sweated out by the plants themselves.

Because the plants are the settlement.

From a distance, from one of the higher fresh ridges at the edge of the basin, it looks unmistakably urban. There are towers, avenues, plazas, colonnades, dense blocks, suburbs. Only as you get closer do you realize the scale is wrong and the materials are impossible.

Trunks as thick as apartment complexes rise a hundred meters and then braid together. Not entwine — *braid*, as if hands had woven them. Their bark is not bark but overlapping leaves the size of doors, shingled like slate. Vines as thick as subway tunnels run horizontally for half a kilometer at chest height, perfectly level, forming elevated streets. Their upper surface has flattened and hardened into a springy, fibrous pavement that gives underfoot and then rebounds. People could live here, if there were people. Instead there are only the structures waiting for them.

Because density here is not about crowding. It's about *layering*.

There is never one canopy. There are seven, twelve, twenty. At ground level — where ground still exists in patches of sharp new stone — the air is close and green-filtered. Ten meters up, a second ground has formed out of interlocked fig-leaves and aerial roots so thick you can't see through them. It holds pools of rainwater where small, animal-less ecologies churn. Another ten meters up, another. You climb not by stairs but by the natural switchbacks where a strangler root has split and rejoined. The whole valley is a vertical city with no foundation, every level inhabited by more plant.

Flowers are rooms. You duck to enter a bell-shaped bloom twenty feet across, its interior cool and humid and faintly luminous, its petal-walls humming. Seed pods swell to the size of grain silos and then — pop — in an afternoon, unfurling into a new colonnade of white ribs that wasn't there that morning.

There is no tech. Nothing was built. No tool has ever touched this place because no hand exists to hold one. Nails, wires, concrete would be superfluous. Why forge a beam when a branch will grow into that exact load-bearing shape if you wait three hours? The city is pre-tool, biological-only, but it has infrastructure: aqueducts that are hollow vines, carrying condensation-sweat from the high leaves down to the lower districts; plazas where the canopy opens and a dozen huge flat leaves tilt in unison to catch the sun, then tilt away at noon to shade the streets below; street-lamps that are not lamps but colonies of pale, translucent fruit that swell with light at dusk — not bioluminescence, exactly, more like they store the day's normal light and release it slowly, so the night is never dark.

And it is *loud*.

### The Deafening Without Animals

With fauna at near-zero, you expect silence. What you get instead is the opposite.

Without birds, without insects, the plants make all the noise themselves, and the heat makes them frantic. This is a turbulent place, hyperactive, storm-state even when the sky is clear.

You hear it before you see the valley:

A low, continuous creaking like a fleet of wooden ships in a gale — that's the trunks growing, the cellulose laying itself down cell by cell so fast you can hear the fibers torque. Then a higher register: the *tik-tik-tik-TAK* of tendrils shooting out and lashing for purchase, twenty meters in a second, whip-cracking the air. The avenues groan as they shift.

Water moves inside the vines with a gut-rumble, audible through the walls. Roots hiss as they push through fresh stone, splitting the young basalt with a sound like tearing paper amplified a thousand times.

And then the ruptures. A flower-room reaches maturity and bursts open all at once, petals slapping outward with a thunderclap that echoes off the basin walls. A tower-trunk, overextended, collapses under its own weight — not with a crash but with a vast, wet sigh and a scream of splintering green, only to have three new shoots already knifing up through its corpse before the dust of pollen has settled.

It never stops. It's an anthill without ants, a bazaar with no merchants — pure vegetal commerce, growth and death haggling loudly in the warm air. You have to shout to hear yourself, and even then the plants shout louder.

### The Weirdness of Low Ground

The elevation is low. The air should be heavy and still. Instead it buckles.

This valley is young in the geological sense — freshly emerged, uneroded, sharp — but it's also young in the ontological sense. Reality hasn't set properly yet. The rules are still wet cement.

You walk down a vine-street that you swear was straight yesterday, and today it loops. Not curves — *loops*. You can see your own back ahead of you, one layer up, walking through a wall of leaves that should be solid. Warm condensation drips *up* along the underside of a leaf-road before falling sideways into a pocket-pool that reflects a sky that isn't today's sky.

Light is normal here — not dim, not blazing, good honest sun through breaks in the canopy — but it behaves badly. Shadows fall toward the light source in the lower districts. In certain plazas, the sun hits the huge solar-tracking leaves and the light *pools*, thickens, becomes almost viscous, so you wade through a knee-high spill of brightness that clings to your legs.

Distance misbehaves at the open, regional scale. The valley is town-sized, you can walk it in a day, but you never can. You aim for the far ridge, a kilometer away past the braided trunk-towers, and you walk and walk and the ridge stays a kilometer away, while the street you left recedes behind you faster than it should. Or suddenly it doesn't, and you are there, arriving without having crossed the middle.

The plants don't seem to notice. Or perhaps this is why they grow so frantically, so densely, so turbulently — trying to anchor a place that won't hold still. Their roots grasp fresh stone that is itself still warm from being made, and the stone itself sometimes flickers, as if deciding whether to be stone or something else.

There are no animals to witness this. No birds to get lost, no humans to map it. Only the endless, warm, damp press of living architecture, building and unbuilding a city for no occupant, in a valley too new to have learned how to be a valley, under a sun that shines normally on a place that has not yet agreed to be normal.

If you stand still too long, a root will politely but firmly grow over your foot, not out of malice — it knows no malice, it knows no animal at all — but because in this place, to be still is to be built upon.
