This is not a valley, though it is shaped like one. It is a bowl.

High up, where the mountain should have thinned to wind-scoured rock and snow, there is instead a bite taken out of the ridge — a cirque, perfectly round, perhaps forty strides across. You could walk its rim in the time it takes to grow cold. The cliffs that make its walls did not so much rise as remain; they are old stone, established stone, weathered down to a softness that looks almost handled. Not jagged, not new. Their faces are rounded at every edge, pitted, fluted by ten thousand years of the same water doing the same work, and they lean inward just enough to keep the sky out.

Light doesn't really enter here. Not directly. The world above the rim might be noon or midnight, blue sky or overcast — it hardly matters. By the time the light filters down through the permanent cap of low cloud that snags on the peak, through the vertical ten feet of cliff-moss that overhangs the lip like a brow, it has been used up. What reaches the bottom is a deep dusk, a darkness that is not black but blue-grey and close. You see by it as you would by starlight reflected in water: enough to make out the sheen on leaves, the pale undersides of wings, the wet gloss of stone. Your eyes adjust and then they don't adjust further. It holds at that threshold.

And everything is wet.

The cliffs weep. That's the only word for it. The rock itself — a dark basalt, smooth where it isn't furred — is porous with age, and water moves through it constantly, gathering somewhere high in the cloud and exhaling here. It doesn't rain so much as condense. Droplets form on every surface, swell, and fall a finger's width to the next surface. The walls are bearded with water. In places it gathers into thin braids no thicker than twine that fall without sound into the basin. In places it just sweats from the stone in cold beads and vanishes into moss before it can fall at all.

The bottom of the bowl holds no lake, and no dry ground. It is something in between, a saturated mattress, a sponge of peat and liverwort and sphagnum a foot deep floating over cold, black, still water. You cannot step on it without understanding you are stepping on top of water. It dimples and sighs under your weight, but it holds. There is no soil as separate from plant; the plants are the soil. Every inch is covered and every plant covers another. Star-shaped alpine mosses pack so tight their stems interlock. Cranberry and cloudberry creep flat and tight against them, their leaves waxed and overlapping like scales. Tiny ferns, no taller than a thumb, unfurl in the gaps between mosses that have no gaps. And over it all, in a second canopy inches above the first, is a low tangle of dwarf willow and creeping heather, stunted by cool and dark and altitude into bonsai, their branches knotted and prostrate, forming a latticed roof at knee height.

You have to crouch to see under it. You have to get low, with your face near the peat, in the cool breath that lives under the leaves.

That is where the fauna is.

At first you think the bowl is empty because it is quiet. The sound here is almost nothing — a drip spaced five seconds apart, the soft pop of a water bubble surfacing through moss, your own breath loud. But the quiet is full. Under the willow lattice, on every leaf, on every stem, clinging to the underside of every frond, are moths.

Not moths as you know them in ones or twos around a light, but a living pelage. Small, high-altitude ghost moths, the color of wet paper and lichen, their bodies thick with fur against the cool. They cover the vegetation so densely you cannot see green without moving them — and they do not move when you do. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Packed shoulder to shoulder, wing to wing, their feathered antennae trembling almost imperceptibly, tasting the damp air. They are overwhelming in number and utterly still, and almost silent. Only if you put your ear inches away do you hear it: a faint, dry rustling like a field of grass shifting without wind, the combined breathing-spiracles of the whole bowl inhaling and exhaling.

They do not fly. There is nowhere to fly to; the bowl is bounded, and above the rim is wind and cloud and a cold that would kill them. There is no need to fly here. Food is everywhere — the willow catkins that never quite open, the perpetual dew, the fungal bloom on the peat. Mates are everywhere. Death is everywhere and nowhere, because nothing seems to eat them in any number. A few dark beetles move slowly among them, and once in a while a blind, pink cave-salamander — its eyes long since filmed over in the dark — will surface from the peat-water, mouth agape, and take a mouthful of moth-fur without disturbing the rest, swallowing and sinking again. The moths close ranks over the ripple.

That is the other law of this place: it does not change.

The water that weeps from the rock is exactly the water that is drunk by the moss and held in the peat and exhaled again as mist that condenses on the rock to weep again. The level never rises. It never falls. The moths hatch, live, lay their pale, bead-like eggs on the undersides of leaves, and die, and their bodies are taken apart by the moss within hours, so softly you never see a corpse. The number stays the same. The willow does not grow taller. The cliffs do not crumble. The air stays cool — not freezing, never freezing, but with that high, thin coolness that makes you want to pull your collar up — cool enough that everything is preserved, slowed to an equilibrium so perfect it feels intentional, like a breath held.

If you stay long enough to be still, some of the moths will use you. Not as food. You will find, on the back of your hand, a tiny arrangement: three or four moths have carried up — with infinite patience in their hairy legs — flakes of stone. Not pebbles. Flakes, sharp-edged, struck from the cliff base by falling water and time. They lay them in a little overlapping shingle on your skin, as they do on their own pupae on the peat, as the beetles do for their burrows. A simple tool, a simple craft, primitive beyond language, the only making in a place that has never been built upon. No nests, no hives, no structures. Only this fleeting, hand-tool habit, repeated in ten thousand places at once, then abandoned, then repeated again exactly the same way.

You will think, in the blue-grey dark, with the weeping all around you and the crowd of soft bodies under the leaves quieter than snowfall, that the bowl is waiting for something. It isn't. It has arrived. This is its state, settled and in-balance, weathered into itself. It will be like this tomorrow and in a thousand years — wet, packed, cool, dark, small and absolutely full — whether you are here to crouch and see it or not.

When you climb out again, over the moss-browed lip and back into the wind, your boots will stay damp for hours.
