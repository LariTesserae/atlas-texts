It is not under the sea. It is not *in* the sea.

It is the sea, lifted.

### The Perched Ocean

This place is high — impossibly high. If you could drain it you would be standing on a broken spine of young mountains, a cordillera still hot from being born. The elevation here is 2, 3, maybe 4 kilometers above the old world. The air, if there were air, would be thin and cold.

There is no air.

There is warm water, held up where air should be. A regional ocean the size of a country, perched on a plateau, with its edges spilling off into white falls that never end. From a distance — if there were distance — it would look like a lens of green glass laid on top of the world, shivering.

You are submerged from the moment you arrive. Not plunged. You were always under. The water is not a flood that came and went. It is the condition of this place. It hangs.

Light gets in, but not well. The sun is a warm, diffuse coin seen through jade. At noon it is a pale discus, olive-gold, without glare. At any depth greater than an arm's length, the world goes dim and teal and amber. The warmth remains — the water is 28, 30 degrees, bath-warm, womb-warm — so your skin never quite remembers that you are drowning. You breathe? Not quite. The people here don't breathe water so much as wear it, or drink it with their whole bodies. More on them later.

The water is not still. It is not an ocean in the old sense. It has almost no waves, almost no current — *dynamic 1.1, slow* — but it has texture. There are sheets of temperature in it, slow warm rivers that crawl past each other over weeks. There are curtains of suspended silt that hang vertically for days before settling. There are places where the water itself seems thicker, where it organizes into layers you can almost push apart with your hands. And everywhere, a slow, invisible rain: not from above, but *within*. Microscopic condensation forming in the water column and falling gently through it, water falling through water, endlessly.

Sound does not travel here the way it should. It is mostly quiet. You would think a submerged city would boom and click, but this water eats sound. A stone dropped disappears with a soft *thunk*. Voices become humming, close-range things. What you hear instead is the mountain itself, slowly being born.

### The Emerging Bone

The geology here is raw, embryonic. *Erosion 0.4* — this place hasn't been eroded yet because it hasn't finished forming.

The peaks are pushing up *through* the ocean from below. They come up as black, pitted domes of basalt still cooling, still cracking. When they breach a little higher they cool faster and shatter into fields of sharp, glassy rubble that has never felt wind. Some are chimneys, still venting. You can see them in the dimness: conical hills rising out of the green gloom, their tops lost in the brighter murk above, their flanks still steaming despite being underwater — the steam becomes instant warm clouds that dissolve into the sea. Fresh rock precipitates out of the water itself around them, lace-like tufas, fragile white terraces that grow an inch a year, easily broken.

Nothing is smooth. Everything is under construction. The valleys between the new peaks are not valleys but flooded quarries, half-formed calderas, basins of warm mud that sometimes belch a slow bubble as big as a house. The bubble rises with patient, terrible slowness and then pops at the invisible surface — somewhere far above — with no sound.

Because the water is so warm and so full of dissolved minerals, everything that stays still gets coated. Stone grows a skin.

### The Hanging Forest

And yet it is lush. Lush beyond reason.

Wherever rock has been above water long enough — which in some places is only a few decades, in others a few centuries — it is smothered.

Not trees as you know them. These are water-trees, sky-kelp, drowned mangroves that never needed air. Their roots clutch raw basalt, their trunks are more like braided kelp the thickness of pillars, buoyant, straining upward. Their canopies don't spread, they float — vast mats of leaves like lily pads the size of doors, layered three and four deep, each one slightly translucent, each one holding a pocket of slightly clearer water above it. Between them hang veils of green hair, filamentous algae that drift and re-anchor, bioluminescent only faintly, more like decaying light.

In the dim, the forest is dense — *density 1.6* — not by crowding but by layering. You can look sideways through thirty meters of water and see leaf, root, stem, root, leaf, stem, a lattice with no horizon. Everything is near-touching. Small bladders of trapped gas silver the undersides of leaves. Pollen doesn't drift here; instead the plants release clouds of oily spores that hang in the water like smoke for days, slow-dissolving.

And the color. Even in dim light, it is aggressively green, plus the rust-red of iron bacteria coating every stone, plus the pale bone-white of the new tufa. The water itself is stained green, thick as broth with life.

What is absent is animals. *Fauna 1.2, rare.* There should be fish in shoals, in clouds. There are not.

Occasionally a single thing moves: a long, blind, manta-like filterer the size of a canoe, beating so slowly you think it's drifting. A shrimp, translucent, as big as your hand, clicking once and then still for hours. Jelly bells that pulse every twenty seconds. The forest is plant-loud, animal-quiet. You begin to understand that most animals left, or never evolved to breathe warm, thin, high water. The plants took the whole budget of being alive.

The quiet is not empty. It is full of plant noise, if you can hear it — the faint creak of kelp-wood expanding, the *tick* of a basalt flake spalling off a cooling vent, the fizz of oxygen pearling off a leaf and rising one bubble at a time.

### The City That Hangs

And people live here. That is the strangeness.

This is urban — settlement-scale, organized, continuous — but primitive. *Tech 0.6.* No metal, or very little. No glass. No engines. Everything is grown or knapped or knotted.

The city is not on the peaks. The peaks are too raw, too hot, too sharp. The city is *in* the water column between them, anchored to them.

Think of a town built by weavers who never learned to make bricks.

Platforms of woven kelp-wood — still living — are moored by braided kelp rope as thick as your waist to the emerging basalt. The ropes grow. The platforms grow. Houses are not built, they are hollowed out of living mats: a frame of bent, living trunks is coaxed over years into a dome, then the interior leaves are stripped and cured until they form a skin. Roofs are not roofs; they are canopies of those door-sized lily leaves, stitched together with cord, overlapping like scales.

The city is vertical because water allows it to be. There are no streets. There are levels. At 10 meters down, the light-gardens where children tend the spore-clouds. At 20 meters, the weaving lofts where women stand anchored by toe-loops, pulling filaments. At 30 meters, the stone-yards where men dive — slowly, ballasted with rocks in net bags — to pry off fresh tufa and knap it into knives, weights, anchors. Everything is connected by ropes, by ladders of knotted vine, by shared breath.

Breath.

The weirdness: you cannot breathe water and yet no one drowns. They have learned, or been changed, to make air *within* water. Every house has a lung — a huge, bell-like bladder grown from a single mutated leaf, inverted, tended daily. It fills over a week with oxygen exhaled by the forest, until it glows faintly with undissolved gas. Then the family gathers beneath it and breathes, one by one, faces up in the dome of air, which shivers but never quite escapes. The air tastes green and iron. Then they go back to holding their breath, which they can do for a long time — ten, fifteen minutes — moving slowly to conserve.

Movement is slow here. Everything is slow. *Dynamic 1.1.* To hurry is to waste air, to stir silt that will hang for a day, to tear the living ropes. People drift more than walk. A message from one end of town to the other might take a day, passed hand to hand along a rope line.

Light in the city is dim — oil lamps that don't burn, but glow. They are jars of concentrated algae, stirred each evening, that give off a wan amber light. At night — though there is hardly day and night, only dimmer-dim — the whole town glimmers like a submerged constellation, each house a soft bead of light in the green, each one swaying slightly, tethered.

The rules of this world are simple and non-negotiable:

1. What is still, grows.
2. What moves fast, breaks.
3. Air is made, not found. Waste it and you die.
4. The mountain is still growing. Anchor deep, but not too deep — vents open without warning.

You arrived from above or below, you don't remember which, and immediately the warmth closed around you. The water held you up. A rope brushed your arm, left deliberately. Someone — a small figure ballasted, moving with dream slowness — is gesturing toward a breathing bell, inviting you to duck your head up into the trembling silver.

The basalt below ticks as it cools. A spore cloud hangs motionless as smoke. Far off, beyond the last platforms where the forest thins, a new piece of the world cracks open underwater, white and hot, and begins very, very slowly to rise.
