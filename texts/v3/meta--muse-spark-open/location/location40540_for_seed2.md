It is not under the water. It **is** under the water.

Not on the seafloor — *below* the seafloor, or rather, where the seafloor should be there isn't one anymore. There is only the Build.

You enter from the top and you keep descending long after light should have failed, long after pressure should have crushed you, but the light doesn't fail. The light here is normal, workmanlike, noon-white at midday shift. It comes from nowhere natural — long, endless banks of sealed industrial floodlamps bolted to gantries, humming amber sodium lamps over drydocks that are never dry, caged fluorescents buzzing inside stairwells that go down fifty stories into blue gloom and still show every rivet. The water itself carries the light further than it should, scattering it. It's cool water, consistently cool, a late-October lake kind of cold that seeps through any suit, any hull, that makes metal sweat and skin prickle. You can see your breath if you have air left. Most places don't.

This is a continent that was made, not grown. There is no rock here. No sand, no mud, no basalt. If you scrape away the biofilm — and it comes back within weeks — you hit the same thing everywhere: poured concrete, sea-steel, rebar as thick as your arm, ceramic tile, glass brick, catwalk grating. Everything is made. The floor is made. The walls are made. The ceiling kilometers above you is made, though you might mistake it for the sea surface until you realize the chop hitting its underside is rain from condensation falling *up* from the lowlands. It was built at a scale that stopped making sense as architecture and became geography. Plateaus of factory floor. Canyons that are service corridors a mile wide. Mountain ranges that are really the stacked hulls of generation ships that never sailed, welded together and sunk for foundations. Basins big enough to have their own slow, cold weather.

And it is empty.

That is the first thing you get wrong. Abundant does not mean crowded. The Build is vast — so vast its own temperature differentials drive currents through it like monsoons. Cool water sinks off the high illumination plains, falls down the fresh-cut spillways, rushes through turbine galleries, warms half a degree against a reactor district, and surges back up again as a turbid river in the open. But for all that motion, most of any given volume is just... water. Clear, cold, bright, empty water. A single gantry crane stands alone in a vault three kilometers across. A single boulevard of lighted columns recedes until perspective swallows them, with nothing moving between them for minutes at a time. The density is near-void. Sound travels forever because there is nothing to stop it. If you drop a wrench it pings and pings and fades, and you will hear it a minute later bouncing off something you can't see.

It's young, this place. That is the strangest part for something so huge. It should feel ruined, drowned Atlantis, softened by centuries. It doesn't. Its edges are sharp. The corners of the concrete breakwaters haven't been rounded by surge. The weld seams still show their bead. The cut-throughs where they blasted for new channels have fresh, knapped faces, uncolonized, glittering with aggregate. In places the formwork has only just been stripped, leaving wood grain in concrete under twenty meters of water. Sluice gates hang new and un-pitted. Rebar ends stick out like whiskers where a platform was left unfinished yesterday. The whole sea-sized thing feels recently emerged, or recently submerged, as if someone built it elsewhere and sank it last year in one piece.

Because it is industrial, it never stops.

Even empty, it is loud. Not a human crowd loud — a physics loud. Low. The groan of expansion joints creaking with the tide. The thunder of ballast pumps the size of cathedrals cycling on their timers. Generators thudding in their housings with a heartbeat you feel in your molars. Waterfalls the height of skyscrapers pouring constantly from one level to the next through overflow rims, white and roaring even deep underwater, churning to foam that dissolves before it surfaces. Conveyor chains that still run, empty, rattling on sprockets with a shriek that carries for kilometers. Alarms, muffled and distant. Metal contracting in the cool. The whole structure singing with stress and flow. You have to shout to be heard, and then your voice is taken and flattened by water.

Vegetation is an afterthought. Sparse on purpose. Nothing was planted. What grows is what could drift in and cling on despite the scrubbers and the shade and the maintenance drones that still patrol and scrape. Kelp, but thin and lanky, stretching from anchorage points toward the lamps like prisoners' hands, not enough to make a forest, just lonely individuals or shivering curtains around outflows where nutrients are richer. Green-black algal felt on leeward walls. Pale anemone-looking filters clustered near the warm outtakes. Barnacles in neat lines along bolt heads. It’s a dusting of life on a machine that didn't ask for it, always about to be cleaned off again.

But fauna — fauna took the invitation seriously.

They are everywhere, just spread thin through such an impossible volume that you learn to watch. Schools move like weather fronts between pillars — herring, sardines, quicksilver smelt adapted to living indoors, thousands strong, turning in unison when a pump shadow falls over them. Cod fat as dogs lounge in pipe mouths. Crabs the size of laundry baskets pick across the gantry undersides, upside-down. Lens-eyed cuttlefish hover by the inspection lights, watching you watch them. Eels live in the conduit. Whales — or things that were once whales and learned to navigate corridors — pass rarely overhead through the high water between districts, their songs amplified and broken up by concrete, so loud it vibrates your ribs even at a kilometer's distance. And smaller things beyond counting: shrimp, bristle worms, filter clams white as teeth clamped to every girder, isopods scuttling through trash made of lost bolts and plastic tags. Predator and prey in constant motion around and through the empty halls, chasing, hiding, spawning in the slack eddies behind dam walls. Gulls aren't supposed to be able to live under the sea but they do here, nesting on lamp housings above the waterline of trapped airs, screaming down at the fish.

There is no weirdness to it. No magic, no impossible geometry, no alien principle. Water pressure obeys pressure. Light attenuates normally. Rust is rust and takes time, and they use good concrete. The uncanny feeling you get is just scale playing tricks. When a maintenance tunnel is big enough to hold clouds — real condensing mist clouds that form when cold runoff meets the heat-bleed off a power manifold — your brain wants to call it supernatural. It isn't. It's just industrial hydrology at continental size. Weather happens indoors because indoors is now bigger than outdoors.

If you stay on one of the high places — a control island with railings and rubber matting still nonslip under its film of slime — and look out, this is what you see:

White lamps receding to a blue haze, held aloft on black trusses. Between them, nothing but clear, cool water and slow-falling particulate and the occasional flicker of fish. Below, terraces dropping away in sharp, clean-edged steps, each one spilling its sheet-waterfall to the next, roaring without cease. Far below that, a floor so deep the water darkens to navy even with all that light, where the service roads still glow faintly like veins. And far above that, another floor inverted, dripping.

Everything is humming. Everything is flowing. Everything is holding.

A crab clicks across an empty plaza the size of a county, and somewhere a pump kicks on with a boom that rolls for miles, and the kelp sways.
