This is not a forest. A forest has ground under it.

Here there is no ground left.

### The High Worn Table

You are standing very high up, though you would not know it from slope. The land itself is so old it has forgotten how to be a mountain. 

This was once a range — you can feel it in the wind-scour of the bedrock far below your feet, if you could ever reach it — but erosion has had an age-of-the-world to do its work. What remains is a tableland: a primordial mesa worn down to a patient, almost oceanic flatness, its edges softened into vast, shallow bowls miles across. No peaks. No valleys sharp enough to name. Just the long, breathed-out curve of stone that has settled into its final shape and will never change again. That is what is underneath.

You never see or touch it.

Because on top of that ancient, flattened height, something else was built. And then grew over everything that was built. Or grew *as* everything that was built.

### The Made-Jungle

Imagine a city-sized greenhouse complex built across an entire high desert plateau, and then abandoned to a kind of agriculture that did not need tending anymore because it learned how to tend itself.

From any vantage — a maintenance catwalk, a gap in the fronds, a cleared vent-shaft — the expanse reads as one continuous material: **packed**. Not dense like trees crowded together, but dense like muscle fiber. Like lungs. There is no room between presences. No clearings, no soil, no empty air. Only mass.

Trunks thick as grain silos fuse into walls. Walls exhale aerial roots as thick as your wrist that become new walls ten feet over. Canopies interlock into ceilings so complete they have become floors for the next layer. Vines the diameter of industrial cable spool themselves around support girders and then crush the girders slowly into their own bark, swallowing I-beams and leaving only rust-veins visible under translucent skin. Everything is made and everything is alive at the same time; you cannot tell where poured concrete ends and lignin begins, because the plant has been bred, over centuries, to entomb the made thing and imitate its function.

It is total. There is no natural substrate anywhere in this open valley-basin of green-black architecture. Even the “ground” you walk on gives slightly under your boot: a mat of interwoven rhizome and dead-leaf polymer six meters thick, warm to the touch, laid down over tier upon tier of hydroponic trays and rebar decks that are now just fossil layers inside the vegetable body.

And yet — there is no water.

The air is brutally hot and utterly dry. 2.9/3 hot — not a jungle-hot of steam, but a sealed-kiln hot, a high-altitude furnace hot. The heat comes up off the ancient rock below and down from low banks of cloudless haze and it stays. Your lips crack in minutes. Sweat evaporates before it beads. This high plateau is a salt-field in the sky, a rain-shadow above the clouds, where water has not fallen as liquid in a human lifetime.

The vegetation doesn't care. It shouldn't exist here, and that is the strangeness.

These are not Earth-plants.

They don't photosynthesize — there is almost no light to do it with — and they don't drink. Their leaves are black-green, waxy to the point of being lacquered, and edged with a fine white crust of exhaled mineral. They run on what this place *does* have: heat and stone-dust.

Deep in the dark, in the still air under the multi-layered canopy, you hear the faint tick of them opening. Thousands of cup-shaped bracts, leathery as cured hide, unfurl along every surface to expose a pelt of hair-fine cilia. They electrostatically pull. The wind over the high desert carries a perpetual suspension of gypsum, basalt flour, iron oxides, nitrates baked out of the rock. The plants comb it from the atmosphere. In the deeper guts of the structure, where the industrial heart still thumps, great fan-houses — whole factory blocks — draw the hot gale in through clarifying louvres and sigh it out depleted, white-hot. Inside their ductwork, root-curtains hang like air filters, shivering as they precipitate metal directly out of dust into sap.

Their sap isn't water. When a frond snaps — and they snap constantly — it doesn't bleed. It oozes a warm, amber, aromatic oil, slow as honey, that smokes faintly in the cool-less air and then skins over. Non-aqueous chemistry. Hydrocarbon blood. The whole jungle smells faintly of hot varnish and crushed thyme and ozone, not rot.

### Light Without Sky

Light is almost non-existent here. **Darkness** is the rule; this is a night-jungle under a roof it made for itself.

A dozen strata of leaf-roof have sealed the sky out. At noon on the plateau, perhaps a few scattered needles of white sun stab down through a rupture — blinding, dusty, moving too slowly — but they extinguish within meters, devoured. True illumination comes from lower and stranger:

From the factories embedded like hearts in the mass. Through gaps in vine-wrapped conduits, you see the pulse of industrial orange — furnace light from the geothermal taps that keep the air-circulation and mineral-crushing mills running. Row on row of amber service lamps, still electrified after decades, now threaded through living tissue like Christmas lights swallowed by a throat.

From the plants themselves. Heat-leaves. In the deepest dark, certain species — broad, flat plates the size of doors — flush dull crimson from within, not with bioluminescence but with sheer waste heat, thermosynthesizing. They glow infrared to the edge of vision, a coal-bed bloom that makes the air waver. You navigate not by sight but by warmth on your face: a corridor lined with quiet, ruddy pads throbbing gently to either side.

There is nothing animal here. No insect whine, no bird, not even the scrabble of a rodent. 0.4 fauna is effectively zero, and the silence of that absence is profound. Leaves fall without anything to eat them except other leaves. Pollination happens mechanically — when pressure builds up enough between two swelling air-bladders on adjacent stalks, they burst against each other with a soft *poof*, exchanging spores in a puff of cinnamon-smelling dust that hangs in the still air for hours, undisturbed.

### Turbulence in Silence

And yet for all its quiet, this place is violently alive in a way that makes you brace your hand on a vine.

It is turbulent — hyperactive, chaotic, ceaselessly creating and destroying itself — but it does it at a vegetal pace that is terrifyingly fast.

Stand and watch any “quiet” wall for five minutes. You will see:

A finger-thick shoot corkscrew out of a seam, lengthen a foot, unfurl a black sail that immediately yellows and is strangled by its neighbors. A fruit-body swells to the size of a melon, cracks along tripartite seams, and collapses into a slump of oily fiber that is already being netted over by fresh hyphal cables before it finishes falling. A whole scaffold-tower — wood and steel and living cane indistinguishable — will groan sideways as roots undermine its base and then, with no crash, simply sag into a new shape, a new archway, a new room that wasn’t there this morning.

There are no seasons here, only surges. When a factory vent clears, a wave of hot mineral wind floods a district and that district erupts. Growth fronts race each other across ceilings like fire across paper — silent, relentless, inches per hour, audible only as a faint, ceaseless creaking and popping, like a house settling in an earthquake that never stops. An anthill energy without ants. A bazaar with no people, only stalls trading dust for oil, light for heat, each leaf-stall opening and closing and collapsing.

If you stand still too long, tendrils taste your ankle. Not predatory. Just opportunistic. You are warm, you shed salts, you are briefly an interesting local concentration of minerals. They will test you, tap you, and move on.

The rules of this world are simple and total:

1. Nothing is left unused. Every photon of waste heat, every gram of airborne grit, is caught.
2. Nothing is allowed to stay as it is. Every form is temporary scaffolding for the next form.
3. Water is a myth the oldest seeds remember but no longer require.

Past the immediate tangle — past the wall currently trying to seal itself over that flickering service door — the landmass continues. Miles of it. The packed, hot, dark, built-jungle stretches to the soft lip of the ancient mesa and spills faintly over it in hanging curtains kilometers long, stirring in the high wind like kelp. From a distance, at night, from the true desert below, travellers say the plateau looks like a low coal seam burning without flame: a vast, rectangular amber smear pulsing with dull red leaf-light and the steady amber of human machines still breathing inside a plant that ate a city to survive thirst.

You realize you have not heard your own breathing. The air is too thick with oil-vapor and heat to carry sound far. Even the turbines thrum as if through cotton. Mostly quiet.

Only the soft tick of cilia opening. Only the creak.
