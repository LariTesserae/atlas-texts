It is a town that was built before its mountain finished being a mountain.

You come up to it along the old mule-cut, breathing a little harder than you should, and the trees pull back all at once. Not because you've reached a summit — the summit is still a long way above you, pale and raw against the sky — but because you've reached a shelf. A cut. A room.

The plateau is small. You could walk its length before your tea went cold. Perhaps four hundred strides north to south, half that east to west, before the land simply ends in air. There is no slope down, no talus fan, no worn approach. The edge is a fresh break, dark basalt still showing the vertical hunger of its uplift, unsoftened by rain or time. The rock looks poured and then cracked. In places it is still warm to the touch from the deep earth, though the air is cool. This is new geography, embryonic geography. The wind hasn't learned its shape yet.

And on this shelf, someone has built a town meant for a thousand.

### The Empty Town

It is urban, unmistakably. Not a village that sprawled, but a town that was planned. Terraces cut into the still-soft stone, low walls of stacked slate and timber-laced retaining walls holding back earth that has not yet decided to be soil. Narrow streets run in a grid that ignores the organic line of the cliff. There are cisterns, plazas, a meeting hall with a roof of hand-split shakes so heavy they need whole trunks as beams. Primitive work, all of it — hand-axed, stone-hammered, lashed with leather and root-cord — but organized, layered, intentional. Someone knew how to make a city.

There is just almost no one in it.

That is the first loud thing you notice, after the loudness itself: the emptiness. The density of this place is wrong. The houses are there — maybe sixty of them, stone footings and wattle walls and moss-thatched roofs — but half are unfinished, half are empty, with doorways opening onto interiors full of nothing but damp air. The plazas are too big for the few people you see. Vast, flat, swept spaces of fitted flagstone where your footsteps echo too long. Between any two presences — a person, a hearth, a standing wall — there is a cathedral's worth of hollow space. The town feels like a lung held open after the breath has left.

The few who live here move slowly through that excess of room. An old woman tending a fire pit that could cook for a street. Two children chasing each other around a pillar that was meant to hold up a market awning that was never finished. They do not seem lonely so much as custodial, as if they are keeping the scale of the place warm until it is needed.

You ask, later, where everyone went, and they look confused. *Went?* No one went. There was never more. The town was built empty on purpose. It was built to the size the mountain told them it would need.

### Water and Green

For all its stone, the place is wet.

At this altitude — high enough that clouds come through at chest-height, high enough that your breath is a small, cool puff even at midday — you expect spare air, thin grass, lichen. Instead, because the rock is so new and close to the surface, water is everywhere.

It does not rain so much as condense. The normal, bright, high-country light — clear and even and without haze — catches on a perpetual exhalation. Moisture beads on every basalt edge and weeps down. The emerging cliffs sweat. The retaining walls weep. On the north side of every building, water finds a path and draws a dark green line down to the ground where it feeds a gutter, a cistern, a basin hollowed by hand.

And because of that, it is impossibly lush. Vegetation at 2.4/3 in a place that should be alpine scrub. Not jungle — cool, careful green. Cushion moss so thick on the roofs it looks like the houses are wearing caps. Ferns shoulder-high in every alley where the flagstones have cracked. Dwarf cloud-forest trees, gnarled and wind-bent, somehow rooted directly into the raw basalt, their roots prying at stone that hasn't hardened enough to resist. Vines with waxy, rain-slick leaves climb the unfinished walls and pry at the lashings. Everything smells of wet woodsmoke and crushed fern and cold stone.

The sound is water, first and always. Drip from eaves onto barrels. Trickle through stone runnels. The soft, endless patter of condensation falling from leaf to leaf to stone. And under it, the louder sound: the wind.

### The Loud Slow

This place is loud despite being almost silent.

There are no birds to speak of — fauna is rare up here, a few high-altitude moths, a hawk sometimes far above that never lands, the occasional marmot whistling from a cliff-hole and then thinking better of it. No bustle, no market cry, no hammer now.

But the wind hits the fresh, uneroded edge of the plateau and has nowhere to go except *through*. It funnels through the too-wide streets, catches the corners of the empty plazas, moans in the hollow meeting hall, thrums the taut cords left hanging from scaffolds that were never taken down. The empty houses act as flutes and drums. A doorway booms. An alley whistles. Water in cisterns ripples and slaps with each gust.

It never stops. It is not a storm; it is the normal breathing of a high, wet shelf. A loud, patient, low roar that makes you raise your voice without realizing it. The people here have learned to talk a little louder, to pause for the gust, to not be bothered when a shutter bangs five streets away and the sound carries perfectly intact through the cold, clear air.

Everything else is slow. That is the covenant of this raw geology.

The mountain is still rising at a pace you can only measure in generations — a finger's width a year, the elders say, though no one has a tool fine enough to prove it. The cracks in the flagstones widen by a hair each winter. The moss advances an inch. The stone the builders cut is still curing, still settling. One house has a wall that bulged gently outward over ten years because the basalt behind it swelled with trapped water and no one hurried to fix it. They will fix it when it needs fixing.

People farm here in absurd, tiny abundance: terraced beds no bigger than tables, but growing fat tubers and bitter greens and endless potatoes, fattened by condensation and cool light. They work with hand tools, with antler picks and fire-hardened spades, chipping away at stone that still flakes like cold toffee. They are not in a hurry. Nothing erodes fast enough to punish delay.

### The Unusual

The weirdness is quiet, which is why you only notice it after an hour.

The town is oriented wrong.

Not spiritually — geographically. The drainage runnels all slope toward a central plaza that has no drain. The water gathers there, in a shallow, perfectly fitted basin, and just... sits. Steaming very slightly in the cool air, though it is not hot. On cold mornings, it does not freeze, though every other puddle does.

And if you put your hand on the fresh basalt at the plateau's edge, under the moss, you feel it: a faint, slow thrum, like a purr or a distant quarry hammer, regular as a heart. The stone is still becoming stone. The mountain is still listening to the pressure below. The builders did not cut this shelf; they found it halfway extruded and leveled what was already there, laid their city down like a lid on a pot that has not yet boiled.

That is why it is empty. That is why it is loud. That is why the few people here sweep plazas big enough for festivals that have never happened, and keep fires lit in halls built for councils that have never met.

They are not waiting for more people.

They are waiting for the rest of the place to arrive.
