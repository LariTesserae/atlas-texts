It is not a mountain. It looks like a mountain from a distance, and it has been mistaken for one on maps, but there is no stone in it.

You are high up. The air should be thin and cold at this elevation, but it isn't — not here. The plateau itself is high, a broad uplifted back of the world where the clouds come low enough to drag. And on that back, the City.

That's the only word that fits, even though nothing in it was ever built by hands. The City is total. From horizon to horizon, from the wind-scoured outer ramparts to the deep interior where the air goes still, there is no bare ground. No rock, no soil that was not first carried, chewed, digested, excreted, woven, orcemented into place. The substrate is structure.

It was not made quickly. That is the first thing you understand when you stand at its edge. This is not a swarm, not a bloom. This is established. The forms are settled and patient, weathered into a kind of permanence. What was once sharp has been rounded by generations of feet and tongues and rain. What was soft has been recoated and recoated until it is hard as old leather, as old wood. The walls lean with the weight of time, but they do not fall. They have found their equilibrium.

The outer face rises in terraces — not cliffs, but combs. Vast, overlapping lunules of paper and wax and mud and silk, each the size of a house, each overlapping the one below like shingles on a roof the size of a valley. They are the color of tea and rust and old bone, streaked black where water has run for a thousand years in the same channels. From a distance they look like strata. Up close you see they are full of mouths: arched openings, breathing holes, drainage spouts, balconies of braided fiber that shiver slightly even when there is no wind.

There is very little light.

Not because it is night — above, beyond the low cloud, the sun is still there — but because the City makes its own dusk. The outer terraces are dim. A few hundred feet in, it is true darkness, broken only by what filters down through chimneys and shafts and the translucent amber of the newer walls. The light that does reach you is brown, diffuse, smudged. It comes down in pillars through the mist and is eaten immediately by the surfaces, which are dark and damp and do not reflect.

It is warm here. Unreasonably warm for the height. The warmth is not weather, it is metabolism. It radiates out of the walls themselves, out of the deep galleries where millions upon millions of bodies press together and breathe. In the outer galleries the air is close and sweet-sour, smelling of yeast and warm wax and wet paper and green rot. Deeper in, it gets warmer still, humid, mammalian. Condensation never quite leaves. It beads on every ceiling, gathers along every fiber, and drips with a slow, patient rhythm. There are no rivers here. Water exists as seep and sweat. A sudden rain on the outer terraces will vanish into a thousand gutters and cisterns, be drunk, be stored in waxy bladders, be passed mouth to mouth. In dry weeks, the only water is what you can lick from a wall. In wet weeks, the lower galleries run ankle-deep for a day and then are dry again. The whole place breathes damp.

And it is lush.

That is the strange contradiction that takes a while to resolve: this is a made place, utterly artificial, and it is also a jungle. Vegetation has not reclaimed the City — it has been invited in, or it has made a perfect bargain with it.

Every horizontal surface that is not actively polished by traffic bears soil — except it isn't soil, it's compost, it's frass, it's chewed cellulose laid down as garden beds. From it grows a dense, low, relentless green. Not trees; there is no light for trees. Instead, a mat of things that love darkness and damp and warmth: liverworts thick as carpets, black-green mosses that hang in beards a foot long, fleshy fungi like ears and plates, vines with pale, translucent leaves that spread flat to catch what little light there is, ferns that unroll from cracks in the paper walls, their roots dissolving the wall itself and being patched over even as they grow.

The vegetation is densest on the abandoned tiers, where the comb has gone quiet and the gardeners have withdrawn. There, the green has taken the architecture and softened it, rounding parapets into hedges, filling cells with loam, turning a street into a tunnel of hanging moss. But even in the living quarters, where traffic is constant, green threads every seam. It sprouts from the mortar. It lines the water channels. It is cultivated in pockets and alcoves — pale tubers, blind white fruits, sheets of lichen farmed for their starch. The City is a hive and a greenhouse at once. You cannot tell where the building ends and the plant begins, because the builders use plant for building, and the plants use building for ground.

You hear it before you see any of this. From a mile off on the plateau, with the wind toward you, it is a low roar, like water over a fall. Closer, it resolves and does not get quieter.

It is overwhelming. Not a sound, but a pressure. Fauna — that is too clean a word. It is presence, body after body after body. In the walls around you, in the ceilings over you, under the fiber under your feet.

Beetles the size of your fist shoulder through detritus, their backs polished. Pill bugs big as loaves roll and unroll. Mites move in sheets that look like shifting dust until you realize the dust has legs. Wasps with paper wings crawl rather than fly in the low galleries, antennae flickering. Ants in columns a foot wide pour along highways that have been grooved an inch deep into the floor by their own feet over centuries, carrying green cuttings, white eggs, brown pellets, each ant following the one ahead so precisely the column looks like a single rippling animal. Higher up, where the shafts open to the sky, things that actually fly come and go in such numbers they dim what little light there is: moths, flies, small dark birds that nest in the paper cliffs and scream at dusk, bats that churn out at true night in a river of wings that takes an hour to pass.

And the sound they make is deafening.

Not sharp, not chaotic — deep, layered, continuous. The stridulation of crickets by the millions, all slightly out of phase, becomes a single high whine that lives behind your teeth. The buzz of wings in the ventilation shafts makes the very walls vibrate. The click of mandibles, the rustle of chitin on chitin, the soft thud of bodies dropping from ceiling to floor, the gurgle of larvae shifting in their cells — larvae fat as your arm, pale and blind, packed in hexagonal cradles and tended by nurses who never sleep. You have to lean close and shout to be heard, and even then your voice is taken and smothered by the warm, damp air and the paper walls that were designed to absorb vibration.

Nothing here is fast. For all its teeming, its deafening life, the City is slow. Its time is not your time. A road is blocked by a fallen fungus-loader and traffic simply flows around it for three days until the cleaners dismantle it. A wall collapses in the wet season and is rebuilt over a year, a mouthful at a time. The queens — if they are queens, the great egg-laying engines in the warmest, darkest heart, each bigger than a horse, each surrounded by a court so dense you cannot see her — lay one egg per second, every second, for twenty years. The gardens grow a millimeter a day. The outer rampart, eaten by wind on one side and rebuilt on the other, has migrated thirty feet to the east in the last century. You can walk for a day in one direction and the architecture will barely have changed, only its weathering, its shade of brown, the species of ant on its highways.

If you stand still for a long time — and you will, because moving through dense, warm, dark, buzzing closeness is exhausting — the system reveals itself as not hostile, not welcoming, simply complete. There is no outside to be found inside it. There is no natural ground to stand on. Even the air you breathe has been through a thousand spiracles before you. The damp on your skin is not yours; it was condensation on a nursery wall a moment ago. A vine brushes your cheek with a pale leaf, tasting your salt. An ant detours over your boot without breaking stride. Above you, a panel of translucent wax glows faintly where the sun hits it far overhead, and dust — which is mostly shed wings and spores — turns slowly in the brown light.

The City does not need you. It did not make a place for you. But it will, slowly, make use of you if you stay long enough to stop moving, in the patient way it makes use of everything that falls into its warm, damp, rustling dark.
