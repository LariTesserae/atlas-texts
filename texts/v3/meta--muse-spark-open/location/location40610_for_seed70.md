It is not an ocean on the ground. It is an ocean in the sky.

High up, where the world is supposed to thin out into cold and wind, instead it deepens. The uplift is young — a brutal, recent shrug of dark rock that threw up a whole province at once. A raw plateau the size of a small country, its edges sheer, its back not flat but broken. Ridges like the teeth of a saw, cones with their craters still sharp and unsoftened, cliffs that have not yet learned to crumble. Black basalt and red scoria, still porous, still smelling faintly of iron and heat. There are no beaches, no smoothed stones. Everything is angular, newly cooled. Valleys between the peaks are not valleys at all but troughs and calderas, deep-cut, vertical-walled.

And all of it is drowned.

The water came with the height or after it — no one carved this to hold it, it simply never left. A warm perched sea, kilometers above where a sea should be. Not a lake. Too big, too open. You can stand on the highest knife-ridge and see water stretch in every direction to a hazy horizon, and still see darker rock shapes looming under it, going down. The sky-sea lies on top of the mountains like a heavy, clear climate. From a distance it would look like the plateau is wearing a slab of atmosphere made liquid.

Up close, the surface is a strange membrane. On calm days it is almost invisible, a glassy trembling where warm air turns to warm water without a line of foam. On other days the rain never stops, except it is not rain — it is the sea condensing out of itself, falling upward from its own depths. Strands of water as thick as your wrist pour down slowly between the peaks and dissolve again. The whole basin breathes.

Light comes down normal and full, as if this were any high summer day. High elevation sun, white and direct, but filtered through ten, thirty, a hundred meters of water. It doesn't darken so much as soften. Everything is lit like late afternoon through amber. Rays make columns between the peaks that shift only a few degrees all day. Down in the troughs, it turns green-gold and dimmer, but never night. The warmth helps — the water is bath-warm top to bottom, uniformly heated by the stone beneath and the sun above. You could be in it for hours and not shiver. It holds heat like a body does.

And that is why it is so lush.

Without built things, without cutting, without any hand to thin it, life has filled every cubic meter. This is not an empty blue ocean. It is a dense, layered, vertical jungle that happens to grow in water instead of air.

The dominant things are not quite trees and not quite kelp. They root — actually root, with woody, gripping feet — on the sharp ridges and the cliff lips, and they grow up through the water toward the light-film. Trunks as thick as old oaks, but pale and buoyant, with bark that peels in translucent sheets. Their canopies do not float on the surface; they make a second surface. Broad, waxy leaves the size of doors overlap and interlock twenty meters down, forming a nearly continuous ceiling that ripples when something moves under it. Light freckles through them. Above that ceiling, a few braver fronds actually pierce the membrane and unfurl in the true air for a few hours, steaming, before curling back down. Below it, everything is in shadow-play.

Under the canopy, the middle-water is a forest of stems. Not open water at all. You could almost climb through it. Hanging vines of algae as thick as rope drop from leaf to leaf, beaded with warm gas bladders that tick softly when they pop. Curtains of feathery red weed comb slowly in the almost-nonexistent current. Giant lilies grow sideways off vertical cliff faces, their pads stacked like plates, each holding a pocket of clearer water over its surface. Everywhere, the rock that is not root-bound is carpeted — mosses that are not mosses, liverwort mats three feet thick, sponges that look like moss, all glowing faintly olive and amber and rust. The water itself is dense with life smaller still — pollen-like spores, drifting seeds, flecks of green that never settle, so thick the water between the great trunks has texture, like looking through warm broth.

You would expect noise. Birds, insects, fish. There is almost none of that. The fauna here is rare and shy, and the young rock has not yet offered many homes for hard things. Occasionally a shape moves: a long, white, almost-blind grazer the size of a deer, pushing slowly between trunks, nipping curtains of weed with a soft, sucker mouth. It does not swim so much as hang, and kick once, and drift for a long time. Or a flock — if you can call five a flock — of manta-like things above the canopy, dark against the bright membrane, flapping once every ten seconds, so slow, so patient they seem to be gliding on the water itself. They make no call. When they pass, the only sound is the faint displacement of water through leaves.

Mostly the place is quiet in a way deep water is quiet. Not silent. A low, continuous hush: the creak of buoyant trunks straining against their roots, the tap of seed-pods detaching and rising instead of falling, the hiss of warm vents on the trough floors releasing threads of bubbles that take an hour to reach the leaves. And under everything, very faint, the groan of the young plateau itself still settling, stone adjusting under the enormous weight of its own sea.

Everything here moves slowly. That is the rule of this place. The water is too warm and too still to drive storms. Currents curl around sharp peaks and die. Growth is constant but unhurried. A leaf takes weeks to unfurl. Sediment falls like snow at a speed you can watch, grain by grain, and lands on other leaves and is lifted again. Even decay is slow — fallen fronds do not rot quickly in this warm bath; they pale and hang suspended for months, forming ghostly second forests under the living one, until they finally soften into silt.

If you were in it — and you would have to be in it, there is no dry path far — you would move as its animals do: with a slow push, breathing differently, feeling the pressure of depth even though the sun is directly on your face. You would pull yourself along trunks because swimming is inefficient in a forest. Up is bright, but down is also bright, reflected light bouncing off pale rock and pale detritus. There is no shore to aim for. Only more ridges appearing out of green haze, more leaf-ceilings, more warm, dense, hanging jungle, going on past where you can see, all of it perched impossibly high, all of it sharp and new and untouched, holding an ocean up where the sky should be and not spilling.
