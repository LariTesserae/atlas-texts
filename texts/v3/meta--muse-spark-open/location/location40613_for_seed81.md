On the high basalt table, the city breathes steam.

You don't see it at first from the approach, because the light never really arrives here. This is high country — 1.6 kilometers is not a number, it's a feeling in your ears and lungs — but not high the way you expect. Not cold, not stark. Hot. A low, pressed-down heat that sits on the plateau like a lid. The air should be thin and sharp up here, and it is thin, in a way that makes every breath a little deliberate, but it is also heavy, wet. Saturated.

The plateau itself is old. Established. This is not a raw, jagged uplift still fighting erosion. The wind and water have had time here. Time to round the shoulders of the basalt, to carve shallow, broad valleys across the top of the world, to polish the stone to a dark, gut-colored sheen. The rock is black when dry and goes deep wine-purple when wet, which it almost always is. It weeps.

That's the water. There's no river here, no lake. The whole tableland is porous — a sponge of vesicular basalt and iron-rich tuff — and the heat below pushes water up. Not in geysers. In a patience. It beads on every vertical face, it strings down every wall in slow, bright threads, it collects in the low places and then vanishes again into steam before it can become anything green.

Because nothing is green.

Look properly: there are no plants. Not a weed in a crack, not lichen, not even the scum that should slick the wet stone. The mineral world won this argument early. Maybe it was the heat, maybe the water chemistry — it tastes of sulfur and rust and old electricity if it gets on your lips — maybe something the city did. The stone is bare except where the stone itself blooms. In the cooler seams you get salt flowers, sulfur crusts the yellow of infected snow, and a low, flat fungus that is not a plant at all — black, tar-like sheets that pull tight over the rock when the steam thickens and crack into geometric plates when it dries. They don't move. They don't need to.

And the city.

It is undeniably a city. Not ruins, not scattered outposts. Settlement-scale, town-or-valley-size, organized. From the edge of the table you can see the whole pattern open out under the haze: the grid of avenues too wide for the few things that use them, the terraces cut and then built up again, the layered construction where a second and third street has been laid over the first on iron trestles, the low factories with their sawtooth roofs, the habitation blocks of pitted concrete and heat-warped brick, the waterworks. It extends past the immediate, it has neighborhoods, a center, a periphery. You could house a hundred thousand here.

You might be the only person in it.

That's the density. 0.0. The emptiness is not desolation in the post-apocalyptic sense — there was no blast, no panicked exodus. It's a more thorough hollow. The city feels sized for a different kind of occupant, or for a density that never arrived. The plazas are vast and flat and wet and completely unmarked. The boulevards are eight lanes wide and empty enough that your footsteps come back from the far facades. Windows are dark, but not broken. Doors are closed, but not locked. The infrastructure is all still there because it has to be — and it is working.

This is the strange, active quiet of it.

It is dim. A permanent low light, as if the sun is always just behind a thick, high overcast. On the tableland, that overcast is not clouds. It's the city's own breath suspended. Heat from below — geothermal, industrial, both, you can't separate them anymore — lifts the weeping water into a low, luminous smog that never burns off. It glows faintly amber from the forges and furnaces still running below, and deep red from the basalt itself where the ground glows through thin places. No shadows. No glare. Just a long, hot dusk at noon.

Through that dim, the industrial things continue.

You hear them before you see them, but not as noise. The sound parameter here is 1.1: mostly quiet. There is no roar of traffic, no chatter, no music. The city has gone acoustic-dead from emptiness. What you do hear is spaced out, and therefore enormous when it happens: the sigh and clank of a trip-hammer three blocks over working by itself; the groan of a water screw lifting brine from a sump; the tick-tick-tick of cooling iron; the sudden, startling rush of a spillway opening somewhere uphill and dumping a sheet of hot, mineral water down a concrete sluice. Then it stops. Then the dripping returns. Everywhere, dripping. Weeping rocks. Condensing pipes. Ticking valves.

Because the dynamic is still active. Nothing is abandoned — it is automated, or habituated. The factories don't need you. The heat engines pump because the heat is there. The condensers drip because the water is there. The conveyors move slates of pressed mineral wool from one hissing building to another, unobserved. The weather cycles too, in its own stunted way: for an hour the haze thickens until the air is unbreathable soup and water runs in rivulets down every inclined face; then a hot, dry wind shears in from the plateau edge and lifts it all into steam again, leaving behind fresh white crusts of salt that will be dissolved an hour later. Erosion and accretion have reached an agreement. So have the builders and the basalt.

The fauna, when you find it, has made the same agreement.

Rare. Very rare. Nothing that would live on vegetation, because there is none. In the hot runoff channels, blind white shrimp the length of your thumb flick through water that would scald you. On the vertical weeping walls, things like damp paper — translucent, veined, pulsing — cling and filter the seep. And very occasionally you see a larger passage: a heat-hound, the workers used to call it — a long, low animal of bare pink skin and mineral crust, more dog-shaped hole in the steam than dog, drinking from a cooling pool and looking at you with an intelligence that is not hostile, not inviting. It knows the rule here: don't waste movement.

The rules of this place are simple and mineral:

1. Water moves up as often as down. It seeps out of stone, it sweats out of air, it is wrung out of steam by iron. There is no watershed. There is only the cycle.
2. The city is not for shelter from the weather. The city *is* the weather. The streets are its riverbeds, the trestles are its clouds, the factories are its rain.
3. Empty does not mean dead. Quiet does not mean still. The most active things here are the slowest: rust blooming, salt growing, basalt weeping, steam turning itself inside out.

If you stand on the highest layered boulevard, where the old administrative terraces look out over the whole open extent of it, you can see the geometry of the established world doing exactly what it has been doing for a long time. The dark, worn rock. The wet, empty grid reflecting the dim amber sky back at itself. A plume of white-grey lifting from a powerplant with no workers. A flock — no, not a flock — a *drift* of those paper-things releasing from a wall at once and hanging in the hot air like ash that forgot how to fall.

The city exhales. The stone drinks. The water beads again.
