It is not under the sea. It is above it.

High up where a mountain should be thinning into snow and thin air, there is instead a bowl. A small, sharp thing — not a valley, not a crater, a room carved yesterday by geology. You could walk its rim in ten minutes if you could walk at all.

The walls are young rock, and they look it. Basalt-black, fresh-broken, unweathered to softness. No soil has had time to gather. The edges are knife-edges, serrations, columns that snapped clean as they cooled. Everything is vertical, or trying to be. It's a high place, you can feel it in the pressure behind your ears, in the way the air wants to fall away on all sides — but the air never gets the chance.

Because the bowl is full of ocean.

Not a lake. Not a flood that came and stayed. An ocean that hangs.

The water stands above the rim, a wall of it, maybe thirty feet high, holding itself upright without a container. Warm water. Bath-warm, blood-warm, dim and dark as steeped tea. From a distance you would think it was a stormcloud that had sunk and gotten caught between the peaks. Up close you see it is absolutely liquid, from surface to depth, but it does not pour. It shivers in place, a vertical sea, its surface facing outward like a cliff-face.

And it roars.

That is the first law of this place: **it is deafening.**

Not the white noise of rain. The water is in violent, constant argument with itself and with the rock. It slams against the sharp basalt without spilling, recoils without splashing, then slams again harder. Low frequencies hit you in the chest so hard your ribs buzz. High frequencies needle the ear. Inside the bowl, under the overhang of liquid atmosphere, the sound has nowhere to go — it reflects, doubles, triples. You feel it as much as hear it: a cathedral-organ chord made of drowning weight, held forever at the instant before collapse. You cannot shout over it. You cannot think in words for long. You open your mouth and the sound fills it.

Light gets in badly. The sun, somewhere far above the water-wall, tries to push through fathoms that shouldn't be there. By the time it reaches the interior it is bruised, green-black, late-evening dim. Columns of particulate — ash, mineral dust, tiny pale jellies — hang lit for a second and then are sucked sideways. Shadows don't behave. Because the water refracts everything twice, you see the back of your own head as a smear on the liquid wall opposite you. You see the rock wall behind you in front of you. If you move your hand quickly, the image of it lags a half-second behind.

That is the second law: **gravity is outvoted.**

Down is not reliably down. The water wants to be the center. Loose stones lifted off the bowl floor drift slowly toward the nearest water-wall and then orbit it, clicking softly against basalt before being flung back by the turbulence. You, standing on the narrow lip of dry rock that still exists at the bottom, feel lighter with your head tilted back toward the liquid sky. Your hair wants to float upward, into it.

Nothing can settle here. That is the dynamic of it — turbulent, hyperactive, ceaseless creation. The water churns and within the churn, things form and are erased.

For a minute, the turbulence will braid itself into a perfect, glassy tunnel, a hollow throat through the sea-wall wide enough to see the pale sky beyond. Then it collapses with a thunderclap that you feel in your teeth. Elsewhere, the water flash-freezes into temporary sculpture — arches, ribs, a fluted pillar — not ice, still liquid, but liquid holding shape as if it forgot how to flow, before shivering and dissolving back into chaos. The air itself condenses and rains sideways, then un-rains. The rock hisses where warm water kisses it, and new mineral fans bloom in seconds, sharp as frost, then are scoured off.

It is too new, too violent for much to live.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn and wrong. Along the sharp ridges where spray never quite becomes dryness, there are mats of black-green kelp that have learned to grow without soil, without sun. They are not leafy. They are straps, whips, two or three meters long, anchored by a single white holdfast sunk into a crack in the basalt. They writhe constantly in the vibration, slapping the rock, reaching into the water-wall and then recoiling. A few pale anemone-like things open and close on the dry stone, filtering the damp air. No trees. No moss. Nothing soft could hold on here without being torn off.

Fauna is rarer still. You might spend an hour with your hands over your ears, eyes adjusting to the dim, and think you are alone. Then you see it: a single swimmer inside the wall.

It is long, blind, the color of wax. Not a fish, not quite an eel. It swims *through* the vertical ocean as if gravity were normal for it, undulating slowly along what to it is horizontal. It has no eyes, only a frill of fine filaments that tremble and taste the endless vibration. Sometimes it presses its flat head against the invisible membrane that holds the sea up, and for an instant its silhouette is huge and clear against the dim light from outside, and you understand how thin the boundary is between your gasping air-pocket and its world. Then the turbulence takes it and tumbles it end over end, and it rights itself without panic. It is not afraid of the deafening. It was born in it.

There were people here, once or briefly. That is the third law: **no one stays.**

Scattered on the only almost-flat part of the bowl floor are their marks. Not ruins — there was never enough here to build a village. Cairns, three or four stones high, stacked with impossible balance and still standing despite the shaking. A low ring of stones, too perfect to be natural, half-melted by mineral bloom. A single stair cut into the basalt leading up to nowhere, its edges still sharp, as if the makers left yesterday. Perhaps they came to listen. Perhaps to worship. Perhaps they tried to contain it.

There are no tools left. No metal, no cloth, no bone. Anything that could be made would have been shaken apart, dissolved, incorporated into the warm, dark water. Only stone endures here, and only barely.

If you stay too long — and the warm wet air makes you want to stay, it is womb-warm, intimate, it seeps into your lungs like breath — you notice the last strangeness. The water remembers.

When the thunderclap comes and the tunnel opens, you sometimes see not sky on the other side, but another bowl. Another ring of sharp stone. Another wall of hanging sea, with another dim figure standing on its floor looking back at you. Or you see this same bowl as it was hours ago, the kelp whips in a different position.

The sound, at its loudest, seems to fold. You hear, beneath the roar, a second roar, slightly out of time. An echo from before you arrived.

You leave before your own echo gets trapped here. You have to climb out while down is still mostly down, scrambling up those young, cutting rocks with your hands bleeding a little, the warm spray lashing your back without ever quite wetting you, the roar following you long after the bowl is out of sight, still vibrating in your sternum like a struck bell.

Behind you, the small sea keeps thrashing against its invisible walls, making and unmaking itself, too loud and too restless to ever be finished being born.
