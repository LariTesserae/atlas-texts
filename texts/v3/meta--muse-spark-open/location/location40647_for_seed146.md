It is not a cave.

Caves are what happens when water finds a weakness in the world and worries it open. This place never knew water. It was born dry, and it has stayed dry - a world-below-the-world that opened up all at once.

Think of a continent of basalt that exhaled. Or a planet that cooled too fast and left its crust propped up on its own ribs. Geologically, it is young - embarrassingly young. The edges have not had time to learn how to be soft.

You descend not through a mouth, but through a tear. On the surface above - which no one lives on anymore, or ever did - there is a cliff of black glass a thousand kilometers long, where the ground simply stepped down. The air is still warm all the way down, a dry kiln-warm that comes from below, not above. 30, 35 degrees Celsius, constant as a held breath, with no sun to make it and no night to take it away.

And then you are Under.

### The Vault

Scale is the first lie it tells you. In darkness, you think "cavern," you think walls. This has no walls. The floor is below you and the ceiling is somewhere above you - high enough that your headlamp, if you had one, would not find it. Far above, the roof is a field of fresh basalt teeth, still sharp from when they cooled, still showing the flow-ripples of when this whole void yawned open. Some stalactites are not stalactites at all, but flash-frozen drips of stone the size of cathedrals, never touched by dripwater, never rounded.

The floor is not flat. It is a broken plain of sharp features - pressure ridges where the cooling crust buckled upward into black-glass scales, collapse fields where honeycomb basalt shattered into knives, salt flats that stretch for days' walk, perfectly white, perfectly dry, made not by evaporated sea but by mineral sweat. Halite and anhydrite exhaled from the warm rock over millennia and settled in geometric crusts that crunch underfoot like frost, only warm. No riverbeds. No shorelines. No beach. The chemistry here is non-aqueous: where in another world there would be a lake, here there is a low, still haze of dense gas pooled in the basins - argon and carbon dioxide, cold-seeming but warm to the touch, that swirls around your ankles when you walk through it and then settles again, undisturbed for centuries.

The air itself has weight. Warm, still, mineral-scented. Iron and sulfur and hot stone. It never moves. There is weather here, in theory - vast enough to have its own pressure systems - but it is weather that never happens. A equilibrium so complete it feels artificial. No wind. No convection. No hot to cold. Just the same warm, close, stale eternity. If you leave a footprint in the salt, it will be there when the rock finally flows again, which will not be for a very long time. Young and never changing. Fresh and already fossilized.

Light does not belong here. There is no sky, no break, no glowworm fantasy. This is 0.4/3 darkness - the darkness that lives underground. If you stand still long enough, your eyes manufacture ghosts, but there is nothing. Only, if you wait, you will see what little light there is moves.

### The Inhabitants

Because it is dense.

Not dense with air, but dense with presences. The floor appears empty until you learn to look low, and then you realize it is crowded.

They are not animals as the surface would know them, though they are abundant. There are no plants for them to eat, so they have cut plants out of the equation entirely. The entire ecology is lithotrophic and necrophagic and strange.

The most common are the Shard-grazers - low, blind, wide things like overlapping iron pans, the size of dinner plates and sometimes the size of doors, that rasp the salt and basalt with a thousand cilia and extract the thin biofilm of chemobacteria living *in* the rock. They move so slowly you think they are stone until you come back an hour later and they have advanced a hand's breadth, leaving a polished trail. They cover the salt flats in their thousands, shoulder to shoulder, a living pavement that clicks and ticks faintly as their shells expand in the warmth.

Above them, attending them, are the Longlegs - stilt-creatures of black chitin and wire, three meters tall on legs thin as needles, that step with infinite care between the grazers and delicately spear the weak with a glassy proboscis. They do not flock. They simply are, at regular intervals, like trees in a forest that has no trees. If you count, you will find one every thirty meters, as far as you can walk, for farther than you can walk.

And then there are the Builders.

You notice their work before you notice them. Scattered, faint, primitive.

Small structures everywhere, but never a city. A cairn of balanced basalt blades, too precise to be a collapse, standing alone on an empty plain. A low wall, one stone high, running for twenty meters and then stopping for no reason. A circle of salt neatly cleared to the black floor. A small, dry hearth made of five stones, with no ash, no fuel, no sign anything was ever burned - only the stones are polished on the inside from many, many hands.

The Builders themselves are small and pale and warm to the touch. They look like six-limbed mole-crickets crossed with naked, eyeless primates, their skin translucent in a way you can only see if you bring light, which you should not. They live in such numbers you will never be alone. Under every upturned plate of basalt, there are a dozen, huddled, breathing slowly. In every crack, their eggs are plastered like mineral beads. You will hear them before you see them - not a sound, really, because this place is mostly quiet. The quiet has a texture: a faint, dry rustle, like paper being unfolded very far away. That's a hundred Builders shifting in the dark, a kilometer away, all at once.

They make tools. That is their weirdness, and the place's weirdness: intelligence without resource. No metal to smelt, no wood to carve, no fire that can live in this still, oxygen-thin air. So they knap basalt.

Everywhere you find their flakes. Razor-edged black glass chips struck from cores with a precision that suggests ritual. Simple hand-axes, scrapers, long needle-like awls for prying grazers from their shells. Some are left where they were made, in little knapping floors of a hundred sharp flakes around a hammerstone that is itself just a harder nodule of basalt. A paleolithic workshop repeated ten million times across the Vast, identical, never improved, never abandoned.

They do not build up. They arrange. Their highest technology is stacking. Their greatest achievement is a cairn four feet high that will stand until the Vault closes, which it will not.

And they watch you, without eyes. If you bring no light, you feel them gather. Not hostile. Curious in the way an equilibrium is curious when something finally moves. They will touch your boots with antennae soft as warm breath. They will leave a single perfect flake by your foot, an offering or a suggestion. They will not make a sound louder than the tick of their own shells.

When you leave - if you leave - they will dismantle your cairn and use the stones to make another like it, a few meters away, for no reason you will ever know.

The Vault does not change. The grazers eat stone. The Longlegs eat grazers. The Builders chip stone and stack it and wait in the warm dark. The gas pools lie undisturbed in the basins. The salt grows one crystal at a time. Overhead, the fresh, sharp ceiling hangs as it has hung since the day it cooled, and will hang the same way tomorrow, and tomorrow, and for a time that makes the word "tomorrow" sound like a joke.
