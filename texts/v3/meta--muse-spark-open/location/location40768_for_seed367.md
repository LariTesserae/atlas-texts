It is not a valley, not really. It is a Room.

High on the old plateau, where the wind has had so long to work that there is no more work left to do, the stone has forgotten how to be jagged. This is a country of age — 2.9 ancient — not mountains but the memory of mountains. The basalt and sandstone up here have settled into a long, warm, near-flat swell that goes to the horizon in every direction like the back of a sleeping animal. Dust the color of fired clay, stone softened to sand, then to flour. No rivers cut it. No rain finds it. The sky is high and pale and dry as bone, and the sun crosses it without ever really touching the ground.

And then the ground simply stops.

You could walk for a day across that flattened nothing and you would walk right past it if you didn't know the markers — a line of cairns, white stones stacked by hands. Between two of them, a crack. A narrow throat in the table-rock, no wider than a doorway, where the plateau has split along an old cooling joint. From above it looks like nothing, a shadow-line. You have to turn sideways to get in. The rock scrapes your shoulders.

Then you drop, and the world opens.

The Bowl.

Perhaps fifty meters across. Perhaps seventy. It doesn't matter; you can see every wall from wherever you stand, and your voice comes back to you off the other side a half second later. This is the scale of it — 0.1 hidden, pocket-scale, single-feature contained. A perfect, intimate, embedded world. The rim overhangs. The floor is perhaps thirty meters below the plateau surface. From the floor, you see only a ragged oval of sky, bright and distant.

The light that reaches you here is dim — 1.0 — not because it is night but because it is filtered. The high walls allow direct sun for only two hours at noon, a single white pillar that sweeps across the floor like a searchlight. The rest of the day is reflected light, clay-colored, bounced twice off stone and dust. It is always late-afternoon here. Warm — 2.1 — because the rock holds heat. At night the Bowl breathes it back out, and during the day it holds it close. The air at the bottom is 10 degrees warmer than the air on the plateau wind-scour above. A permanent thermal inversion.

And there is no water. 0.3.

No spring. No pool. No seep. The stone is dry through. The air tastes of warm dust and salt. If you licked the lower wall, your tongue would go white. Minerals have sweated out of the stone over epochs and formed a faint white crust, then flaked off as powder. A salt-field turned vertical.

Yet it is lush. Impossibly, chokingly lush.

This is the trick of the place. The vegetation does not drink from the ground. It drinks from the air.

On the plateau, the wind is a dry, unceasing scrape. But at night, as the plateau radiates heat to space, that warm air tumbling over the cold lip of the Bowl condenses. Not as rain — never as rain — but as a dense, hanging fog that pours silently over the rim like an invisible waterfall and pools in the Room. For six hours before dawn, the Bowl is full of cloud. You cannot see your hand. Droplets bead on everything.

And everything has evolved to catch it.

The walls are not bare stone. They are fur. From rim to floor they are draped, layered, thatched, *dense* — 1.6 — with life that shouldn't be able to live here.

There are no trees as you know trees. There are nets.

The dominant form is what the people of the Bowl call veil-moss and widow-curtains — great hanging lattices of black-green fibers, succulent, waxy, miles of filamentous leaves no thicker than string, interwoven into curtains and sails that hang from every ledge and crack. They are dry to the touch during the day, stiff and grey. At night they unfurl microscopic hairs and comb the fog. By morning each curtain is strung with a thousand bright beads of water which they channel down central gutters into swollen, turnip-like corms bulging from the rock. You can hear it if you get close, the morning drip, tick-tick-tick, from veil to corm.

Below that, shelf-plants. Rosettes the size of shields, thick leaves like felted suede, pale green and dusty rose, stacked in terraces one over the other so densely they form a living shingle on the walls. Each leaf is a bowl, fluted to funnel dew to its center. Each rosette is angled precisely to catch sun for one hour and fog for six.

And on the floor, the gardens. Not soil — there is no soil, only generations of powdered rock and decomposed veil-moss, a soft, peaty dust a foot deep that smells of tannin. Here the vegetation is lower, tighter, a carpet of creeping, woody succulents with swollen, water-storing stems and hard, varnished leaves that reflect heat. Pillow-cactus without spines. Stone-lettuce that lies flat to the ground in tight spirals. They are not green so much as olive, ochre, bronze. They thrive on the spillover from the walls — the corms that split, the leaves that fall, the morning drizzle of excess fog-water that never hits the ground but is drunk before it lands.

It is a vertical jungle made of air. Normal biology pushed to an extreme, but still normal — 0.5 weirdness. No glowing sap. Just physics and desperate adaptation.

And where there are plants this dense, things eat them. Overwhelmingly so. 2.7 fauna.

You notice the sound before you see anything — 1.5 loud. Even at midday when the Bowl is sleepy and hot, it hums.

The veil-curtains are alive with motion. Not one species, but thousands: beetles the size of your palm, black and glossy, their wing-cases pitted to collect dew; humming moths with wings of translucent paper, hovering endlessly before the succulent flowers that open only in the dim hour; colonies of pale, almost transparent lizards that live their entire lives in a single hanging curtain, never touching stone, leaping from thread to thread. Little finch-like birds — rust-red, dust-brown — build pendulous nests of fiber under the ledges, darting in and out in flocks so tight they seem like a single organism turning.

The walls buzz and chirp and click. On the ground, the succulent carpet rustles without wind: armored isopods the size of fists bulldoze through; millipedes in glossy bands patrol; long, thin snakes the color of dry moss slip between pillows. At the base of the walls, where guano and detritus accumulate, clouds of flies rise when you step, and above them dragonflies with four-inch wingspans hawk, their wings making that dry papery rattle.

A curtain tears under its own water-weight and the whole section of wall ripples as insects swarm the wound. And among it all, built into it, is the town.

Because the Bowl is not wild. It has been inhabited for as long as anyone can remember. 1.6 urban, 0.6 primitive.

The people did not build houses so much as they hollowed what was already there. The rock of the plateau is soft now — that ancient erosion — so soft you can carve it with hard wood and sandstone picks. Generations have cut back under the overhang, making shallow caves and connecting them with ledge-paths. From the floor it looks like a cliff dwelling, a honeycomb of dark doorways rising in tiers five and six stories high, fronted by woven screens and drying racks and terraces shored up with stacked stone. No metal is smelted here; there is no ore. Tools are chert, bone, fired clay. Ladders are lashed poles of an imported, wiry stem that grows nowhere in the Bowl but is cultivated in lines along the brightest part of the wall.

There are perhaps two hundred souls here. In a place this small, that is a city. You can hear them too, layered under the buzz — children shouting, mortars pounding dried corm to flour, someone scraping a hide, someone singing to keep rhythm while they re-weave a torn veil-curtain to keep it productive.

They farm the air. Each family tends a vertical plot of curtains and rosettes, repairing nets, guiding drip-lines made of braided fiber into clay cisterns the size of fists. They harvest corms. They keep hives of stingless bees in pottery hives tucked into the rock. They keep the small, hopping rodents that eat pillow-cactus and themselves get eaten. Nothing is wasted. Even the white salt crust is scraped and used to cure.

They have rules. Unspoken but iron. You do not cut a living curtain at the base. You do not take water from another's drip-pot. You do not shout during the fog hours; sound carries and it scares the moths that pollinate. You do not climb to the rim without reason — the wind up there will dry you in an hour, and the light blinds, and the void is endless and flat and makes you forget the Bowl exists at all.

The Bowl is the world. The plateau is sky. Beyond the lip, nothing is real.

At noon, the sun-shaft hits the floor. Dust glitters. The succulents open tiny, fierce yellow flowers all at once. The air is hot and smells of warm rock and nectar and guano and smoke. The buzz rises to a roar. Two hundred people pause in their carving and weaving and mending and look up for that one hour of true light, faces tilted. Then the shaft moves on, the dim returns, and the curtains begin to prepare, unfurling their invisible hairs, waiting for the fog to pour over the lip again.

This is how it holds itself — a hidden, dense, loud, dry, lush pocket of motion, waiting out the age of the world above it, one night's worth of fog at a time.
