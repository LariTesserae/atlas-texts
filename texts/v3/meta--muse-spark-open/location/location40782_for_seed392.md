This place is low.

Not low like a valley cut down into something higher — low like a first decision. The world here was made flat and then made again flat on top of that, a broad plain of made-ground that extends to a white-hazed horizon in every direction. There is no soil beneath it, no bedrock. If you lift an edge — and edges exist everywhere, because this is a young place — you find more making underneath: fired clay brick still sharp-cornered, the thumb-prints of the makers still in it, woven mats of blackened reed, palm trunks laid side by side and pegged with wooden dowels, all of it stacked in strata. The ground is a floor. The swamp is a floor with water left standing on it.

It is fiercely hot and impossibly bright. The sky is not blue but white-hot, a polished brightness without a visible sun, a light that comes from everywhere at once and leaves almost no shadow. Color hurts. The greens are brutal, the water flashes like metal. You squint without knowing why you're squinting. At noon there is no noon — the light does not move.

And yet it is wet.

Water weeps out of everything. It beads on the underside of every platform and girder and thatched roof and falls again without ever having rained. It sighs up between bricks where no mortar quite sealed. It hangs in the air as a warm condensation that never quite becomes mist, that gathers on your skin and on leaves in perfect round drops that do not roll. Every vertical surface is dark halfway up with perpetual damp. Every sharp young cut and channel in the made-ground is half-filled with water the color of tea, absolutely still.

The makers — whoever they were, with their primitive tools — built everything. That was the rule. There is no patch of this world that was not laid, woven, stacked, thatched, tied. And then they planted it, or it planted itself through them.

Vegetation is total and ecstatic and it grows only from what was built. There are no fields. Instead there are towers of woven basketry twenty feet high, now acting as trunks, from which living figs and air-plants erupt in every direction, their roots splitting the weave and drinking the weeping water. There are causeways of bundled cane, sagging now under the weight of orchids and creepers and small pale gourds that grow straight out of the thatch. There are ziggurats stepped in unfired brick, each step a paddy, each paddy a dense mat of taro and lotus and rice gone wild, their leaves huge and waxy and overlapping, steaming in the heat. The vegetation is not wild, exactly, because there was never a wild to be. It is lush the way a garden left to swallow its gardener is lush — intentional shapes overwhelmed by living that remembers the intention.

It is an open scale, town-sized, valley-sized, but there is no town. Only scattered presences. Density is low: a great stepped cistern-house here, empty, its timber doorway hung with curtains of living moss that never sway. A half-mile of shining open water, a foot deep over a perfectly flat brick plain, dotted with egret lilies. Another island-platform with its leaning granary whose stilts have become mangroves, prop-roots gripping the brick. Distance deceives because the bright air flattens everything. You can see for miles to the next structure, and the next after that, and they do not get smaller with distance the way they should. Some are too large. Some are too near when you look away and look back.

Because the reality is thin here.

Things do not behave. Water lies perfectly still on a slight incline and does not flow down. It condenses on the underside of leaves and falls *up* into the white sky in slow drops that vanish halfway. Sound is mostly absent because the air is heavy and stable — no wind ever moves here, no leaf rustles unless an animal parts it. Even the water makes no lapping. And when you walk, your footprints fill in behind you, not with water but with brick-texture, as if the ground remembers being flat.

The fauna is everywhere and colludes in the quiet. Dragonflies the size of swallows hang motionless in the bright air, wings blurred then still, then blurred — never moving position. White frogs crowd every wet surface, throats pulsing silently. Wading birds stand on one leg in the warm shallows for hours, not fishing, not sleeping, simply placed. Ants build black highways up the sides of the living basket-towers, carrying leaf-cuts that never get smaller. Everything is alive and nothing is hungry. Nothing decays. A fallen fruit on a brick will be glossy and perfect for weeks, covered in silent flies that do not eat it. This is the frozen equilibrium: hot, wet, bright, lush, teeming, and utterly without change.

The young erosion shows you how young. The corners of canals are still ninety degrees, not yet softened by years of water. The bricks have not rounded. The thatch has not rotted. Everything looks like it was finished yesterday and then left outside time. And in that stillness, the world repeats small errors: the same egret stilt-house appears three times around the horizon if you turn in a slow circle. Your own shadow, when you can find a faint one, sometimes points the wrong way.

You are not supposed to stay. Not because you are unwelcome — there is no welcome or unwelcome here, no gradient to push you along — but because if you stand still long enough in the hot bright quiet, with the water beading on your shoulders and not evaporating, you begin to feel made as well. Like a platform. Like a place for something green to grow out of.
