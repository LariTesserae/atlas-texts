It is not a valley. It is not a forest. It is a room inside those things that you could walk past a hundred times and never know it was there.

You find it by sound first.

Low ground. That is the first rule of this place. The land around it is flat in the way old land goes flat - not leveled, but tired. Glacial till and river plain, a wide cold meadow of sedge and stunted birch that has settled into itself over thousands of winters. The soil is thin over limestone. There are sinkholes here, shallow as soup bowls, grassed over and full of frost. The world is broad, pale, wind-scoured, and almost empty. And then the ground gives way.

Not far. A collapse, old enough that its edges have forgotten they ever broke.

A limestone throat, maybe thirty feet across at the lip, thirty feet deep. The walls dropped and stayed. Rain and thaw found the fault and widened it by a hair's breadth every century. Now it is a cylinder with one side slumped where a slab fell inward, making a ramp of rubble and black earth. From above, you wouldn't see it until you were at the edge - a dark lens in the meadow, breathing cold air.

The air is always cold here. Not winter-cold in a sharp way, but cellar-cold, stone-cold. A temperature that lives in the rock. Even in late summer the air that sighs up out of the hole is cold enough to make your breath show for a second before it thins. The sun never reaches the bottom directly. It is darkness down there, not pitch black, but the darkness of a room with the curtains drawn - a deep, permanent dusk.

Because the sink is so narrow and so deep, light falls only as a tilted column for an hour at noon, if the clouds let it. Most days there is only scattered grey from the sky overhead, reflected down the damp limestone walls until it turns to soft gloom. The walls themselves are wet-black and amber-grey, worn absolutely smooth in places by water, pocked with solution pockets in others. They weep. Condensation beads on every surface. In the cold, it never quite evaporates. It gathers and trickles.

This is where the water lives. Not as a lake, not as a river you can see coming for miles. Pockets. Intermittencies. The back wall of the sink never dried out. A spring line seeps from the bedding plane about two thirds of the way down, a dark stain that never stops even in a drought. It sweats out of the rock itself, runs in fingers over moss, collects in shallow rimstone basins the size of palm-prints and stew pots, overflows them, disappears into gravel, reappears again lower down as a trickle. After rain, it comes stronger - a brief, clear sheet falling five or six feet from an undercut lip before the rock drinks it again. The rubble ramp at the bottom is always damp to the touch. Your boots darken as soon as you step down into it. The floor makes a soft sucking sound.

And because of that damp, because the wind cannot scour it, because the cold is steady and the light is dim and constant - the walls are lush.

Not lush like jungle. Lush like a northern spring. Every surface that is not actively running water is covered. Moss in deep cushions, so thick and green it looks black in the low light. Varieties layered on each other: star moss and feather moss and a liverwort that spreads like wet leather. From cracks hang ferns. Not many species - just one or two that love cold limestone - but they are extravagant where they find hold. Hart's-tongue ferns in glossy rosettes, their fronds arching out a foot long, dripping. Maidenhair in the most sheltered corner where a slab leans and holds humidity like a cupped hand. Lichens, pale and crusty on the driest upper walls, give way to algal slick that glints dark green when your lamp catches it.

There are no trees down here. There is not room. One birch tried, on the slumped ramp - its seed blown in, its trunk now as thick as your wrist, leaning desperately toward the light it will never reach. It is white and stunted and bare for most of the year, but it is wreathed at its base in that same moss, and it holds fast.

There is almost nothing else. That is the quality of the sink - its emptiness. Scale is intimate, pocket-sized; you can see every wall from where you stand. And within that, the density is a held breath. A vast amount of space between presences. You could lie for an hour and watch a single woodlouse traverse a moss hummock. The moss itself seems to be the only citizen. This is not a crowded ecosystem, not a teeming floor. It is a hollow, a room, with one lush carpet and a lot of air.

What animals come here come rarely and do not stay. A cave wren, maybe, that has learned to nest in a limestone pocket just out of sight of the sky. You will not see it, but sometimes find a few twigs and lichen packed into a crevice. Slugs, grey as the stone, that leave silver trails across the moss at night. On the coldest nights, a fox will stand at the rim and look down, its eyes catching what little light there is, then move on. Very occasionally, in the dampest depths of autumn, tiny flies rise in a column in the still air - live for a day, die, and are gone. The rarity makes each one startling.

Someone has been here before you. More than one someone, over a long time, but never many.

Near the bottom, where the seep pools into a basin that actually holds water year-round - clear, perhaps two feet across and six inches deep, black with reflected wall - there are scattered traces, faint as fossils.

A low wall, no higher than your knee, built of unmortared limestone slabs, now tumbled and mossed over so you have to look to know it was built at all. It once cupped the pool, perhaps to keep it from silting. The stones are lichen-spotted and soft-edged. Beside it, a niche cut or widened by hand in the wall, flat-bottomed, just big enough for a person to sit with legs tucked. The tool marks are still there when you brush the moss away: short, chipped strikes made by a stone or poor iron adze. Primitive work, patient and uneven.

A few other things, left and lost: a birch-bark container collapsed to black mulch; three stacked cairns the size of fists along the ramp to mark the easiest way down, now half toppled by frost heave; a wooden ladder, or what was a ladder - two poles with rungs lashed by sinew and root - now fallen and slick, its lower end sunk in moss and slowly becoming peat.

No one lives here. It is not a dwelling. It is a station. A place to come down to collect the cold clean water in a dry August, to sit out of the wind, to cut fern for bedding, to be out of sight. You can feel the slowness of its use. Generations apart, same gestures. The built things are not decaying fast; they are being absorbed. Moss grows over stone at a rate you can measure in years per millimeter.

And the sound. The sound is what makes the emptiness feel full.

From above, in the meadow, there is only wind. Down here, the shape of the sink is a drum and an ear trumpet. A subterranean river moves not far below - you never see it, but the limestone transmits it. A low, ceaseless roar of water moving through a passage too tight and too deep for light or people, grinding past rock. It is deafening. Not loud like an explosion, but loud like being inside a waterfall. The walls focus it until it seems to come from everywhere - from the stone itself. You have to raise your voice to be heard a foot away. When you stop speaking the roar rushes back in and fills your chest.

It overlays everything: the drip from the spring line, *plok... plok...* into the pool, the occasional rustle of a fern frond as condensation finally weights it enough to spill, the soft sigh of cold air sinking down the throat. It drowns them and makes them precious. You find yourself listening for the tiny sounds inside the huge one.

The dynamic of the place is slow. Almost geological. Water finds its way, freezes, widens a crack by a fingernail. Moss creeps a quarter inch. The pool silts and is cleared by a hand. Light shifts a degree every week. Nothing happens quickly here except the roar, which has been roaring the same way since before the wall was built, since before anyone found the rabbit-hole and slipped down to drink.

If you stay long enough your eyes adjust to the dusk and you see the green is not uniform - it is a hundred shades, from near-black to luminous yellow where a shaft of diffuse light catches a fern. Your skin goes cool and damp. The smell is mineral and peat and cold living green. The sound settles from a wall of noise into a presence, a weight of air pushing softly on your ears.

It is a hidden place, in-balance with itself. It does not want to be larger. It keeps its dampness, its darkness, its cold lushness perfectly within its broken circle, while the wind passes over the lip a few feet above and knows nothing.
