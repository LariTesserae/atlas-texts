This is a high place that shouldn't be wet, and shouldn't be warm, and yet is both.

A plateau lifted two miles into the air, or near enough. Not a peak - a broad, lifted country, a table of stone the size of a county. You can stand at its edge and see the world fall away in a single, young cliff, so sharp it looks cut. The rock is fresh. That's the word that stays with you here. The world hasn't had time to soften it.

The escarpment is still jointed and blocky, the basalt and sandstone laid down in clean, flat courses, then wrenched upward. There are no softened hills below, no fan of scree and slope. The wall comes up in steps and sheer faces, with ledges still square-shouldered, with cracks that haven't been widened by frost into valleys yet. Water pours over that lip everywhere. Not in a few great waterfalls, but in a hundred thin ones - braids of silver that slip over the stone, find a ledge, gather, slip again. From a distance the whole rim of the plateau seems to be weeping.

Up top, the world is flat, and tilted just slightly inward, like a very shallow bowl. That is why the water stays.

### The Stone That Never Dries

This plateau catches cloud. Not storm-cloud, not violent monsoon - the steady trade of warm air pushed up its cliff-face, cooled just enough to condense, but never enough to chill. By mid-morning every day the air going over the lip turns to mist, thickens, and then falls as a fine, windless rain that doesn't really fall so much as settle. It beads on everything. It sheets down every rock face. It fills every joint in the stone.

The ground itself is young stone with barely any soil on it. In places the bedrock comes right to the surface in broad, pale pavements, scored with fresh glacial or tectonic striations. Elsewhere it has broken into great flat slabs, tilted slightly askew where the uplift was uneven, leaving long, straight-walled fissures between them, a foot to ten feet across, and dark with water. You hear water before you see the town - everywhere, low and constant, not roaring, but trickling, dripping, seeping.

Springs come up under pressure where you wouldn't expect them. A wall of dark basalt will be dry one moment and then, a hand's breadth lower, be black and streaming, as if the rock is sweating. In the early morning, before the sun burns the mist off, every vertical face is covered in droplets that catch the light and slide down slowly, joining, falling. The sound is small. A plink into a pooled fissure. A hush over flat stone.

There are marshes here at altitude, which feels wrong. Where the tilt of the plateau gathers the seepage, the pavement drowns under six inches to a foot of clear, warm water. Reeds and cotton-headed grasses grow straight out of the flooded rock, their roots finding no earth, just anchoring in cracks. Between them are lilies with broad, waxy leaves, and mats of bright green liverwort that float like rafts. The water is so still it mirrors the sky perfectly, so you get a doubled world - cloud below and above - broken only where a footbridge crosses or a reed trembles.

And there are no birds to break it. No frogs. No insects stitching the air. The water is clean to the point of emptiness.

### The Sparse City

People live here. They have for a long time, though the land looks like it hasn't.

It is, by any measure, a city - not a village, not a scattering of huts - but it doesn't fill the space the way a city should. It is an urbanism of intervals. That's the first thing you notice walking its ways: how much emptiness it allows to remain between things.

The buildings are low and heavy, made the way people build when they have stone everywhere and metal nowhere. Walls of hand-split basalt block, fitted without mortar, or with a pale lime mortar that has already been washed to softness by the constant damp. Roofs are thatch, thick and steep, or slab roofs weighted down with stones against the updrafts at the rim. Every house stands on a plinth a foot or two above the wet, with a shallow channel cut around it to lead water away. Between any two houses there might be a full house-lot of open pavement, or a flooded fissure crossed by a log, or just a garden.

The streets are not streets. They are the plateau itself, modified. Where the natural pavement was flat enough, they left it. Where it cracked, they bridged it. Where it held water, they set stepping stones, worn smooth and dark in the middle from generations of feet. You can walk for five minutes from the weavers' quarter and be in a place where there is only stone and reeds and the sky, then find another cluster of dwellings ahead - a dozen houses around a cistern, a communal oven, a yard where clay pots are drying under eaves.

It is organized - unmistakably. Terraces have been cut into the eastern rise where the plateau is not quite flat, and those terraces are too even, too sustained, to be natural. Each one holds water for part of the year, a shallow warm paddy for taro and rice and a reed whose starch they pound into cakes. The wall of each terrace is the same hand-laid basalt, and weirs of notched wood regulate the flow from one to the next with a precision that speaks of agreement, of law. Aqueducts of hollowed logs on stone piers carry spring water over marshy ground to where it is needed. Cisterns, deep and square-cut, are covered with slabs to keep them clean.

Near the center, where three of the largest fissure-springs converge, the building grows denser. Here the stonework becomes finer: a plaza paved with flat slabs so tightly joined that marsh cannot come through, a long hall with a timber roof that spans thirty feet, its posts whole tree trunks smoothed by adzes. The stones of its threshold are hollowed where feet have passed. Around it are storehouses, workshops where looms clack softly, a market space that is mostly empty most days. The scale of it all says: thousands live here, across the whole plateau. But you rarely see them all at once.

They build like this because they can afford to. There is room. The plateau is a valley-sized country, open to the sky in every direction, and the water is everywhere, so there is no need to crowd around a single well. And the plateau is stable. That is the strangest part.

### A World in Equilibrium

Nothing here erodes quickly, even though it should. The water should be cutting gullies, tearing the soft thatch, carrying soil off the terraces. Instead, it moves as if it has always moved this way and always will. The waterfalls over the rim have no plunge pools. The fissures do not widen year to year. The thatch does not rot, though it is wet every day.

The temperature does not change. It is warm - not hot, not cool, the warmth of late spring held indefinitely. Sweat dries slowly in the damp air. The stone stays warm to the touch even in shadow. There are no seasons. The mist comes in the morning, lifts by noon, returns as dew by evening. The reeds grow, are harvested, grow again at the same rate. The water table never rises to flood nor falls to drought. It breathes in place.

And because the water and the warmth never fail, the plants have become lush without becoming dense. This is not a jungle. It is a sparseness made of abundance. Ferns as tall as a man grow singly out of cracks, each one perfect, its fronds unfurled and beaded with water. Taro fields are emerald and orderly. Moss covers the north side of every wall in a velvet an inch thick. Flowering vines climb every house-post, waxy white and pink, scentless - because there is nothing to scent for. No bees. No hummingbirds. The wind does the work of pollination, and the water carries seed. The result is a landscape that looks tended even where it is wild, because nothing competes desperately. Everything has enough light, enough water, enough warmth. Nothing needs to choke anything else.

That absence goes deepest in the ears. A city of several thousand should be loud. Here, you hear your own breathing. Without animals, the usual texture is gone. No birdsong at dawn, no dogs, no cicadas at dusk, no lowing or bleating. Only human sounds, and the water. A loom shuttle. A mortar and pestle. A child's voice carrying a surprising distance over flat stone and still water. Footsteps. Then quiet again. Even the wind is quiet, broken by the low profile of the buildings and the moisture in the air.

At night the normal light thins but never quite leaves. The high air is clear, and the stars are hard and innumerable, unfiltered by haze. Their reflection doubles in every marsh and cistern, so the town at night looks as if it is floating in space - points of light above and below, with the dark shapes of houses and reeds stitched between. The weirs go on trickling. The weeping rocks go on weeping. The terraces hold their level.

It is a young world that acts old. A raw, freshly lifted piece of crust that should be barren and shattered, but instead has been given, all at once, water and warmth and people who knew how to lay stone so it would last. Nothing is in a hurry to become something else. You get the feeling if you came back in a hundred years, you would find the same stepping stone still just above the same still water, with the same moss on the same wall, and the same quiet between houses where the plateau keeps its emptiness intact.
