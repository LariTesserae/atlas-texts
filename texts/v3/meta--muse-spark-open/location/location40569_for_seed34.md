High on the back of the world.

Not a mountain. A mountain has a peak and sides. This is the top itself - an uplifted country, a plateau so broad its far edge is only a variation in the haze, a tableland lifted into the cool air where the sky sits close. The elevation is not dramatic in the way of cliffs, but absolute: you have climbed for days to get here and now there is no higher to go, only this vast, level breadth going on under a huge sky. The air is thin enough to make your breathing audible, cool enough that every shadow holds a little winter in it.

It is a land of stone first.

Old stone, settled stone. Not jagged, not raw. This is established country, weathered into its final shape long ago and now simply maintaining it. The plateau is not flat - it is creased and stepped, a labyrinth of shallow basins, broad-backed ridges, and long, low scarps where darker layers of rock outcrop. The rock is a pale, stratified limestone and sandstone, wind-scoured to a softness at all its edges, pitted and channeled by water until it looks like weathered bone. Everything here has been worked over for a very long time. The gullies have found their courses. The wind has found its corridors. Nothing eroding catastrophically, nothing collapsing - just the slow, patient balance of a place that has been as it is for millennia.

And it is wet. Not under water, not oceanic, but wet in its skin and breath.

Water is everywhere at the surface, close enough to touch. The cool air condenses as it crosses the plateau, and low cloud comes down to brush the ridges most mornings, leaving everything beaded and dripping. The stone weeps. Every scarp-face glistens with seep-springs that never quite become waterfalls, just a constant dark varnish of moisture feeding moss-black streaks down the rock. The basins between ridges hold marsh, or mere, or mire - not deep lakes, but broad, shallow pans of cold water reflecting the sky, fringed with black peat and linked by slow, braided streams that have no hurry. After rain - and it rains often, briefly, decisively - the whole plateau shines. Footsteps fill with water behind you. The clefts in the rock gurgle. For a place so high and so cool and so spare, it is loud with water, quietly so.

What lives here in green does not try to dominate. Vegetation is sparse by volume, though tenacious where it gets a hold. There are no forests up here, no canopy to break the wind. Instead, the green is low and flat against the wet stone: mats of lichen like old leather, grey-green and rust-orange, crusting every boulder; tight cushions of alpine grass in the cracks; sedge and cotton-grass shivering around the marsh edges; here and there a stunted willow or birch, no taller than your knee, growing sideways away from the prevailing wind, its bark polished by weather. Between them, the bare rock shows through more than the plant. From a distance the plateau looks almost naked, a pale stone country with a sparse green stubble. Up close, you see how much work those little plants are doing, holding peat together, filtering seep-water clear.

And yet the place is dense. Not with trees. With presence.

Because this plateau is built.

Not in the way of steel and glass. There is no machine here, no wire, no hum, no device more complex than a lever and a stacked stone. But there is a city, if a city can be made entirely by hands without tools beyond hands.

It covers the middle of the world. As far as you can walk and still see the same pale rock underfoot, there are walls.

Low, thick, dry-stone walls, built from the same limestone the plateau is made of, so that at first you think they are natural outcrops until you see their regularity. Terraces. Enclosures. Houses like hives and barrows, round-shouldered, half-sunk into the ground for warmth, roofed with flat slabs canted to shed water. Cisterns cut straight into the rock, brimming with dark, cold rainwater. Channels, aqueducts of no more than fitted troughs running along the tops of walls, carrying seep-water from the weeping scarps down into every basin, every dwelling. Bridges of single slabs over every rill. Pavements of fitted stone where the mire would otherwise be impassable.

It is dense, and it is layered. No part is empty. The walls touch and join and re-join. A terrace that is a field becomes the roof of a byre below it. A causeway that carries water on its upper side carries people on its lower path. Families of buildings cluster like barnacles, sharing walls to conserve heat and stone. Alleyways turn into waterways in a heavy rain and no one minds; they were built for both. From above, from a ridge, the settlement looks less like a town laid out than like a vast, deliberate encrustation - a coral of stone grown by generations upon generations, filling the natural basins and stepping up the low scarps until human construction and plateau become one thing, a continuous stone skin.

It is vast enough to have weather. To the west, a squall darkens the marshes while to the east, a mile or ten miles away, sunlight still lies warm on the cisterns. Clouds form on its breadth, rain on its own damp. You can walk for a day through the city and still be in the city, only a different quarter where the walls are built a little higher against a different wind, where the cisterns are round instead of square. The people who live here - and people do live here, quietly, in numbers that match the density of their houses - will tell you the city has no edge, only a thinning, where the walls grow lower and farther apart, and then there is only the wet pale rock again, going on to the horizon under the same big sky.

Light falls normally, cleanly, without drama. This is not a strange light or a colored light. It is mid-latitude daylight, high-altitude clear, a cool white sun that makes shadows sharp at noon and long and blue in the evening. Because the air is clean and thin, colors are true: the grey of stone, the black of water, the dull green of sedge, the white of cloud reflected. At night the stars are excessive.

It is a quiet place. For a city so large and so dense and so wet and so active, the sound level is startlingly low. There are no engines, no loud animals, no great concentrations of calling birds. What you hear, if you stand still on a causeway, is:

The faint, constant drip of seep-water from stone into trough.
The wind riffling cotton-grass.
The soft splash of a foot in a shallow channel.
A distant cough. A child’s voice carried oddly far in cold air and then gone.
Water finding its level, everywhere.

Fauna is rare. Not absent, but spare as the vegetation. A few hardy sheep the color of stone, tended close to the houses. Small, quick brown birds that dart between walls. Insects that live brief, cold lives over the marsh pans. Perhaps a high hawk, very far up, a speck turning slowly over a country that is too large for it to matter. No herds. No choruses. The ecology is not fecund; it is careful. Energy moves slowly here, through lichen and sedge and sheep and people, without waste.

But it is not still. This is not a ruin. For all its silence and its balance, this world is active.

Every day the cloud comes down and lifts. The marshes breathe in rain and breathe out mist. The streams rise and fall between their stone banks. People are always moving - not hurrying, but always doing: clearing a silted channel with a stone hoe, lifting a fallen slab back onto a roof, herding sheep from one walled grazing cell to another, carrying water, mending wall. No single act is loud or dramatic, but multiplied by ten thousand walls and ten thousand hands across a continent-scale plateau, it is a perpetual, low-grade tide of maintenance and growth. The city is not finished. It never erodes. It simply continues itself, stone by stone, season by season, as water continues itself from cloud to seep to channel to cistern to marsh to cloud.

You understand after a little while that the rules here are simple: stone endures, water moves, what is built must respect both. No wall is built to fight the water; every wall is built to guide it. No house is built to fight the cold; every house is sunk and shared and thick. Nothing is imported. Nothing is wasted. The plateau provides stone and water and a little grass, and that has been enough for a very long time to make a dense, humane, high country that holds weather on its back like a thought.

If you stay until evening, when the low sun turns the seep-stains on the scarps to gold and every cistern holds a perfect, still piece of sky, the vastness and the density stop feeling contradictory. The city is vast because the plateau is vast. It is dense because life up here must huddle and interlock to stay warm and dry and together. It is silent because stone and cool air swallow sound, and because no one here has need to shout.

You leave your footprints in wet peat between two walls that are older than any memory, and behind you, water very quietly fills them up.
