This isn't a valley so much as a bowl that got caught in the mountain and never got taken out.

High up - high enough that the air thins a little and every breath feels rinsed - the ridge breaks and folds inward. A perfect, almost circular declivity, maybe three hundred meters across, rim to rim. Old. Established. Not a crater, not a quarry, a weathered thing. The basalt underneath shows through in places where the lip has been worn smooth by a thousand years of water coming over, but for the most part you don't see rock at all. The bowl has had time to settle into itself, to find its balance.

Water is everywhere here, but not in a lake, not in a river. The bowl catches cloud.

It rolls over the eastern rim most mornings before the sun clears the peaks, a wet, low bellied mass that snags on the trees and stays. It condenses on everything. Needles, leaves, moss, thatch, skin, stone. By mid-morning it has become drip. By noon, weep. The rock itself weeps - the exposed basalt on the upper rim is black with it, streaked with mineral tears, seeping from pinholes and bedding planes in cold, clear threads that never quite stop. They join, they braid, they fall. Not waterfalls - veils. Stair-splashes over terraces. The bottom of the bowl is not ground. It's a saturated mesh.

Because the vegetation took it.

You go expecting a forest in a hollow and what you find is a hamlet that *is* forest, or a forest that decided to be a hamlet. Plant-as-substrate doesn't cover it. There is no line where tree stops and building begins. At this elevation it's all cool-temperate wet growth run riot - mountain cedars and a kind of broadleaf myrtle that isn't supposed to get this big, clubmosses the size of shrubs, sheets of liverwort like blankets, filmy ferns in every crack. And people have lived here long enough to learn not to clear it but to persuade it.

The houses are grown and woven and lashed and only secondarily built. Young cedars are bent while green and trained together into arch-ribs, and over decades they fuse. Myrtle trunks are coppiced into living walls that put out leaves every spring right through the side of someone's kitchen. Roofs are frame - simple timber, mortise-and-tenoned with wooden pegs and the occasional forged iron nail, hand-split shingles - but no roof stays bare for a season. Moss takes it, then sedums, then ferns root in the moss. From a distance you don't see a village, you see a particularly dense thicket with smoke coming out of it, and light in the gaps.

It's dense. Impossibly dense for such a small place.

The bowl's interior is all terraces, cut into the slope long ago and held with dry-stone walls that are themselves gardens. Narrow. Intimate. You can stand in the middle - where the cistern-mere is, black water, always rippling from the drip - and see every house at once, hear every house at once. There are perhaps sixty structures crammed onto those rings, but because they're layered vertically it feels like three times that. A walkway of cedar half-logs runs at knee height through living roots, then another walkway five meters up weaves through the lower branches, then another braid of rope and wicker footbridges higher still where the children go. Gardens hang over gardens. Undercrofts under floors full of mushrooms and stored tubers. Every cubic meter is claimed. Vines for beans climb house-posts that are still alive. Chickens roost in the rafters of a bridge. It's a hamlet that uses volume the way a city uses sprawl.

Primitive is not empty. The tech here is hand technology refined to obsession. No mills, no engines. Everything is adze-smoothed, draw-knifed, split, bent with steam, tied with bark cord and sinew and root-fiber. The big communal tools are in the lowest terrace, under a cedar that has grown to roof them - a huge stone anvil for cold-beating native copper, a pit-loom big enough to weave a house-wall, a set of bone and alder-wood pumps that lift the mere-water up to the top terraces for washing. People here can make anything from the forest, and they have.

It is cool, always. Even at noon when the light breaks flat and normal through the cloud-cap - no strange sun, no gloom, just clean high mountain daylight filtered through leaf - the air stays at that edge where your hands want to be in pockets or around a bowl. Your breath ghosts a little in the mornings. The water coming off the rim is snow-melt cold a day earlier on the peaks. And so everything steams a little, or seems to. That helps the sound carry.

The sound is overwhelming. The bowl is a small amphitheater and it is *loud*.

Water first, incessant: drip from ten thousand leaf-tips onto ten thousand lower leaf-tips, trickle along stone channels cut to direct it, patter into barrels, gurgle under walkways. Then fauna - because this dense, dripping garden five stories thick is one solid mass of animal life. 

It should not support this much, by rights, in the cool uplands. But the constant wet and the total vegetation mean insects never stop. Cloud-midges in humming pillars, jewel beetles as big as your thumb working the dead wood, moths like folded leaves that flare eyespots when you pass. And where there are insects, birds in numbers that become noise. Warblers and finch-like things and a crow-sized species with a white collar that lives only in this bowl, thousands of them, calling, scolding, hammering bark, dragging moss for nests in the eaves. Swifts cut the air over the mere at dusk in such numbers the light flickers. Tree-frogs chorus from every water-channel with a sound like wet fingers on glass. Salamanders the length of your forearm slip through the cistern pools. Pine martens and small bears - not tame, not wild, just *there* - pad across the high bridges at night, indifferent to people.

No one hushes them. The people match them.

This is not a quiet mountain village. It's turbulent, bazaar-busy, hyperactive with work. The bowl is too small and too rich to allow idleness, and too wet to allow anything to wait till tomorrow. Harvest is constant because something is always fruiting on some level. Rot is constant because everything molds if you don't turn it. A cry goes up - honey, the myrtle hives have swarmed again - and half the village is up the bridges with smoke pots and wicker baskets yelling instructions at each other. Children scream with laughter chasing frogs under houses. Someone is always mending a walkway, someone is always splitting tubers with a wooden mallet that echoes. The air smells like cold smoke and ripe fruit and leaf-rot and wet wool.

The rule of the place is simple and non-negotiable: you do not dam, you do not clear. You redirect, you train, you borrow. A stone moved must be replaced with moss. A tree cut must be a cutting planted. The balance is old, weathered, established - the bowl has been like this long enough that the stones fit. If you plug a seep, another opens and undermines your floor. If you cut a cedar for light, the ferns take the new sun and drink twice as much water and your thatch rots. The elders don't enforce this so much as remind you when you forget.

At night it gets cooler still, and the cloud often comes back down and sits in the bowl like milk. The houses button up - shutters of oiled bark-paper, hearths in stone pits vented through hollow living trunks as chimneys. Light from those hearths speckles the whole thicket, warm pinpricks in the cold green. The weeping rocks never stop. The frogs get louder. The martens thump overhead. And somewhere in the murk a baby wails and a neighbor sings louder to drown it and two old women laugh in a way that sounds exactly like crows, and the whole dense, dripping, living hamlet mutters and drips and rustles and holds its shape inside its ring of stone - small, soaked, crowded, and utterly, insistently alive.
