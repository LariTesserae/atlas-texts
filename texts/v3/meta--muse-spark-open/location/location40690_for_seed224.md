It is not a desert you walk across. It is a place you find, very high up, after the air has thinned and dried to the point where breathing feels like inhaling powdered glass.

The mountain is the first thing, and then it stops being the thing. From the outside, from the approach along the wind-scoured ridge, it looks like nothing: a low, unremarkable fold in the basalt caprock, at about the elevation where even the lichens have given up. No snow — not really. It is too cold and too dry for snow to survive here; what little atmospheric moisture there is was wrung out two thousand meters below. The temperature sits far below freezing, a steady, soaking cold that doesn't bite so much as leach. The rock is black glass and rust-pale tuff, weathered smooth not by water — there hasn't been water here in an epoch — but by wind carrying its own dust against itself, patiently. The edges are rounded, settled into themselves. Old.

The entrance is not built. Or, not anymore. It was once cut, a perfect vertical slit barely wider than shoulders, but the mountain has had time to forget it. The wind has beveled its lips. You have to know where to kneel and brush aside the loose shale to see it. Inside, the sound of the wind simply stops.

---

The pocket.

It is the size of a small chapel, or a buried cistern. Perhaps eight paces from wall to wall, though it is difficult to be sure, because the geometry does not want to be measured.

At first you think it's a cave. The floor is the mountain itself, leveled — not cut flat, but *persuaded* flat. A slab of unjointed stone the color of cold iron, without tool marks, without seams. Its dust is so fine it feels oily, undisturbed except where you step. Your boot prints remain too sharp, too loud. The air inside is absolutely still and absolutely dry, sealed, mineral. It smells of nothing, then, after a minute, faintly of ozone and hot ceramic, although nothing is hot.

The light is dim and does not come from a source. It is a low, ambient pearl-grey, like light remembered by stone. It has no direction, makes no shadows until you move, and then your shadow stutters, as if it had to think about which way to fall. In the upper reaches of the chamber, near the low vaulted ceiling, the darkness is thicker. Not shadow; a texture.

Scattered.

That is the word for what is in here. Not ruins. Not occupation. Offerings, maybe. Or instruments left behind by someone who understood that leaving things sparsely is a kind of tidiness.

There are five of them. Or six, depending on when you count.

1. A low plinth, knee-high, made of something that is not stone and is not metal. It looks like stacked smoke that decided to be solid. Its surface is matte and warms slightly when you are not looking at it.

2. Three slender stelae, each as thin as a page of slate, set equidistant in the dust. They stand without base or anchoring, their bottom edges hovering a finger's width above the floor. They vibrate very slowly. If you hold your hand near one, you feel not vibration but a faint push, as if the air between you and it is being gently stretched.

3. A tangle in the far corner that at first looks like wire, but closer is revealed to be empty: thin, black lines drawn in space itself, about a meter across, knotted. They describe angles that shouldn't close. Dust motes that drift near them fall *around* the knot. Once every few minutes, one of the lines ticks, without sound.

4. The last is not an object. It is a shallow depression in the center of the floor, about the span of your arms, perfectly circular, perfectly smooth. It holds nothing. The dim light pools there a little deeper. If you stare into it, your depth perception gives way. It is not a hole. It is a place where distance is behaving differently — where the bottom of a centimeter-deep dish feels like you are looking down a mine shaft that bottoms out somewhere behind you.

There is no animal life. No plant. No fungus, no bacterial slick. Nothing that lives has ever lived here; the sealed air, the absolute lack of water — not even a trace film — forbids it. The only chemistry here is mineral and other. The world inside the pocket is pre-life, or post-life, a clean room kept clean by its own stillness.

And the stillness is almost total. Sound here is a trespass. Your blood is the loudest thing. If you hold your breath, you can hear the mountain very faintly — not through the walls, but *as* the walls: a deep, subsonic settling, a groan so slow it takes an hour to complete a single cycle of stress and release. You hear it not with your ears but with the soles of your feet.

This is the established quality of the place: it is not decaying. It is not new, waiting to be weathered. It has weathered already, to exactly this state, and now it maintains. The stelae do not rust. The knotted lines do not unravel. The cold does not deepen. The slowness is intentional — a dynamic held at low idle.

Until you notice the weirdness, and then you notice nothing else.

It starts with the count. You were sure there were five structures. Now there are six. Now five again. No movement. No flicker. The number simply resolves differently when you blink.

The vaulted ceiling is closer than it should be when you look straight at it, and farther when seen peripherally. The chamber is eight paces across if you walk the perimeter, but if you walk directly across, it takes eleven. The discrepancy does not correct itself. Both distances remain true.

If you stay long enough — and the cold encourages you not to — you see that the dim light is not steady. It pulses, but not in brightness. It pulses in *correctness*. For a long, comfortable stretch of minutes, everything agrees: stone is stone, distance is distance. Then, for a fraction of a second, the agreement lapses. The far wall seems to be both behind the plinth and in front of it. The corner where the knotted lines hang seems to contain more corner than a corner should. Your own hands look, for an instant, too far from your wrists.

Nothing dramatic happens. There is no tear, no portal. It is quieter than that. The engineering here — if it is engineering — does not break physics so much as wear it thin by holding it in an awkward posture for too long. The stelae are not antigravity machines. They are places where weight has been politely, locally dissuaded. The depression in the floor is not a well; it is a footnote that got misplaced into three-dimensional space. This is technology a long way beyond modern, not because it is shiny, but because it is patient, nanoscale and cosmic at once, willing to let reality crease rather than force it.

The scattered things are not ruins. They are functioning as intended. Quietly. Minimally. Slowly trading something — spin for position, time for curvature — at a rate you would have to measure over seasons to notice. They will still be doing it when the mountain above has finally worn away to dust, because the mountain is irrelevant to them. The pocket was never really *in* the mountain. The mountain was convenient place to fold around it to keep the wind out.

When you leave — and you will leave quickly, because the silence begins to feel not empty but full, as if it has been holding a place for you and you do not fit it — you turn back once at the slit.

From outside, from two steps away, there is no slit. There is only the weathered fold of basalt, settled, long-existed, balanced. The cold wind starts again immediately, as if uncorked. Your boot prints in the dust inside are the only proof, and the mountain will keep them for a long, long time, perfectly sharp, perfectly dry, in the dim, low, quiet room where geometry waits.
