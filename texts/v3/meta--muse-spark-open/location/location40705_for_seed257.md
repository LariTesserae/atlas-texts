It is a city that was built to be a mountain, and then the water came and decided to keep it.

You are very high up, which you can feel even though you are deep underwater. The pressure tells you: you are down deep. The cold that *should* be here tells you nothing, because the water is warm. Lukewarm as blood, as a bath left for an hour. It holds the heat. It does not move.

This place is on a peak. Not a peak that points — a peak that was flattened a long time ago, before anyone can remember, and then flattened again by water and time until it became a table. An ancient mesa of built ground, standing alone in open water. If you swim far enough from its edge in any direction there is nothing but the fall. The stone — which is not stone, it is *made* stone — goes vertical and goes down until darkness swallows it. So the whole world is small, the size of a hamlet, a temple compound, a single plaza and its houses. You can see its boundary everywhere by the way the water gets darker beyond it.

Everything you touch is made. There is no natural substrate left here, if there ever was. The original mountaintop was quarried away generations upon generations ago, and replaced block by block, terrace by terrace, by hand.

The technique is primitive and it shows. This was not poured or printed or grown. It was split and stacked. You can still see the chisel marks on every surface, softened now to a ripple. Basalt blocks the size of chests and tables, limestone bricks the size of loaves, all fitted without mortar so precisely that you cannot slip a shell-shard between them, and then left for so long that the water has blurred their seams with mineral skin.

Time has done its work. Erosion here is not a violence. It is a settling. For thousands of years warm water has licked gently at worked edges. Everything ancient becomes round. Stairs rise in soft steps, their corners pillowed. Doorways are ovals now where they were once rectangles. Courtyards slope shallowly toward central drains that lead nowhere anymore. Columns have become almost like standing stones, thick at the base, narrow at the top, leaning a degree or two under their own patience. The whole built-mesa has settled into its final form: low, broad, incurved, like a piece of pottery worn smooth in a river. It will not get any smoother. It is done.

There are no plants. Not one frond, not one weed in a crack. The water is too deep for sun, and something in the stone itself rejects it — too clean, too mineral, too completely occupied. What would be vegetation elsewhere is replaced by other covers: a thin, warm felt of bacteria that makes every horizontal surface slightly slick and slightly amber; pale, flat sponges that crust over walls like paint; mineral flowers — chimneys of white and rust-red calcite that precipitated up slowly, inch by inch, around warm seeps, and now stand brittle as spun sugar on rooftops. They are not alive, but they grow.

And it is crowded. So crowded you cannot move without negotiating presences.

The houses — if they are houses — are dense, close, wall-to-wall, but small. A hamlet for a people who did not need height. The lanes between them are not streets, they are gaps, sometimes only a shoulder-width, sometimes opening into a cistern-plaza where the water is a fraction clearer. Everything is built low, one story, two at most, tiered like steps so that each roof is the terrace of the next. All of it is occupied, just not by builders.

The fauna have taken the city as reef.

They are everywhere, and they are abundant in the way deep warm water can be when there is structure to hold it. This is not fish in the way you think of fish darting in light water. This is a slow, dense suspension.

Count them by touch:

The Filterers: great, silent curtains of jellied bells, each the size of a cloak, anchored by one trailing foot to a lintel or a stair, pulsing once every long minute, glowing faintly from within with a deep amber chemiluminescence. There are hundreds of them. They hang in alleys and doorways like laundry, and you must part them to pass. They do not sting. They close gently around you and let go.

The Crawlers: blind, white, long-legged things like stone-crabs and isopods and something between, with backs encrusted in the same block-stone, so that when they stop you think they are part of the wall. They pick at the bacterial felt all day and all night with soft clicks of primitive tools they carry — not made tools, but found ones: a sharp shell, a spine, a chip of obsidian they hold in a forelimb and scrape. They have always done this. They are the maintenance.

The Dwellers: eel-like, but warm-blooded, with whiskered, almost human faces, as long as your arm, as thick as your leg. They live in the old rooms, dozens to a house, coiled together in the dark interiors where no light reaches at all. Their eyes are vestigial pearls. They navigate by warmth and pressure. They are not afraid. If you lie still in a plaza, they will come and rest against you for your heat, layering over you, breathing very slowly through slits that flutter like curtains.

There are more. Clouds of tiny shrimp that live only in the drain-shafts. Hard, barnacle-like things that chime. All of them together make the water thick. You feel you are moving through a broth of living things, all close, all touching, all patient. Nothing is in a hurry.

Light is a rumor here. There is no sun. Far above you — impossibly far, the ocean overhead like an atmosphere of black glass — there may be weather, but it does not reach this place. What light there is comes from below and from within. The filterers carry their amber glow. Some of the bacterial felt glimmers faintly blue where it is disturbed. The warm seeps that feed the mineral flowers pulse with a dull red heat, like embers under water. It is enough to see by, after your eyes adjust, in the way you can see inside a sealed cathedral by candlelight. Edges without glare. Darkness that is complete a few body-lengths beyond the mesa's rim.

And sound — there is almost none. Water carries, but nothing here speaks loudly. The slow pulse of the bells makes a soft, low *thud* you feel more than hear: ... *huh* ... ... ... *huh* ... The crawlers tap and scrape, a quiet industry. The eels exhale through their slits with a sigh that barely ripples. Otherwise, the warm high water is silent. So silent that your own heart sounds intrusive, your bubbles, if you still breathe with lungs, rise too fast and too loud. The inhabitants have learned not to make noise. Noise would be crowded here.

Weirdness is low, but definite, and you notice it only after you have been still a while: the water does not wish to mix. There is a thinness to the cold above and a thickness to the warm held around the mesa, and they do not blend. The city sits inside a lens of warm, still water that holds its shape like a dome of air would. Dust motes — fecal snow, mineral flecks — hang suspended at its boundary for a long time before they fall. You can see the dome if you look out from the edge: the open ocean beyond is a blacker black, clearer, colder, and it presses in but does not enter. Something in the geometry of all that primitive, dense, interlocking stone, that settled, ancient, perfectly smoothed weight, holds its own held sea around itself. A pocket of here that refuses to dissolve into elsewhere.

The rule of this place is simple: what was made by hand and left for a long time and warmed gently becomes a place that keeps itself. No current can scour it. Nothing grows over it that was not invited. The builders are gone — whether they left before the flood or drowned waiting for their roofs to become reefs, no one remembers; their tool-marks remain as the only signature — but their work continues to be used, exactly as they set it, only more slowly, by softer tenants.

If you stay, you will learn to move slowly too. To scrape gently for your food. To glow faintly. To not need light or air or hurry.

The mesa will have room for you. There is always one more doorway smoothed to an oval, one more plaza warm to the touch, one more slow breath held in common.
