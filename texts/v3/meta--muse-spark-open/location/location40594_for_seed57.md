It is not an ocean. It is a room inside the ocean.

You find it by dropping - not swimming, dropping - through cold dark water that has no surface anymore, until the open water suddenly ends. Not with a bottom, but with a lip.

A mouth of young rock, still sharp where it broke. Black basalt, fresh as cooled glass, its edges not yet smoothed by time. It hasn't learned how to be a shore. The water tries to wear it and fails, slipping off its knife-edges. You tuck under the lip and you are inside.

The pocket is small enough to hold in your mind all at once: perhaps as big as a chapel, as a lung, as the cup of two hands if your hands were a giant's. Low-ceilinged, low-lying, sealed on all sides except for that one ragged entrance where the outside ocean hangs like a vertical wall. That wall of water is the sky here - a dark, breathing membrane of pressure that holds without spilling in, or spilling out. It shivers sometimes, very slowly. Light from outside does not pass through it. What little illumination there is comes from within.

Inside, there is no air. Not a bubble. You are submerged in the contained sea, which is not the same as the sea outside.

This water is thicker. Colder — cool enough to ache in the teeth, to make your thoughts slow and clear. It does not circulate. It hangs, packed, saturated, holding everything in suspension without letting anything settle. Silt, spores, pollen-like motes of algae, tiny translucent skins that were never animals, all occupy the same volume at once. There is no empty water. If you move your hand, the water dimples and holds the shape of your hand for a long moment before forgetting it.

And it is lush.

From every surface — floor, ceiling, wall — it grows.

Not kelp as you know it, but something that borrowed the idea of kelp and forgot restraint. Black-green and bottle-green and a deep red that is almost black, fronds as wide as doors, as narrow as hair, all growing into each other without soil, rooting directly into bare basalt. They don't sway. There isn't current enough. They *lean*, densely, packed so tight you cannot see rock for plant. They grow through each other, lamina through lamina, translucent where they overlap, making layered stained-glass canopies that filter what little light there is. The leaves are waxy and cold and beaded with a water that is somehow wetter than the water around it, like dew inside the rain. They exhale tiny, slow pearls that rise an inch per hour and then pop silently.

This is a young place. The rock is still deciding what shape it wants to be. You can see where it blistered as it cooled, forming shelves and teeth and sudden, perfect hexagonal pools within the pool. Its sharpness cuts some of the fronds, and where it does, the plant bleeds a luminous sap that hangs in the water as a cloud of cold stars, unmixing for days. Nothing rots here quickly. Growth is patient, inch by glacial inch.

On one of the shelves - scattered, small, out of place - there are marks.

A cairn of three stones that no tide could have stacked. A circle carved into the basalt, too perfect, filled with a sediment that is finer than anything should be. A single stair cut upward that ends in solid rock, as if someone started to build an exit and remembered there was nowhere to go. No rust. No plastic. No mechanism. Just faint human - or once-human - intentions, left where hands could work in the cold, then abandoned to be slowly stitched over by the green. The fronds have grown over them politely, not hiding them, incorporating them.

You hear almost nothing. Mostly quiet is different from silence. The water presses on your ears until your own blood sounds like surf. Then underneath that, the place makes its one sound: the fronds, packed so densely, shift. One leaf expanding a millimeter pushes another, which pushes another, all the way around the pocket. It is a long, slow creak, like an old house settling or ice thinking about cracking, happening over minutes. Then stillness again for a long time.

The weirdness is not that this pocket exists. The weirdness is how it holds.

Gravity here is forgetful. Seeds and stones and those rare animals — a blind white shrimp, a single slow fish the color of dusk, never more than one at a time — they don't fall so much as choose a direction and slowly commit to it. Vertical is negotiable. Some fronds grow sideways then down then up, following a pull that doesn't exist outside. If you stay still long enough, up and down begin to feel like suggestions. The water in the wall at the entrance sometimes shows you not the ocean beyond, but a double of this same pocket, inverted, where your own back is turned to you. If you reach for it, your hand comes back colder.

Light is at 0.2. Which is to say: there is almost none, and what there is is made locally, unreliably. The sap-stars. A faint phosphor in the veins of the largest leaves that brightens when you are not looking directly at it. The collective, damp sheen of life itself. It never adds up to day. It is a darkness you can see in, the way you can see in the moment after closing your eyes in a lit room — details emerging not because they are illuminated, but because the dark itself has texture.

It is cold and crowded and dark and drowned and yet it is not dead. It is the opposite of dead. It is so full it can't move fast. It changes, but on the scale of lichen, of stalactites, of thoughts forming deep underwater. A leaf unfurls over a week. The stair fills with a millimeter of new stone in a year. The ocean outside holds its breath at the doorway and does not enter.

You are not meant to stay long. Lungs, whether you have them or not, begin to feel that coolness as weight. But the pocket does not eject you. It simply continues to be packed and patient and quietly, impossibly growing in the dark, holding its own water apart from the world, a hidden, lush, drowned room that built itself yesterday and will outlast you by forgetting you were ever there.
