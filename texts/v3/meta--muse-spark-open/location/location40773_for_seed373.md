It is a high place, and a small place — small enough to hold in your head entire.

A mountain shoulder cut off flat, like a table left behind by something larger. You can walk its rim in an hour if the wind lets you. Beyond the rim, the world just drops into pale distance; hazy blue-white air and fallen rock, leagues of nothing down to the salt haze below. The air up here is thin and cool on your skin, not freezing, but a clean, late-October cool that makes your breath show if you stand still. Sunlight is normal, frank, midday-bright at altitude, with sharp shadows and no filter to it, except the sky is a little too dark at the zenith, as if you are near the top of an atmosphere that never quite decided to be there.

There is no water here. Not in any way you would recognize.

No streams, no ponds, no seep, no damp soil under a turned stone. The stone itself is wrong for water — a weathered, settled bedrock, old pink-grey granite melted and re-melted and then sanded by a million years of dry wind into whale-back curves and scoops and shallow bowls. It has been this shape for a long time. Lichen-etched. Patient. You can see where the wind has done its slow work: scooped potholes polished glass-smooth, ripples frozen in hard stone, natural chairs and basins and lintels that look placed. Everything is in balance, worn to equilibrium.

And yet it is choked, absurdly, impossibly lush.

The vegetation does not root. That is the trick.

Anchored to the bare stone by pale holdfasts no thicker than spider silk, or to nothing at all — simply hovering a hand's breadth above it on a cushion of static — is a meadow that isn't a meadow. Everywhere you look, the air a foot to ten feet above the ground is *occupied* by forms.

Big ones like folded laundry, like translucent kites. Smaller ones like ferns made of frosted cellophane, like dandelion clocks a meter across. Hanging curtains that shiver and billow though there is no stem to hold them. The dominant growth is what your eye keeps wanting to call "leaf," because you have no other word: wide, waxy sails, deep green and iron-red and sulfur yellow, veined with black fiber, edged with cilia that hum. They overlap and interlock but never crowd; that is the second trick. There is so much space between everything. If you stripped away the living sails for a moment you'd see the emptiness — cubic meters of clear, cold air between one hovering frond and the next, the rock far below visible through gaps. The grove is vast in its sparsity. A cathedral with only a few saints floating in it. But each sail is lush unto itself, glossy, fat with stored chemistry, breathing hard despite the aridity.

Because they do not drink. They eat fog that isn't water.

In the cool of morning — which is almost all the time — a low, dry mist pours over the rim of the mesa and falls apart. Not water vapor; it never was. A heavier-than-air aerosol, faintly oily, smelling of ozone and hot iron when it passes your face. Boron-hydrides, ammonia salts, volatilized minerals sublimating off the desert flats thousands of feet below and condensing in the cold high trap. The sails open. All of them, at once. The meadow sighs and bares its undersides, which are lined with fur, with combs, with electrostatically charged filaments that comb the passing chemistry out of the air. You can hear the tiny clicks when a particle is caught, amplified by millions.

That is how a jungle lives in a desert with no rain. It harvests air.

And it is LOUD.

You expect high places to be silent. This one is deafening.

First, the wind. Because the mesa is isolated and the thermal differential between the superheated salt-flat below and the cool peak is extreme, the wind never stops. It is not a gusting wind; it is a river. A constant, low roaring fall of air spilling up and over the rim, accelerating, splitting around every stone bowl and hovering leaf. And every leaf is an instrument.

The smaller sails tremble at high frequencies, a ceaseless glassy chattering. The larger curtain-forms beat like slack sails on a boat, BOOM - flap flap flap - BOOM. The holdfast forests sing where wind forces through their threads, a billion-harp chord in a minor key. The stone itself has been tuned — the old bowls and potholes whistle, hoot, moan. One natural arch near the center, no higher than your knee but beautifully weathered, screams constantly at a pitch that sits behind your teeth.

Then the animals answer.

For an empty place, it is crawling with life. Again, not crowded — you might look for minutes and see nothing between leaves — but teeming if you listen. Pale moth-things the size of your palm with wings like clear film ride the wind river, clamping onto sails to scrape the chemical frost with radulae, screaming back at each other in ultrasonic bursts that your ears translate as needle-pricks of sound. Armored beetles with counter-rotating gyros instead of wings hover absolutely still in turbulent air, impossible, then dart. Something like swifts but lighter, made mostly of membrane stretched over hollow bone, kite in endless circles above the meadow, never landing, snapping up the moths — their calls are long rips of white noise that doppler past.

Everything moves. Constantly. The dynamic is frantic, unstable, bazaar-like. Leaves chase the best flow and collide softly and rebound. Predators and parasites make their hunts in three dimensions with no ground to hide on. Even the non-living moves: a dusting of mineral snow — precipitated aerosol — is always falling sideways, always being caught, always being shed again. Nothing is static for more than seconds. If you stand still you feel the turbulence as vibration through your soles from the stone.

Scattered through it are the buildings, if you can call them that.

They were not built to shelter. There are five, maybe six on the whole mesa. Each is small — a pylon, a tilted ring, a latticework bulb half-swallowed by hovering growth. No doors. No paths between them. Weathered, dust-scoured, but not as weathered as the stone. Dark matter that drinks light along its seams. When the wind hits them right, they phase.

One near the eastern rim is active now: three nested toroids of matte black and copper wire-thin filament, humming too low to hear and too high to ignore, holding up a volume of air that *looks* different — clearer, as if dust refuses to enter it. Inside that invisible cylinder, floating dead-center, is a crystal of some kind, slowly rotating, shedding exfoliated pages of itself into the wind. The pages become new sails. You see the fresh ones unfurl, still glowing at the edges. No support, no assembly, just matter being instructed to be leaf-shaped from afar. The other pylons do other jobs — one bleeds excess charge into the sky as a silent violet corona, another kneels invisibly and seems to bend the wind around a nursery of juvenile kites. They do not talk to you. They maintain. They have maintained for a long time in the settled, eroded patience of the rock, even as their creations thrash wildly around them.

The rules here are simple and strange:

1. Never touch the ground. Not water, not root, not animal foot. Everything lives off the *between.*
2. Stillness dies. If you cannot shake or shiver or hum, the dry air will take your chemicals away.
3. Sound is how things hold together. Leaf jitters to stay aloft, insect buzz to find mate, stone whistle to mark territory, machine drone to hold the sky from falling. Silence is the only real predator.

By evening — though the light hardly changes, just becomes more slanted — the aerosol thickens. The whole hamlet-sized garden closes a little, sails drooping toward the stone to glean the richer fog. The deafening chord lowers half a tone. The swift-things race lower, skimming your hair. The pylons tick brighter.

It smells like cold metal and crushed geranium.

It feels like being inside the lung of something enormous, high up where it meets thin, cool air, breathing forever without ever needing a drink.
