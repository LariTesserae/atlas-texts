There is a bowl at the top of the world.

You do not get to it by climbing steadily. The mountain underneath it is too young and too steep for that — a recent cone of black glass and basalt that rose all at once, its flanks still sharp, unsoftened, stair-stepped with cooling terraces and pressure ridges where the rock buckled as it came up. The flanks are bare, wind-scoured. But at 4,000 meters, give or take, the cone truncates. It was blown or collapsed or never finished, and so instead of a peak there is a perfect, abrupt absence: a caldera perhaps half a kilometer across, its walls vertical and serrated, still glossy in places where the rock never had time to weather.

From below you would think it was empty, a dry stone throat full of cloud. It is not.

The bowl holds weather.

Warm air gets trapped. The mountain is warm itself — not hot, but patiently, geologically warm. Veins of heat run close under the thin new crust and breathe out everywhere inside the enclosure. You feel it first in the wind over the rim: one step the air is thin and cold and clean with altitude, the next step it is humid and heavy and smells like wet iron and green.

Inside, the caldera is a single room.

The walls drop fifty, sixty meters straight down in knife-edged pleats and columns, weeping continuously. Water does not fall here so much as exude. It beads on every surface of that young rock, gathers in the tool-mark-like striations left by cooling, runs together into a thousand temporary rills that appear and disappear by the hour. It condenses out of the air itself against the sun-warmed stone in the mornings and drips all day. The stone is never dry. It is dark and slick and covered in a first skin of life so dense it looks like the rock is velvet.

Because on the floor of the bowl, the jungle inverted itself and climbed up to live in the sky.

The caldera floor is flat — not marsh-flat, but dish-flat, made of still-warm tuff and ash that holds water like a sponge. It does not drain well. There is nowhere for it to go. So water sits just under the surface and the surface itself is everything growing at once, layered and crowding.

Moss first, ankle-deep and saturated, the kind that makes a squelching sound when the steam shifts and presses on it. Out of the moss, dense stands of tree fern, their trunks shaggy with epiphytes, their fronds unfurling to the size of doors and overlapping until the light has to work to get through. Between them, broadleaf things that should not be at this elevation at all — glossy aroids, heliconias gone stunted and dark from the UV, ginger that grows to your shoulder — all jammed stem-to-stem. Vines as thick as your wrist hang straight down from the caldera rim where they found purchase, then root again in the air before they ever hit the ground. Flowers pack in wherever there is a gap and then make their own gaps. It is lush to the point of claustrophobia. Ten square meters here holds more biomass than a hectare of lowland forest.

It is warm. Not tropical warm, but greenhouse warm, bathhouse warm. Twenty-four, twenty-five degrees in the shade, ten degrees warmer than the air on the ridge fifty meters above your head. The heat comes from below and stays because the walls keep it. At night the bowl breathes out a slow column of mist that can be seen from valleys a full day's walk away. By midday that mist has burned off to a hanging haze, bright, normal daylight filtered through fern and vapor until it falls in soft shafts onto the black pools.

The water is everywhere, but not as a lake. It braids. Shallow, tea-colored channels cut between root masses, widen into knee-deep basins full of fallen fronds, narrow again to a trickle over warm stones that hiss faintly. In the center of the bowl the channels meet in what is almost a pond, almost a swamp — a sheet of open water no larger than a village courtyard, its surface constantly troubled by gas bubbling up from below, by condensation dripping from above, by the tremor of machines.

Because this impossible garden is tended.

Scattered around the caldera, half-swallowed by the growth, are the works. Not a town, not even a hamlet — a handful of structures built with the confidence of industry and then left to negotiate with the jungle.

The largest is on the northern wall, where a seam of steam was found. A low, riveted power house of soot-blackened steel and poured concrete straddles a fissure. Its boilers are long cold, but its turbines still turn, driven now by the planet itself. A pipe organ of geothermal piping climbs the wall behind it, ticking and pinging with expansion, lagged in asbestos and white mineral crust where the weeping rock has been flash-boiled for decades. From it, a single high-tension line on ceramic insulators makes a slow catenary across the bowl to a pump house, to a winch house, to a sorting shed — all small, all practical, all in that particular palette of industrial green and rust red that the humidity has turned mostly to rust anyway.

None of them are staffed. Or rather, they are self-staffed. The turbines turn slowly. The pumps breathe. Every seven minutes, exactly, the central pump house exhales a gout of warm water drawn from deep under the caldera floor and spills it in a controlled flood over the moss beds — irrigation, or cooling, or both. The sound of it is enormous inside the stone room. Water on water, steam on metal, the low 60-cycle hum of electrified air, the drip of a thousand weeping walls amplified and returned. It is loud enough you have to raise your voice under the tree ferns. But nothing happens fast.

This is a place of slow dynamics. The rock cools a fraction of a degree per decade. The vines grow over a conduit, and the conduit sweats a little more and grows a little more mineral and the vines thicken. The pond silts with ash one millimeter at a time and is dredged by a single automated bucket on a cable that creeps across once a day and drops its spoil in the same place it has for thirty years, building a small, sharp, new hill that will not erode because the roots take it immediately. The machines do not accelerate or break. They patiently persist.

There are almost no animals. Too high, too isolated, too recently made, too loud with hiss and thrum. Occasionally a high-altitude swift loses its way over the rim and circles the bowl once, confused by the warm updraft and the green where there should be none, and leaves. At dusk, things click in the ferns — insects, perhaps a few frogs that arrived as spawn on a worker's boot and now breed in the warm pools — but you rarely see them. You hear the click against the industrial throb and the endless drip. You see the slime trails, the chewed leaves, the faint phosphorescent fungi that grow only on the rubber insulation of the old cables and nowhere else, an unusual little signature of this place: life that has learned to eat industry.

The rules here are simple and are enforced by the walls:

1. Everything that falls in stays in. Water, seed, heat, sound — the caldera keeps it and recirculates it. Nothing leaves except slowly, as vapor over the rim.
2. Height does not mean cold. The mountain makes its own climate, and the climate is wet, close, and fertile.
3. The sharp stays sharp. Wind and rain have not yet had time to round this place. Every edge, whether basalt or steel I-beam or fern frond, is still as it was cut.
4. Growth is dense, but bounded. The jungle cannot climb out of the bowl — the outer flanks are too dry and too sheer. So it grows into itself, layer over layer, in a space no larger than a few city blocks, making a world that is complete and impenetrably full.

If you stand on the rim at noon, the normal sun directly overhead, you can see the whole of it at once: a cup of black rock full to brimming with green, steaming gently, humming to itself, hidden. A small, unlikely, intensely alive factory of a valley that the rest of the mountain does not know is there.

If you find the service ladder — there is only one, bolted into the weeping wall and already half-colonized by moss — and climb down into it, the sound gets louder, the air gets warmer and wetter, the fronds close over your head, and the sky becomes a perfect, serrated circle far above.
