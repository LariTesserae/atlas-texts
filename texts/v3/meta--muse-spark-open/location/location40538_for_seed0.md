It is night here always. Not the night of a moonless sky over familiar hills — this is a deeper, older dark, the kind that exists under a canopy too thick and too alive to ever let the sun through.

You are standing low. The elevation here is low: a broad, steaming basin, a valley the size of a small country cupped between young shoulders of stone. The rock is new. You can tell. It hasn't learned how to be soft yet. It juts in black, sharp-edged ribs and sawblade ridges from the flesh of the jungle, all fresh fracture and unweathered basalt, still flaking, still cooling in places. In the warm air you can smell the mineral tang of it, iron and hot glass. These are hills that were born yesterday, geologically speaking — a recent upwelling, a shrug of the planet that cracked the lowlands and froze mid-motion. No rivers have had time to carve them smooth. Instead water finds its own, stranger way down.

There is not much standing water. 0.8, if you needed a number. No lakes to mirror, no wide river to follow. This place is *damp* the way a lung is damp. The heat — a thick, consistent, summer-night warmth that sits on your skin at about 28°C and never breaks — pulls moisture up from the dense rot below and wrings it out of the air above in fits. It doesn't so much rain as it condenses.

Every surface weeps.

Leaves the size of doors collect the humid air on their waxy uppers until droplets bead, swell, and run off in sudden silver threads. Pockets of water live in suspension everywhere: in the cupped bromeliad throats fifty feet above you, each one a tiny black lake; in the axils of the giant ferns; in the air itself, which is heavy and visible, a low mist that catches briefly on spidersilk and vanishes. You are intermittently rained upon without any clouds — a branch shivers leagues away and a cold shower falls on your shoulders. The ground underfoot is not mud, but it is never dry. It's a felted mat of roots, living wood, mycelium, leaf-litter, and sharp young stone, all sweating. You step and water sighs up around your ankle, then is gone, soaked back into moss in seconds.

And it is all, impossibly, lush. In this darkness the greenery has solved light differently.

This is not photosynthesis as you know it. The canopy — a hundred, a hundred and fifty feet overhead, layered and interwoven until it is a continent — is black to your eyes until you adjust, and then you realize it is not black at all. It is glowing. Not brightly. Suffused. The vegetation here generates its own dim, deep light in order to feed itself. The leaves phosphoresce. Veins of sickly chartreuse and bruised violet run through trunks as thick as houses. Fungi the size of boulders bloom along the sharp basalt spines and pulse faintly amber, teal, corpse-green. The forest floor is carpeted in mosses that light under pressure — with every footfall you leave a fading footprint of cool fire. It is enough light to see by, if you have eyes for it, but it is not day. It is the light of a laboratory at 3 a.m., of foxfire and angler lures, a light that lives only inches from its source and leaves everything beyond ten meters in absolute, warm, breathing black.

That density — that is the first thing that becomes oppressive. There is no empty space. The scale is open, yes; this basin goes on for kilometers, valley after valley of the same jagged, young, choked lowland, but within any cubic meter of it there is almost no air that is not *something*. Vine upon vine upon vine, root strangling root, epiphyte growing on epiphyte. Flowers that bloom directly out of bark. Leaves layered over leaves until the rain of condensation has to find a five-minute path down. Pollen hangs in the dark like dust in a sunbeam, except here the motes glow faintly as they drift. The smell is overwhelming — sweet rot, fresh sap, crushed chlorophyll, musk — a perfume so saturated it becomes a taste.

And the fauna.

You hear them before you understand them. The sound here is deafening.

2.6/3 doesn't mean a single loud noise. It means there is no such thing as silence to retreat into. At any moment a thousand species are calling, chewing, clicking, stridulating, slapping water, cracking seed pods, screaming. Insect analogs as big as your hand rattle in the ferns with a sound like dry maracas. Something high in the canopy hoots in a two-tone wail that is answered instantly from five other directions, creating a chord that never resolves. Wings beat — not the thin whine of mosquitoes but the leathery thrum of things the size of kites passing inches over your head in the dark, displacing air that smells briefly of cinnamon. On the sharp rocks, colonies of chitinous, crab-like climbers pick endlessly at lichen with stone hammers, tap tap tap tap in a frantic, unsynchronized percussion that rolls down the ridge for miles.

It is not a chorus. A chorus has a conductor. This is turbulent, hyperactive, bazaar-like — millions of lives all doing something urgent all at once. The forest flickers with their bioluminescence too, so the darkness is strobed with mating flashes, predator-lures, warning blinks, clouds of startled gnats that ignite like green fireworks and then are gone. You sweep your hand and disturb a fern, and a hundred tiny amphibians, translucent as glass, burst from beneath it, each with a lit belly, scattering like spilled embers. You breathe in and something lands on your face, walks across it, is eaten off your cheek by something else before you can flinch.

There is nothing built here. No walls, no roads, no ruins. For all the teeming, it is untouched in the human sense.

And yet it is not without technology.

Watch closer. That deafening tap-tap-tap on the basalt — that's not random feeding. The stone-using crab things, low and black and dense as cast iron, are knapping. They select a nodule of chert veining the young rock, hold it with three limbs, and strike it with another stone held in a specialized forelimb. A flake comes off, razor-sharp. They use it, immediately, to slice open the tough rind of the lantern-fruits that hang from the vines. When the flake dulls, they discard it and make another. On the next ridge, larger, lemur-like creatures with four opposable thumbs weave. Not nests. Traps. They strip living vines — which continue to glow faintly as they are braided — into wide, sticky nets and string them across gaps in the canopy to catch the drum-winged fliers. They tend their nets, repair them, fold them up when not in use.

Primitive. Pre-metal, hand-held, manual, simple-fabrication. No smelting. No permanence. The tools are made, used, left to be swallowed by the moss and the fungus within a day. The forest does not allow monuments; its growth rate, its wet heat, its chewing fauna would take any hut or wall and turn it to humus before you finished the roof. So intelligence here does not build *up*. It builds *out*, disposable, woven, knapped, gone.

The rules of this world are simple and strange:

1. Light is made, not received.
2. Rock is temporary, life is permanent.
3. Everything eats everything else, immediately and loudly.

The weirdness is not in gross violation — you still have gravity, still have thermodynamics — but there is a slight wrongness to how alive it all is. The vines seem to lean toward movement, not light. The condensation seems to form *before* the air cools. And when a great wing passes overhead and its downdraft briefly clears a hole in the canopy's mist, you can look straight up and see no stars — only more layers, fainter bioluminescence receding into infinity, as if the forest has no top at all, as if elevation is just another kind of depth.

You stand in warm, absolute dark that is not dark, in a damp that is not wet, in a silence so full it hurts your teeth. A fruit falls somewhere far away with a splash into a cupped leaf, and instantly you hear the frantic, beautiful, turbulent rush of a hundred creatures racing toward that sound.

They will make tools to get it. They will be eaten while doing so. The forest will glow a little brighter with their bodies.

And then it will weep again.
