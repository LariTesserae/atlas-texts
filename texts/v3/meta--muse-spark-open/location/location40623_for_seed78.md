It is high. Not a mountain, not a range — a lifted world.

A continent that wasn't there before has been shouldered up into the thin part of the sky. The basalt is still young. That is the first thing you feel even before you see it: nothing here has been rounded. The uplift is recent on a geological scale, a fresh wound of a plateau the size of a country, its edges sheer cliffs nine kilometers straight down into a sea of cloud that never clears. The top is not flat. It broke as it rose. It is all teeth and fins and knife ridges, black glassy rock, still sharp enough to cut rope, still steaming faintly in places where the interior heat leaks out. No rivers have had time to carve valleys. Water hasn't learned the shape of it yet.

And yet it is covered. Totally covered.

Not with snow. Not with ice, though it is cold enough for both — a deep, high, starved cold where the air is thin and your breath falls out of you as a clatter of crystals that never quite hits the ground before sublimating. 40 below, 50 below. Cold that makes stone ring.

What covers it is plant. A single, continent-wide, continent-deep organism that decided that rock was a suggestion.

You have to reimagine what "vegetation" means. This is not a forest on land. The land is an excuse for the plant.

From a distance, under the dark, you would think the plateau was furred. Pale, ashy green and iron-black. Closer, you see the fur is architecture.

The dominant form is not a tree. It's a tower-sponge, a relative of moss and lichen that has forgotten how to be small. Each tower starts as a tight rosette of waxy, black-green leaves, each leaf thick as your forearm, rimed with frost, and then it extrudes. It grows hollow. It grows up, hundreds of meters, sometimes more than a kilometer, a lattice of living struts and interwoven aerial roots as thick as houses, enclosing mostly nothing. Ninety percent air by volume. A cathedral made of plant-matter that is still growing. The walls are living wood, but wood that is porous, fibrous, and warm to the touch — the plant ferments slowly inside itself to keep its sap from freezing, and the towers exhale.

They stand so far apart you can lose sight of the next one in the haze. The density is near-void. Vast distances of hard, bare, wind-scoured black rock between each living spire, rock striped with lichen-crust where the spore has just begun to take hold. And then another tower, leaning slightly into the jet that never stops up here. And another. For a thousand kilometers.

From above, the plateau looks empty. From inside, it is infinite.

Because each tower *is* a landscape. Step up to one — and you have to climb; the young rock is shattered into stair-sharp talus — lay your hand on it. It thrums. The outer skin is tight-woven vine, bark that feels like boiled leather stretched over a drum. Cut it with a stone adze and it weeps a clear, sweet sap that freezes into edible beads. Inside, through a split that the tower left for its own respiration, is hollow space.

Light from outside does not reach here in any useful way. It is darkness most of the year. The sun, when it comes, is a thin blue rumor at the horizon that never climbs, because this uplift is polar, tilted away from its star. The real illumination is starlight on frost and the aurora crawling overhead in silent green sheets, and the plant's own faint answer: speckles of amber fungus-light along the growth seams, bioluminescence that pulses with the draw of water.

You climb inside. The interior is a vertical country. Walkways of branch, chambers of leaf, all grown, not built. The air hangs damp. That is the water here — it is not in lakes, not in rain. The atmosphere is too cold and the rock too young to hold it. Instead the water lives as cycle: the towers drink the wind.

Every night the temperature crashes and the whole plateau sweats frost. By morning every tower is bearded white. By midday that frost melts into the lattice, is wicked down thread-roots into cisterns the plant has grown for itself — bulbous, hanging bladders the size of houses, half-full of tepid, tannin-brown water, sloshing gently. Some have burst under the strain of growth and left rust-colored tide marks on the rock below. Some are tended, tapped.

It is damp like a breath is damp. Condensation on every surface. Drip and freeze. Drip and freeze. Pockets of true river only where two great root-trunks meet and split the basalt, forcing a hidden, steaming trickle that vanishes again under stone.

And it is loud.

You expected high cold to be silent. It is not. The wind at this elevation never stops, and the towers are aeolian instruments. Wind through a kilometer of hollow lattice makes a low organ howl that you feel in your molars, shifting notes as the plant slowly, perceptibly, grows and tightens. The living wood creaks and pops — loud as gunfire — as new struts swell in the cold, splitting stone beneath them. Water bladders groan and sigh. Ice sheets on the bare rock between towers boom and detonate. And the fauna —

Because this empty place is full.

They live entirely inside and between the towers. There is nothing on the naked rock long enough to be eaten by the wind.

The towers are a complete ecology, animal as architecture. Pale, blind hoppers the size of dogs spring along the elastic interior vines. Colonies of moth-things, silent in flight, their wings a meter across and translucent, feed on the fungus-light and spiral up the hollow shafts by the thousands, so many their wingbeats make a second, softer wind. Things like feathered lizards with hooked claws graze the leaves from the outside and vanish inside at night. Predators follow — lean, six-limbed gliders that spread membranes between their elongated ribs and ride the tower-drafts from spire to spire in the dark, crying once, sharply, before they strike. You hear their cry carried for kilometers in the thin air. Everything echoes.

You are not alone either.

People are here. Or something like people. They were not here when the plateau rose — no one was — but they came after, or survived it.

Their mark is small, scattered, faint. A ladder of bone and frozen vine lashed to the outside of a tower, leading to a split where smoke has stained the bark black. A platform of woven branch grown slightly outward, coaxed with urine and patience over years, not built. A cluster of three huts inside a hollow, walls are just cured leaf-leather, floors of beaten bark, a fire pit of broken basalt where fat-rich lichen-cakes roast. They use simple tools: chipped glassy stone, scrapers of horn, needles.

They do not farm. They tend. They carve little channels in the plant to divert sap. They tie off water bladders to keep them from bursting. They carve faces into the living struts, not for worship but as wayfinding — each face means: this way to the fissure water. This way to the tower that has not yet been hollowed by rot. Each tower is named.

They do not try to control this place. You cannot. The plant grows faster than they can cut it — you can hear it in the night, a slow, persistent stretching, the sound of a forest becoming a building becoming a weather system. In storm, the towers sway ten meters at the top and fling snow from their crowns like spray from a ship. The people tie themselves in.

The weirdness is quiet but insistent when you stay long enough to notice: the plant does not grow toward light. There is so little light to grow toward, and what there is comes sideways, weak. It grows toward sound. Toward vibration. Cut an opening and make a steady noise — a drum, a chant — and over weeks the vines will thicken there, will reach. Abandon a tower and let it fall silent, and it thins, hollows further, until wind makes it sing itself to death and it collapses into fibrous ruin on the sharp young rock, already being colonized by the next generation of rosettes.

So the people sing. To keep their houses alive. Low songs, in the dark, in the cold, in that vast hollow emptiness that is somehow totally alive, damp with breath, loud with wind and wingbeat and growing wood, under aurora that never sets.

Between towers, the emptiness is so complete you can hear your own heart. Inside them, there is no room left that is not life.
