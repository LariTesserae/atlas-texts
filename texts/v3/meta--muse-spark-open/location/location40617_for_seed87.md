It is not an ocean.

It is a room that wanted to be an ocean and didn't have the space, so it became more intense instead.

You find it by going down - not far in distance, but far in kind. Below the sea-ice, below the continental shelf, below where light has any business being, where the ice is not white anymore but a deep, pressure-blue that presses like a lid. Miles of it. And then, suddenly, not ice. A hollow.

A pocket.

If you put your arms out you could touch both walls at once, if the cold didn't stop you. If you held your breath you could hear the chamber breathing. It is maybe three meters across. Maybe four. It is an intimate scale, a secret kept by weight. A single bubble trapped when the world was still cooling, and never popped.

It is completely full of water.

Not filled — *full*. There is no surface in here, no air-water interface, no sky. The water is the atmosphere. It hangs dark and cold and absolute, about minus one, the kind of cold that doesn't bite so much as clarify. It is so clear you see it only by the things in it: faint slow snow of marine detritus falling endlessly from the low, domed ceiling of ice, never landing. Dim light filters down through the kilometers of glacier above, arriving here thinned to a deep indigo dusk, a perpetual late evening. You can see, but only just enough to understand how much you cannot see. The walls sweat that light back at you.

The chamber itself is new.

Geologically new. Embarrassingly new, like it hasn't decided what it is yet. The floor and the lower walls are not ice at all but raw stone, pillow basalt still soft-edged from its birth, black and glassy, undersea lava that belched here last year or a hundred years ago — no time at all — and cooled too fast into rounded, overlapping lobes. It is pre-erosion. No sand. No smoothed corners. Just these blind, bulbous knuckles of rock still exhaling the faintest heat into the water, so that where water meets stone there is a shimmer, a mirage of temperature difference. The only warm thing for a thousand miles in any direction is this infant floor, and everything lives because of it.

And everything lives tightly, densely, urgently together.

Because this is a city.

Not built. Grown. There is no toolmark in here, no metal, no cut, no fire. There is also no plant to speak of — only the sparsest vegetation, a few sullen veils of black-red algae clinging to the upper ice where the light is strongest, brittle as frost on a window. Not enough to feed anyone. This city does not run on green.

It runs on animals.

On the new basalt, covering every centimeter of that warm shimmer, is a metropolis of bodies. It is tiered and stacked and tunneled like a coral reef folded in on itself to fit inside a closet. White tube-worms as thin as pencil lead and as tall as your forearm stand shoulder-to-shoulder in crowds so thick you cannot see the rock between them, their plumes extended, filtering, filtering, a silent field of flowers that are mouths. Around their feet, amphipods — pale, translucent shrimp the size of your thumbnail — have cemented themselves into place with their own secretions, building balconies and chimneys out of glued grit and their own shed carapaces. Each one lives in a single cell, but the cells connect, share walls, share warmth. Streets run between them, not planned but worn, where water flows a little faster.

Above them, swarms of isopods, blind and opalescent, move in slow, constant traffic. They farm bacteria on the backs of larger, sessile things. They carry eggs. They die and are immediately taken apart by neighbors. There are snails here with shells thin as paper, grazing in herds. There are things you have no name for — five-armed, soft, with cilia that beat constantly to keep the water from stagnating. Nothing moves fast in this cold, but everything moves. It is furiously active, a market-day bustle conducted in absolute silence.

That is the rule here: silence. Sound does not carry the way you think it should in water. The ice dome above absorbs. The dense living mat below baffles. What remains is the faintest suite of near-sounds: the click of a shrimp closing its tube, the sigh of water venting through a worm's body, the almost imaginary fizz of the bacterial mats respiring on the warm rock. Your own heartbeat becomes loud and then, strangely, stops seeming like yours. You hear it as part of the city's quiet.

The weirdness is not that this city exists in such an impossible pocket. The weirdness is that it is complete.

It has neighborhoods. Down near the warmest fissure — a hairline crack in the baby basalt where mineral water still weeps out black and iron-rich — the density becomes almost solid, a vertical slum of filterers growing on top of filterers, alive on alive, old shells serving as foundation for new tubes. The water there shivers with heat and with bacteria blooming white as spilled milk, and the things there are paler, sightless, older-seeming. Higher up on the lobes of basalt, where it is colder, the architecture opens up: solitary tunicates like translucent lanterns pulse slowly, and the predatory worms make their runs, long and red, probing between buildings for the unwary.

And at the very top, inverted, hanging from the ice ceiling like stalactites, are the strangest citizens — long, clear jellies that have anchored themselves to the ice with a sucker-foot and let their bells hang down, bell after bell after bell, each one inside the next, fishing the falling snow of detritus with threads so fine they are invisible until something touches them and sticks. They are the city's roof. Its strange, patient fishermen. Through their bodies the dim blue light refracts and scatters, so that the whole chamber glows faintly from within, not from a source but from many small lives holding light for a moment and passing it on.

There is no above and below here, really. There is warm center and cold edge. There is dense inside and ice outside. The city does not know it is hidden. It does not know it is tiny. For the creatures in it, this pocket is the cosmos — wall-to-wall with presences, every surface occupied, every current a highway, every minute a negotiation of space that hasn't existed long enough to be contested, only colonized.

You are too big to stay. You can only visit as a diver visits the deep, or as a god visits a model. The cold presses your face. The dark water presses your thoughts quieter and quieter until you are as silent as they are.

And behind you, the city continues to build itself, grain by grain, body by body, upward and inward, not outward — because there is no outward — making more city out of the only thing it has ever had, which is each other.
