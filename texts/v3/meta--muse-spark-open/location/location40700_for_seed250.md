It is not an ocean that you go down into. It is an ocean you go up into.

The world here is high. Not high like a mountain, high like the sky forgot where the ground was supposed to be. You climb for days through thinning, cool air where breathing goes shallow and clean, past raw, embryonic stone that looks poured yesterday and left to cool — black basalt ribbing, pale unweathered cliffs without lichen, without talus, without the softening of age. The geology is under construction. Edges are too sharp. Valleys are too shallow, as if water hasn't learned how to cut yet. Everything is nascent, pre-erosion, a planet still in its first draft.

And then, without warning, the air ends in water.

Not a surface. A suspension.

### The High Water

At about the height where clouds should be, there is a sea.

It has no basin. It has no shore. It hangs — a continent-thick lens of ocean held aloft in the cool, bright air, vast enough to make its own weather, planetary in scale, bending light at its edges until the horizon goes glassy and false. From below it looks like a second sky, darker and moving. From within, it is everything.

You do not crash into it. You pass through a kilometer of thickening atmosphere first: the Wall. It is not rain, it is not fog, it is both — the air itself becomes wet, heavy, vertical. Condensation that never falls, water that never quite decides to be liquid, a dense, luminous suspension where every breath is half-drunk. Daylight comes through it normally, even brightly, filtered to a cool noon-white that scatters in every droplet until the whole transition zone glows. Then the droplets join hands and you are inside.

Submerged is not quite the right word. Subsumed is closer.

The pressure does not crush. The cold does not bite — it's a high, late-autumn cool, 8 or 10 degrees Celsius held constant by the sheer thermal mass of all that water. You can open your eyes. You can breathe, after a fashion, because the water here is not entirely water. It is saturated with dissolved air, with engineered breath. The boundary between liquid and atmosphere was smudged on purpose.

Inside, distance collapses. The water is impossibly clear and impossibly empty. Density 0.2 — there is almost nothing per cubic kilometer. No schools, no plankton bloom, no drift of marine snow. It is a hollow ocean. An abyss turned inside-out and emptied. Light penetrates hundreds of meters straight down and still you can see, a pale, even illumination that fades not to black but to a deeper, purer blue, until the blue itself seems to be a kind of light.

And it is loud.

Not with life — there are no animals here. No fish, no whales, no insects. Nothing moves under its own will except the water itself. And the water never stops moving.

This sea is turbulent. Chaotic. Solaris-alive. Currents are born and die in minutes, colliding in silent explosions that you feel as a deep thump in your chest. Shear layers rip past each other forming transient cliffs of differing clarity and temperature. Vortices bloom the size of cities and unravel before they can complete a rotation. Somewhere above you a storm is happening *inside* the water — not on its surface, because it has no surface — a storm of pure hydrodynamics: pressure fronts that chase each other, cavitation booms that roll like thunder without ever breaking.

The sound is constant, physical. Low roars as two bodies of water grind, sharp cracks as vacuum bubbles collapse, the endless hiss of the Wall above and below you where water is always trying to become air and failing. In the emptiest reaches, you can hear the planet's mantle groan through the water column, transmitted perfectly. You are inside a bell that is always being struck.

### The Sparse Gardens

Vegetation should not be here. No soil, no sunlight in the old sense, no bottom to root in.

Instead, sparse is right — 0.9 out of 3, and that feels generous until you learn to see it.

There are structures, but they were not built. That's the paradox that takes you a day to understand. The tech rating here is off the charts, beyond modern, post-physical, and yet there is nothing made. No towers, no roads, no ruins. No artifacts at all.

Because the tech *is* the place.

The water is held. That is the first miracle. Some field — no generator visible, no seam, no hum of machinery — holds a lens of ocean at stratospheric elevation against gravity and diffusion. Trillions of tons of water hovering where water cannot hover, stable and yet intentionally unstable inside. The field is invisible, but you can see its flaws shimmer when a large vortex brushes the edge and sends ripples across the bottom of the sea — ripples in *nothing*, like wind on glass.

And within that field, the sparse vegetation grows — if you can call it that. Each one is solitary, kilometers from the next, which is why the density feels so hollow.

They are anchors. Or anchors grew around them.

From a distance they look like black kelp, or burnt trees. Up close, they are not plants. They are lattices of mineral and engineered cellulose, braiding themselves out of dissolved silica and carbon, growing downward from nowhere and upward from nowhere, raw geology made briefly organic. Their fronds are wide, translucent plates that orient to the light, photosynthesizing in the cool, even glow. Their roots — if they are roots — dissolve into threads thinner than hair and vanish into the field itself, drinking power directly.

They do not sway with the current. The current breaks around them. They are under construction, always. You can watch a new plate unfold over an hour, wet and pale, then harden. Pieces slough off unfinished and tumble, dissolving, never reaching any bottom because there is no bottom — just the lower Wall, where water becomes air again and the lost plates become rain that never lands.

There are no animals to eat them. Nothing has evolved to use them. They grow, reproduce by fragmentation, and die alone in the loud, empty water, unobserved for centuries until you came up here.

The weirdness is quiet, at first. You expect weirder. But it accumulates:

Light behaving correctly despite the impossibility of the ocean. Sound traveling too fast. The cool water that does not make you cold. The fact that you can hold your position without swimming, as if the turbulence itself has decided to leave a pocket of calm around you. The way the embryonic stone of the world far below reflects perfectly in the underside of the sea, even when clouds should obscure it.

There are no structures because structures would imply someone left. The builders — if there were builders — didn't build *in* the world. They tuned the world until it *was* the machine. A high, cold, vast, empty, loud, churning, sunlit ocean in the sky that maintains itself, that gardens itself sparsely with mineral kelp, that makes weather inside weather.

You float there, suspended between two Walls of rain that stretch to every horizon, listening to water argue with itself in a language of pressure and collapse, watching slow plates of impossible kelp grow in water that should not be there, over stone that has not yet decided what shape it wants to be.

And far, far below, through kilometers of clear, turbulent blue, the raw new earth waits in cool normal daylight for the sea to make up its mind and fall — which it never does.
