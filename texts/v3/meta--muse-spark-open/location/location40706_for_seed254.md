It was a pit before it was a town.

Not a crater, not a cave. A quarry, cut square into the low coastal plain a hundred and fifty years ago for limestone, down three, four stories deep, and then left when the water came up to meet it. They kept cutting anyway. They kept building anyway. The floor never dried out again.

You come to the rim where the flat fields end in a sudden lip of rebar and weathered concrete, and you look down, and the whole other world is there, contained. Small enough to take in at one glance — a hamlet, a neighborhood, a single city block sunk into the earth — but so packed you can't see where one thing ends and another begins.

The air changes at the edge. Up on the plain it's dry wind and open sky. Down inside it's warm. Warm, wet, still warm like a greenhouse at night. The stone walls that make the pit weep constantly. Water was never pumped out successfully; it was accommodated. It beads on the limestone, runs in dark curtains down the terraced houses, gathers in the gutters and the cisterns. Everything sweats. The brick and the poured concrete have gone soft-edged from a century of condensation. Rust has bled into the mortar and set there, established, balanced. Nothing here is eroding anymore — even decay has reached an equilibrium.

Because everything is growing.

There is very little built surface that is still just built surface. The town was constructed dense to begin with — rowhouses and workshops and boiler-buildings stacked tight, stairways over alleyways over culverts, balconies cantilevered out until they nearly touched — and then the planting took them. Not wild overgrowth. Cultivation so total it stopped being decoration and became substrate.

Vines were trained up every wall for coolness and then let go. Bananas and figs were planted in every courtyard and broke out of them. Taros and elephant ears line the canals in stands so thick you can't see the water between the stalks, only hear it moving. Roots have found every crack and been encouraged; trunks rise through old factory floors where holes were intentionally cut to let them; roofs are soil now, three feet deep, carrying tomato terraces and bean trellises and hanging gardens of ferns that drip all day. The power lines are trellised. The pipes are trellised. Even the moving parts are trellised — the waterwheels turn slow between curtains of pothos, the belt-drives run through tunnels cut in living hedges.

It works because it is below.

The pit traps heat. The walls shelter wind. Humidity pools there, visible in the evening as a low mist that never quite becomes fog, just hangs chest-high over the lowest tier and makes bare bulbs halo. Light is dim at noon. What sun gets past the rim has to get past the canopy too — the canopy is not forest-over-city, it is city-and-forest as one thing, palm fronds interleaved with corrugated tin, mango limbs holding up laundry lines and telephone wire. By mid-afternoon the pit is in its own dusk, green-filtered and steamy, lit mainly by the warm yellow worklights that have been left on for decades in windows, in shops, over doorways.

It is loud.

That is the strangest part until you've been there an hour and stop hearing it. With no birds, no dogs, no insects — a complete and unremarked-upon absence; nobody keeps chickens, there are no pigeons in the eaves, the water is strangely clear of skimmers and frogs, as if animal life simply never took hold here — you would expect silence. Instead the town fills the acoustic bowl with itself.

Everywhere, the industrial murmur: the thud of the big stationary engines on the lowest terrace that drive the pumps that keep the water from winning completely, the clank of chain lifts bringing crates of limes and greens up to the rim road, the hiss of pressure cookers and stills and canning retorts from the communal kitchens, the generators' drone, the endless tick-tick-drip off leaves onto tin roofs and into barrels. Water is everywhere and it is always moving — sluices cut beside stairways, aqueducts carried on pillars of old concrete wound thick with creeper, millraces brown-green with fertilizer. You hear feet on wet wood, voices muffled by leaves, but mostly you hear system.

Nothing moves fast.

The pace of the pit is set by growth and drip. The pumps pulse once every few seconds, patiently. Laundry lowered on pulleys steams and dries almost not at all. A man prunes a branch that has grown over a gear housing, carefully, a centimeter at a time, and tucks the cuttings into a trough to be mulched. A woman scrapes algae from a glass pane that has already greened over again behind her. Silt settles in settling tanks. Breadfruit ripens and falls with a thump into standing water and someone retrieves it later. Change happens over weeks — a new root cracks a stair, a wall sags and is propped and planted over — not in sudden collapses.

The rules are simple and unstated:

1. Nothing is taken out of the pit without something coming back as compost.
2. No surface is left bare to the weather if it can bear weight.
3. Keep the water moving.
4. Do not expect quiet.

People who live there — a few hundred, intergenerational, practical — do not think of it as romantic. It's an urban-industrial farm sunk into the ground, a machine for living that learned to photosynthesize. They tend boilers the way others tend hives, they pollinate squash by hand because there are no bees and have not been in living memory, they know which stair will be slick and which pipe sweats potable water. In the evenings they gather on the roof-gardens and speak loudly over the throb of the engines, their faces damp, picking dinner from the vines around their chairs.

From the rim at night, it looks like a lit grove. From inside, at night, it looks like a city. Warm vapor rises off it no matter the season, carrying the smell of wet cement, hot oil, jasmine, and turned earth, straight up out of the ground like breath.
