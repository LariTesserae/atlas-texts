This place doesn't end so much as it forgets to stop.

You are on the Roof.

That's what anything that lives here would call it if anything lived here. A tabletop continent lifted two miles into the air in one clean, tectonically fresh shrug, so young the rock hasn't had time to learn how to soften. The planet's crust here broke and rose like a shoulder blade under skin - basalt and black shale, stair-stepped, glass-sharp, uneroded. There are no hills. There are escarpments. Sheer faces hundreds of meters high where the ground was tugged up and left faceted, still holding the chisel marks of its birth. Between them, the land is almost perfectly flat for a thousand kilometers in any direction, a plain so level you can see the curvature of weather on it.

It is cold the way high places are always cold, even under a normal sun. The light is white and ordinary, a late-morning light at noon that never quite warms. It falls straight down through thin air onto stone that holds no heat. Frost never fully leaves. But this is not a dry cold. This is a wet cold.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a sea. The aquifer is kissing the surface here - pushed up with the plateau, trapped under a thin skin of rock and moss. You walk and hear it under your boots. Every step on the peat-moss gives and weeps. The air itself is supersaturated; it condenses on every cold edge without cloud or rain. Water beads on basalt, gathers, runs in sheets down every fresh scarp in black weeping curtains. Not waterfalls - weep-walls, thousands of them, silent individually, seeping from the pores of the new rock. At the foot of each cliff, the water collects not into rivers but into long, shallow marsh-skirts, reedless mires of amber water and luminous green.

That green should not be here.

Everything is lush. Impossibly lush for this altitude and chill, for soil that barely exists. The vegetation is not forest - there are no trees that can root in this thin skin and sharp wind. It is a carpet, deep and spongy, a tundra-jungle of moss, lichen, liverwort, and fern-forms that grow thick as mattresses, hummocked and rolling in dark emeralds and acid yellows. They drink straight from the air. Condensation rolls off their fronds all day. They have no grazers to keep them in check, no insects to eat them, no birds to seed them - there is no animal life here at all, not a gnat, not a microbe large enough to make a footprint - and so they grow unchecked, in perfect, undisturbed equilibrium. Nothing eats. Nothing rots quickly in the cold. It just accumulates, layer upon living layer, a peat city of plants building on its own dead. The air smells of iron and chlorophyll and wet stone.

And then you remember you are not alone.

The City.

It was built - or grown, or extruded - across this impossible plain to the same scale as the plain itself. Urban, unequivocally urban: avenues, blocks, factories, habitation stacks, transit lines, power conduits. Its infrastructure is organized, right-angled, industrial. Electrified. Stacks breathe steam. Turbines turn. Assembly sheds run on a clock that never stops. From a distance you see its lights by day, not because it is dark but because the sodium-amber windows burn constantly. Its bones are steel and poured slag-concrete and sheet metal left to frost over.

But the density is wrong.

There is a district the size of Greater Tokyo and then, sixty kilometers of nothing but moss and weeping stone, then another district the same size, then a hundred kilometers of emptiness where a single elevated rail line stitches across the void with no stops. The city has the organization of a metropolis and the population of a void. It is vast on purpose and empty on purpose, like a continent-sized factory floor laid out for workers who never arrived, or who left and left the machines on. 

Because the machines are still on.

This is the sound.

Even from kilometers away you hear it - not a single sound but a chord held forever at the threshold of pain. Low turbine roar, high pressure-whine, the clatter of stamping presses miles wide operating in unison, the crackle of ten thousand transformers. Loud enough that snow shivers off railings though there is no movement. Loud enough you feel it in your molars. It is the sound of industry without product, production without consumption.

And it never changes.

That is the strangest law of the Roof. The sound does not wax or wane. The water does not flood or dry. The light does not move into afternoon. The moss does not spread or die back. The steam from the stacks rises to exactly the same height and shreds at exactly the same altitude into clouds that never drift, that were born there and will die there. If you watch a droplet trace down a basalt seep for an hour, it stalls mid-rock and reforms at the top again. Time here is not stopped, exactly - clocks tick, water moves - but all gradients have been erased. Everything that happens is happening in a loop so wide you can't see the seam. Cold stays cold. Wet stays wet. Loud stays loud. There is no weather here, only climate held in perfect, humming stasis. Fresh rock stays fresh. A footprint in moss springs back within a minute.

The weeping cliffs weep at the same rate forever, feeding the marshes that never overflow, feeding the moss that never starves, feeding the water table that feeds the cliffs. The factories draw power from water that draws power from the cold condensing air that draws power from... something quieter underneath the plateau, something that likes equilibrium.

People designed this, obviously. Someone wanted a loud, cold, wet, high garden-factory the size of a moon to run forever without animals to foul it, without erosion to soften it, without night to pause it. Or perhaps the Roof built itself around that desire after the builders forgot why.

If you walk its avenues, your footsteps echo impossibly between empty tenements whose windows all look freshly lit, whose doors all open to warmed, ventilated rooms with chairs that have never held weight. Vines of cold-climate ivy - or something like ivy, something that learned to love electromagnetic fields - have laced themselves lovingly around gantries and substations, lush and indifferent, drinking condensation off hot metal. The city and the moss have reached an agreement in this changeless place: neither will win.

You can walk for days between districts and see no other moving thing. Only moss, only stone, only water shining on stone, only the great sharp geometry of new earth and the even greater, sharper geometry of old industry, both held under glassy white light and a noise that has become a kind of silence.

It goes on.
