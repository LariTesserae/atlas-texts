This is the Low Works.

Not built. Never built.

You are standing — or, more accurately, *perched*, because there is nowhere to put your feet down without putting them on something else — on a plain that has forgotten how to be a plain. Low elevation, near sea-level if this world has a sea, which it doesn't anymore. This is the bottom of everything. An ancient shield peneplain, erosion 2.6 ancient, ground down for so many billions of years that topography has given up. There are no hills here, only a memory of hills: long, smooth swells in black and iron-red stone like the breathing of something asleep under a sheet. The bedrock is exposed and polished by time and water into a pavement of interlocking plates, each one the size of a house, a town square, each one beveled at the edges where they have settled against each other. Between them are not cracks but sutures, oozing.

It is wet. It is cold in a way that has nothing to do with winter.

The air is dim, 1.2/3, a perpetual blue dusk even when the light source is overhead. The sky is low and thick — not cloudy, exactly, but saturated. Condensation forms on nothing and rains upward as often as down. The atmosphere weeps. Water is not in rivers here. There are no rivers; rivers require gradient and youth. Here water is *near-surface*, *on-surface*, *in-surface*. The stone sweats it. Every concavity holds a mirror of cold melt that never quite freezes and never quite drains. The weeping rocks — vesicular basalt, banded gneiss, sheets of black glass — constantly exude beads of water that run together, sheeting off their flanks in slow, oily films. Fog pools ankle-high, knee-high, chest-high, then abruptly clears. Your breath doesn't plume; it falls.

And the stone is not bare. It is packed.

Density 2.6/3. There is no room. Scale 2.4/3: this goes on for kilometers, for tens of kilometers, a valley the size of a county with no walls, and every square meter is occupied.

Not by plants. Vegetation 0.4/3 — there are no plants. No grass, no lichen, no weed in a crack. This is a mineral world. What looks like moss from a distance is a thin felt of iron bacteria, black-green and rust-orange, slicking the stone where water seeps warmest. What looks like soil is just finer stone: grit, ash, cold sand. The only vertical things are accretions of mineral, not growth.

It is packed with fauna and with industry, and here those are the same statement.

Because built is 0.3/3 — none. There are no structures. No walls, no factories, no roads, no ruins. No hand has assembled anything. And yet tech 2.5/3 — industrial — is everywhere, loud, electrified, mechanized. The contradiction resolves itself when you look closer at what is covering the rocks.

They move.

Abundant fauna, 2.3/3, but not animals as you know them. Imagine crabs forged out of overlapping plates of hematite and white ceramic bone, each as big as a bathtub, as small as a fist, as big as a truck, all at once, all crowded flank-to-flank so that their legs interleave. Imagine isopods made of riveted boiler-plate that respire steam. Imagine headless, eyeless things like living trip-hammers on six stilt legs, lifting a piston-body and slamming it down onto the stone, again, again, again. Imagine low, wide filterers that drag a squeegee-mouth across the weeping rock and excrete neat, steaming ingots of slag behind them. Imagine eel-things of copper cable and cartilage that snap with arc-light when they touch.

None of them were made. They *are* the making. This is not a factory with workers; this is a biome that *is* a factory.

The sound is 2.3/3 — loud — and you understand it now. It is not wind, not water. It is the Works working. A low, continent-wide clanking, a hissing as pressure vents from a thousand back-valves, a wet squeal of metal on wet stone, the percussive THUD-THUD-THUD of the hammer-fauna in arrhythmic unison, the fizz and crackle of bioelectric discharge leaping between antennae. It is loud enough that you feel it in your molars. The cold water carries it perfectly. You cannot hear yourself think, and then you realize there is no thought to hear — the place has replaced it.

Dynamic 1.6/3 — active — everything is in motion, in cycles. Water seeps, is drunk, is boiled, is pissed back out as mineral-rich steam that instantly condenses on the cold air and falls again. The hammer-fauna pound the ancient rock and fracture off flakes; the filterers eat the flakes; the eel-things eat the filterers' charge; the filterers eat the eel-things' shed skins. No plant in the middle, no sun needed. The energy loops tight. The ground trembles faintly, continuously, with their industry. Puddles ripple outward from every impact. Steam vents open in the sutures between stone plates, sigh warm for a few seconds, then suck shut.

And weirdness 3.0/3 — reality breakdown — because this cannot hold and yet it does.

The plain is flat and low, but distance does not work. You look north across the packed, steaming, clattering carpet and you can see details a mile away as if they were inches away, then blink and they are a mile away again. The light is dim but some of the fauna cast no shadow while others cast three. Gravity has opinions here; a lifted foot feels heavier on the way down than it should, as if the planet inhales when you step. Time stutters to the rhythm of the hammers — THUD — and for a fraction of a second the water hanging in the air stops falling and hangs like glass beads — THUD — and then resumes. If you watch a single creature long enough, you will see its rivets slowly migrate across its back while you look at them, like raindrops on a window, and reform.

In some places the air folds. You can hear a vent hissing that is visibly closed. You can see steam rising from a puddle that is capped with thin, black ice that never melts. You can see your own back, very far away, small and dim, still perched on the same stone, while you are still perched here. The horizon, low and indistinct in the wet dusk, occasionally *tucks under*, like a sheet being pulled, and for a breath you are looking down at the plain from above, even though you haven't moved.

There are rules here, but they are not yours:

1.  Everything that is made makes itself.
2.  Cold preserves. Water connects. Stone remembers being flat.
3.  Nothing is empty for long. If you clear a plate — shoo away the clanking, squeegee things — the fog will immediately condense into a new one, half-formed, already hissing, already hungry for the film on the rock.

You stand a while longer, cold soaking up through your boots from the weeping stone. Your socks are wet. The dim light does not change. The hammering does not stop. The plain extends past seeing in every direction, packed, wet, ancient, unbuilt, and utterly, deafeningly productive.
