This is not a forest that grew over ruins. This is not ruins overgrown by a forest.

There is no distinction anymore. There is no bedrock here to build *on*.

### The High Weave

You are very high up, though you would not know it from warmth. The air is warm and soft and heavy, a greenhouse warmth at altitude, the kind that should be cold and thin. It *is* thin. You can feel it when you breathe deep and when you look out — light travels too far, too clear — but it holds heat like a lowland swamp after rain.

What’s beneath you is not ground. The geology here is unfinished, raw. Far below the living mat, if you part the weave and look straight down through the breathing shafts, you can see it: fresh black stone, still warm to the touch, glassy, unscarred. No valleys cut into it. No riverbeds. No soil. It rose all at once, or is still rising, a broad shield of new plateau pushed up from the deep still slick with its own making. It has no age. Water has not yet learned how to carve it. It is pre-erosion, embryonic earth. An anvil waiting for a first hammer strike that never comes.

And over that anvil, *everything* has been made.

Imagine a city designed by something that never invented a tool. No quarries. No kilns. No nails. The entire region — open to the horizon, valley-wide, town-wide and larger, a country of a single structure — is architecture grown cell by cell. Not built. Braided. Woven. Grown.

The vegetation here is total. It is not something that *covers* the landscape. It *is* the landscape. There are no meadows between buildings. The buildings are meadows. The roads are roots. The walls are living trunks the width of houses, pleached and anastomosed together centuries ago into colonnades and arches and vaulted halls that continue to live, to transpire, to photosynthesize. Leaves the size of doors form the roofing slates, overlapping by the thousands, shingling the sky. Where you would expect brick, there is cork and pith and dense, iron-hard heartwood. Where you would expect mortar, there is resin, amber still soft, still weeping.

And it is sparse.

That is the strangeness you feel first. For all its totality, for all its green mass that fills every cubic meter from the raw basalt up to a hundred feet in the air, there are only a few *kinds* of presence. Perhaps three. Perhaps one. A single titanic fig-like organism that has become cathedral and city and continent. A companion vine like green cable as thick as your torso that sutures everything together. A moss-felt that upholsters every surface. No undergrowth. No clutter. No weeds. Just immense, spaced-out, load-bearing trunks rising every few hundred yards like the pillars of a living basilica, with vast, empty, dim naves of humid air between them. You can walk for a long time in the cool green shadow between two pillars and see nothing but the next pillars receding.

It is a jungle reduced to its architectural essence. Scattered, low-saturation, monumental.

### The Wet That Never Dries

It should be rotting. Warmth + total plant mass + water should mean riotous decay, humus, frantic insects, death and rebirth.

It doesn’t.

The water here is constant, 2/3rds of the world. Not in lakes or rivers — there are no rivers yet, the geology is too young to hold them — but *on* and *in* the air. The whole Weave weeps.

Every leaf-tip condenses. Every hanging root drips. The undersides of the canopy collect dew the size of grapes that never fall, held by surface tension and some other, stranger adherence, beading and swelling and trembling in place. The air itself exhales against the thin high-altitude cool and precipitates again. Sheer curtains of mist pour slowly between the pillars like drapery, never fully evaporating, never fully falling. The bark is always slick. The moss is always wrung-out. Pockets and cisterns and leaf-basins and cupped flowers the size of bathtubs are always full to the brim, overflowing in a silent, endless spillover that runs down living gutters, down grooved trunks, into lower cisterns, and then — impossibly — is wicked back up again by capillary.

You hear it before you see it. And you never stop hearing it.

It is loud. Not in a crashing, storming way. Loud the way a great engine room is loud, when every surface is doing its work at once. The drip is not intermittent. It is a chorus of thousands of metronomes, out of phase but unceasing. A low creak and groan of wood under its own water-weight, a sigh of leaves shifting. A constant fat patter on leaves below, a trickle, a gurgle in wooden pipes. There are bell-flowers here, inverted, ten feet across, that collect mist until the weight tips them and they pour in a sudden, loud, hollow *glunk* — and then right themselves and begin again. Hundreds of them, across the valley, glunking out of sync.

And it never changes.

That is the rule of this place, the weird equilibrium that holds it frozen. The Weave does not grow. It does not die. It does not drop its leaves. The water does not actually go anywhere. The level in every cistern remains exactly the same from hour to hour, from year to year, despite the endless movement. The air stays exactly warm. The mist hangs at the same density. The sound is exactly as loud as it was when you arrived and will be exactly as loud tomorrow. You can sit and watch a single suspended droplet for an hour and it will swell and shiver but never fall, never evaporate. Time has been paused at the moment of perfect saturation.

You will find no seedlings on the forest floor, because there is no forest floor — only root-mat. No rot. No fallen branches. No birdsong building to a dawn chorus and fading. If a fauna exists here — and rarely, it does — it is a solitary thing perfectly adapted to stasis. A pale, translucent frog the size of your hand that lives its whole life in a single leaf-basin, not moving, not croaking. A beetle like polished jade that hangs motionless from a vine for years, drinking condensation. They do not breed in flurries. They do not swarm. They *persist*.

Light comes in normal, honest, midday light, which makes it stranger. This is not a dark fairy tale. The canopy is not closed; the builders left oculi, great round windows of sky exactly framed by living branches, letting columns of sun fall straight down through the mist. In those columns dust motes — or pollen that never settles — hang suspended, illuminated, absolutely still. You can see the whole regional structure in that light: the repetition of pillars to the hazy horizon, the high, wet, green vault, the raw black stone glimpsed far below like a foundation that was laid and then immediately forgotten.

Everything is made, and nothing was ever made *by* anyone. No tool marks. No joints. No dead material. The city grew itself into the shape of a city because, at this altitude, on this new and wordless rock, that was the most stable shape for a plant to take to hold water and light and warmth in equilibrium.

You are walking inside a lung the size of a principality, a lung that inhales mist and exhales mist and never tires, never ages, never needs to breathe out.

You can live here, for a while, drinking from any cupped leaf. The water is warm and clean. The air is kind. The sound will fill your ears until you stop hearing it, until you think silence would be wrong.

Just don’t wait for anything to change.
