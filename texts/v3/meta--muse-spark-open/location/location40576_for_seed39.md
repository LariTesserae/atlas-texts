This is the New Range.

Not named on any map yet because there hasn't been time for maps. The rock itself hasn't decided where it will be.

You are standing very high up. High enough that breathing takes a deliberate inhale and exhale, high enough that water boils wrong, high enough that the sky is a darker, thinner blue-grey even at noon. Elevation 2.9/3 - not a peak, but a lifted world. A high plateau that was sea-floor not long ago, thrust straight up in one raw, unfinished shove. The air is cold - 0.4/3 cold, a steady just-below-freezing that lives in your hands and ankles - but not polar-dead, not wind-scoured to sterility. It's a wet cold.

The geology is embryonic. That is the first thing you understand. This is not a mountain range that has been weathered into character. There are no carved valleys, no talus slopes sorted by age, no river canyons. The stone has only just arrived.

It came up as a single block, miles across and tilted slightly eastward, and its surface is still the surface it had when it was buried: flat-ish, stepped, scarred with slickensides where it tore free. Basalt and shale in raw, unoxidized blacks and iron greys, so fresh they still shine where they are wet. Which is everywhere. There are no rivers here because rivers have not been invented here yet. No channels have been cut. So water just - is.

Water is at 1.7/3. It doesn't flow so much as appear.

The cold traps it against the rock. Low clouds drag directly across the plateau like wet wool, snagging on every slight rise and wringing themselves out. They don't rain so much as condense. You can watch it happen: the mist thickens, beads on the rock, on your coat, on every pebble, and begins to weep. Every exposed face weeps. The vertical steps in the plateau - two feet high, ten feet high, a hundred - have dark beards of seep where groundwater forced up by the uplift finds the air for the first time and spills over. It runs in sheets, in films, in black glass runnels a finger wide, not yet gathered into streams.

Between the steps are flats, and the flats are marsh without soil. The water sits on the impermeable new stone in shallow pans, an inch deep, a foot deep, sky-colored and perfectly clear except for the silt of rock flour suspended in it. Frost rims them every morning, lacy and then melted by midday. Your boots make a sound like *skiff-skiff* through sedge that hasn't earned the name grass yet.

Because vegetation is sparse - 1.4/3. There was nothing here to seed it except what the wind carried up last season and the season before. So it grows in the only places that make sense: in the cracks. Dark green and olive and rust-brown, low and creeping. Pillow mosses that hold water like sponges and swell to twice their size. Lichen still pale, still powdery, just taking hold on the north sides of stones. In the wettest pans, mats of a hair-like sedge, thin as wire, shivering in the wind, not yet dense enough to be called meadow. From a distance the plateau looks mostly stone. Close up, you see it is prickling into life. Every crack is stitched with green.

And it should feel dead. The scale is open, 1.6/3, big enough that you can see weather coming an hour away. Big enough that the far edge of the uplift hazes into cloud and you can't tell where plateau ends and sky begins. The density is 0.2/3. Empty. There is almost nothing per volume. No trees, no buildings, no people. Built is absolute zero. No cairn, no fence line, no cut mark, no plastic. The rock is un-hand-touched. The silence between things is huge.

But the plateau is loud with animals.

Fauna is 2.3/3, abundant, and dynamic is 2.0/3 - active, cycling, hungry. The emptiness is not lifelessness; it's just that life here is mobile, thinly spread across a vast table, always moving.

You hear them before you see them, because sound is 1.1/3, mostly quiet. The background is so still that every living sound carries.

First, the insects. Despite the cold, the wet pans hatch clouds of black midges the size of pinheads. They don't bite; they rise and fall in columns over the water, humming faintly, and they are the base of everything. Where there is open water, the air above it vibrates.

Then the birds. Flocks of them - small, grey-white, with dark wingtips - what you would call snow buntings or high larks if you needed a name. They come in bursts of twenty or fifty, low and fast over the stone, turning on a single hinge. They land, pick midge larvae from the mats, lift again. Their calls are thin and seep-like: *tseep tseep.* Further out, larger shapes circle: broad-winged raptors the color of the rock, hardly moving, watching the flats.

And the herds. This is what makes the place feel industrial without a single made thing.

In the middle distance, moving north along the line of a great seep-step, is a herd of something like musk oxen, but taller at the shoulder, with coats that hang heavy and wet. There are hundreds. Maybe a thousand. From here they look like a slow, dark smear on the stone. They move constantly, grazing the sedge mats to stubble and moving on, breathing steam that hangs in the cold air long after they pass. Where they walk, the thin mud is churned and pocked with deep, round hoofprints that immediately fill with water.

Behind them, wolves - or a wolf-like thing, lean and pale - lope at the edge, never quite pressing. You can see the tracks intersecting, old and new overlapping in the soft ground.

It is an ecosystem in full motion, predator and prey and grazer and hatcher, running at full speed across a landscape that has no cover, no shelter, nowhere to hide. Everything happens out in the open, under the dim light.

Light is 1.2/3, dim. There is no direct sun. The cloud deck never fully lifts, just thins in the early afternoon to a brighter, diffused pearl. No shadows. The light is flat and even and shadowless, like light inside a tent. It makes distances hard to judge and colors oddly true: the orange lichen impossibly orange, the water impossibly sky-blue. At dusk it doesn't set so much as leak away, very slowly.

And tech is 2.5/3 - industrial - without any industry at all.

This is the clever contradiction the plateau solves for you if you stay and listen.

Because the rocks are still settling. The uplift is not finished. A few times an hour, somewhere deep under the plateau, a slab adjusts with a sound so low you feel it in your molars before your ears: a groan, a grind, like a millstone turning against another millstone the size of a county. *Hrrrrrrrrrrm.* Then silence. Then, on the far eastern scarp where the plateau drops away in a raw cliff that has never felt a rockslide, spontaneous steam vents open - white, roaring jets of geothermal breath forced out by the pressure of new rock. They don't pulse like geysers; they run steady for hours, howling, factory-loud, and then shut off as abruptly as a valve closing.

Water hammering over new stone sounds like sluices. Wind forced through the cooling cracks in the basalt whistles at a perfect, high, electric pitch, like turbines spinning. The air itself is electrified - this much wet moving over this much new iron makes the hair on your arms lift. Before the mist thickens to rain, you can sometimes see Saint Elmo's fire crawl along the ridge edges, blue and silent, a natural neon.

It is an industrial landscape made entirely without builders. Engines made of mountain weight and steam. Factories made of weather. Production made of midges and lichen doubling and herds calving on cold stone. It is scaled, it is mechanized, it is electrified - but there is no artifact to point to. Only processes.

At night, when the dim goes almost dark, you can hear it all at once in the mostly-quiet: the drip and sheet of water over rock, the faint hum of insects even in the cold, the distant howl of a steam vent opening, the subsonic groan of the earth still coming into being, and, far off, the yip of wolves and the soft, continuous spatter of a thousand hooves through shallow water.

No roads lead here yet. No mine will ever find it profitable until erosion - still only 0.3/3, embryonic, just beginning to think about making a gully here or a pothole there - has done ten thousand years of work. For now it is simply being made, wet and cold and empty and absolutely full.

You are not supposed to be here, but nothing will stop you.
