It is a height that has forgotten it was ever a mountain.

This is not a peak, not a range — it is the roof of a continent that has had so long to erode that it has become almost level again. You are standing very, very high — two or three miles of air beneath you, thin and blue-black at the edges — but the ground under your boots is not steep. It rolls. It sighs. The ancientness has worn everything down to a primordial plain: broad shields of exposed basalt and gneiss, planed smooth as old flagstones, then shallowly scooped into basins the size of counties, and lifted again into long, whale-backed ridges that take a full day to cross on foot. There are no sharp teeth left here. The world ended its orogeny an eon ago and settled into its final form. Even the river valleys are not canyons but soft, U-shaped troughs ground out by ice that came and went and came again until it polished the stone to a gloss.

It is cold. Not the killing, dry cold of a pole, but a damp, high, breath-steaming cold. Normal daylight falls here — a pale, level sun the color of paper — but the air is so clear and so thin you can see weather being born fifty miles away as a bruise on the horizon. It snows, a little, almost every afternoon. Not a storm, but a dusting of fine ice crystals that never quite accumulates. What water there is does not come in oceans or lakes.

It comes in hanging.

The entire plateau breathes moisture in its own cycle. In the morning, the low places exhale. Every scooped basin, every lee behind a ridge, fills with cloud that has condensed overnight against the cold stone. For an hour or two you are walking through a sea of low fog that wets your coat and your eyebrows and the wool of your hood until it drips. By midmorning the fog lifts, torn into rags by the wind off the ridges, and leaves everything rimed in dew and frost-melt. Water collects the way old high places always find water: not as a dominant feature, but as pockets and seams. A cliff that was polished by glaciers will weep for miles along one dark band of stone, a thousand little springs born from last week's snowmelt. They join into quick, braided creeks that run clear and shockingly cold over pink gravel for twenty miles across a flat, then vanish without ceremony into sinkholes in the basalt.

Nothing is wet for long. Nothing is ever truly dry.

And everything that is damp is green.

This is what is strange first: how lush it is without any animal to eat it. There are no birds here. No insects to hear. No grazing things. The fauna dial has been turned almost to zero, and the silence of that absence takes a while to understand — you keep waiting to hear a call or a rustle that never comes. And yet the vegetation has taken that vacancy as permission.

Where the wind has laid down a hundred thousand years of silt, there is tundra, but tundra as a forest would understand it: knee-high mats of moss the color of rust and emerald, lichen like pale coral, dwarf willow and birch that grow sideways, huddled and dense, their trunks thicker than they are tall, forming continuous carpets that can be walked on like a mattress. In the sheltered troughs where the springs run, the carpet becomes extravagant. Thickets of black-stemmed reed, ten feet tall, shiver without wind. Ferns unfurl fronds as wide as doors. Fleshy, pale-green bladder-plants cluster around every seep, swelling with condensation they collect on hair-fine cilia. You will see entire hillsides covered in flowering things that should not survive cold like this — low, star-shaped blossoms of white and deep violet that open all at once when the sun hits them, and close with an audible, soft pop as the cloud rolls back in.

It is active. It has to be seen to be believed how *busy* a place with no animals is. Without grazers, without pollinators, the plants have learned other motions. The mosses creep visibly at the edge of meltwater, an inch a day. The reeds lean toward any passing heat. On windless afternoons you can watch puffs of spores and seeds move in deliberate, corkscrewing clouds, riding electrostatic gradients above the plateau, seeking damp stone. The willow-mats tighten and loosen with the freeze-thaw, making a faint ticking under the snow. It is a vegetable prairie the size of a small planet, going through all the business of growth and competition and seeding and death at high speed in the short cold summer, and doing it completely alone.

Scattered across this impossible, even vastness is a city that is not a city.

If you climb one of those long ridges and look out — and you can look out for a hundred miles in this air — you will see them. Never close together. Never sprawling. Each one sits alone in its basin like a stone in a bowl of moss.

They are urban, unmistakably. Not villages, not camps. Layered, organized construction on a scale that must have taken generations. But the technology is all hand-tool, all pre-industrial: unmortared walls of fitted basalt blocks, each block the size of a sleeping ox; terraces cut back into the soft old hillsides to make level streets; aqueducts made of hollowed stone troughs that coax the weeping cliff-springs for miles into cisterns; towers that are not for defense but for watching weather — tall, narrow, open at the top like chimneys, with iron bells that never ring because there is so little wind down in the basins.

No one builds new ones. No one needs to. The plain is so old and stable that a house built of its own stone will stand until the stone itself softens. So the inhabitants — human, or near enough — build *inward*. You walk into one of these basin-cities and find that the outermost wall is low and tumbled and entirely overgrown with lichen, a thousand years old. Inside that is another wall, newer, sharper. Inside that, houses huddle shoulder to shoulder around a central green where the cistern is, roofs made of reed-thatch weighted down with stones against the rare high wind, smoke coming from low chimneys, doors made of willow-wood boiled and bent. Streets are paved with the same pink gravel as the creeks. People move quietly, well-wrapped, carrying water, carrying cut fern bundles, carrying stones. They do not shout. Sound does not carry well here; the moss eats it, the thin air thins it further.

Mostly quiet. That is the rule. Your own boot on the path is loud. Your breath is loud. If you stand still you can hear the land itself: the faint hiss of a spring disappearing into basalt, the tick of dwarf birch expanding, the almost inaudible sigh when a cloud bank lifts off a ridge and the pressure changes.

And then, past the farthest houses, where the reed-thatch stops and the wild moss begins again, the reality weakness starts.

You notice it first as a problem of distance. That ridge you thought was a day away — you walk toward it all morning and it does not get closer. Or it gets closer too fast, lurching a mile inward when you blink. The sky in that direction is too low, as if the blue has sagged down to rest on the stone. Light pools there strangely. Shadows fall at the wrong angle; a person's shadow might fall *toward* the sun for a few steps, then correct itself with no snap, as if it had simply changed its mind.

This is a high place on the edge of physics. The plateau is so vast it makes its own weather, and the weather has been rubbing against the edge of the world for so long it has worn a hole. In some basins, gravity is lenient. Water runs uphill for a yard before remembering to fall. In others, a thrown stone hangs a fraction too long at its apex. The builders know. They do not build towers tall enough to poke the sag. They lay out their streets to avoid the places where straight lines won't stay straight — you can see it in the maps they carve on cistern walls: a city plan with a gentle, deliberate warp in it, a street that bows out around nothing, a courtyard that is purposefully left empty because if you stand in the exact center you hear your own voice a second before you speak.

At noon, when the sun is directly overhead and the light is most normal, the breakdown is almost invisible. At dusk, it shows. The horizon does not darken evenly. In patches, the sky seems to have depth *behind* itself, a deeper night visible through a scrim of day. Sometimes a second, paler sun tracks a few degrees off the real one for an hour before fading — not a reflection, but a memory of where the sun was yesterday. The people do not remark on it. They have lived with a thin place overhead for longer than their walls have stood. They hang wind-chimes made of slate shards in the courtyards that only tinkle when the air forgets how to hold sound, and so they know when not to go out to collect water.

Night comes cold and absolute, with stars so numerous and so close — because you are so high — that they throw faint shadows on the snowless ground. The cities glow, each a small, warm bowl of amber light in the vast dark, far from each other, far from anywhere. Between them, the moss and the reeds and the ferns continue their quiet, animal-less industry under the strange sky, growing, sporing, drinking the damp that will be fog again by morning, in a silence so profound you can hear the faint, endless settling of a world that finished becoming itself a very long time ago and now simply continues.
