It is not an ocean as the surface knows it.

You are below. Far enough below that the word *surface* has lost its meaning — a kilometer down, or two, where pressure stops being a number and becomes a condition of existence. There is no sky here. There was never a sky here. The world above might be air, or ice, or nothing at all anymore; from this depth it registers only as a faint, colder dimming somewhere impossibly high and unreachable.

This is a plain. Not a trench, not a slope. A true plain, at the bottom of the world.

### The Ancient Floor

The stone beneath you is the oldest thing you will ever see. It has finished happening.

Billions of years of water have worked it until there is nothing left to erode. No peaks, no edges, no novelty. The basalt and shale have been ground down to a pavement so flat and settled that the eye refuses to understand its scale — like a continent-sized floor tile. It dips perhaps a meter over ten kilometers. It is pitted and scarred not with violence but with age: shallow potholes polished smooth as bowls, vast hairline cracks filled with black silt that has had a million years to settle, low scars where glaciers passed overhead when this plain was not yet drowned and left their last signature.

There are no hills. There is no horizon in any terrestrial sense — the darkness swallows distance before topography can define it. But if you could light it all at once, you would see it goes on and on, valley-wide, region-wide, a country made only of floor. Open and empty and occupied.

Because it is not empty. That is the first shock.

The floor is **packed**.

Edge to edge, seam to seam, without a hand's breadth of free mud between them, the plain is paved with presence. Nodules of manganese and iron, each the size of a fist, a head, a barrel, grown by accretion around a shark's tooth or a fragment of bone over epochs. They lie shoulder to shoulder in a cobbling that covers the abyssal plain for hundreds of square kilometers. Black, rust-red, green-black with thin biofilm. Where they touch they have worn divots into each other. Between them is not sand but a dense, cold ooze of the finest particulate — dead diatoms, volcanic ash so fine it floats for years before landing, the powdered remains of the nodules themselves.

There is no room. You could walk for a day and never touch bare rock. It's saturated coverage, a natural tiling, as complete and mindless as scales on a fish. This is what density means here: not life, not buildings, just *matter*, having had an eternity to arrive and nowhere else to go.

And it is untouched. No road cuts it. No foundation breaks it. No wreck sits upon it. Whatever made those nodules did so without intent.

### The Water That Is The World

Water here is not a substance *in* the world. It is the world.

It is cold in a way that predates cold — 1°C, 0°C, the knife-edge before ice forms but never quite does because the salt and the pressure will not allow it. It burns the skin through any suit in minutes. It is viscous, heavy, absolutely transparent and absolutely opaque at once, because after twenty feet there is no light left to carry.

Light exists only as a rumor. Far above, there is a gradient from black to a slightly-less-black indigo, which is not light at all but the memory of light your brain invents. Down here, true darkness lives. On rare occasions a creature makes its own brief, chemical lantern — a flicker that illuminates three meters of nodules and then dies, leaving the retinal ghost more profound than the dark itself.

And this water, for all its weight and silence, is **violent**.

That is the paradox that defines this place: an ancient, final-form floor that has given up on change, drowned beneath a sea that has never, for a second, stopped moving.

There is no storm in the atmospheric sense. No wind. But the deep currents come roaring across the plain like invisible rivers the size of countries. Thermohaline cataracts falling off unseen continental shelves, turbidity flows of cold, sediment-laden water that move with terrifying, silent speed. They do not break against anything; there is nothing to break against. So they scour.

You feel it not as a wave but as a sudden push — a wall of pressure that lifts the fine ooze between the nodules into clouds that hang for hours, tilts you, tries to tumble you. Then another current from another direction shears it in half. Eddies form the size of houses and spin and die in minutes. Vortices of snow — marine snow, the endless fallout of organic debris from above — spiral upward, downward, sideways. It is hyperactive and chaotic and utterly quiet.

You hear almost nothing. Sound here is swallowed. The water carries vibration too well and too far, so everything becomes a low, distant hum, below hearing. Your own breathing, if you have a suit. The faint tick of nodules clicking against each other as a surge rolls them a centimeter — the only real sound, like a field of cold knuckles knocking together once an hour, across a plain the size of a nation. Otherwise: silence pressed into your ears by pressure.

### Sparse Life, Primitive Mark

Nothing *should* live here, and almost nothing does.

Vegetation is vestigial. There are no forests, no kelp — not enough light. Instead, where a current exposes a slightly higher nodule, there are sparse, desperate growths: black bacterial mats like dried tar, hair-fine white filaments of glass sponge that rise six inches and filter what passes, a solitary stalked crinoid waving slowly, its arms spread for whatever speck drifts within reach. One per ten meters. One per hundred. They are not a community; they are hermits, far apart, barely holding on. They make the pack of stones look even more absolute by contrast.

Fauna is rarer still. Most of the time the plain seems dead. Then, out of the murk, something massive and pale will pass — a sleeper shark, old and blind, moving with insulting slowness, displacing tons of water without disturbing it. Or a colony of small, eyeless shrimp clinging to a single sponge, legs beating constantly against the current. Lights flicker where a dragonfish hunts and vanish again. Every animal is solitary, quiet, energy-conserving. There is not enough to sustain chatter or schools or swarms.

And yet — there was someone here. Or something that uses tools in the oldest possible way.

You find them only if you stay a long time and learn to tell nodule from nodule: little stacks. Two or three flat stones balanced atop a larger one. A nodule moved to cap another. Not natural — the current knocks them down, it doesn't build them up. Small cairns, barely knee-high, already half-toppled, fuzzy with bacterial mat. In one place a line of nodules has been cleared — a path perhaps a body-length wide where the ooze has been brushed aside to expose the paler rock beneath. An offering place. A trap guide. A marker for safe passage across a featureless void.

No metal. No carving. Only the rearrangement of what was already there, by hand or claw or blunt stick. Early-tool-use, pre-intelligence-as-we-know-it. The builders are gone, or hiding in the dark beyond your light, watching their faint lanterns. They left nothing built, because building implies permanence, and permanence is impossible when the sea never stops moving. They left only a temporary suggestion against eternity, and the sea is already taking it apart, grain by grain.

You stand — or float — in cold, total dark, on the oldest floor in the world, packed solid with stones laid down before continents took their current shape. Above you, thousands of feet of roiling, invisible, silent turbulence rages without end, turning the water to a slow hurricane. Around you, almost no living thing moves. And beneath your feet, in the mud between two iron nodules that have touched each other for a million years, a tiny white tube worm has just extended itself into the current, filtering the dark for food that may not come today.

The place does not need you to see it. It was finished long before you arrived, and will continue to unmake itself forever.
