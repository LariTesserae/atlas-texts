It is not a mountain, though it thinks it is going to be.

High up where the air should be thin and cold, there is instead a bowl. A small country, no larger than a hamlet's commons, maybe three hundred meters across if you walked its rim - a perfect, artificial caldera set on the roof of the world. Beneath it, the real mountains fall away in blue and hazy distance, but here you are standing on something made. Everything is made.

They poured it.

You can see it in the stratigraphy of the walls: not rock but pour-lifts of aggregate, pale limestone concrete still showing the grain of its formwork, bands of darker slag-concrete, bands of brick that was never meant to be seen but now is, courses of rebar like exposed ribs where a section slumped before it cured. This is what *emerging* erosion means here - not that something old is wearing away, but that something new has not yet decided what shape it wants to be. The edges are soft. Corners have not hardened. Water finds a seam and makes a new gully overnight in material that is still chemically alive, still off-gassing heat as it cures. In one place the wall weeps lime-white milk. In another a heap of raw basalt gravel, tipped last month and already half-colonized, steams gently in the cool air.

Because the air is warm. It shouldn't be, at this elevation. Down in the valleys they are pulling on jackets. Up here, inside the bowl, it is bathhouse-warm, greenhouse-warm, womb-warm. The bowl holds heat.

### The Works

The entire bowl is terraced, step after step after step down to the center, not for farming in the old way but because the whole structure is a machine that was later taught to be a garden, or a garden that was taught to be a machine. Hard to tell which came first now.

Each terrace is a factory floor, open to the sky.

Industrial tech, but not the clanking, stamping kind. The older kind. Low-pressure, patient. Riveted boilers the size of cottages squat on the middle terraces, their brass fittings gone green-black, feeding heat not into pistons but into pipes that run like veins under every walkway. Condensers shaped like giant steel lungs breathe slowly. Conveyors, stopped or moving at a crawl, carry not ore but compost, not coal but seed husk. Dynamo housings hum at the edge of hearing. Everything is electrified, but the electricity is used mostly for light and for pumps.

And everything leaks, by design.

Water is everywhere, not as a lake or a river but as a condition. It is 2 a.m. after-rain wet, permanently. The boilers throw their spent steam across the bowl in low clouds that never quite escape the rim. It hits the cooler concrete on the upper terraces, condenses, and runs. Every surface weeps. The vertical faces of the terraces are faced in cracked white tile - old swimming-pool tile, sanitary tile - and water beads on them constantly, gathering, falling in a transparent curtain a millimeter thick. The walkways are iron grating over dark cisterns; you hear your own footsteps and, beneath them, the trickle and plink of overflow moving down one level to the next.

At the very bottom, where all the terraces drain, there is no drain. There is a shallow, perfectly circular pan, thirty meters across, lined with black ceramic. It holds warm water to your ankles, opaque with life. It is the heart.

The sound here is wrong for industry. It should be loud. It is mostly quiet. A low, 60-cycle thrum from the dynamos that you feel in your molars more than hear. The tick of cooling metal. The endless, irregular percussion of water: drip from a pipe elbow onto a fern frond, sheet-flow over tile lip, plop into the pan, gurgle in a half-blocked channel. Occasionally a pressure valve somewhere sighs and releases a plume of white that rolls across the terraces like a shy ghost and is gone.

### The Lush

Nothing is planted in soil because there is no soil. There is no natural substrate anywhere in the bowl. And yet it is savagely, chokingly lush.

Vegetation has taken the industrial mandate literally. Engineers specified growth, so growth happened. Every pipe is lagged in moss so thick you could lose your hand in it - emerald, lime, and a deep, almost black sphagnum that glistens. Ferns uncurl from expansion joints. From the rusted teeth of a broken gear, a tomato vine has rooted in the accumulated detritus and now sprawls three meters, its fruits small, hot, too sweet from the sulfur in the air. Hanging gardens drape from catwalks: engineered philodendrons with leaves as big as doors, their aerial roots dangling down to sip the humid air and never touching ground. On the sunniest tier, rice paddies were built in stainless steel trays, still perfectly level, still flooded an inch deep, the rice now gone feral and mixed with cattails and taro, all of it whispering against itself when the steam wind moves.

It is dense. Not jungle dense, but workshop dense - every cubic meter is used. Vines use pipes as trellises. Moss uses rivets as anchors. A fig tree has grown sideways out of a cracked boiler inspection hatch, its trunk flattened by the metal lips that birthed it, its canopy forming a low roof over a walkway. You have to duck under its figs. They fall constantly into the water channels with soft splashes and are carried down to the pan.

And it is all active. Nothing is ornamental. Bees - fat, slow, engineered bumblebees that can handle the thin air - work the tomato flowers in silence. Water striders dimple the pan. The rice grows visibly, a millimeter a day. Rust grows too, flaking off in orange scales that become substrate for lichen. Concrete blooms with nitrifying bacteria, pink and white. The whole bowl is respiring: plants exhale oxygen, boilers exhale steam, water exhales mist, concrete exhales heat. You can see the cycle if you stand still long enough: steam rises from the boilers, condenses on the fig leaves, drips to the moss, seeps through grating, is pumped back to the boilers.

Fauna, aside from the engineered insects and the microbes and the plants pretending to be animals in their eagerness, is rare. Once an hour a swift cuts across the open sky above the rim, looks down at this impossible warm wet island in the sky, and does not land. No rats. No pigeons. Only you, and sometimes a maintenance worker in rubber boots who does not speak, scraping algae off an intake with a long-handled brush.

### The Rules

Here is what you learn if you stay a day:

1. Nothing leaves. Water that evaporates inside the rim condenses inside the rim. Seeds that fall grow inside the rim. Even heat is reluctant to leave. The bowl is a closed loop that pretends to be open to the sky.

2. Everything drips. If something is dry, it is broken.

3. The structure is still being born. The ground under your feet shifts, hairline cracks propagating with faint pings at night as the deep concrete cures and shrinks. In the morning you will find a new seep where there was none, a new fern where water now reaches. Do not trust a map. Do not trust a stair; last week's stair may be today's waterfall.

4. The light is normal, cruelly normal. High mountain sun, hard and clear at noon, pouring straight down into the bowl and making every tile gleam, every leaf transparent. Then, as steam gathers, it softens to a diffuse glow, like light through frosted glass. At night, worklights on rusted standards click on, amber and buzzing, and the whole place looks like a cathedral workshop that never closed.

You stand on the upper rim at evening, when the air outside the bowl finally cools and the warm breath of the bowl pours over the lip like an invisible river. Below you the terraces are lit, each level a ring of green and white and rust, each humming quietly to itself. Steam coils up from the central pan and catches the worklight. The fig tree shivers though there is no wind. Somewhere a tomato drops.

It is a small place. You could walk its entire circumference before your tea went cold. But it contains layers, like a throat - sky, steam, leaf, tile, iron, water, concrete, water again - and because it is so dense and so wet and so alive, it feels larger inside than its walls suggest. A hamlet that contains a rainforest that contains a factory that contains a spring.

It has not eroded. It is eroding into being.
