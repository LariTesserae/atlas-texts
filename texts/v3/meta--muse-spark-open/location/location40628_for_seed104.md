The world is high and drowned.

Not a sea at the bottom of the world, but a sea at the top. You are on the Summit Ocean, a continent-wide water that settled over the roof of the planet and never left.

In any other world this elevation — miles above any baseline, where the air should be thin and cold and scoured to stone — would be dry, sharp, wind-burned. Here it is the opposite. The high country is low, flattened, worn down to a patience that looks like surrender. This is ancient erosion. Not jagged peaks but the memory of peaks. Everything that could be broken has been broken. Everything that could be carried away has been carried away, grain by grain, over a time so long it stopped being geology and became manners. The stone learned to be still.

What remains is a vast, gentle, impossible tableland of soft marble and basalt, rounded to the smoothness of river stones the size of provinces. Broad domes and shallow bowls, terraces as wide as countries stepping down by inches, basins sunk no deeper than a breath. And over all of it, the water.

It is not deep the way a trench is deep. It is deep the way the sky is deep. Warm, dim, translucent water that holds from the stone at your feet up and up until distance turns it to haze and then to night. On a clear — if you can call it clear — day you can see light filtering down from a sun that is not a sun but a brighter patch of water, a warm diffuse glow somewhere impossibly above that never quite resolves. The light comes down in columns, not rays. It bends. It pools. It collects in hollows like dust.

You are submerged, completely, and you breathe it anyway. Or you don't breathe and don't need to. The rule here broke long ago: this water is atmosphere and ocean at once. It has currents like jet streams and thermoclines like seas. Warm strata slide over cool ones without mixing. Sometimes a slow rain falls *within* the water — heavier, clearer lenses of it falling sideways or up, passing through you with a faint pressure change on your skin. Suspended in it is a fine, luminous silt that never settles, turned over forever by the motion.

And it is lush.

You expect a drowned high desert to be barren. It isn't. From every softened stone grows something. Not forests — there is no up to make a forest — but a density, a saturated thickness of life that occupies every cubic meter. Kelp that is not kelp: great warm-water ribbons as wide as roads, amber and black-green, rooting without roots, anchoring themselves by weight and will to the smoothed table. They hang in the water column for half a mile, swaying though there is no wind, only current. Between them, meadows of feathered filter-plants, each frond the size of a house, opening and closing on a slow pulse. Sponge terraces build upon themselves in layered cities, pink and sulfur yellow, porous and humming with trapped water moved through them. Everything grows into everything else. Vine-things like braided rope, thick as your body, stitch the kelp towers together. Mats of floating liverwort a mile across drift and snag and become islands for a season before tearing free. The water is dense with presences, near-touching, layered, a vertical jungle with no ground or canopy, only middle.

It is warm.  Blood-warm. The kind of warm that makes you forget your edges. The water holds heat like a bath that has never been left to cool, and the stone beneath radiates it back. In the dim, the warmth is a kind of light of its own.

The scale defeats you. You can swim — or fall — for days in any direction and the scene does not change in kind, only in arrangement. Weather happens *inside* the ocean. Not on its surface. Storms of turbidity roll through like fog banks, cold fronts of clear, mineral-heavy water that make all the kelp lean one way for a week as they pass. Halos of heat bloom and dissipate, and with them come blooms of pale flowers that exist for forty-eight hours, opening all at once across a thousand square miles, then dissolving into snow. Tides occur with no moon, a slow breathing of the entire mass, lifting and pressing, felt in your inner ear.

It is quiet. So quiet you hear the small mechanics of the place: the creak of a kelp stipe growing, a sound like wet wood; the faint, endless sigh of the sponge-fields pumping; the *tick* of silt striking silt; your own heart too loud. When the currents shift, you hear them as a low brush across the stone, a sound like someone turning in sleep far away.

Animals are rare, which makes each one an event. Life here mostly does not move. It grows, it filters, it waits. Fauna, when it comes, does not swim so much as interrupt. A grazer the size of a whale but shaped like a manta, mouth a silent barn-door, passing once a year through this territory, trailing a wake of torn fronds that will feed the meadow for months. A shoal of glass minnows that are not minnows but single eyes with fins, here and gone. Something large, far above in the dim, that eclipses the sun-patch for an hour and is never seen clearly. You do not feel watched. You feel outnumbered by plants.

And the world does not hold together properly. This is the weirdness of the high drowned place. Its rules have eroded along with its mountains.

Depth is unreliable. You look down and the stone seems ten feet away; you reach and it is a hundred. You look up and the light is close enough to touch; you rise toward it for an afternoon and get no nearer. Distances stretch when you are not looking. Kelp forests repeat. You pass the same distinctive split boulder three times although you have traveled in a straight line. Gravity is a suggestion that the water politely overrules; down is toward the stone only because the stone is there to agree upon. If you stay still long enough, you begin to drift upward without moving, as if the water has decided you belong higher.

In places, the water stratifies into visible layers, like glass sheets stacked to the heavens, each with its own refraction. Fish — rare — cross a boundary and invert, becoming their own mirror for a moment. In other places, the water thins to almost-air and you can see, for a breath, what this plateau would look like dry: a blinding white plain under a violet sky, cracked, utterly still, before the ocean remembers itself and rushes back without a splash.

There were people, once. Or something that made. Scattered, almost erased. This is not a ruined civilization; it is a faint annotation. A single stair cut into a dome of stone that leads nowhere, its steps grown over with crimson crust. A circle of standing stones too perfect to be natural, each now a garden of anemones, leaning together like conspirators. A lone tower — no door, no window — made of the same basalt as the floor, worn until its seams are only shadows, top lost in kelp. No tools remain. No metal. Only shape implying intent. They understood this place enough to mark it and not enough to stay, or they became part of the density.

Nothing here is fast except its slow processes. The kelp grows while you watch, a visible creep. The water turns over. The light dims further toward evening — though there is no evening, only a deeper dimming of the far glow — and the filter-plants close with a soft, collective sigh that moves across the plain like a prayer. Warmth lingers. The ancient stone holds.

You hang there, in the middle of everything, in the quiet, in the thickness, high above a world that no longer has a below, and the ocean-sky presses warm on all sides, and the ancient, flattened earth breathes beneath you through its coverings of living lace.
