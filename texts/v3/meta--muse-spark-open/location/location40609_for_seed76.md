On the High Dry Terrace

You are very high up. Not a peak — there are no peaks here anymore. There was never a need for them. The world is a plateau, and then another plateau on top of that plateau, rising like a set of weathered stairs cut for gods who never came. The elevation is such that the sky has lost its blue depth and gone pale and tight, a bright, flat dome of light that presses down without warming you.

The light is immense. At noon, it is white and total and shadowless, as if you were standing inside a lamp. But the air is cool. Thin, clean, mineral cold that lives in your lungs. It is a high-desert cold, a crystal cold, not winter but altitude — the cold of being closer to the sun and further from any heat that might hold. You can see too far. To any horizon the light carries without haze, until distance just bleaches out.

There is no water here. Not in any way you expect water to be.

No rivers scored this land, no lakes pooled, no rain fell long enough to matter. This is a world before or after water. What little moisture exists is bound — locked as frost in shaded stone pores at dawn, vanishing before midday, or sealed inside life itself. The ground tells that story. It is an ancient seabed lifted and dried and left to cure for epochs.

Erosion has finished its work. This is not a raw, crumbling place. It is established, balanced, in its own state. Wind is the only sculptor left, and it worked slowly. The bedrock is exposed in vast, smooth undulations — limestone-white, ash-grey, iron-ocher — polished into whaleback domes and shallow bowls and long, parallel troughs that run for miles like petrified dunes. In places the rock has split into terraces and mesas with softened edges, as if melted slightly and refrozen. Potholes have been sanded into perfect circles. Ridges are rounded off, humble. Nothing jagged. Everything has been touched for so long that it has agreed to its shape. Fine dust collects in the lee of every rise, pinkish loess so light it suspends for minutes if disturbed, then settles again.

And the sound — there is almost none. The wind moves high above you, hissing across the dome-tops, but down in the troughs where you walk, it falls quiet. You hear your own breath, the faint grit underfoot, a periodic tick as the cooling rock contracts. That is all, for a long time. Then something else.

Life here does not drink. It learns around thirst.

It is sparse. For hours of walking you might see nothing but stone and light and those long troughs catching shadow. The emptiness is continental; the terrace goes on until it makes its own weather, until clouds form below you in lower basins you've never descended to. Scale defeats the eye. One dark smear on a far ridge, which you take for a boulder, resolves after half a day's approach into a grove the size of a forest.

Because when there is life, it is lush — shockingly, impossibly lush.

They are not trees as you know them, not needing soil or rain. They are water-collectors turned inside out. Call them Aeroliths.

From a distance they look like low, dark reefs or spilled ink — mats of dense, waxy black-green spread over the rock bowls where dust has gathered. As you come closer, detail pulls you. The colony rises a meter, two meters, a tangle of thick, interwoven trunks that are not wood but layered fiber and salt, cool to the touch. From these, broad leaves lift — translucent, pale jade edged with rusty red, each one large as a tabletop, stiff as ceramic. They do not wilt. They are angled precisely to the sun, and their undersides bristle with tens of thousands of silver hairs, each beaded at the tip with cold.

These hairs do not photosynthesize. They condense. In the cool dawn, when the upper air still holds a thin veil of vapor sublimated from deep stone, the hairs ice over. A few degrees of warming at sunrise and the frost melts directly into oil inside the leaf — not water, a slow, clear hydrocarbon brine that the plant uses as blood. You can see it moving if you slit a leaf: thick, fragrant fluid, bitter as camphor, that never evaporates.

Each grove is an island oasis that makes its own damp. By midday, the leaves exhale the lightest fraction of that brine as vapor, and it hangs trapped under the canopy — a cool, medicinal mist that never reaches higher than your knees. Step into the grove and the temperature drops five degrees, the bright light fractures into green shafts, and the silence changes. Things drip without water. Leaves click and sigh as they expand and contract. The air hums faintly with static as trichomes discharge.

Within that mist, smaller things thrive. Cushions of moss-like growth that are not moss — dense crimson and ochre felt that grows directly on the stone, holding its brine in tiny glass beads like caviar. Lichen-brooms, pale turquoise, that shiver even when there is no wind. Vine-cables that throw themselves from mat to mat across bare rock, leathery and jointed, rootless along their entire length until they find another bowl to colonize.

There are no forests continuous. That would be too much. The rule here is: abundance, then absence. A hectare of furious, dripping, waxy jungle crowded with scent and micro-movement, then ten kilometers of empty, ringing stone to the next one.

And because the groves are few and far between, animals are rare. But they move.

You will not hear them coming. In the quiet, you hear instead the sudden stop of sound — the insects falling silent.

They are gliders, mostly. Long, incredibly narrow ribbons of membrane and hollow bone, stretched between elongated forelimbs, with small, hot bodies tucked in the center. Think manta rays made of paper and tendon. They spend most of their lives aloft in the thin bright air, riding the thermals that rip off the white rock at midday, circling so high they are specks. They descend only to feed. They fall upon the groves in a slow, spiraling drop, unfurl feeding fans that brush the condensation-hairs and drink the brine directly with fringed tongues. Their wings are translucent amber and creek faintly in the cold. They never land. If they touch stone, they die — they cannot take off again from the ground. So they hover, beating almost imperceptibly, stealing moisture and lifting away with gut fulls of plant fat.

Everything else — the crawlers, the rollers — stays hidden inside the matte during the day, rustling once in a while, turning over a bead-fruit.

By late afternoon the light slants and finally warms by a degree, stretching shadows a hundred meters long from a knee-high ridge. Each grove throws a perfect dark blade across the rock. The dust in the air catches fire. Then, fast, the light goes. Because the air is so thin, night falls without dusk. One moment white, the next deep indigo, stars appearing hard and near, too bright, too many. The stone begins to tick loudly as it cools.

In the groves, leaves fold shut with audible pops, sealing their brine inside for the night. The gliders vanish upward. The mist sinks and freezes onto the silver hairs as rime.

Soon, again, silence. Vast, established, waiting silence. The plateau breathing cool and dry under alien stars, patient between islands of secret, oil-brisk green.
