It is low country, not quite sea-level but close enough that the earth never quite dries.

You come down to it - not much of a descent, a long shallow roll off the agricultural plain - and the air changes before the ground does. It gets heavier. Warmer. The light stays the same, a normal open-sky white-yellow of late morning, but it comes down thicker here, filtered through a humidity that never burns off. Even when it hasn't rained, it has just rained, or is about to. The atmosphere condenses on everything.

This is a basin town. A single bowl, maybe half a mile across, with a definable edge where the fields stop and the structures begin. That smallness is the first thing you notice after the wet: that the whole place is one thing. Not a sprawl. A hamlet-city, dense as a knot, built edge-to-edge inside its own low rim like water collected in a palm.

And it is built. Thoroughly built.

Brick row-houses and three-story tenements, machine shops, a brickworks, a textile mill and a dye works, warehouses, switching yards, water towers, corrugated sheds added onto sheds, all of it organized on a grid that has been forced to warp slightly to follow the old creekbed. Streets are narrow, straight where they can be, then jogging around a boiler house that was there first. Pipes run overhead between second floors, lagged in asbestos and sweating. Iron staircases bolt to exterior walls. Washing lines cross the alleys. Everything is electrified - not futuristic, but industrial electrified: ceramic insulators, humming transformers in cages, bare bulbs behind wire mesh, the yellow sodium glow of the mill yard at night that never fully goes out during the day.

It should be a hard place. It was a hard place.

Then the plants took it without breaking it.

This is not a ruin and not an overgrowth in the way of abandonment. The vegetation index here isn't "a lot of trees." It's total. Plant-as-substrate. The town and the jungle became one organism a long time ago and reached an equilibrium, and now they operate together.

The bricks weep. Every wall that faces north or east is faced in a living mat - not ivy alone, but a thatch of it: creeping fig and kudzu-type vine and split-leaf philodendron and mosses that have grown thick enough to be soil themselves, ten, twenty centimeters deep. You can push your hand into a wall and feel warmth and damp. The roofs are meadows. Not green roofs by design - originally tar and gravel - but now sedge and creeping fern and young willows whose roots have found the cracks and widened them slowly, slowly, just enough to hold. The gutters are small rivers, always running, even in dry weeks. The pipes overhead are wrapped in vines that use the waste heat. The columns of the old rail trestle are now trunks, bark growing around iron.

In the streets, the cobbles and poured concrete have heaved into soft ridges where the roots of banyan-like figs run underneath, but the surfaces are still tended. People sweep them. They cut back the vines from doorways every morning. It is a gardening as constant and serious as factory work. If you stop cutting for a month, you would lose the stairwell. If you cut too much, the building overheats and the wall crumbles, because now the root-mat is what holds the mortar from washing out in the daily rains.

Water is everywhere at hand. Not a lake, not a flood, but a condition. The creek that the town was organized around never went away; it was channelized, then it softened again, widening into canals between the mill buildings, dark green and slow. Sluice gates, still hand-cranked, control its level. Every low spot is marsh-edge: reeds and taro and water hyacinth thick in the drainage ditches, dragonflies you almost never see because the birds are so few. Second-growth cypress and mangrove-type trees - though this is not the tropics, just a hot temperate lowland - stand knee-deep behind the dye works, their knees poking up through the chain-link. The rocks - foundation stones, curb stones, the big limestone blocks of the mill's old spillway - weep constantly. Condensation beads and runs down them all afternoon. After a rain, which comes briefly and heavily almost every day at three or four, everything drips for hours. You hear it long after the clouds pass: ticking off leaves, off eaves, off the power lines.

And sound: it is loud here. Not chaotic loud, but dense, layered loud.

The mill never really stops. Even at half-capacity it puts out a low, patient thrum you feel in the brick - the turbines, the looms, the big drying fans turning and turning. Steam valves sigh and let go on a timer. A generator buzzes behind a wall of jasmine that does nothing to muffle it. Over that, water moves constantly: the spillway chute, the sluice, the gutters, the rain barrels overflowing in chain after chain down every alley. And over that, the rustle and tick of the plants themselves, growing. You notice how few animal sounds there are to punctuate it. No gulls. No pigeon flocks. A heron might stand motionless in a canal for a whole day, unmoving. A cat moves along a pipe-rack, thin and careful. The mosquitoes are there, and the ants in endless lines along the mortar, but the larger fauna is rare - pushed out not by toxins but by the simple fact there is no niche left unfilled by leaf and brick and the hum of machines. This town feeds itself on its own leaves and its own labor.
