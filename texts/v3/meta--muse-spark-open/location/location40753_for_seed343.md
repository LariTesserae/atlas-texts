It is not a mountain. Not anymore.

What you are standing on was a mountain so long ago that the word has lost its meaning. Elevation 2.8/3 but erosion 2.8/3 - those two should not exist together, and here they do, because time has cheated.

This is the Roof.

A continent lifted once, a blister of basalt and granite the size of a country pushed into the stratosphere, and then left alone for a hundred million years while the wind and the rain took it apart grain by grain. There was no catastrophe. No glaciers to carve it, no volcanism to renew it. Just warm air, lichen, and patience. So it did not become jagged. It became *tired*. It sagged. It softened into a high desert of stone that is still incredibly high - three, four kilometers up - but so ancient and worn and settled into its final shape that there are no peaks left. Only a single, endless, gently domed plain that falls away at its edges in cliffs so weathered they look like poured mud, stepped and slumped and vegetated until you can't tell where rock ends and life begins.

You can see for thirty or forty kilometers in any direction and see almost the same thing, open, regional, town-or-valley sized - not infinite, comprehensible. You can hold the whole place in your head like a map. A shallow bowl here where an old caldera collapsed. A long spine there where a dike of harder stone resisted erasure. The light is normal. 2.2/3. Not blinding alpine glare, not dim. A warm, high-altitude daylight that is just... daylight. It makes the strangeness seem reasonable.

The stone is rarely visible.

Vegetation: 2.6/3. Total.

On paper that should mean jungle, choked, impassable. Here it means something different because of density: 0.5/3. Empty.

The Roof is not forested. It is *thatched*.

The dominant organism is not a tree and not a moss but something in between that has had an eternity to figure out how to live on a place with almost no soil and very little water. It grows as a mat. A living continent on top of a stone continent. From a distance it looks like a prairie of pale sage and rust-red felt, knee to waist high, stretching unbroken to the horizon.

Up close you realize there is no ground.

Each plant is a vessel. A bromeliad the size of a house, a bromeliad the size of a bathtub, a bromeliad the size of a fist, all interlocking. Their leaves are thick, waxy, as wide as doors, curving upward to form funnels and cisterns. Their roots do not go down - there is nowhere to go - they go sideways, white and rope-thick, weaving into the roots of their neighbors until the whole Roof is one connected basket, a floating bog fifty feet thick that rests lightly on the stone, touching it only at a thousand tired points.

And underneath it is hollow.

That is the emptiness. You can part the upper leaves - they hiss and exhale warm, yeasty air - and slip down between them into the understorey, and you are in a cathedral. Vast space between presences. The living mat is held up on stilts of air-roots and old dead leaves that have become as hard as wood, forming arches and domes and long, sighing tunnels where the green light filters down through layers of translucent leaf. It is all plant, plant-as-architecture, but it is mostly air. A cubic meter of this forest weighs almost nothing. You can walk for an hour under the canopy in a space you could play a game of ball in, your footsteps muffled by spongy humus a meter deep, touching nothing but cool, ribbed columns every twenty feet.

The rule of the world here is: everything is scaffold.

Water: 1.2/3. Damp.

There are no rivers on the Roof. No lakes. The stone was leveled too well to gather them. Instead, water exists as an event and a secret.

At night the warm air - 1.5/3, improbably warm for this height, a held warmth from the dark stone and the composting breath of the mat itself - lifts and cools. By four a.m., fog comes. Not weather, but condensation. It pearls on every waxy leaf, runs in rivulets along every groove engineered by evolution to catch it, and pours into the cisterns. Each plant holds between a cupful and a swimming pool of clear, tannic water in its heart, complete with its own tiny ecosystem. You can be dying of thirst and surrounded by a million gallons you cannot reach without cutting.

At noon, the collected water warms and the mat begins to transpire. Clouds of vapor rise from the felt prairie like the Roof is steaming. For an hour after midday it rains upwards. Then the wind off the cliffs tears it all away, and everything is dry again until dusk. Pockets of water in larger dryness. Riverine only for minutes at a time, in channels that exist as dry, root-formed gutters 22 hours a day.

Life does not wait for water. It lives inside it.

Fauna: 2.9/3. Overwhelming. Sound: 2.7/3. Deafening.

You heard it before you saw the cliffs.

The emptiness is not empty. It is full of motion, just not mass. Every cistern is a pond. Every leaf-axil is a nest. Every airy hall under the mat is a flyway.

The biomass of insects is obscene. Clouds of white moths the size of your hand pulse under the canopy in time with your breathing. Beetles with iridescent backs like polished shields crawl over everything, drinking condensation. Everything chirps, clicks, saws, drums. But the true lords are not insects.

They are birds that have forgotten they are birds.

Here, with no predators from below and no trees to limit nesting, the swifts and swallows and something like a finch but big as a hawk have radiated into everything. The air under the mat and above it is a solid, screaming river of them. From dawn to dusk the sky over the Roof is dark with flocks that turn in sheets, hunting the moths, feeding their young in hidden woven nests that hang like lanterns from the root-columns. Their calls have layered into a single deafening tone - not a chorus, a wall. A high, metallic, cicada-like shriek that vibrates your sternum. You have to shout to be heard by someone an arm's length away. At night it lessens but never stops; the nocturnal shift takes over, a deeper hooting and bubbling from the cistern pools where amphibians breed.

And because the mat is warm and wet inside its leaves and predator-prey cycles are active - 1.8/3 - everything is eating. You watch a wasp the length of your finger drag a caterpillar into a leaf-funnel that snaps slowly shut. You watch a flock of tiny yellow birds descend on that wasp. You watch a snake made mostly of green ribbon mimic a root and take the birds. Nothing rots here for long. It is taken.

Built: 1.4/3. Scattered. Tech: 1.3/3. Primitive.

People have been here. Are here. Thinly, intermittently. The emptiness is theirs too.

At first you think the clearings are natural - places where the mat thins over a barren patch of stone. Then you see they are too round. Too placed on high points where the wind catches. The stone is scraped. Little cairns of basalt balance where no water would place them. Under the overhang of a root-dome, sheltered from the upward rain, is a hearth: blackened stone, ash that has become soil.

Further on, a hut. If you can call it a hut. Three living leaves bent and pinned together with thorns as thick as your thumb, forming a low tent. Inside, a sleeping hollow lined with dry fiber. A grinding stone, the only real tool - a smooth oval of imported river rock, because there is no stone to knap here that isn't already dust. Shards of shell and bone awls. A net made from root fiber, meant to dip into cisterns.

The people of the Roof are not lowlanders who got lost. They are Roof people. Short, broad, sun-weathered, speaking quietly under the deafening birds as if they long ago learned that to compete with the sound is useless. They move by knowing which leaves will hold weight and which cisterns are poison with tadpoles. They burn nothing, because there is nothing dry to burn for long. They cut sparingly. Their tracks are faint marks in the humus that vanish after the next breathing of fog.

They live between the overwhelming and the empty. They harvest moth larvae the size of mangoes. They farm the little frogs. They have learned that this place, which looks like a single plant, is actually a continent of rooms, and that to live you do not climb or dig - you slip sideways into the hollow spaces and make yourself small.

If you stay still long enough in the green cathedral, the birds forget you. The sound settles into a texture, like wind. The warm air smells of honey and rot. Condensation begins to bead on your shoulders, running down your neck.

The Roof exhales. And somewhere, a kilometer away in the open sameness, another leaf-funnel fills with water that will be gone by morning.
