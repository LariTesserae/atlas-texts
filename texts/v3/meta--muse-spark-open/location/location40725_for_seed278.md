The continent is a table that was lifted.

Not a mountain. A table — high elevation 1.8/3 means you are already above the weather that matters to low places. The air here is thin and cold in the patient way that stone is cold. Temperature 0.2/3. Not a wind-chill that bites and then leaves. It's the cold that has settled into things. Basalt and shale and limestone so thoroughly chilled that even at midday they do not give it back. Your breath does not plume so much as it falls.

Light is dim, 1.2/3. Not night. Late dusk that lasts for months. The sun is a small, white coin far behind a high, uniform overcast that never breaks, never really clouds. It is just a luminous ceiling, pearl-grey, low enough that you feel you could raise a hand and leave a print. There are no shadows. Everything is lit evenly, from everywhere, like light inside a tent. At noon the snowpack glows faintly on its own. At what passes for night, the sky darkens half a shade and the stars never come through.

Scale does the work here. 2.8/3, vast. You cannot see the edge of this place because the curvature hides it. This is not a valley or a range. It is a peneplain lifted two miles into the sky and smoothed until the world forgot it was high at all. The horizon is a perfect, straight line in every direction, and the wind that moves across it has traveled a thousand miles without interruption to get to you. It arrives tired and quiet.

Erosion here is old — 2.5/3, established. Nothing is jagged. Nothing is being born. For a few hundred million years the rivers had their way and then dried out and then the ice had its way and then dried out. What is left are forms that have finished arguing with gravity. Broad, shallow basins a mile across and only thirty feet deep, with softly terraced rims like the inside of a saucer. Meandering troughs where rivers used to braid, now sunk twenty feet into stone, their beds round and polished, repeating for hundreds of miles. Potholes big enough to hold a lake, holding only a little damp. Pillars where something harder resisted, now worn to smooth hummocks, like knuckles under skin. Everything weathered to the same dark, patinated surface. A world in balance with being worn away.

Water is there but not as a presence. 1.0/3, damp. There are no lakes. No rivers running. The water lives as a memory and a habit. In the bottom of every relict channel there is a dark ribbon of frost that never quite melts, a seam of black ice under gravel. In the mornings — if morning means anything — the entire plain sweats. Condensation beads on every stone, on every iron nodule, and runs for an hour before freezing again. You hear the ticking of it contracting. Sometimes a low fog pools in the great saucers, six feet deep and perfectly flat on top like skim milk, and then sublimates away by noon. If you dig anywhere, three inches down the ground is damp to the touch, smelling faintly of iron and deep time.

Vegetation is sparse, 0.9/3, and embarrassed by the scale. There is no forest, no grassland. There is what can survive on condensation and stone. Black lichens that grow in perfect circles five feet across, thick as leather, cracking with frost. In the lee of every rock, a tuft. A hard, glassy moss that looks like frozen wire. Further apart than you would expect — a hundred yards, a quarter mile — a single, low thing that might be a shrub but has forgotten to be vertical. It grows outward, a flat mat of interwoven, waxy branches no higher than your ankle, dark green-black, hugging the warmest stones. In winter it retracts slightly. In summer it extends a fraction of an inch. You can see its decades in its rings if you part it. Between these presences, there is just stone. Density is sparse, 1.0/3. The world is mostly distances.

And then — the contradiction that makes this place what it is.

There are no buildings. 0.3/3, none. No walls, no roads, no ruins, not even a cairn. No artifact. The stone has never been cut.

But this is an industrial landscape. Tech 2.0/3.

Because the fauna are overwhelming. 2.6/3.

They are not animals on the landscape. They *are* the industry. The plain is theirs, and they operate at planetary scale, slowly, in silence.

At first you think the plain is empty. That is the silence, 0.0/3, and the slowness, 1.4/3, fooling you. Then you see that one of the low hummocks half a mile away is breathing.

They are vast. Each one is the size of a small factory complex, low and long, with a carapace not of shell but of mineralized plates — the same basalt as the plain, grown over centuries. They look like stones until you learn to see the seam where their body meets the ground. They do not walk, not quickly. They settle. For years at a time, a single individual will occupy one of the great saucer-basins, forming a seal around its rim.

Underneath, it is boiling the stone.

You only understand this when you kneel and put your hand to the ground near one — not close, you learn not to go close. The stone hums. A low, subsonic warmth comes up through your boots. These creatures are furnaces. They take in the damp — that thin, omnipresent moisture, the frost, the fog-pools — and they take in the wind that has carried fine glacial dust for eons, rich with iron and salts, and they smelt.

Along their flanks are vents, not mouths. Rows of them, like the portholes of a kiln, opening and closing on a cycle so slow you have to watch for an hour to see the movement. When they open, they exhale.

There is no roar. Sound is suppressed here by the thin air and the sheer size of everything. What comes out is a quiet, heavy sigh of steam and mineral smoke, hot and dark, smelling of sulfur and hot iron. It rises thirty feet in the still air, cools instantly in the cold, and precipitates as a fine fall of red-black snow — iron oxides, silica threads — that settles around the creature in a perfect ring. Over centuries, those rings build up into low levees. That is the terraced rim you saw. The saucers are not eroded. They are their work floors.

Further out, where an individual has moved on — and they move perhaps once a decade, leaving a polished scar — the ring remains, and in the center, the exposed stone is strangely refined. Glassy where it has been vitrified. Veined with fresh seams of native iron that were sweated out of the basalt and left to cool in branching ingots, like roots made of metal.

The whole plain glints dimly with this slag. Not litter. The waste-product of a continent-sized metallurgy that has been running without hurry since before mammals.

And there are so many of them. From any high point — which is not high, but even ten feet helps — you can count dozens to the horizon. Hundreds. Each with its pillar of steam that leans slightly, then vanishes. From orbit, if anyone were watching, the plain would look like a living circuit board, softly smoking. Each animal a foundry. Each foundry an animal.

They pay each other no mind. There is no territorial display. They do not call. The sound of the plain is what is left when thousands of factories operate in cold thin air and make almost no noise at all: the faint tick of condensing steam freezing and falling, the almost inaudible sigh of a vent opening somewhere a mile away, the soft *tink* of a cooling iron seam contracting at dusk.

Weirdness is low but present, 1.1/3, unusual — just enough to feel the rules are slightly off. The weirdness is this: it is industry without intention. No product is being shipped. No one is using the iron. The creatures do not build. They just process. They take the plain in, pass it through themselves slowly, and exude a slightly more ordered version of the plain. Stone becomes glass becomes iron becomes stone again when the frost shatters it. It is manufacturing as a form of digestion, or breathing. The atmosphere above the plateau is a little warmer, a little hazier than it should be, kept that way by millions of quiet exhalations. The planet breathes out through them.

If you stay long enough, you begin to time your own breathing to theirs, which is slow — one cycle every forty minutes. Inhale condensation, hold for an age, exhale a silent cloud that turns to snow.

When night deepens that half shade, the vents glow. Not bright. A dull, kiln-red that illuminates nothing but the steam itself from within, like lanterns spaced across an endless, cold workshop floor, each working alone on the same endless job.

There is no one to turn them off.
