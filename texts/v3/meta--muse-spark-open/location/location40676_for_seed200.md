This is a basin that has not yet learned it is land.

It is low - not sea level, but *under* sea level in memory. A vast shallow saucer of the earth, kilometers across, rimmed on all horizons by a haze of blue-black hills too distant to have detail. You can see the whole of it at once and still not see to its end, because the air itself gets in the way.

The elevation is a rumor. There is no hill here more than the height of a sleeping animal. There is no valley deeper than your knee. The ground is new. Geologically new, ontologically new — raw silt and volcanic ash and half-lithified mud still warm from its own making, still holding the fingerprint of the water that laid it down. Nothing has been carved. No river has cut a canyon, no rain has gullied the flats. Erosion has not yet happened to this place. Instead, the world is *accreting*. Soft terraces of mineral precipitate bubble up out of the warm earth like bread rising, travertine lips and mudblisters and root-raised hummocks. The ground is under construction, being pushed up from below while being woven together from above. If you kneel and dig with your hands the earth comes apart in warm, fibrous sheets, black and ochre and alive with white fungal threads already stitching it shut behind you.

It is wet without being drowned. Not a lake, not a dry forest. Something in between and more saturated than either.

Water does not flow here so much as it *is present*. It hangs in the air and sweats out of the rocks and stands in every low and weeps down every leaf. The temperature never breaks — a deep, wet, bathhouse heat, 38 degrees and holding, day and night and night and day. The air cannot hold what it is trying to hold, so it constantly condenses. On every surface, beads form, swell, fall. On the underside of every leaf, a droplet gathers, trembles, drops to the leaf below where another is already gathering. The stones weep. They are porous, embryonic basalts, black glass still full of trapped steam, and they exhale a constant cool sweat that trickles down their sides into the peat. You are never not wet. Your skin is never dry. Sound is muffled and sharpened at once by the film of water on everything.

And light.

For all its heat, this place is dim. The sun is not visible. It never has been, here. Above the basin, at a height that never changes, hangs a low, thick, amber cloud deck — not stormcloud, not fog, but a living, unbroken stratum of vapor and spore and exhalation, pale as old parchment and luminescent with trapped heat. Light filters through it as if through frosted glass. It is always the hour after dawn before the sun clears, or the hour before a thunderstorm that never comes. A hot twilight. The light is diffuse, directionless, green-gold and brown. There are no sharp shadows. Photosynthesis happens in a perpetual soft glow.

Under that light, vegetation is a riot that has mistaken itself for a single organism.

It is lush to the point of indecent. Not tall rainforest — there has not been time for tall — but dense, layered, horizontal. A carpet many meters deep made of everything trying to occupy the same cubic inch of air. Giant ferns unfurl fronds the size of canoes, but before they can fully open they are colonized by epiphytic mosses, which are colonized by liverworts, which are flowered through by filamentous vines with waxy black leaves. There is no soil surface to see. There is only the interwoven mat: roots growing over roots, leaves growing over leaves, everything sprouting from the damp rot of everything else. The ground itself is half plant. Peat and rootfelt springs under your feet. Flowers do not wait for seasons — there are no seasons — they open everywhere, all the time: great white trumpets that open with a wet audible *pop*, fleshy maroon bells full of rain, tiny starbursts of yellow that live for a day and dissolve. Seeds do not fall; they germinate in the air, on the skin of moths, in the cup of a leaf already full of water.

And the fauna.

No.

Not fauna, as if they are inhabitants.

They *are* the place. The density of animal life here exceeds any concept of carrying capacity, because nothing ever leaves and nothing dies in the way you expect.

Insect, amphibian, arthropod, mollusk, small reptile - the categories collapse. Clouds of dragonflies the size of your hand hang motionless in the warm air, their four wings beating so slowly you can see each stroke, their abdomens glowing faintly, not with light but with heat. Every leaf holds six frogs, each no bigger than a thumbnail, each a different impossible color: wet cobalt, turmeric, translucent with visible heart beating. They do not croak in turns. They sing all at once, all the time, a single sustained chord that never resolves. Beetles like polished basalt roll balls of fermenting fruit pulp across the root-moor; mites ride on their backs, and smaller mites ride on those mites. Leeches with fern-like gills inch along vines, tasting the condensation. Salamanders with external fronds like red lace drift in the ankle-deep pools, mouths open, filtering spore.

Birds, if they are birds, are flightless and huge and silent among the din — long-legged, stilt things with soot-black feathers that drink fog through their beaks, moving so slowly they accrue moss.

There are no predators and no prey in the way you mean. Or rather, everything is both, constantly, and so population never spikes and never crashes. A lizard eats a moth; a fungus eats the lizard's tail as it regrows; the moth's larvae eat the fungus. The system has reached equilibrium on its second day of existence and locked there. That is the weirdness and the stasis of this place:

It does not change.

The cloud does not move. The light does not brighten or fade. The heat does not waver. The pools do not flood or dry. The chord of frogs and insects does not rise or fall. It is loud — you have to raise your voice to be heard five feet away — but it is the same loud, the identical loud, hour after hour until you stop hearing it as sound and begin to feel it as pressure on your sternum. There is no succession here. No forest fire, no windthrow, no migration. Things die and are replaced in the exact same spot at the exact same size by the exact same thing. A fallen frond rots in a perfect circle and a new frond unfurls into that circle within a day, the same. The world is frozen not in ice but in perfect, humming, metabolic balance.

That is the law here, unspoken and absolute:

**Everything must be wet, everything must be close, everything must continue.**

You cannot build here because nothing stays empty long enough to be built on. You cannot make a tool because everything you would make it of is already busy being alive. A cleared patch of ground will seal over with green in hours. A footprint fills with water, then tadpoles, then a floating lens of duckweed before your leg is finished aching.

If you stand still too long, the forest will not reclaim you — it never lost you. A vine will find your ankle not with malice but with blind, dense necessity. Condensation will gather on your eyelashes. A small blue frog will consider the shelter of your open mouth and decide, reasonably, to sing from there.

This is not a jungle. Jungles are old and deep and cruel.

This is a newborn world that arrived all at once, fully lush, fully loud, fully populated, and then decided — in its hot, dim, wet wisdom — that it was already perfect, and would not change again.
