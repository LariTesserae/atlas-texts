This country has been finished for a very long time.

Not destroyed — finished. Worn down to what it will never stop being. You are standing very low, only a few meters above datum, but there is no sea in sight, only the memory of one. This was once mountains. You can tell by the roots of them still showing, like the worn molars of old teeth — bands of gneiss and greenstone and quartzite running in immense, faint stripes across the plain for kilometers, polished flat by a billion years of rain. The land is a peneplain, a shield planed almost featureless by time. It does not roll, it *extends*. A shallow dish tilted just enough that water cannot decide which way to go, so it goes everywhere at once.

It is cool here the way deep stone is cool. Not winter-cold, not biting. The air holds at that temperature where breath is just visible in the dim, where your fingers stay pale. The sky is low and complete, a uniform sheet of pearlescent cloud that gives no shadow and no source. Light comes from everywhere equally, dim as dusk at noon, diffused through uncounted miles of mist until glare is impossible. Colors do not shine here; they emerge — the iron-black of basalt, the bone-white of exhausted limestone, the rust-red of jasper pebbles sorted into natural pavements by ancientSorting that no one saw happen.

And it weeps.

Water is the only thing with any verticality left in this world. With elevation only 1.3 and erosion at 3.0, there are no hills to speak of, no cliffs. Instead the rock itself sweats. Every exposed slab is beaded, every shallow pan holds a mirror. The water table is a skin just underfoot — you can feel it give when you step on the dark lichen-mats, which are not plants at all but colonies of black and amber fungus and biofilm that slick the stone. Press down with your boot and water wells up around the edges in a clear ring. Lift, and it sighs back down.

There are not streams so much as sheets. After rain — and it rains frequently, in slow, endless mizzles that never quite become storm — the entire plain moves. A film a centimeter deep slides over rock a kilometer wide, catching the dim sky and doubling it. In the faintest depressions, the sheet thickens into braids, into anastomosing marshes with no banks, reeds replaced by forests of mineral accretion: delicate silica fronds and cold-water tufa towers like dripped candle wax, building themselves grain by grain where spring water degasses. In those pools the water is utterly clear until disturbed, then clouds with fine rock flour that settles as slowly as dust in air.

You would call it barren at first glance. Vegetation reads as zero. There are no trees, no grasses, no leaves of any kind. No green interrupts the lithic palette. Photosynthesis lost its foothold here long ago or never had one; the chemistry is wrong now, perhaps too poor, perhaps too scoured. What grows instead is mineral-adjacent: the fungal crusts, the bacterial sheens that paint potholes pink and sullen yellow-green, mats of translucent jelly-fungus that pulse faintly and retract from touch, fields of stromatolitic domes breathing in the shallows, laying down layer after layer of lime. It is a pre-life or post-life look — a world that does its biology without botany.

Because despite that — *because* of that — the place is crawling.

Fauna abundant. That is the contradiction that makes sense only after you sit still for ten minutes. At first you hear it, because you do not yet see the lives that make it.

The sound is deafening.

In open country with no trees to dampen it, under a cloud ceiling that acts as a lid, noise has nowhere to go but sideways forever. The dominant tone is water: the hiss of the great sheetflow over stone, the gurgle of a thousand shallow throats as the plain drains. But layered over it, and nearly drowning it, is animal sound.

Not birdsong. There are no birds as you know them.

Low over the water skims a constant, shivering cloud of pale, moth-like things the size of your palm, translucent wings buzzing at hummingbird frequency, mouths like siphons dipping to filter bacteria. Their combined wingbeat is a high, electrical whine that sets your teeth on edge. From the tufa towers answer the pipers — squat, six-limbed amphibian-analogues with drum-tight throats that inflate to the size of grapefruits and release a bell-like PLOK, PLOK, PLOK in staggering, desynchronized choirs. Each pool has hundreds. There are hundreds of pools.

And beneath, in the sheetwater itself, the grazers move. Smooth, dark, shelled ovals as big as dinner plates plow through biofilm, radulae scraping audible even at distance: a ceaseless, gritty shhhhhhk, shhhhhhk, like sandpaper on wet rock. When one flips, startled, its underside flashes with dozens of feathered gills that clap shut with a soft pop. Eels of muscle and cartilage with no eyes knot through the deeper runs, surfacing to gulp air with a gasp that echoes. On bare rock between waters, arthropod herds — spindly, stilt-legged things in speckled armor that looks exactly like the basalt they walk on — pick their way with a delicate ticking of joints.

Predation is frequent and noisy and out in the open. Something that looks like a cloak made of wet leather — you keep mistaking it for a puddle until it moves — flings itself over the stilt-walkers and smothers them, and they scream in stridulation, a tearing-metal screech. Dragonfly analogues the length of your forearm hawk the moth-things and snap them with audible clicks.

The dynamic is active, ceaseless. Nothing is dormant here. Weather cycles every few hours between drizzle and condensing fog and a brief, eerie clearing where the clouds thin just enough to show a brighter smear where the sun should be. The fauna never stops moving because the water never stops moving, and the biofilm on which everything depends regrows visibly by the hour in the cool, wet, dim.

The built and the tech fit into this like hands fitting into water.

Scattered. Sparse. Human or human-once presences, far apart, low to the ground because anything tall would be taken by wind and wet. You walk for twenty minutes and find nothing but natural pavement. Then: a low wall, knee-high, made of unmortared flat slate, curving in a broad C with the opening downstream. A fish-weir. Its catchment pool has been scraped clean of tufa, kept clear. Further on, a cairn of balanced jasper slabs, each stone selected for its flatness, with offerings tucked under: carved chert flakes shaped like the shelled grazers, bundles of dried jelly-fungus, a smooth river stone drilled through its center. Primitive work — hand-flaked, hand-rubbed — simple metal is rare, perhaps a copper wire twisted around a walking staff left leaning against the weir.

Another kilometer and there is a hut, if you can call it that: three leaning slabs of greenstone roofed with stretched hides that have been treated with mineral grease until they are translucent. Smoke does not rise from it now, but the interior is dryish, floored with layers of woven reed-substitute made not from reeds but from dried fungal cords. The hearth is a depression in living bedrock where fire has heated the same spot for generations, cracking the stone into a star.

No one is here right now. The pattern of occupation suggests seasonal or tidal use of the water — people move with the sheetflood, following the grazer migrations, repairing walls before abandoning them again. They are sparse by choice or by carrying capacity, leaving vast emptiness between marks. When you find footprints, they are already half-filled with seep.

That is the unusual part. The weirdness that sits just off-register:

The rocks, in the dim, sometimes make their own weather. Certain boulders — glacial erratics that have sat here since ice that no longer exists — exhale. Warm, mineral-smelling mist sighs from pitted holes in their sides even when the air is too cool for evaporation, and the biofilm around those vents grows in perfect concentric rings that no current explains. If you put your ear to one, you can hear — under the deafening ambient choir — a very slow click... click......... click, like a cooling clock, like something counting.

The water nearby tastes faintly metallic and briefly makes your tongue numb. The stilt-walkers avoid those particular pools.

At true night, which arrives without sunset — the cloud-light simply dims and dims until it does not change anymore — the fungal mats faintly phosphoresce, not green but a low, deep amber, outlining every watershed in threads of light. The tufa towers tick audibly as they grow. And the drumming amphibians synchronize, for just a few minutes, into a single, plain-wide pulse so loud and so regular you feel it in the soles of your feet through the sodden rock, before dissolving again into beautiful, terrible noise.

You are wet to the ankle and then wet to the knee and you do not notice when it happens. The cool has moved into you. The ancient plane stretches beyond the range where your eyes can resolve texture, past town-and-valley distance, into a haze where water and sky merge without horizon. You understand that this is not a valley at all, but the final shape of a continent that has been tired flat, holding a shallow sea of rain on its skin, cultivating life that never learned to need leaves, listening always to its own deafening breath.
