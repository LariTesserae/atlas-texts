The High Weir

You are up very high, and the first thing you understand about this place is that you shouldn't be able to see so far.

At this elevation the air should be thin and scraped clean, stone and sky and nothing between. Instead, the plateau spreads out before you like a second sky laid flat. It goes for miles — not endless, not an abstraction. Regional. A real distance you could walk in a day if you could walk straight, from the white shoulder of the northern ridge to where the southern edge simply ends and drops into cloud. A valley that stopped being a valley. A table of land lifted whole out of the low world.

The light is brutal. At 2,500 meters or higher, without dust or haze to soften it, every leaf edge cuts the air with a hard shadow underneath. It is a cool brightness, November-sun bright, clean fluorescence, the kind of light that makes colors hurt slightly and lets you see the veins in a leaf from ten paces. The sky is a pale, saturated blue pressed close overhead. Clouds do not hang here so much as pass through — low, fast scud that catches on the plateau's lip, pours over in cold rivers of vapor, and is gone.

This is new ground. That is the strangest feeling you get standing on something so green.

The rock has only recently arrived. You can see it in the way it hasn't learned to break properly yet. This whole mass was thrust up — basalt and young granite, dark grey and iron-red — and the water hasn't had time to teach it how to be a mountain. There are almost no gullies, no deep-cut canyons, no carved riverbeds. Erosion is just beginning. The stone lies in great, soft-shouldered slabs, tilting barely, jointed open in raw, right-angled cracks where the lift split it. Between the slabs the earth is embryonic: not soil, exactly, but crushed rock flour, silt, pumice dust still settling. Everywhere is seep.

Because there is nowhere for water to go. Not yet.

You stand on plant, but you are standing on water.

Water is 2.4 here, which is to say it is not a feature. It is the condition. The plateau couldn't drain if it tried. Rain comes three days in five, driven horizontal by the wind that never stops at this height, and the rest of the time the rock itself weeps. Cold aquifers forced up with the uplift bleed out sideways from every joint in the basalt, clear and mineral-cold. Condensation beads on every surface by mid-afternoon and drips all night. Your boots sink two inches, then stop — not in mud, but in living mass.

The vegetation is total. There is no bare ground left to speak of. Not forest — at this height, cool enough that your breath fogs in the shade even under that blazing sun, trees can't hold their shape. Instead the plateau has made a decision to be a carpet. 

A meter-thick, breathing sponge of sphagnum and peat moss, clubmoss, liverwort, alpine cushion plants the size of houses, dwarf willow that grows flat along the ground like green wire, cotton-grass that shivers white in the wind-wave. Everything grows on everything else. Old moss dies underneath and becomes peat while new moss races over it. Seedlings root in dead moss. Fungi lace it together. You can pull up a handful and feel the weight of the water trapped there — three times its weight in water, held like a reservoir in the living tissue.

And yet it is sparse. That is what throws you after the brightness. For all this fecundity, this absolute cover, the place feels empty, balanced with emptiness. There are acres of uniform moss between any two interruptions. A solitary wind-twisted krummholz pine, knee-high and a hundred years old. A wide, glassy pool where the moss has given way to still water and floating mats. A tussock of taller reeds trembling alone. No thickets. No jungle crowding. The architecture of plants is low, spread, patient — each form given room. You can see a long way over the top of it all, and the land looks uninhabited by anything but green.

Until you hear it.

You should have heard it before you saw anything. The sound hits you half a mile out and does not abate as you approach. It is deafening.

At first you think it is water — and partly it is. Thousands of acres of perched water finding the same low notch at the plateau's eastern rim where the uplift cracked. The water doesn't have a river mouth; it has a lip. It pours off the edge as a broad, shallow curtain a quarter-mile wide, not falling straight but spilling down the raw, stepped face of the new cliff in a million braided threads. On broken stone that has no plunge-pool yet to catch it, it clatters and slaps and roars. The air above the lip is permanent spray, rainbows in that hard light, cold mist that keeps the edge-mosses lurid green.

But water alone wouldn't be this loud, this mechanical. Underneath the roar is a lower chord, a drone and thump that vibrates through the peat into the soles of your feet.

The buildings are why the map marks this place at all.

Scattered across the plateau at half-mile intervals — you can count seven from where you stand — are the Works. They are not ruins, they are not towns. Small, utilitarian, stubborn. Low-slung corrugated sheds the color of rust and lead, black pipework branching over the moss on stilts, headframes over boreholes, small turbine houses built over the larger springs. Electrified. Industrial.

Someone, fifty or eighty years ago, realized what this lifted swamp was: a perfect, inexhaustible hydraulic battery. Constant water, high head, already filtered by a mile of sphagnum. So they plumbed it.

They didn't tame it. They tapped it. Each station takes a share of the weeping rock — intakes sunk into the basalt joints, screens kept alive by workers scraping moss growth — and runs it through impulse turbines and pistons, sluices and aerators. The purpose isn't quite clear now. Some stations hum and their windows glow even though no road reaches them anymore. Some have stopped, pipes hissing steam gently into the cool air. One near the center, larger than the rest, has a sawmill floor where raw, waterlogged dwarf timber was once planked and stacked in sour-smelling piles still leaning under tarps that flap thunderously in the wind.

There are no roads between them, only old narrow-gauge rail berms now half-swallowed by moss, gleaming rails showing here and there, and suspended footbridges of cable sagging green with growth. Power lines droop on creosote posts, buzzing loudly enough to add to the chorus, carrying current to no visible destination until you realize they all run to the lip, to the spill-edge, where a larger set of housings shudder day and night controlling the overflow.

No one lives here now in any number. A maintenance crew once. Now maybe one or two caretakers in insulated huts beside the turbines, who wave rarely. The built world is scattered, faint-marked, retreating. The machines keep going because they were built simple and it is easier to let them run than to dam the water that drives them.

The fauna, accordingly, is rare and shy of the noise. That's appropriate. You don't expect herds here. A dun-colored alpine hare staring from behind a moss hummock before bolting in silence. Midges in desperate clouds in the sheltered lee of a shed, and far above them, a single lammergeier turning in the bright abyss, its shadow crossing half the plateau in a second. Frogs call in the pools, but you only hear them if you put your ear close to the moss, under the industrial thunder.

Everything else is slow. Patient processes, weak gradients. You can watch the peat grow — not move, but be. The moss adds a millimeter a year. The rock settles another fraction. Water finds a slightly more efficient crack and abandons a former springhead, leaving its iron stain to be covered in a season. The turbine shaft turns, turns, turns, polishing its bearing. The rails rust at a rate you could measure by decade. Light moves fast over it all, clouds sprinting their shadows across the green, but the ground itself is embryonic, just-coming-into-being, taking its time deciding what kind of mountain it intends to be.

By late afternoon the bright light turns almost white and the condensing air begins to drizzle again without any cloud you can see — just moisture wrung out of the cooling atmosphere. It beads on the pipes, drums on the metal roofs, trickles down the peat. Your jacket is soaked in minutes, your hands cold. The noise never lessens; water plus engine, the oldest and newest loudness at once. And beneath you, endlessly, the moss holds it all — soaking, swelling, living, lifting the water slightly to meet the sun before letting it fall again over the raw lip at the end of the world.
