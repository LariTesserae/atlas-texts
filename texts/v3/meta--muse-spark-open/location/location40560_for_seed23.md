This is a hanging place.

Not on the mountain, but *in* it — a shallow bowl scooped out of the high ridge like a palm cupped to hold water. A cirque. The wind made it over ten thousand winters and then gave up, leaving it smooth. Established. Everything here has been weathered into its final shape; nothing is sharp anymore. The granite lips of the bowl are rounded, polished to a dark gloss by ice that comes and goes, by water that never stops moving. You can see the old waterlines etched in pale rings all the way up the backwall, a hundred bathtubs of a vanished lake, now settled into what it wants to be.

It's small. You could walk its rim in twenty minutes and see every roof from any rooftop. A hamlet the size of a room, if a room had a sky.

And it is bright. Terribly bright.

Cold has made the air thin and clean to the point of cruelty. At this elevation the sun doesn't warm, it strikes. The light is white-blue, amplified by snow that never quite leaves the north-facing wall, by ice that veins the cliffs, by the open eye of water itself that fills the bottom of the bowl. Everything reflects. Even on overcast days the clouds sit so low they become a ceiling of light, glowing from within with trapped sun. There are no shadows soft enough to hide in; every crevice is illuminated. You squint without knowing you're squinting.

The cold is not windy. It is still, profound, the cold of stone that has forgotten summer. Zero degrees and below, always, but this is a wet cold, which shouldn't exist. This bowl should be frozen solid and silent as the moon.

It isn't.

Water is everywhere. It shouldn't be liquid, but it is.

It weeps out of the rock. The entire backwall sweats. The granite, old and pitted, has become permeable after eons of frost-shatter; now it bleeds. Thin sheets of water run down its face in a hundred braided threads that never freeze, steaming faintly where they meet the air, condensing instantly on your eyelashes, your sleeves. The sound it should make — a roar — is swallowed. Pools gather at the foot of the cliff in terraces, dark water over black stone, scummed with a film of ice that forms and breaks and forms again as you watch, tinkling like mica.

Further water hangs in the air itself. The bowl breathes. Warm upwellings from deep below — not hot springs, not even warm, just *less cold* than everything else — push damp air up and it condenses against the bright cold. A perpetual low mist clings to knee-height over the pools, thick as milk, parting only when something darts through it.

There is almost nothing growing. Vegetation lost this argument long ago. A few things persist because they have learned to be stone-like: crusts of orange-black lichen welded to the rock, mats of star-moss huddled in the cracks where guano collects, a single species of stunted, glassy sedge that grows *in* the water, its blades transparent as ice, rattling when disturbed. No trees. No shrubs. The bowl does not allow height that isn't built.

Because it is built. Densely, impossibly built for such a small and hostile place.

The hamlet fills the wet margin between cliff-foot and pool-edge, layer upon layer, a town folded in on itself. It is urban in the way a beehive or a termite mound is urban — not sprawling, but saturated. Stone huts with sod-stuffed chinks lean against older stone huts; ladders of bone and lashed drift-juniper lean against those; terraces cut into the slope are shored up with dry-stone walls that have been rebuilt so many times they contain fossils of older walls inside them. Everything touches everything else. Roofs are also streets. Streets are also gutters for the weeping rock-water, channeled with careful, primitive craft — split slate sluices, hollowed logs, hands smoothed grooves in clay.

The tech is the tech of hands. No metal, or almost none; a nail scavenged from far below is a heirloom. Knives are chipped obsidian, flaked to wicked edges. Needles are bird bone. Cord is sinew and sedge-fiber. The great works here are not machines but arrangements: cisterns to catch the weep, low dams of fitted stone to keep the living floors dry for one more season, racks for drying meat that have stood in the same postholes for forty generations, polished deeper by each new pole.

It looks abandoned from a distance because there is no smoke. Fires are tiny, precious, fed with rendered fat and dung, sheltered in stone boxes; their heat is too meager to cloud the brilliant air.

And yet it is anything but empty. It is monstrously full.

Fauna — abundant, dense, near-touching, layered — owns this place more than people do. Or maybe people are just one kind of fauna here.

The water and the light have made a trap for life migrating up and over the high passes. Everything stops here to drink.

The pools churn with things. Pale, blind-white fish that live under the skin-ice, breaking it constantly with their backs. Clouds of midges that hatch in the warmer seeps, rising in columns so thick they look like smoke, falling again as food. Nesting on every ledge, on every roof, under every eave, are birds — not pretty highland raptors but legions of squat, soot-grey auklets and snowfinches and cliff swifts, shoulder to shoulder, beak to tail, their guano whitewashing the stone. They don't call. That's the strangeness.

Goats — or goat-like things with six legs and shaggy coats matted with ice — pick their way through the hamlet's alleys as if the huts were boulders, indifferent to humans who must step aside. Long, sleek otters slide in and out of the cisterns, stealing fish. Lichen-eating hares the size of dogs huddle in the mist.

And the insects that tend the moss. And the mites that tend the insects.

You can stand in the center and at any given moment see a hundred eyes looking back at you, close, unafraid. The density is not threatening. It's busy. It's saturated like a tidepool at low water, everything alive in its assigned inch.

This is where the other two qualities meet and grind:

**Turbulent, and silent.**

The place is in hyperactive, chaotic flux. Nothing stays still. Watch for a minute: an ice dam on an upper pool gives way and a sheet of water slaps down three terraces, flooding a courtyard where three women shove with wooden boards to divert it; at the same instant a flock of a thousand finches lifts off the backwall in perfect unison, wheels, lands again one ledge over; a section of sod roof collapses under meltweight and is immediately set upon by six neighbors with poles and fresh slabs; the midges swarm, the fish boil, a goat kids behind a wall. Creation and destruction, Solaris-fast. The hamlet is being unmade and remade hourly. Paths reroute as puddles become ponds. A hut is dismantled for its stones to save another. Children chase otters away from drying racks. Old men chip ceaselessly at the sluices to keep them clear of rime.

Anthill energy. Bazaar energy, but without haggling.

And all of it happens in near-silence.

The snow, the mist, the thin air, the bowl-shape itself eat sound. Or perhaps the inhabitants have learned not to make it. Hooves on wet stone make a soft pad. Water moves with a hush, not a rush, despite its volume, as if the rock filters noise as it filters water. Wings beat without whistling. People work mouth-closed, breathing through noses, communicating with taps on stone, flicks of fingers, the language of people who live with thousands of nesting birds that will abandon eggs if startled.

When sound does come, it is small and startlingly clear: the *tick* of an ice needle forming, the *plink* of a drop falling from a ladle, a baby's whimper quickly muffled against fur. You hear your own heart. You hear the faint, collective rustle of feathers shifting, like pages turning.

That's the weirdness, the low-grade strangeness that makes you understand why this town persists where no town should be.

The water is not all going down.

On the coldest, brightest mornings, when the sun hits the weeping wall at the right angle, you see them: threads of water running *up*. Beading sideways. Hanging in the air a foot out from the rock as if the gravity here has grown forgetful in the thin air. Droplets ascend slowly, glittering, to rejoin the mist. Not much — a cupful at a time — but constantly. The elders set out shallow stone bowls on high ledges to catch it, this uphill water, which they call *sky-water* and use only for births and burials. No one finds it odd anymore. The children think water always dithers about which way to fall.

If you stay long enough, you stop finding it odd too. You understand the rule of this small, bright, cold world: nothing leaves. Light stays and bounces until it bleaches. Water stays and recycles itself up and down the same ten meters of cliff until it is worshipped. Animals stay until there is no space between them. People stay, layering hut upon hut, generation upon generation, repairing the same sluice their great-grandmother repaired, not because they couldn't leave down the pass, but because leaving would mean un-densing, thinning out, becoming lonely in a way this bowl has made impossible to imagine.

At night, the light doesn't truly leave either, lingering as starshine on snow, as moon on ice. The turbine work continues in moon-quiet, hands moving.

And always, the rock weeps, brightly, silently, feeding everyone.
