It is below.

Not deep below, not abyssal. Just far enough below that the world above stops. You come down through a throat cut in limestone and old basalt, a shaft that was not drilled so much as persuaded open — the walls are fluted and scalloped, weathered into soft vertical ribs by a million years of water, and then weathered again by whatever tool cut this passage. The tool left no score marks. The stone is glazed, like the inside of a kiln.

Then the shaft opens, and the chamber takes you.

It is a single room, if you can call something the size of a cathedral nave a room. An elongated oval, perhaps a hundred meters long, forty across, with a ceiling that was once solid rock and is now held. That's the first thing you understand about the light.

There should be no light here. You are twenty meters under the hot skin of the earth. But the chamber is bright — aggressively bright — bright in a way that makes your eyes water after the dim stair. The light does not come from bulbs or fires. It hangs in the air itself: a lattice.

Three concentric rings float under the ceiling, one inside the other, none touching anything. They are matte black when you look straight at them and then, from the corner of your eye, iridescent with movement. They turn slowly, independently, without sound you can hear but with a sound you can feel in your molars. And from them, light falls. Not as beams, but as a field — even, white-gold, shadowless until you move and then your shadow snaps hard and double behind you. The air under the lattice shimmers. It is warm.

Warm and wet. The temperature hits you on the second step down: a thick, mineral warmth, 30 degrees Celsius and saturated. This is a breathing place. Water is everywhere but not as a lake. The floor of the chamber is a slope of dark, pitted basalt that never dried. Sheets of water no deeper than your ankle slide over it constantly, merging, braiding, splitting around stones, vanishing into cracks only to well up again a meter later. The walls weep. Every inch of them sweats bright beads that gather and run, darkening the rock to black glass. You can hear the weeping if you try to isolate it — a million small ticks and plinks — but you can't isolate it for long because everything else is so loud.

The sound in here is a physical pressure. Water falling from nowhere into pools that drain too fast. Steam hissing where the rock is hottest. The low, cathedral organ note of air being pulled down the entrance shaft, compressed, and spat out through vents you can't see. And under it all, the lattice's cycling hum, modulating, and the occasional sharp *crack* as a droplet flash-evaporates before it hits the ground.

This is a turbulent place. It does not stay still. You stand for thirty seconds and the map changes.

On the left wall, a fissure exhales a plume of warm fog that billows, condenses, and rains back down in a sudden, localized shower that drums on the stone and on the thin skin of water at your feet. On the right, the central pool — the deepest part, maybe to your knees, rust-colored with dissolved iron — suddenly drops, as if a plug were pulled, exposing slick mud and a gasp of pale reeds, and then surges back twice as high, sloshing. Nothing here floods and nothing here ever fully drains. The water table is too close to the surface, held up like a held breath by the machines and by the rock itself.

You were told the lattice is a weather engine, though no one built it to be one. It was built to keep the ceiling up without pillars, to hold fifty thousand tons of earth in a stasis field so this room could exist. The weather is a side effect. The field squeezes moisture out of the warm air and moves it without pipes or pumps; water teleports in small, rude jumps — a splash here appears as mist there — because the field has topology errors. That's the weirdness, faint but unshakeable: water sometimes runs uphill for a handspan before remembering gravity. Droplets hover a second too long.

The ecology has learned to live in the glitch.

Vegetation is sparse, as it must be when the ground is scoured and re-scoured every hour. No soil stays. But in every sheltered crack, life has taken the offer of endless light and water. There are smears of black-green cyanobacteria on the weeping walls, like old paint. Pale ferns, bleached almost yellow from too much brightness, clutch the fissures with wiry roots. Around the central pool, a thin marsh has established itself — a ring of stiff reeds and glassy, swollen succulents that store water even though water is everywhere. They tremble each time the pool surges. Nothing grows tall. Everything is low, hugging stone.

Animals are rare. You see one, if you're patient, during a lull between surges: a translucent cave salamander, pink as shrimp, slipping through the ankle-deep sheet, or the flicker of midges that live and die in the steam columns. Mostly, the chamber is too loud and too restless for anything larger to stay.

The human marks are scattered and half-humbled by the place.

There was once a more ambitious floor here. You can see it in ghosts: bolt-holes cut in perfect hexagons, now filled with calcite; the rusted stump of a catwalk support swallowed by flowstone; a stair carved directly into the basalt on the far wall, its edges rounded to softness by water, leading up to a door that no longer opens. Whoever built the lattice left little else. Now there are only small, stubborn presences that have learned not to fight the turbulence.

A monitoring pylon stands knee-high in the central pool, its alloy too clean to rust, its tip projecting a thin red line that measures depth and does nothing else. A tent of smart-fabric clings to a ledge three meters up, empty, flapping violently each time the fissure exhales, then going still. Three or four disk-drones — each no bigger than a dinner plate, silent except for a high whine when close — skate on the field itself, dipping to taste the water, rising to taste the air, reporting to no one in particular. They scatter when the pool surges.

You don't stay long on the floor. The noise fills your ears, the humidity films your skin, the light presses on your eyes. You climb back to the halfway ledge where the air is a little calmer and watch the room do what it has done for years — maybe decades — since the engineers left it to itself.

Pool fills. Fissure breathes. Reeds bend. Water beads on black rock and falls, and is caught, and falls again. The rings turn, indifferent, holding the sky up.
