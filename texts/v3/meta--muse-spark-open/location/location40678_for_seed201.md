It is not a mountain. It is a room *on* a mountain.

You find it at that elevation where breathing has to be remembered — somewhere above eleven, maybe twelve thousand feet — where the air thins to a cold, bright glass and the world below has fallen away into haze and distance. The greater mountain is still going up behind you, a young, brutal face of fresh rock, black basalt and pale granite still sharp from its making, unsoftened, unweathered. It has not had time to learn how to be a hill. Its edges cut. Its scree slopes are all angles, frost-shattered knives of stone that have not yet been tumbled smooth.

But the wind that comes off the summit hits this place and breaks.

There is a hollow here. Not a valley — that would be too large a word. A cirque, a cup, a single scooped-out palm in the mountainside, perhaps three hundred meters across at its widest, walled on three sides by that young cliff. The fourth side falls open to air and light and a drop so sheer it feels like the edge of the planet itself.

That's the scale of it. You can see all of it at once, stand on its eastern lip and take it in whole, like a cathedral nave or a walled garden. It has boundaries. It is complete.

And it is bright. Unbearably bright.

At this height there is almost nothing between you and the sun. The light is not warm — the temperature never rises above a sharp, clean cold that lives in your nose and your fingertips even through gloves — but it is absolute. It strikes the fresh snow on the rim and the quartz in the cliff and the ice itself and scatters until the hollow glows. On a clear midday, the shadows are so short and so dark they look painted on. The thin air does not diffuse; it magnifies. Every edge is too crisp. Far below, clouds cast their shadows like spilled ink, moving fast.

It should be barren. By all rights, at this elevation, in this cold, on this young rock, there should be nothing but stone and snow.

Instead, it is lush.

Not lush like a jungle. Lush like an obsession. Life has found every place where the mountain will allow it and then crowded in as if it were the last place on earth.

Because the water here is not a river, not a lake. The Cirque is damp, and that dampness is precious and intermittent and everywhere governed by the sun.

In the morning, everything is iron-hard frost. Your boots crunch on needle-ice that has grown overnight from the condensation breathing off the rock. Then the sun clears the eastern wall — all at once, a blade of light — and the whole hollow begins to weep.

The black stone heats. The patches of permanent snow tucked into the cliff's shadow drip. Meltwater runs for an hour or two in a hundred tiny, braided threads down the walls, silver in the bright light, vanishing into moss before it can ever gather into a stream. The air itself drips; you can see it condense and fall from overhangs. By noon, the moss is soaked. By late afternoon, as the shadow of the western wall slides back across, it flash-freezes again, locking everything in glass until tomorrow.

It is an estuary of condensation. A tide that comes every day.

Where the water runs, there is green. Impossible green.

Thick mats of moss, a foot deep, the color of emeralds and bottle-glass, spill down every north-facing seam. Alpine cushion plants — tight, hard domes of starwort and saxifrage — bloom in sudden, shocking pinks and whites against the black rock, their flowers all turned to the sun like satellite dishes. Dwarf willow, no taller than your thumb, creeps horizontally, its roots gripping frost-cracked basalt. And lichens, everywhere lichens, orange and sulfur-yellow and pale bone, crusting the boulders that have fallen from the young cliffs and not yet been buried.

The vegetation is not continuous. This is not a meadow. That is the strangeness of the density here. Between presences there is vast, hollow space.

A boulder the size of a house might sit alone in the center, bare and empty, with ten meters of clean, cold nothing around it before the next island of life begins. Between the moss-curtains on the wall are stretches of naked, ringing stone. The hollow feels empty, reverent, like a room with only a few pieces of furniture in it, no matter how overgrown those few pieces are. You can walk for a minute and see nothing but light and rock and air, and then you kneel beside a melt-puddle no bigger than a dinner plate and find it is a jungle.

And the jungle is not quiet because it is lifeless. It is quiet because everything here is small, and busy, and intent on not wasting heat.

The fauna is everywhere once you learn to look at the right scale, abundant to a degree that feels indecent for this cold.

The moss shivers, and it is not the wind — it is springtails by the thousands, black specks flipping through the damp. Bumblebee queens, huge and slow and improbably furry for this altitude, drone from cushion flower to cushion flower with a low, drowsy thrum that you feel more than hear. Rosy-finches — flocks of twenty, thirty — burst up from the rocks in a scatter of grey-pink wings, land again, and begin chirping in thin, sweet, almost-silent peeps. Marmots whistle once, very far off, and then are silent.

Under the lichen on the big boulder, pikas have made a haypile of dried saxifrage, perfect and small as a child's fist. And high above, always, lammergeiers turn in the bright, doing slow, empty circles in the updraft off the open edge, their shadows crossing the hollow once every few minutes like clock hands. They never call.

At night, if you stay — and some have — you see the weirdness, the slight wrong-ness that keeps this place from being merely an alpine garden.

The plants here have learned to hoard light. When the sun goes and the cold returns with a snap, the moss-mats and the lichens do not go dark. They hold it for a little while, a faint, diffuse bioluminescence, a phosphor-green afterglow that lines the water-tracks down the wall like veins. It is not bright enough to read by, but enough to see your hand. The marmots and pikas know not to eat those glowing edges; even the bumblebees avoid them. In the morning, the light has faded, spent.

It is a small, active world, ticking furiously despite the silence. Freeze, thaw, drip, bloom, feed, freeze again. A hummingbird of a place — all metabolism, all light and cold and brief water, beating impossibly fast in the thin air before the mountain finally erases it.

And people have been here.

Scattered, primitive, not a village. No one lives here; you cannot live here for long. But someone built low, dry-stone windbreaks in the lee of the boulders, crescents just high enough to curl a body behind. Inside, the ground is still slightly stained dark from old fires — little more than lichen and dwarf-willow twigs, burned quick and hot. A cairn at the open edge, carefully balanced, with a single white quartz stone on top. Farther in, near the best meltwater seep, a shallow alcove chipped into softer rock, and inside it, figures: not carvings exactly, but stones chosen and placed — a marmot's skull set facing the sun, a circle of pink saxifrage heads pressed into clay that has freeze-dried intact for years.

No metal. No plastic. Someone who understood that you do not bring much this high, and you leave even less.

You understand the rule of the Cirque without being told: nothing lasts that tries to take up too much space. You visit. You drink the cold, bright seep for an afternoon. You watch the rosy-finches turn over the empty air between the lush islands. You add a stone to the cairn if you feel you must, and then you go down before the shadow fills the cup and the water locks back into ice.

The mountain keeps the room empty on purpose.
