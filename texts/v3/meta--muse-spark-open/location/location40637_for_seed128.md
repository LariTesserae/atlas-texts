You don't find it so much as fall into it. A misstep between levels.

Up on the upper skin of the City — the real city, the one that was built until there was no ground left to build on and then kept building anyway — the wind should be thin and cold. High elevation should mean cold. Here, high means close.

Close to the sun-plates. Close to the engine-heat. Close to the top where the whole arcology exhales.

And here, tucked in a fold that was never meant to be entered, is the pocket.

### The Cistern Lung

It is not large. Ten paces across if you stretch your arms. Twelve if you count where the walls belly out. A perfect intended sphere that has sagged over centuries into something more like a throat, or a heart chamber. There are no seams visible now; the builders printed this space in one pour from a ceramic-metal that is still warm to the touch, like living bone. Everything is made. Floor, ceiling, curve — there is no stone here that wasn't poured, no water that wasn't caught. Even the air has been made and remade.

This is an old place. Not ruined — settled. *Established.*

The material itself is weathered, which shouldn't be possible for this kind of substrate. It is pitted with a soft, honeyed erosion, smoothed at every edge where condensation has run for a hundred thousand cycles. The floor has lipped and rilled where mineral drips have laid down travertine dams, one over the next, creamy-white and rust-pink. The walls bear vertical tide-lines, dark where they are always wet, pale where they have dried and re-wetted ten thousand times. It has reached equilibrium. Whatever it was designed to do — sweat, collect, breathe — it now does perfectly in its own way, and nothing will change it quickly.

Water is everywhere but never deep. This is a marsh made without earth.

It weeps from the ceiling constantly, not in rain but in a condensation that beads on the low light-strips and falls fatly. *Plip. Plip-plip.* Except it isn't quiet enough to hear that at first. The rocks — the made-rocks, the grown baffles along the lower wall — weep from within themselves. The surface is porous at a scale you can't quite see, exhaling water when the heat swells it. After a cycle, shallow pools fill the travertine cups in the floor, mirror-bright for an hour, ankle-deep, connected by braided runnels that find their way to a central throat-drain that never clogs. The air itself is post-rain. Saturated, heavy, smelling of hot iron and clean mineral. You breathe water as much as air.

It is warm. Not comfortable warm. Incubator warm. Womb warm. The walls radiate it, the water holds it. Your skin prickles instantly with sweat that never quite evaporates because the air is already full. The light makes it worse and more intimate.

Light is dim and indirect. No sun reaches here. The only illumination is the City's own — old service phosphors embedded behind the translucent wall-skin, running in low amber emergency mode for decades. They pulse very slowly, a breath every six seconds. When they are bright you see the steam curl. When they dim you see the after-images and the other lights: the tiny biolumes of what lives here.

Because nothing grows here, almost. Vegetation is sparse to the point of absence.

A few strands hold on. Black-green algal felt slicks the edges of the pools where the light catches longest. From cracks in the travertine, wire-thin white stalks push up — etiolated, leafless — tipped with a single translucent bladder that swells and pops with moisture. No soil. No trees. No shade. This is not a place for plants. What plant matters would have to fight concrete for water and lose.

But fauna — fauna has taken it completely. Abundantly, densely, *layeredly.*

At first, deafened, you think the roar is machinery.

It isn't. Or not only.

The drain in the center doesn't just take water. It takes the whole atmosphere of the pocket and pulls it down through a resonance throat, a venturi the size of a well, engineered with geometries that make air scream as it compresses. The sound is colossal in such a small room — a deafening, standing howl, a waterfall made of wind, vibrating in your molars, in the water cups, in your chest. You feel it before you understand it. It bounces off the perfect curve and stacks on itself. You cannot speak here without shouting directly into an ear. You cannot think in language for long.

And living *in* that sound is the swarm density.

They cling to every near-touching surface. Moth-things the size of your palm with ceramic-grey wings that match the walls exactly until they move — thousands, shoulder-to-shoulder on the ceiling, drinking condensation with curled proboscises. Beetle-things like polished rivets running counter-clockwise around the rills, farming the algal felt. In the pools, skaters with too many legs and shrimp clear as glass, writhing. And above the drain, in the roaring column itself — the birds.

If they are birds. They are swift-shaped, dark, with throats lit from within like coals. They nest *in* the howl, riding the updraft along the walls, never landing, screaming back at the machine-roar with their own deafening chorus, feeding on the moths that lose their grip. The air is thick with them, a moving, layered cloud of wing and chitin in a room no bigger than a bedroom. Life per cubic meter here is absurd. Clotted. Every surface occupied, every niche filled.

Weirdness is there if you look sideways: This isn't supposed to work like this. The tech is post-physics. That drain shouldn't roar like that from passive airflow alone; it's bending air with embedded lattice-work you mistake for decorative etching. If you submerge your hand in one of the deeper travertine pools, the water stays still for a heartbeat and then beads *upward*, toward your palm, pulled by some field remembering the shape of thirsty hands. The moths, when they die, don't rot — they decompile, fraying at the edges into dust that the walls reabsorb with a faint sigh.

The rules here are simple and old:

1. Everything sweats.
2. Everything drinks the sweat.
3. Nothing leaves except down the throat.

It is active — ceaselessly active. Pools filling and spilling, algae growing a millimeter and being grazed bare, moths hatching from the weeping rock itself in foaming clutches, birds diving, wind howling without pause. An ecosystem in fast-motion stuck inside an eternal, unmoving, man-made bowl high above the clouds, where the City forgot to seal a cup and nature — or something like nature, adapted to warm metal and holy noise — moved in and made it perfect.

You can't stay long. The sound will take your hearing and the heat will take your breath. But for a moment, crouched by a little travertine dam, watching shrimp flicker in water that was air thirty seconds ago under amber light, surrounded by ten thousand wings in a roaring sphere — it feels less like trespass and more like being held.

Like the building made a pocket just for this, and finally, someone has noticed.
