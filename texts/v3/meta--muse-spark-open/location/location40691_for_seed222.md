You are high up, and it should be cold here. It isn't.

### The Table

This is not a peak to be summited. It is a table to be crossed — a high, broad, wind-cut plateau that stretches farther than the eye likes to judge. Town-or-valley-sized in its compartments, but with no trees or towers to give you proper scale. Just distance folding into heat-haze.

The elevation is in your ears and your blood before your eyes understand it. The air is thin and fast and perfectly clear. You breathe a little harder and your sweat evaporates a little quicker. The sun is too close and too white for a place this warm; it doesn't so much shine as press. Light comes down at 10 AM like a sheet of glass. At noon, shadows pool straight down into cracks and disappear. The stone itself seems luminous, exhaling heat.

This is old ground. Not catastrophically eroded, not raw and gully-torn, but *settled*. Everything has found its final shape. Wind from the west has had ten million years to work on this sandstone and basalt and it has left it polished, fluted, undercut into low, whaleback ridges and shallow basins the size of houses and neighborhoods. The mesas are not dramatic — they are tired, rounded, pitted with tafoni hollows you could sit inside. The talus slopes have stopped falling. The dust has found where it wants to live. It is a landscape in balance with itself.

And it is warm. A dry alpine warmth, 26 or 28 degrees in the shade, though there is almost no shade to be had. High desert, equatorial highland. The warmth never breaks. Nights are cool, not cold. The rock holds the day well past sunset and radiates it back to your back long after.

### Water Without Water

On paper this should be arid. And it looks arid, until you learn how to see the water.

There are no rivers here. No lakes. Water exists in the way dew exists — as an intermission.

Each dawn, before the bright hits hard, the thin air drops its burden. Condensation beads instantly on everything that cooled overnight. For twenty minutes the entire plateau glitters, a vast field of cold stone sweating. Then the sun crests the eastern scarp and it vanishes.

It doesn't vanish, actually. It goes where it was meant to go.

Almost invisibly, set into the basins and along the lee-sides of the ridges, are the catchments. Not built, exactly — *grown* would be closer. From a distance they look like patches of frost or spiderweb that survived too long into the day. Up close they are lattices: black filaments no thicker than hair, strung between low cairns of stone in perfect catenary arcs, sometimes spanning ten meters from anchor to anchor. They hum with a faint, cold stillness you can feel in your molars. The air moving through them leaves its water behind.

Drip, drip, drip.

Each lattice drains to a single point, a throat of dark stone that opens into a cistern cut deep under the plateau — cool, round, bell-shaped pockets holding water so still and clear it looks like glass. Some are as small as a bathtub, some as large as a house. You will walk for half a kilometer over dry, ringing stone and then find one at your feet, sudden and perfect, reflecting the blinding sky. Around its lip, everything lives.

There are maybe thirty of these in any given valley-cell of the Table. Enough to make this place damp, if you know where to stand. Damp in the way a prayer flag is damp just before dawn.

Between them, the ground is dry as bone and warm to the touch.

### The Sparse Green

Vegetation is an afterthought here, or a guest that knows not to overstay. There is no forest, no grassland in the Earth sense.

What there is: low, hard things that have understood the deal.

In the shallow grit that gathers in wind-scours: dense, dark-green cushions like moss that is not moss, tight as knuckles, that can lose ninety percent of their water and look dead for months. They green in hours after a rain that comes maybe four times a year.

Around each cistern-mouth: a sudden riot, relatively speaking — tussocks of silver wire-grass, flat rosettes with waxy leaves the size of your hand that funnel every drop toward their center, and a kind of dwarf succulent that grows in spirals, its skin dusted white to throw back the light. The flowers, when they come, are startlingly large and paper-thin and last only a day.

And everywhere, in the cracks where the lattices throw a sliver of shade at noon, a black-green lichen-like crust that is not lichen. If you look close it has a grain, a nap like velvet, and it shifts away from your shadow very slowly. It is photosynthetic, but not quite. In late afternoon it faintly phosphoresces, holding a little of the brutal light for the night.

Coverage, total? Maybe fifteen percent. The rest is stone — ochre, rust, pale bone, streaked with desert varnish that has cured to a dark chocolate glaze. You can see for miles. There is so much empty ground between green things that emptiness itself becomes the dominant presence.

That makes the animals all the more impossible.

### The Abundance

The first rule you have to unlearn here is that big life needs big plants.

The air above the Table is full.

At first you think it's dust motes in the bright. Then you realize the motes are navigating.

They are everywhere but they keep their distance — a low density of presences spread across a huge openness, so that you are never crowded but you are never alone. In any 50-meter circle there will be three or four kinds of movement if you stand still long enough to see them.

Close to the ground: things that run. Low, six-limbed skimmers the size of rabbits that don't hop so much as kite, their dorsal sails spread to catch the thin wind and tack across the slickrock. They graze the crust, scraping it with radial mouths, and freeze perfectly still when you look directly at them.

A little higher: the gliders. Papery, translucent kites two feet across, like manta rays made of dried leaves, moving without flapping — just riding thermals off the hot stone, circling the cisterns. They are not birds. They have no eyes you can see. Every so often one will fold and drop like a seed pod to sip from a pool, and you will hear the faintest *tick* of it touching water.

Higher still: the shepherds. You will see them only against the sun, so high they are specks, so large they must be something else. Long, slow, ribbon-bodied things that undulate on the stratospheric winds that rake the plateau. They are pale against the pale blue and they never land. Sometimes a whole flock of gliders will rise in a spiral to meet one, and disappear into its wake.

And the night belongs to other things — small hard clicks in the rock basins, choruses of chirping from inside the cisterns, brief traceries of cool light as something with too many legs darts from shadow to shadow.

What do they eat? Yes, some eat the plants. But most eat each other, or eat the air itself — a soup of aerial plankton, spores, and bacteria that breeds in the condensation lattices and is thrown high each morning when the sun boils the dew. The tech is the base of the food chain here, not the plant. The Table is a farm for wind and mist, and the animals have adapted to harvest the harvesters.

It is active, ceaselessly. Predator-prey in slow motion in the thin, quiet air. A glider casting a shadow to spook skimmers into running. A ribbon high above tipping slightly to change course, though toward what you cannot tell. Life happening at the pace of weather.

### The Quiet and the Strange

It is profoundly quiet. At 0.4 on any honest scale, sound barely exists.

The air is too thin to carry. Your footsteps are padded even though the rock is hard. Wind makes almost no noise because there is nothing to rustle — just a low sigh over fluted stone, a faint ting when the lattice filaments contract in the heat. The animals make almost no noise. The gliders are silent by design; the skimmers communicate in ultrasound or vibration through stone. Even your breathing sounds private.

In that quiet, other things become audible: the blood in your ears, the faint crack of stone expanding at noon, the drip of a cistern in its deep throat, echoing like a chapel.

The quiet makes the strangeness louder.

Because the light here doesn't behave correctly all the time.

You will be walking between two lattice arrays and see, from the corner of your eye, a second sun flicker on just for a second — or a cistern reflecting a cloud that isn't in the sky. If you stand directly under one of the larger filament nets at midday, your shadow falls not in one place but in two, the second a hand's breadth to the left and a little more violet at the edge, as if light were being sorted.

Some stones float.

Not many. Not obviously. Just in the most eroded basins, where the wind has scooped the Table down to a smooth bowl, you will find pebbles — and sometimes a boulder the size of a dog — hovering a finger's width or a foot above the ground, perfectly still, casting a sharp little shadow on the dust below. They have been there a long time; dust has settled on their tops. You can push one and it will drift back, slowly, as if through honey.

This is the tech, if that word still applies.

### What Was Left Here

There are no cities. No roads. The built environment is at 1.1 — a rumor.

You will find a cairn that is too perfect to be natural, three stones balanced where no rockfall could have put them, the top stone cut at an impossible interior angle. You will find a low wall, knee-high, running for a hundred meters absolutely straight before it stops for no reason, its material fused sandstone that rings like metal when tapped.

And you will find the anchors.

Each lattice has them: two squat posts of matte, non-reflective material that is neither stone nor ceramic nor metal — warm even at dawn, cool even at noon. No seams. No writing. No doors. If you put your hand near one you feel the hairs on your forearm lift. If you sleep near one, your dreams are quiet and well-ordered.

They are not ruins. They show no wear. The erosion that has softened everything else has not touched them. The lichen will not grow on them. The skimmers avoid them by exactly one meter, always.

There is no one here to explain them. There may never have been anyone in the human sense. They were placed — or grown — to do a job: to wring a living out of high, bright, thin air, to hold water in stone, to make abundance where there should be scarcity. And they still do it, flawlessly, without maintenance, without noise, without hurry.

You get the feeling the whole Table is a single instrument, tuned long ago and left to play itself. The lattices are the strings. The cisterns are the resonators. The wind is the bow. The animals are just listeners who learned to live inside the music.

You walk back toward where you came from. The light is still brutal, the stone still warm through your boots. Somewhere to your left a glider touches water with a tick smaller than a whisper, and ripples expand in a perfect, silent circle, reflecting a sky that for a moment has two suns.
