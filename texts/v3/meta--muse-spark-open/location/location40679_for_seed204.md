You don't find it from the outside.

From the outside there is only the high ridge — a wind-scoured knife of young basalt at about four thousand meters, too cold and too sharp to hold snow for long. It sheared up last century when the fault slipped; the geologists gave it a number instead of a name. From a distance it looks like any other black spine in the range: columnar joints still crisp, edges unsoftened by weather, scree fields that haven't yet learned to be soil. No plants. The altitude and the cold and the newness won't allow it. Even lichen hasn't made the argument yet.

You have to know the seam.

On the leeward face, where the wind has polished the rock to a dull glass, there is a vertical cleft no wider than a doorway, breathing cold air. It weeps. Even on dry days water beads along its lips and runs down in dark fingers that freeze overnight and split again by noon. Squeeze through sideways, boots on slick stone, and the wind cuts off.

Inside is the pocket.

---

It is not a cave. It is a geode that never finished.

A near-perfect ovoid chamber, perhaps sixty meters tall and forty across at its widest — you could fit a small neighborhood in it, and someone did. The walls are raw, young basalt, still showing the conchoidal scars of its opening, hexagonal columns truncated mid-growth where the void interrupted them. They glisten. Water is everywhere but not as a lake or a river. The rock sweats.

Because the chamber is warm — not warm — but *less cold* than outside, maybe -4°C inside versus -20°C out on the wind, the temperature differential pulls moisture from the outside air and condenses it on every surface. The ceiling, which is a dome of interlocked basalt teeth, drips constantly. Not rain. A perpetual post-rain. Fat, cold drops gather at the points of the columns and fall in irregular, arrhythmic plinking onto everything below. The walls run with thin sheets of water that never quite freeze, never quite flow, finding paths through soot-black stone and leaving behind pale mineral beards of calcite and salt.

The floor of the pocket is not floor. It's construction.

Someone — some group, generations ago by the look of it, though the tech says otherwise — built a city in the vertical. Not across the bottom, but *in* the volume.

### The City in the Throat

Imagine scaffolding that decided to become permanent. From the lower walls, cantilevered platforms of matte ceramic and brushed alloy jut out without visible welds or bolts; they simply grow from anchors sunk into the rock, their undersides beaded with condensation like the stone itself. Narrow bridges, no wider than two people, span the void in catenary curves that hum faintly when the wind outside changes pressure. Stairwells spiral around basalt columns, hugging them. Cubicles and habitations — some no bigger than a shipping container, some three stories tall — are stacked and slotted into the rock face wherever a ledge allowed, connected by conduits and pipes that sweat and drip alongside the natural water.

It is sparse. That's the first urban falsehood it corrects: a city doesn't have to be dense. There is immense, respectful emptiness between structures. A single lit dwelling hangs alone on the western wall with twenty meters of dark, dripping air on all sides. A market platform — you can tell by the retractable awnings, now furled and icy — sits empty, large enough for fifty stalls, holding only three. The builders left air and silence as part of the plan. You can see from one side of town to the other without obstruction, all the way across the cold cathedral of rock.

And it is quiet. Mostly.

Water does most of the talking: tick, tick, patter on metal roofs, a thin continuous sheeting whisper down stone, the occasional larger splash as an icicle gives up and falls thirty meters to shatter on a walkway. Under that, the city itself breathes. A low, almost sub-audible thrum from the infrastructure — heat exchangers, you realize — and the soft sigh of pressure doors equalizing. Footsteps carry. When someone closes a hatch three levels up, you hear the *thunk* echo four times, cleanly.

No roads. No cars. Movement is vertical. Small open lifts — just a plate with a rail — ascend on filament lines so thin they're invisible until the light catches them. They move without motors you can see. Children, rare here, learn early not to drop things over the railings; whatever you drop will fall a long way, and someone below will have to retrieve it from a net of woven carbon.

Light is normal, which is strange. There are no windows to the outside except the cleft you came in through, a bright vertical scar that throws a single hard shaft of alpine sun across the chamber for two hours at noon. The rest of the time, the light comes from the city itself — not from lamps, but from the materials. The underside of each platform glows with a diffuse, daylight-balanced luminescence, as if the ceramic was taught to remember sunlight. It is even, shadowless, and makes the falling water glitter like mica dust. At night-cycle, it dims to a soft amber without ever going dark. No one remembers a switch.

### What Keeps It Alive

There is no soil, no plant. Nothing green has ever rooted here; whatever airborne seed blows in through the cleft germinates and dies in a week on cold stone. The mineral world is absolute: basalt, iron, frost, alloy, condensate, skin. Even the air smells of wet rock and ozone.

And yet it is active — furiously, quietly active. The whole pocket is a machine for managing water and cold.

You watch for an hour and you see the cycles: Along the upper dome, condensate collectors — shallow, lily-pad dishes of black glass — fill drop by drop, tip, and pour their load into channels carved directly into the basalt. Those channels feed cisterns behind the walls. Excess is shunted to the lowest point of the chamber, where a perfect circular drain, ten meters across, never overflows. Steam, or rather, cold mist, sighs up from it as the water is reprocessed. The heat exchangers that keep the chamber at -4°C bleed their waste heat in controlled pulses that melt the weeping ice, which refreezes, which melts again. It is a small weather system, closed, deliberate.

Fauna is rare and mostly accidental. A high-altitude swift got in once and now lives here year-round, too confused to leave, nesting on a girder. Its thin cry on occasion is startling. More common are the maintainers — not animals, not quite robots — small, dark, crab-like things that pick their way along pipes on needle legs, wiping mineral scale from joints, clearing ice from walkways. They ignore you. The residents ignore them. There's an understanding: you are sparse here, you share the space with emptiness and with processes larger than you.

The people — there can't be more than two hundred — move with the unhurried economy of those who live in a place where falling is a real consequence. They wear heavy, matte thermal layers that shed water. They speak softly because they don't need to shout. Many have the faint sheen on their fingertips of those who work with assemblers — the post-modern tech that is everywhere and nowhere. Need a new wall? You extrude it. The material comes from the rock itself, reconstituted at a molecular level in vats that hum behind unmarked doors. The bridges that hold without cables, the lifts that rise without motors, the light that lives in matter — none of it is explained, because explanation would require physics the outside world hasn't agreed on yet. Here, it is just municipal infrastructure. Someone has to unclog the channel on level three when the mineral beard grows too thick. That someone has a wrench that unfolds like origami.

The rule of this place, unspoken but obvious from the architecture, is: *do not fill the void.*

You could pack this geode tight with housing. You could stack it floor to ceiling. The tech would allow it. Instead they built little, left much air, left the water its paths, left the rock its dominance. The city is a guest in a lung. It hangs lightly, drips with the stone, and listens to the mountain settle around it — that faint, once-a-year *tick* of young basalt contracting in the cold, a sound you feel in your teeth.

If you stay until the sun shaft moves off, until the city-light dims to amber, you understand why it's hidden. Not for secrecy. For scale. Some human things only work when they are small enough to be intimate, high enough to be cold, and wet enough to keep remembering that they are not separate from the rock that holds them.
