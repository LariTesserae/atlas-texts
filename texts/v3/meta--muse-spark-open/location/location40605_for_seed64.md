High up, where the air has already given up most of its weight, there is a pocket.

Not a valley. Not a plateau you could put on a map. It's an interruption in the mountain itself — a perfect cylinder cut straight down into the summit basalt, about the width of a house and twice as deep as a man is tall. From the outside, in the wind-scour above the frostline, you wouldn't see it until you were right on its lip. The world up there is all shattered black rock and dirty snow that never melts, never quite freezes either, just stays as a hard, dry crust. No streams. No tarn. No seep. The wind that comes over the ridge is so cold and so thin it carries no moisture at all; it peels the moisture from your lips in seconds.

You drop down into it on a rusted ladder to get out of that wind, and the sound hits you.

Inside, it is loud.

The cylinder was a breather caisson for a mine that never went deep enough — an air-cyclone, built to pull dead air out of shafts that were never finished. The machine is still running. It has to be. Four industrial bellows the size of coffins are set into the curved wall at the cardinal points, their pistons sheathed in corrugated steel, their intakes grated over with heavy mesh. They don't pump air so much as *beat* it. A slow, inexorable four-count: THUD-drag — THUD-drag — THUD-drag — THUD-drag, each bellows exhaling a gust of warm, iron-smelling exhaust and then sucking it back in, never exchanging with the outside. An equilibrium engine. On the north wall, a control booth the size of a shipping container hangs cantilevered, its windows caked with black dust, a single amber trouble-light still burning inside behind the grime, buzzing at 50 hertz so steadily you've stopped hearing it as a sound and started feeling it in your teeth.

It is dim. The sky is a far, small circle of bruised white far above, but the walls of the cylinder cut most of it away. The only real light is that amber bulb and two sodium work-lamps on caged arms, their glass long-sanded opaque by grit. They cast a low, sourceless brown-yellow that doesn't throw shadows so much as stain things.

And everything is packed.

This is the strange part, the part that keeps you on the ladder a little longer than you need to be. There should be nothing alive here. The basalt is dry as sintered bone. The air inside has been cycling for decades with no water in it — not a drip, not a puddle, not even condensation, because the cold is a dry cold that holds nothing. The engineers designed it that way: sealed environment, non-aqueous coolant, filters to strip any humidity that might rust the pistons.

The vegetation doesn't care.

It is lush — obscenely lush for 4,700 meters. Not green, not at first. A deep, sooty olive-black felt, thick as carpet, swallowing every surface that isn't moving. It grows on the basalt, on the bellows housings, on the ladder rungs, on the cables, on the roof of the control booth. It has swallowed one of the catwalks entirely; you can see the shape of the railing only as a ridge under moss. It is not moss, exactly. It is closer to a lichen made of wire-wool and rust. Each frond is no thicker than a hair, but there are millions, packed so tightly you can't fit a fingertip between them. When the bellows thud, the whole mass shivers.

It doesn't drink. It eats the air.

The old filters were meant to trap magnetite dust and sulfur salts from the exhaust. They clogged, then failed, then were colonized. This vegetation — the miners called it smog-fern, though it never had a proper name — precipitates metal directly out of the dry wind. In the dim light you can see it: each black-green filament ends in a tiny glassy bulb, a nodule of condensed iron and copper salts, catching the amber light like frost. No chlorophyll green; these things are dark because they are full of metal. They taste the exhaust, they split the fumes, they lay down more filament. They grow *toward* noise and vibration, not light. The densest, tallest plumes — knee-high, swaying though there is no breeze — are clustered right on the seams of the pistons, where the roar is loudest. Where the booth's transformer hums, the felt has grown into a perfect, velvet antler, branching in fractal repetition into the empty air, listening.

There are no animals. Not one. No mites in the felt, no moths around the lamps, no raven passing over that small circle of sky. The cold and the dry and the metallic air have allowed only this one kingdom. If you pull a clump away — and it comes away like peeling industrial insulation, with a soft tearing — there is nothing underneath but clean, weathered basalt, pitted and softened by a century of wind, and the faint, stenciled marks where men once wrote DEPTH and DANGER and PSI, now half-grown over. The rock itself is settled, old, not crumbling anymore; it has reached the shape it wants to be.

Nothing here changes.

That's the most unnatural thing. The bellows beat at the same rate they did when the last man left. The lamps burn at the same dim wattage. The smog-fern has filled every inch it can fill, so it no longer spreads; it just *maintains*, each filament replacing itself with exact precision, the mass breathing in and out with the pistons. Dust falls, is caught, is incorporated. No growth spurt. No die-off. No seasons. The temperature never wavers from that cool, cellar-cold that makes your breath faintly visible and then instantly dries it away. You can sit on the lowest rung and listen until your ears ring, and you will realize you are not hearing a process. You are hearing a state — a chord held forever.

It is a hidden, pocketed garden made of smoke and iron, tucked like a secret lung into the top of the world, roaring loudly and unchangingly to itself in the dark, with no water and no witness, and no need for either.
