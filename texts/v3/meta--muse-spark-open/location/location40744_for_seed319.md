The High Wet Town.

It is not built on a mountain so much as in one that has learned to be flat. A high intermontane basin, maybe a caldera that forgot it was a volcano, or a plateau that was lifted like a table and then left there. The air is thin enough that sound carries too well and sunlight hits too clean, but the temperature is warm — a high-altitude warm, dry in the sun on your shoulders and immediately damp in shadow. At noon you sweat. At dusk you steam.

This is old country. Not in a ruined way, in a settled way.

The rock here is established. Erosion level: 2.5/3. It has done its work and come to an agreement with gravity. The slopes around the basin are soft-shouldered, smoothed into long, convex spines of basalt and tuff, terraced over centuries both by water and by hands until you can't tell which made which step. The cliffs weep. The gullies are U-shaped and deep, polished. The boulders in the valley floor are not jagged; they are rounded, pitted, lichen-stained, half-buried like the knuckles of something sleeping deep. Nothing is sharply cut. Everything looks wind-licked and water-rubbed for ten thousand years.

And water is everywhere it shouldn't quite be, for how high and warm this place is.

Water 1.8/3. Not a lake. Not a river. A condition.

The plateau should be dry. Instead, the rock sweats. The water table sits just under the skin of the world. Step off the paved industrial yards onto the brown flats and your boot leaves a dark print that slowly fills. Springs appear at the foot of every terrace wall in thin clear sheets. The retaining walls weep constantly, black with damp, threaded with mineral white. At the eastern lip of the basin, where the plateau breaks, a whole long cliff-face drips, always, even in high summer — a thousand-foot veil of slow falls that never quite becomes a waterfall, just cold mist and silver threads falling into marsh below.

The town itself has made accommodations. Every street has a canal beside it, but not a Venetian canal — a shallow industrial runnel, knee-deep, concrete-sided, full of fast, tea-colored water that smells of iron and hot metal. Under every factory floor, culverts roar. Condensation gathers on every pipe, every sheet-metal roof, every underside of every elevated tramway, and falls as a constant warm rain at man-height. Ladders and gantries are slick. The air itself feels close to dewpoint in the low sectors, in the old marsh-edge that the city never fully drained.

Vegetation is sparse, 0.6/3, because nothing is allowed to be soft here for long. And because the water is mineral and hot. On the unpaved slopes between districts you get tough mats of rust-colored sedge, clumps of salt-tolerant glasswort, solitary umbrella acacias that have learned to root into a crack and survive on steam. In the marsh where the veil-falls land — the Plashes — there is a sudden riot of reeds, ten feet tall, hissing and rattling, but the town keeps cutting them back with barges. Everywhere else, vegetation is what grows on old concrete when no one scrapes it: dark moss on the weeping walls, orange sulfur-bacteria threads in the outflow pipes, a few stubborn figs splitting a warehouse foundation. Green is an accent color, not a ground cover.

You would expect fauna, in a warm wet place, but there is almost nothing. Fauna 0.8/3 — rare. The water is too loud, too hot, too full of sluice-oil and alkali for fish. The insects that should cloud the marsh don't. Instead there are the pale, long-legged marsh skimmers that flick over the canals — you see one every ten minutes, solitary — and the high circling bone-kites that ride the thermals over the basin, far up, watching. At night, the reeds click with something that is not frog nor cricket but a single sustained note, one creature answering itself across a mile of dark water. Mostly, the city has replaced animals with motion: shuddering conveyor belts, reciprocating pump-arms.

Because the density is sparse — 1.2/3 — but the built is urban — 2.5/3. This is how those two things coexist.

This is not a dense city. It is a sprawling, low, spread-thin industrial town the size of a small region, built to the scale of the basin. It goes on for miles, but it never stacks. Single-story and two-story longhouses of riveted steel and weather-paled brick, machine shops with sawtooth roofs, textile sheds a quarter-mile long, powerhouses, refineries, kilns, roundhouses, all separated by huge empty intervals: gravel yards full of stacked pipe, evaporation pans white with precipitated salt, abandoned rail spurs that glint in the wet heat, broad avenues where nothing moves but hot wind and water in its channels.

It is urban because it is continuous infrastructure. You can walk for six hours in any direction and never leave the town's logic: here a water-tower, here a switching station, here a workers' row, here a factory, here again a marsh that has been squared off and made to work. The power lines march on concrete pylons across the reeds. The narrow-gauge freight lines stitch everything together, embanked over the soak. Layered construction: you can see where a street was raised after a flood and the old shopfronts became basements, where a new pipe rack was built over an old canal, where a temple was turned into a turbine hall without bothering to take down the stone saints on its cornice — they stand there now, sweating, looking out over a yard of coal.

Tech is industrial, 2.1/3. Not sleek. Not digital. Electrified, mechanized, scaled.

Everything turns, pumps, stamps, exhales. The factories here do not make small clever things. They make cement from the soft basin limestone, and soda ash from the marsh brine, and steel fittings, and heavy cloth, and glass. Big stupid necessary things. The machines are open to the air under corrugated roofs; you can see the flywheels from the street, ten feet across, spinning. Diesel generators thump behind chainlink. Arc lights buzz. Transmission shafts run the length of buildings and drive a hundred looms by leather belts that slap and hum. Nothing is automated enough to be quiet.

Sound: 2.6/3 — deafening.

This is the town's defining fact after the weeping rocks. It is incredibly loud and it never stops being loud. Not chaotic-loud. Occupied-loud. The low, constant, bone-conducted roar of the veil-falls on the eastern scarp, miles away but audible everywhere like a freeway of water. The mid-range thunder of stamping presses and drop hammers that you feel in your molars. The high scream of steam being let off, of cutting saws on metal, of the tram wires singing in wind. And underneath it all, water rushing in culverts under your feet, under every street. You have to lean close to talk. Workers wear stuffed cotton in their ears and develop a shout. At night, when the factories throttle down by a third but never stop, the sound opens up and you can hear the town's true size: a vast, low, patient roar coming from every direction at once, bouncing off the basin walls. Newcomers don't sleep for a week.

And yet — dynamic 0.9/3 — slow.

For all that noise, nothing really changes quickly. This town is not boom or bust. It is established-erosion, patient-process. The machines run at the same speed they ran twenty years ago. The canals run at the same level. The veil-falls have been falling since before anyone remembers, and the rate at which they are dissolving the scarp is maybe a pebble a century. The lime scale builds on pipes half an inch a year. The rust advances a shade per season. Work shifts change with sirens that have sounded at the same hours for three generations. People are born in the brick rows by the soda works and go to work in the soda works and the only surprise is which machine they'll tend.

Light is normal, 1.8/3. Unremarkable, high-altitude normal. Sun white at noon and gold in evening. Shadows razor-sharp because the air is thin. At noon the puddles in the yards throw back the sky and the sawtooth windows throw grids of light onto wet streets. At night the town glows amber from sodium lamps reflecting off low cloud and steam, a dome of light you can see from the surrounding peaks as if the plateau itself were warm.

And weirdness, 1.6/3 — strange. Not magic. Just enough off to make you stare.

It's the water. The water is wrong.

It seeps up. On hot afternoons you can watch it: the gravel flats between factories will darken from below, moisture rising against gravity, pooling without a source. In the oldest district, the Kiln Quarter, there are columns of basalt that are always wet on their topsides, dry underneath — condensation collecting upward, as if gravity were reversed for dew. The weeping walls sometimes weep upward for a few inches before falling. Workers don't comment on it. If you leave a cold glass out, the film on the outside will crawl up and over the rim and down the inside, emptying itself.

The Plashes at the basin's edge do something else. The veil-falls land and make no ripples. The reed-marsh surface is glassy even while being struck constantly from above. And if you stare long enough on a hot, loud afternoon, the falling threads seem to hesitate mid-air, to hang, to thicken. No one goes wading there. The few maintenance crews who clear reeds wear tethers.

Rules of this place, if you stay:

1. Wear boots. The ground is never dry.
2. Shout, and listen by watching mouths.
3. Don't look for shade in trees. Look for shade under pipe racks and gantries. Trees are rare and they leak.
4. Don't trust a dry floor. It won't stay dry.
5. The town is bigger than it looks and emptier than it feels. You can walk for an hour past nothing but hot gravel, hissing pipes, and a single heron-like bird standing in a canal, and still be downtown.
6. Everything was built to outlast you, and slowly, it will.

You leave, finally, by the western pass road, climbing out of the basin. Behind you the whole flat glitters — not with lights, though there are lights — with water on metal, with a thousand slow falls catching sun, with steam plumes flattening in the wind. The roar fades very slowly, because the air is thin and carries it. For a long time after you can't see the factories anymore, you can still hear them, a low hum in the rock under your tires, as if the plateau itself had an engine.
