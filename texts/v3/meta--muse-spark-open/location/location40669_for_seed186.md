On the high spine.

Not a peak, but a *country* of peaks — a corrugated plateau that was lifted too far and never came down. The air here is too thin to hold heat and too thin to carry sound.

Elevation is the first law. 2.9/3 extreme.

You are four, maybe five kilometers above anything that could be called sea level, though there is no sea to level against anymore. The horizon is not a line but a successive shaving-away of ridges into darker air, each range a little paler and less real than the last until even mountains become weather. The sky is low. Not cloudy-low, but *close* — a deep indigo press of atmosphere that never fully commits to day. This is a latitude and altitude where light has to travel a long way through cold to get to you, and most of it gives up.

Light: 0.3/3 darkness.

It is not night. It is the hour before dawn stretched until it becomes a climate. A starless dark-blue, like being inside dusk. The sun, when it exists, is a rumor behind six layers of high haze and never rises above a cold smear on the southern sky. What illumination there is comes from below and beside: from snow-glow, from lichen-glow, from things that make their own light because they have waited too long for any other.

And yet, it is lush. 2.3/3.

That is the contradiction that defines the place. Cold: 0.4/3. A dry, lung-hurting, iron-bitter cold where breath falls as a clatter of ice crystals rather than fog. Your exposed skin goes numb in seconds. And still the rock is furred.

Not with trees. Trees gave up two kilometers ago. Here, vegetation means something older and more patient. The entire plateau is a boreal cloud-tundra, a single continuous organism of moss and cushion plant and black-green lichen and liverwort so thick you could lose your hand in it. It grows in hummocks the size of houses, in blankets that ripple over glacial erratics, in curtains that hang off leeward cliffs a hundred meters long and drip once an hour. Alpine azalea, starved to the size of a coin, bloom in sudden, shocking fields of white and arterial red, opening and closing on no schedule you can perceive. The vegetation is not tall, but it is deep. Step and you sink to the knee in a cold sponge that has been accumulating itself for ten thousand years. Underneath is peat, then permafrost, then peat again — a black, sweet-smelling archive of every summer that never really came.

Water: 1.1/3 damp.

There are no lakes here. No rivers worthy of a name. Water exists as an intermittent promise. Hoarfrost grows every evening on every leaf-tip and lichen beard in cathedrals of white needles, then sublimates by mid-morning. Fog pours over the windward escarpments in a slow, silent river ten meters deep and leaves everything beaded and dripping for an hour, then it is gone. In the deep clefts where the plateau has cracked, snowmelt gathers for a week in summer and makes temporary tarns — black, perfectly still mirrors that reflect the dark sky — before draining away through unseen throats in the rock. The whole place is damp the way a cave is damp: not wet, but never dry; everything cold to the touch and faintly slick with condensation that never quite becomes a drop.

It is an old place. Erosion: 1.5/3 established.

This is not a jagged, new range. These mountains have been here long enough to find their shape and decide to keep it. The wind has had its way with them. Ridges are rounded like the backs of sleeping animals. Cirques are scooped clean and hold their shadow like a memory of ice. Talus slopes have sorted themselves by size over millennia, larger stones at the bottom, fines at the top. Nothing is actively collapsing. Nothing is raw. Even the cliffs look weathered into a kind of rightness, their faces striped with mineral varnish, their bases pillowed with centuries of moss trying to digest them.

Scale: 2.0/3 open — a valley the size of a small country.

You can walk for a day and still be in the same system: the same high moor, the same wind-pattern, the same species of lichen on the same species of rock. The plateau is broken into immense, shallow bowls, each five to ten kilometers across, each holding its own weather. From the lip of one bowl you can see the next, and the next after that, receding into the dark haze. It's too big to be intimate, too enclosed to be planetary. A town could have fit in one of those bowls. Nothing ever did.

Density: 0.5/3 empty. Built: 0.1/3 none.

In the ordinary sense, there is nothing here. No roads, no walls, no hearths, no cairns. No straight lines. If something was ever built, the moss has taken it so completely that it is now moss. You can walk an hour between notable features — between one erratic boulder and the next — across a plain of hummocks where the only vertical thing is a sporophyte stalk no taller than your finger.

And yet —
Tech: 1.7/3 industrial. Fauna: 1.8/3 abundant. Weirdness: 2.5/3 strange.

Because the emptiness is not empty, and the unbuilt is not unmade.

Listen. Sound: 0.2/3 silence.

At first you hear nothing but the blood in your ears and the faint creak of the peat under your boots. The wind at this altitude is strangely laminar; it slides over the low vegetation without rustling it. There are no birds to call.

Then you hear the mountain exhale.

Far across the bowl, a plume rises — not smoke, but a sudden, warm, wet column of air that condenses instantly into cloud and then is gone. A minute later, another. From your vantage on the rim, you realize the plateau is breathing through a dozen invisible vents. Geothermal? Yes, but not like any geyser field you've seen. These are *regular*. Rhythmic. A deep, subsonic *thump* — felt more in the sternum than heard — followed three seconds later by that plume, and a low sigh as air is drawn back in. Thump. Hiss. Draw. Thump. Hiss. Draw. Like a bellows the size of a hillside. Like a forge.

That is the industry.

There are no factories because the mountain is the factory.

The fauna build nothing and run everything.

They are everywhere once your eyes adjust to the dark — abundant, but spaced with that vast, empty density, one per hectare, one per ridge, each solitary and immense. You thought the hummocks were plants. Some are. Some uncoil.

The Gravers are the engineers. Low, broad creatures the size of small bulldozers, covered not in fur but in overlapping plates of black chitin and living moss, so that when they are still they are indistinguishable from the ground itself. They move on many small, silent feet, and they do not eat in any way you understand. They *tend*.

Each Graver has a vent — a warm throat in the rock that it has claimed. It spends its life in a slow, endless circuit: scraping lichen with a broad, keratinous lip, digesting it, exuding a dark mineral oil from glands along its back, and then, with meticulous care, painting that oil around the lip of its vent with forelimbs that end in soft, brush-like pads. The oil seals the vent, pressure builds below — you can hear it ticking in the rock — until the Graver steps back and the seal blows. The *thump*. A gout of superheated, metal-scented steam and a spray of fine ash and sulfurous snow that fertilizes the entire bowl. The Graver then lumbers forward again to reseal it. Combustion without fire. Production without product, except more moss, more lichen, more Gravers.

Elsewhere, the Condensers hang. They are not animals so much as living cooling towers — tall, translucent, jellyfish-like columns that root themselves on windward ridges and inflate to ten meters high with cold air. They pulse faintly with bioluminescence, amber and sick-green, the only real light in this darkness. Wind passes through them and its moisture is wrung out, dripping down their fluted sides into the peat below, feeding the damp. They make no sound at all. When they are full of water, they slowly deflate and go dark, and another nearby inflates to take its place.

And above them all, very high in the indigo, something huge and silent migrates between bowls, blocking the haze-light for a long moment. You never see it clearly. Only its underside, pale and faintly striated like the inside of a lung, and the almost inaudible displacement of air as it goes — *whuff* — and the way every Condenser below briefly brightens as it passes, as if in deference.

This is why the system is so dynamic. 2.4/3 active.

It is not a static tundra. It is a working landscape. A cold, dark, high-industrial prairie where the machines are alive and the workers are the mountains and the fuel is mist. The cycle is tight and ceaseless: Condensers water the moss, moss feeds the Gravers, Gravers fire the vents, vents fertilize the moss and make the updraft that lifts the high grazer-things, whose droppings seed new Condenser polyps. Predator and prey are replaced by stoker and furnace.

It runs day and night — especially night, because here it is always night — without hurry and without pause, with the patient thunder of an industry that has no owner, no product, and no end.

If you stand still long enough, the cold finds you, the dark finds you, and a Graver will mistake you for a rock and try, gently, to paint your boots with oil before the next firing.
