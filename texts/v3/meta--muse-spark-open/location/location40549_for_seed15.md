It is not a sea. It is a room that decided to hold one.

You come down to it. The surface is far above you — not deep in the way caves are deep, but *below* in the way a throat is below a mouth. A trapdoor, a flat stone set flush with the baked ground, lifted. A shaft, square-cut, laddered with iron spikes hammered into the stone every two feet. The air that comes up is warm and wet and loud.

Everything you see is made.

That is the first thing. Not a single face of this place is natural. The shaft you descend is four walls of fitted limestone blocks, each one as wide as a man is long, each one still showing the tooth-marks of the adze that dressed it. No bedrock shows through. No earth. The mortar between them is newer than it should be, still pale, still a little soft at the edge where you brush it with your thumb. This is a young place. Sharp. The corners where wall meets wall have not been rounded by time. The chisel-slips are still there, dark little crescents where an apprentice's hand jumped. Someone built this chamber recently, within a living memory, and they built it to last.

At the bottom of the ladder-shaft, the light hits you.

It is unbearably bright for an underground place. Directly overhead, the shaft is open to the sky, a perfect square of white-gold desert sun, and two more shafts just like it puncture the vaulted ceiling further down the chamber, three hard columns of light standing in the humid air. You can see dust motes turning in them, and the water vapor itself, and the way the light doesn't diffuse but *cuts* straight down through the chamber until it strikes the water. There is no softness to this light. It is noon, concentrated and thrown underground like a spear.

The chamber itself is intimate. You could hold its measure in a single shout and hear the shout come back to you too fast. Perhaps thirty paces long, ten wide, the ceiling a low barrel vault of brick and plaster, not high enough that you could forget it is there. This is not a reservoir for a city. This is a pocket, a hidden cistern for a single estate or monastery, embedded like a lung under the courtyard. A made womb. The scale makes everything else feel outsized — the water especially.

For the chamber is almost entirely water.

It was not designed to be this full. You can see the high-water intention in the ring of mineral stain around the walls, about chest-high when you stand on the submerged floor. But the water now comes to your throat if you stand on the bottom, to the vault's springing if you are small. It has taken the room. It is warm — bath-warm, body-warm, the heat of stone that has been under the sun all day radiating down through the shafts into the trapped liquid. When you slip from the last iron rung you do not enter cold. You enter a heat that is seamless with the air. There is no thermocline. The atmosphere and the water have negotiated a single temperature and agreed to hold it.

The water itself is not still. This is not a stagnant cistern.

You hear it before you understand it. It is loud in here, impossibly loud for such a small room, because every surface is hard and close and everything is made to echo. The three light-shafts are also rain-shafts, or spring-shafts — some engineered catchment up above has been diverted here, and now, even though it is not raining on the desert today, water comes anyway. It pours down the central shaft in a continuous, heavy column, not a waterfall but a thick, braided rope of water that slams into the cistern's surface with a ceaseless, concussive drumming. The two other shafts leak in thinner, ecstatic curtains that slap and chatter against the vault walls on their way down. And inside the cistern, the overflow is working — a low, arched sluice near the ceiling at the far end, cut with a sharp, new lip, gargles and sucks as it takes the excess away, somewhere else underground. The system is cycling. Inhale down the shafts, exhale through the sluice. Active, endless.

You wade, or swim. Your feet find the floor — also made, great flat slabs of limestone fitted without a gap you could slip a knife into — but it is strange to walk when the water lifts you. The light columns go down through the water, too, turning the whole volume milky-gold with suspended lime dust. You can see the bottom clearly. You can see every block. Nothing is hidden.

There is almost nothing living here. That is the other strange thing about the warmth and the brightness. You expect fish, insects, frogs. There are none. The builders keep it clean, or the water moves too fast and too loud for anything to settle. No animal life. Only a sparse, stubborn fringe of green where the air meets stone: a black-green smear of algae in the mortar joints just above the waterline, a few pale ferns that have levered themselves into the chisel marks near the overflow arch where the spray keeps them wet. They tremble constantly in the movement of air pulled down the shafts by the falling water. Their roots are white and tiny. This is all the vegetation the place allows — scattered, low, patient.

If you press your back against the wall and float, you understand the rules of this world:

1.  Nothing here is found. Everything is placed. Even the water has been invited and channeled and given a path.
2.  Sound does not leave. The drumming of the central column, the slapping of the curtains, the throaty swallow of the sluice, your own breathing — it all stacks up in the vault and stays, bright and hard as the light.
3.  The sun keeps time, not the season. At noon the columns are vertical and blinding. In late afternoon they will slant and paint a burning bar across the far wall. At night this place will be absolute dark, filled with the sound of invisible water.

It is a simple machine made with primitive tools — hammers, chisels, plumb lines, buckets — and it has made something that feels like an ocean, but an ocean contained in a single room, warm and bright and roaring, just below the skin of the ordinary world.
