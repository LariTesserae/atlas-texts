It is a basin, not a valley. A low place that was made lower.

You come down to it by a long, shallow concrete stair cut into the levee, and the air gets cooler by degrees even though the sun hasn't changed. Cool the way stone is cool — not winter, not biting, just the cool that lives in the shade of heavy things, in the breath off wet brick. Normal daylight falls straight down into it, open sky, no haze, no filter, a plain white-grey noon that never seems to get brighter or dimmer. It should move. It doesn't.

The town is hamlet-sized but it has the bones of a city. That's the first wrongness.

From the rim you can see the whole thing at once, edge to edge, like a model left in a washtub. Two or three streets in a deformed grid, a canal straight as a ruler that cuts the town in half and then stops, abruptly, against the far levee wall where it should continue. Warehouses of dark brick, soot-stained but not crumbling. Factory halls with sawtooth roofs, their glass all intact but clouded with condensation on the inside. A water tower on iron legs. A substation humming behind chainlink. It is dense with infrastructure — too much for how small it is — pipe-bridges over alleys, loading gantries, brick smokestacks that don't smoke, catwalks, gutters, culverts, valve wheels the size of doors. Layered construction, generations of it, each one built right on top of the last without ever sprawling outward. The erosion here is old and patient. Corners are rounded, mortar is scooped out between bricks by a hundred years of damp wind, iron is scaled and flaked, concrete is pitted and shows the aggregate like bone. But it's in-balance. Nothing is falling down. Nothing is being built up. It has weathered into exactly what it is and stopped.

There are no plants. Not a weed in a crack, not moss on the north sides, not a tree beyond the levee. The mineral world is undisguised: brick dust, iron oxide, cold concrete, river-silt dried to a pale skin, coal fines pressed into the seams of everything. Where green should be there is instead a thin fungal felt, grey-yellow and velvety, that grows only on the undersides of pipes where condensation beads and drips. It doesn't spread. It doesn't die.

Water is here without being present. The canal is not empty and not full. A few inches of dark water lie in its bottom, too still to be a flow, too even to be a puddle. It reflects the sawtooth roofs perfectly and doesn't ripple. Along every eave and girder and underside, beads of water gather, swell, and fall. *Plink* on metal. *Plink* on concrete. *Plink* in the canal. The gutters run for a minute, then dry. The culverts gurgle and then swallow dry air. You hear a distant rain on rooftops sometimes, but if you look up the sky is clear. The whole basin sweats. Intermittent moisture. Riverine pockets in a larger dryness, as if the tide that used to come here still remembers its schedule and arrives as humidity.

And the town is sparse. That's the second wrongness.

For an industrial urban place, it should be crowded with things. Instead there is so much space between presences. One bicycle leaned against a wall with no rust on its chain. One coal barge in the canal, half-loaded, moored with a perfectly taut rope. One factory door ajar exactly six inches. One sodium lamp buzzing over one intersection. The emptiness is not abandonment; it's a low saturation, balanced-with-emptiness, as if the town was designed to have only one of each necessary thing and no more.

Except for the fauna.

The fauna is overwhelming and wrong and it makes you understand why the town is loud.

They are starlings. Or they are built like starlings — tight, oily, iridescent bodies, sharp beaks, eyes like polished rivets. But they are large, almost the size of cats, and there are thousands upon thousands of them, and they never stop.

They coat the sawtooth roofs in a dense, chittering mat, shoulder to shoulder, facing all the same direction. They line every pipe-bridge, every gantry, every sill. They pack the interior of the warehouses where the condensation drips, perched on dormant looms and drill presses and conveyor belts. They fill the sky above the basin in a slow, roiling torus that never leaves the levee walls, turning and turning.

They are loud. Not birdsong loud. Industrial loud. Their combined chattering and wingbeats and claws on metal make the same broad-spectrum roar as a factory floor at full production — a clattering, whirring, shrieking wash that rattles the chainlink and vibrates the water in the canal without ever making a ripple. You have to raise your voice to think.

And they do not behave like birds.

None of them eat. There is nothing to eat — no plants, no insects, no seeds, only that thin fungal felt and bare mineral. No droppings foul the sills. They don't fight for perch space though they are crammed tight; there is a precise gap between each one, a sparse regularity inside the overwhelming number. When one flies up to join the torus, another drops down to take its exact spot within a second, so the density never changes.

No chick begs. No bird dies. No feather falls.

Closer, you see that where a bird's legs should grip the rusted pipe, there is no distinction between claw and rust. The scale blurs. Some of them have bellies that are not feathered but sheeted in the same clouded glass as the factory roofs. Some of them, when they open their beaks, show not a throat but a dark, empty flue, and the sound that comes out is more like a pressure valve releasing than a call. They are not mechanical and not flesh, or rather, the difference has eroded away here as thoroughly as the mortar between the bricks.

This is the rule of the basin: **it is in equilibrium that is not peace.**

The engines run. You can hear them now under the bird-roar if you listen — a diesel thrum from the brick power station, the sixty-cycle hum from the substation, the sigh of a big fan somewhere turning slowly. The belts move. The starlings turn overhead. The condensation gathers and plinks. The light stays at its even noon. No gradient forms. No cool day warms. No damp place dries or floods. No machine wears out though it runs forever. No bird starves though it never feeds.

It established itself a long time ago, settled into its own state, and froze not by being cold but by being perfectly balanced. The weathering that smoothed its corners was not decay but finishing. The levee holds back a river that is no longer there. The factories make nothing and need to make nothing. The overwhelming fauna tends the stillness by making constant noise.

If you stay on the concrete stair, you can watch for what feels like hours. A beading will swell on a pipe and fall. A starling will shudder and resettle. A gurgle will move through a culvert. And then — if you blink — the bead is back in the same place, swelling again. The same starling shudders. The same gurgle moves. Not a loop like a recording skipping, but a state like a held chord, where every vibration sustains every other vibration so nothing can ever resolve.

It is a small place. You could walk its perimeter in twenty minutes. It feels larger while you are inside it because the sound and the birds press the walls outward. It feels smaller when you leave, because you realize you have seen all of it and it has not seen you at all.
