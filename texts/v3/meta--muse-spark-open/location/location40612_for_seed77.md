It is not a desert. It is not a city. It is what happens when the two become the same thing and are left in the heat long enough to forget which one started first.

You are standing very low.

Not low like a valley — low like a continent that decided to lie down. The curvature of the world is visible here because there is nothing to interrupt it. The horizon is a perfect, unbroken line in every direction, hazy with heat, and the ground beneath you is so flat and so vast that weather is born and dies without ever hitting a mountain. Clouds form directly overhead out of clear air, gather themselves, rain, and evaporate again, all within the bowl of this one place.

The elevation is nominal. You could walk for a lifetime and never gain fifty meters. What little relief there is, is not natural.

Everything is made.

For as far as you can see — and that is very, very far — the plain is divided. Not by roads, not by property lines, but by construction. Low walls, lips, basins, pans, terraces, and combs made of the same stuff as the ground itself: sun-baked silt, salt-cemented clay, lithified mud the color of bleached bone and rust. The walls are never higher than your knee, often lower than your ankle. Some are crisp as fresh pottery, with hand-smoothed crests and thumb-pressed corners. Others are soft, slumped, half-melted back into the earth they were scooped from. The pattern is irregularly regular, like a wasp nest scaled to the size of Texas, or a drying mudflat that was taught geometry.

There was no machine that did this. There is no metal here, no glass, no wire, no ruin of a greater technology. The marks are biological. You can see where something — many somethings, over a very long time — scraped and piled and patted. Fingertip-wide drags. Palm scoops. The faint, overlapping crescents of a million bodies moving earth when it was wet and letting the sun fire it into brick. It is architecture without architects, or with architects so small and so numerous and so patient that their work looks like geology now.

That is the strange part about the erosion, or the lack of it. This place is not being worn down. It is being made. It is embryonic.

In a normal landscape, water carves. Here, water is still learning how. When it rains — and it does, intermittently, violently, without warning — the water does not have rivers to follow yet. It sheets across the pans, fills the shallow basins one by one, spills over the lowest lip in a braided veil a centimeter deep, and sits there, hot and still. It has nowhere to go because the builders haven't finished giving it somewhere to go. Some of the basins are connected by little notches, little spillways, unfinished canals that end in a blunt wall of damp earth. Some just hold their water until it is gone. This whole continent is a watershed under construction, a pre-river, raw and undeveloped. The channels are proposals. The gullies are first drafts. If you come back in ten thousand years, there might be a river system here. Right now there is only the intention of one, drawn in mud.

And the water itself is sparse but insistent.

It is hot. Not a romantic dry heat — a pressing, physical weight of heat that comes up from the baked ground as much as down from the white-yellow sun directly overhead. Normal light, relentless light, with no shade except what the low walls cast in thin, sharp lines at noon. The air shimmers. The clay burns through the soles of your feet. And yet, it is damp.

Not wet. Damp, like a breath held. In the early morning, before the sun clears the haze, every surface sweats. Condensation beads on the cool undersides of the basin lips, runs down in dark tracks, and collects in the lowest corner of each pan as a palm-sized mirror. By midday it is gone, or rather, it has gone into the air and into the clay. If a cloud passes over — and the scale of this place is so vast that it makes its own clouds — it can rain hard for seven minutes on one square kilometer and not at all a kilometer away. You can watch a grey pillar of rain march slowly across the plain like a living thing, drumming briefly on the pans, turning the pale earth to dark chocolate where it touches, and then moving on, leaving steam.

In between, pockets. A deeper basin, perhaps the size of a house, where the water stays for weeks, thickening to brine, filming over with dust. A long, shadowed trough where the ground stays cool and dark to the touch all day. A pit where the clay has cracked and the cracks have been carefully widened, not by weathering but by digging, to coax more condensation out of the night air.

That is where the life is. Sparse, scattered, exacting.

There are no animals here. No birds cross the sky. No insects whine. The silence is profound and complete except for the quietest sounds, and even those belong to non-living things: the faint tick of clay contracting as it cools, the whisper of wind over a thousand lips, the almost inaudible fizz when a new drop of rain hits hot dust. When you breathe, you hear your own breath.

The vegetation knows better than to cover the ground. It punctuates it.

A single, wiry shrub every thirty or forty meters, rooted precisely in the corner where water lingers longest. Its leaves are small, grey-green, waxy, curled inward. Its roots, you suspect, are enormous, a slow grey net under the walls. Elsewhere, a smear of dark green-black cyanobacteria in the bottom of a basin that held water last week, now drying to a papery crust that will revive with the next rain. A clump of stiff grass-like tufts growing along the spine of a wall, where dew runs down both sides. Nothing touches. Nothing crowds. Each plant has its basin, its cistern, its made place, and a great deal of empty, sun-flached clay between it and the next. The density of presences is very low, balanced with emptiness. It feels tended, not wild — but tended by something that left, or by something so slow you cannot see it move.

And it is active. For all its quiet, nothing is still.

The clouds build every afternoon from the moisture the morning left behind. The pans fill and empty, fill and empty. The salt blooms white on the drying edges and is washed back into solution. The crust of algae cracks, curls, flakes, and is blown as dust into the next basin. The walls soften slightly with each rain and are re-hardened by the sun, a little lower, a little wider each cycle. You can see tiny collapses, tiny slumps where a wall has given way and spilled its load, and nearby, equally tiny repairs where wet mud has been freshly daubed and smoothed over. Whether that is wind or water or the last, unseen builders continuing their endless, techless work, you cannot tell.

The rule of this world is simple: water is precious and heat is constant, so you shape the earth to catch what little there is, and you do it without tools more complex than a body.

You walk, and your footsteps echo slightly off the hard pans. You step over wall after wall after wall, each one identical and each one slightly different. In one, a perfect circle of damp soil around the roots of a solitary plant. In the next, dry and empty except for a cracked pattern like shattered porcelain. In the next, a few centimeters of milky water reflecting the sky, perfectly still, with no ripple, because there is nothing to make a ripple.

In every direction, the same. To the horizon, and beyond it. A made plain the size of a sea, low and hot and quiet, damp to the touch, unfinished, waiting for its first true river.
