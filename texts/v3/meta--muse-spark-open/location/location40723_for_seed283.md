It is low country, and has been low country for a very long time.

Not the drastic low of a canyon or a trench, but the broad, patient low of a basin that forgot how to be anything else. You can walk for days and never gain a hundred feet. The horizon, when you can see it at all, is not a line but a soft interruption where one dark mass of green gives way to another. This is a place on the scale of a watershed — a valley without walls, a floodplain too wide to be called a plain, braided with old channels and new ones and channels that haven't decided yet. It goes on past where you can hold it in your head all at once.

The heat here is not sharp. It is a weight. Even after the sun goes down it doesn't leave; the air holds it like water in a cupped hand. Daytime has a white, ironed-flat kind of glare high above somewhere, but down here that hardly matters, because almost none of it reaches you.

The canopy closes over like a roof. It is not one layer but five or six, staggered and interlocking — emergent crowns high up catching what light there is, then smaller trees tucked beneath them, then saplings, then shrubs, then ferns and broad-leaved herbs fighting for the last scraps of dimness, then the ground itself which is hardly ground at all. Everything grows into everything else. Vines as thick as your leg stitch tree to tree. Epiphytes load every branch until the bark is invisible underneath mats of green and trailing root. There is no bare earth to see. Even where a giant has fallen, and it has laid down its long body in a slow, creaking collapse that took years to finish, its trunk is already a nursery for seedlings standing shoulder to shoulder in the moss of its back. It is packed, saturated, fully occupied. There are no gaps. If something dies, something else is already living in the space it left before the air has cooled.

There are no buildings here. No cut stumps, no roads, no brick, no wire, no concrete. No straight lines that stay straight for long. The old rivers have done their work and come to an equilibrium — their banks are rounded and rooted, their oxbows cut off and slowly becoming marshes, their meanders wide and lazy and certain of themselves. This is an old landscape, settled into its shape. Erosion still happens, but it happens as manners happen in an old house: predictably, respectfully, grain by grain. Mud slumps into the brown water and is carried a little way and put down again.

And for all that green, it is not wet. Not really. This is not a swamp you wade through. The water lives in pockets, in manners intermittent and miserly despite the heat. A creek runs for half a mile and then simply sinks between roots. A hollow in clay holds rain for a week after a storm and turns soupy with tadpoles and then dries to a cracked plate again. At dawn everything is wrung with condensation; huge leaves tip and spill cold droplets onto your shoulders and neck in steady arrhythmic plinks. The air itself is damp enough to drink in small breaths, but the ground underfoot squeezes damp rather than splashes. Your footprints fill slowly, if at all.

You would think a place this full would be loud. It isn't.

The fauna is overwhelming — incomprehensibly so — but it has learned to be quiet about it. Or perhaps you have simply failed to learn how to hear it yet.

In the darkness under the leaves, which is a true darkness even at noon, every surface moves if you watch it long enough. Beetles the size of your thumb work their way up trunks in columns dozens wide, mandibles clicking just below hearing. Ants farm. This is where the industry is. There are no factories, no engines made by hands, and yet the whole basin manufactures without pause. Leaf-cutter avenues run for hundreds of yards like green highways, stripped leaf confetti moving steadily into mound-cities as big as hills, ventilated by chimneys of precisely tempered mud, exhaling warm, fungal air. Termite towers rise higher than a man, thrumming faintly with the metabolic heat of millions inside. Beehives hang like black drums in the mid-canopy and pulse with a low, electrical thrum that you feel in your sternum more than hear — not a buzz, but the idling of a great dynamo. Wasp paper mills whir. Caterpillars in their thousands, arrayed side by side on the underside of a single leaf, chew in synchronized silence. Spiders repair vast sheets of silk that sag between trees and collect everything that falls.

Nothing roars. Nothing chatters for show. Birds sit puffed and glossy in the dark and watch with eyes adjusted to nothing. Even the frogs call only rarely, a single deep bottle-tone that carries a long way because nothing else competes with it. Mammals pass as shadows and ripples — snouts in the leaf litter, a slow coiling weight shifting on a branch above — but they do not crash or scream. Sound is expensive here, heat-expensive. Everything conserves.

At night, which is not much darker than day except it is more complete, you understand the economy better. A few fungi bloom with a pale, greenish-white luminescence along a nurse log, just enough to outline the ceaseless traffic around them. Beetles carry their own cold lanterns. But mostly you navigate by touch and by that low industrial hush — the sound of a thousand thousand small jaws, wings, and feet doing work at scale, electrified not by wires but by the sheer density of muscle and chemistry. The air smells of warm pulp and turned earth and crushed leaf and the faint, acrid tang of formic acid, like a workshop floor where oil has soaked into wood for a century.

Nothing happens fast. Growth is relentless but unhurried. A fig strangles a host tree over twenty years. Water finds a new course over a season. A colony buds off a daughter-colony a few feet over and spends a year arguing the border. Predators wait perfectly still for hours because hurry would waste water, would make noise, would disturb the great, quiet, packed, breathing production line that feeds everyone.

If you stand still long enough, something will walk over your boot, and then something else, and then something else, until you are not separate from thoroughfare. The forest does not notice absence of builders; it never needed them. Its industry was already here — slow, damp, hot, dark, and saturated with life doing exactly what life does when given enough time to become efficient.
