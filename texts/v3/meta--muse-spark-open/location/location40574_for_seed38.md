This is not a forest. A forest has ground.

This is a Roof.

You are high — not mountain-high, not sharp and stony-high, but continent-high. The world rose here a long time ago and then forgot to fall. An ancient lift, a swell in the crust so old it has been weathered back down to almost flat. The elevation remains — the air is thinner than it should be for how gentle the land is — but the topography has been erased. No peaks. No ridges. Just a plain at the height where clouds live, a peneplain that goes on until curvature and mist take it.

It is warm here. Not hot. Warm in the way deep compost is warm, in the way breath is warm. The air at this altitude should be cold, but it isn't. It holds heat close to its skin.

The light is dim. Not night-dim, never night. Day here is a perpetual veiled noon. The sun is a brighter smear behind a stratum of cloud that never breaks and never quite rains. It condenses instead. You can see it happen. The air gets too full and then gives up, beading directly on every surface. Everything drips without a storm ever having passed.

Because water is everywhere, but rarely as open water. This place is wet at the level of saturation.

There is no river system left — erosion finished its work an aeon ago and there is nothing left to cut. Instead water moves as a sheet, as a seep, as a slow down-slope creep just under and just through the living mass. Step and your foot sinks two inches and water wells up around your ankle, clear and brown-tinged, smelling of tannin and green iron. Lift it and the imprint fills and stays. Weeping rocks are everywhere, though you have to dig to find the rock. Old shield-stone, smoothed to whale-backs, black basalt and rose quartzite polished by a billion dull weathers, now just occasional hard bones under the mat. They are always wet. Water beads from their blind undersides continuously.

The mat is the place.

Vegetation here is not *on* the land. It *is* the land. Mosses upon mosses upon mosses, but that word is too small. There are trunks that are not trunks — columnar growths of fern and epiphyte and strangler and vine and lichen and something with no name, all growing into each other so thoroughly you cannot tell where one individual ends and another begins. There are trees that exist only as a scaffold for other trees that exist only as a scaffold for curtains of hanging liverworts the size of sails.

You walk not on soil but on a living sponge 30, 50, 100 feet deep. The surface is an uneven, rolling meadow of giant bromeliads and fleshy, light-seeking plates that turn slowly toward that smudged sun, each plate big as a door, slick with condensation. Under that, a dark. A loam made of living and half-living weave — roots like cables, hollow stems that act as wells, chambers where air is held.

It is dense, but not choked. This is the density of a library, not a thicket. Layers. Everything is layered with air and water between. You can push aside a hanging veil of translucent, veined leaves — each leaf longer than you, humming faintly with trapped moisture — and find behind it a cathedral aisle where the great pillars stand, their bark not bark but a colony of interlocked, photosynthesizing scales. Far above, maybe 200 feet up, the top of the canopy makes its own false ground, a second surface that collects mist and hosts its own upside-down meadows. Light filters down in shafts that are green, then amber, then gone.

And it is vast enough to make its own weather. The whole plateau breathes. Warm air rises from the warm, wet mass, hits the low cloud deck, condenses, and falls again as perpetual dew. At noon the condensation thickens into a light, warm fog that rolls *under* the upper canopy, a slow white river flowing between the column-trunks. Things that live in the middle-air come out then.

The fauna is abundant, but quiet and slow, as befits a place where nothing needs to hurry.

There are no predators that chase. There are browsers the size of deer but with the body plan of insects — six-limbed, plate-backed, moving with immense, careful slowness, their mouths huge soft funnels that vacuum moss-spores and mist. They step so gently the mat barely dimples. Above them, things like stingrays drift — flat, photosynthetic animals, animal-plant middles, that flap once every minute to keep their altitude, filtering the fog for midges.

Sound is mostly water and weight. Drip. Creak. The long exhalation of a bromeliad plate releasing its stored rainwater all at once in a silent pour. The soft *pop* of air bladders in the deep mat adjusting. Sometimes a low, resonant boom as a distant column, waterlogged beyond bearing, slowly topples over centuries — not a crash, a sigh that lasts for days.

You hear your own breath too loud.

You are not the first here. But no one ever lived here.

The built things are scattered and do not belong to a system. A stair carved down into the living mat and then abandoned, its steps now each hosting a complete miniature bog. A hut woven from living stems that still grows, keeping its shape even though no one maintains it. A line of standing stones, each stone now tilted at a different patient angle as the sponge under it flows, lichen having drawn perfect white circles on their north faces. Smaller things: a clay bowl that has become a frog-pond. A road that was only ever a line of packed peat and is now just a slightly drier stripe where different flowers grow.

No metal. No glass. No cuts that didn't heal over. Whoever was here used only what would become part of the place again. Or the place took it back. It's hard to tell which.

The weirdness is not violent. It's a wrongness in the rules.

Gravity here feels lateral. Water doesn't always run down; sometimes it wicks up along a hanging root a hundred feet and gathers in a bulb-leaf that never spills. Seeds germinate before they hit anything, sprouting in midair on the static charge of the fog. Some of the plants have eyes — not metaphorical eyes, but clear, lens-like nodules that track the dimness of the sun without seeing. If you stand still long enough, the mat around your feet will adapt to your shape, growing tiny, warm root-hairs against your skin not to eat you, but to drink the salt of your sweat. It is not hostile. It is profoundly indifferent and faintly curious.

This is a place at the end of geology. Nothing is being made anymore. Nothing is being destroyed quickly. It is settled into its final form: a warm, wet, high, flat, endless garden with its own dim sky, patient beyond any human schedule, quietly and endlessly growing into itself.

You could walk for months and the light would never change. You could lie down and be covered, gently, and you would not be smothered. You would become a slightly drier stripe where different flowers grow.
