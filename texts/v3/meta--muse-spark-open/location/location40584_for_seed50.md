It is not an ocean. It is not a lake. It is a *room* of ocean that got lost on its way up a mountain.

You find it by accident, because it is not supposed to be there. Three hours above the treeline, where the air thins and turns cool and clean in your lungs — the kind of cool that makes stone taste like stone — the main trail switchbacks away toward the pass. If you follow the cairns instead, the small ones, the ones made from three flat stones stacked by hands that liked balance, you come to a notch in the ridge that shouldn't be deep enough to matter. A fold. A pocket.

And inside the fold: water.

### The Bowl Before It Becomes A Bowl

The geology here is wrong in the way new things are wrong. Erosion hasn't had time to learn manners yet.

This was not carved by a glacier and then softened by a thousand summers. This was torn open recently — a winter landslide, a frost-heave, a shrug of the basalt — and the rock is still raw. The edges of the basin are sharp enough to cut rope. The walls are unweathered, black-grey, flecked with quartz that hasn't dulled yet, rising in clean, vertical sheets like pages of a book that was just bound. No scree slope. No smooth lip. When you lay your hand on it, it feels *manufactured*, as if the mountain was cut with a saw yesterday and left damp.

It's small. Intimately, impossibly small for what it holds. Maybe thirty strides across, maybe forty. The size of a chapel. Or a cistern for giants. A hidden scale, an embedded world — you could miss it entirely if the wind shifted. From above, it just looks like shadow between rocks.

But it holds too much water.

### A Wall That Forgot To Fall

The water does not behave.

At elevation, water is supposed to run away. To find the lowest place and flee. Here it stays. The basin is full to bursting and then beyond — a lens of water perhaps fifteen feet deep at its center, impossibly clear, cool enough to ache your teeth — and it doesn't spill over the sharp lower edge the way water should.

Instead it hangs.

The entire western side of this pocket opens to the drop — to empty air and the thousand-foot fall to the valley you climbed from — and the water meets that drop and *stops*. It forms a vertical face. A wall of rain that never rains. A liquid cliff, shivering, taut as skin, from the raw rock lip down to nothing. You can stand on the dry stone two feet away from a sheer, standing wall of water that goes from your knees to over your head.

It's not ice. It moves. Constantly, turbulently, chaotically. The surface of the pool inside churns without wind, and the wall itself roils — currents race up it as often as down, eddies bloom and collapse like lungs. If you stare too long you get vertigo, because light goes *through* it normally — bright, high-altitude noon light, unfiltered, normal-daylight bright — and you can see the valley *through* the water-wall, warped and rippling, as if you were inside an aquarium looking out at the world.

The rule here, the only rule this pocket seems to have kept, is: *water remains here*. How it remains is negotiable.

Sometimes a bead breaks off the wall and hangs in the air for a long second before snapping back. Sometimes the whole surface shivers and throws a cold spray three feet inland, scenting the rock with mineral and far-ocean salt — though you are four thousand feet above any sea that ever was.

You understand, after a minute, why erosion is so low. Nothing erodes. The water is not allowed to cut.

### The Sound

The sound is why no animals stay.

It is deafening in a way that has nothing to do with volume and everything to do with enclosure. The pocket acts as a perfect, raw-rock amphitheater for the water's turbulence. Every swirl, every collision of current against impossible surface tension, is amplified and thrown back at you from three sharp walls.

It is not a roar. It's a low, ceaseless, thunderous *thrumming* — the sound of a storm heard from inside the cloud. A deep bass churning as if the sea were trying to invent itself inside a bathtub, overlaid with higher, glassy shrieks when the wall ripples too fast, plus the constant slap-slap-slap of water hitting water that has nowhere to go. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself think. When the wind hits the outer face of the wall, the whole thing booms like a drumhead the size of a house.

And yet the air a few feet beyond the water-wall is silent. The valley below is quiet under its blanket of cool air. The deafening belongs only to the pocket.

You feel it in your sternum before you feel it in your ears.

### What Lives, What Was Left

Because of the cold and the noise and the lack of soil — just raw stone and raw water — almost nothing grows here.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of reverence. In cracks where windblown grit has lodged, there are mats of black lichen and a few hardy alpine cushions, star-shaped and low, flowering white for a week a year. A single, stunted krummholz pine has rooted at the high eastern edge, its trunk twisted away from the pocket as if leaning away from the sound, its needles dusted with perpetual spray. Its roots grip raw rock; there is no duff, no humus. Everything is just coming into being.

Fauna is rarer still. You might see a water strider on the inner surface, impossibly skating on water that is vertical a foot away. Once, a high lammergeier will pass overhead, reflected and distorted in the wall, and veer off, disturbed. In the deepest part of the pool — if you dare to lean over the churning, cold center where light refracts in strange, oily rainbows — there are pale shapes. Not fish, not quite. Translucent, hand-sized things that flicker in and out of visibility, never surfacing, never touching the wall. They live only in the turbulence, born from it. You only see one at a time, and when you blink it is somewhere else.

People have been here before you, but not many, and not to stay.

Scattered. That's the word. A half-collapsed low wall of dry-stacked stone on the sheltered side, built to break the spray. A fire ring that has been used maybe three times, with charcoal that never quite dries. Carvings on the softer rock — not letters, but lines: wavy marks counting the motion of the wall, hand-deep grooves where someone tried to record which way the water swirled on which day. Primitive tech — chipped stone, a rusted iron piton hammered into the basalt with a wooden toggle still tied to it with sinew and frayed cord. Someone once tried to lower a bucket into the pool. The bucket is still there, half-submerged near the wall, spinning slowly forever without sinking or being pulled out, turning with the chaotic current.

No houses. No footings. Nothing that tried to claim this place. Only markers left by people who came, saw a room full of ocean on top of a mountain, listened to it try to roar itself into existence, and left an offering of their attention before the noise drove them back down.

If you stay long enough, the cool spray soaks your sleeves. The light stays normal, noon-bright, indifferent. The wall shudders. A colder draft cuts down from the ridge. And for a half-second, the turbulence aligns — the churning stills — and the water becomes perfectly, impossibly flat. A mirror held upright. And you see yourself reflected in a vertical sea, small, empty space all around you, a single figure in a stone room that hasn't decided what it wants to be yet.

Then it breaks, deafening again, and churns on.
