It is not a valley. It is not a desert, though it has the logic of one.

It is a pocket.

High — very high — in the raw, unfinished spine of a mountain that hasn't decided what shape it wants to be yet. Up where the air thins and cools, but here, inside, that doesn't apply. Here is hot. A sealed, dry heat like the inside of a kiln after the firing, when the door is still closed. Not a mountain-top heat from sun; there is no sun here. It is geothermal heat, mineral heat, the heat of pressure and chemical hunger. The rock itself is warm to the touch.

You find it only if the wind has pried a slab loose. A crack, no wider than your shoulders, breathing hot, dry air out into the cold alpine. You squeeze through — stone still sharp here, unweathered, just-born — and the crack opens not into a cavern, but into a chamber. Perhaps four meters long. Perhaps three across. A lung. A hidden room with no business being hollow.

The light is almost none. 0.5/3 is not pitch black, it's a darkness with work-lights in it. The walls themselves give it: veins of cooling basalt and raw feldspar still exhaling the dull red-black glow of deep rock, like coals banked for the night. It is enough to see edges, to see movement. Not enough to see color correctly. Everything is iron-red, sulfur-black, heat-haze amber.

There is no water here. Not really. What little there is — 0.4 out of 3 — exists as a slick, as a chemistry. The air is impossibly dry, a salt-flat dry, a desert dry, sucking at your mouth and eyes. If you lick the wall, it tastes of bitter salts and hot metal. In the coolest corners, where the hot breath of the chamber meets the cold seep from the high rock outside, a thin efflorescence forms — not dew, not frost, but a dry crust of mineral salts that sweats out of the stone and then instantly desiccates. It crackles. It never pools. It never flows. This is a non-aqueous place. The hydrological cycle here is replaced by something with sulfur and vaporized minerals, cycling from hot wall to cold wall and back.

And there is no plant life. Not a blade, not a lichen. The walls are too hot, too dry, too new. This is pre-plant rock, just-extruded, still angular where it fractured. The floor is not soil but grus — raw, un-sorted, just-broken mineral sand that has never been touched by root or rain. Small pyramids of it have slumped from the ceiling where the chamber is still forming itself, embryonic geology. The edges are all sharp. There has been no wind here to round them, no water to smooth them. Everything is fresh fracture and cooling joint. Emerging terrain. Undeveloped.

And yet it is packed. Completely, impossibly packed. Density 2.9/3 in a space the size of a small bedroom.

Because of the occupants.

At first in the dark-red glow you think the walls are moving. That the rock is boiling. Then you understand: the walls are covered. The floor is covered. The low ceiling is covered. Fauna — abundant — but not as you expect it.

They are beetle-things. Furnace-things. Each about as long as your thumb joint, flat and black as slag, with carapaces that shine dully in the mineral light. No eyes. No need. They tile themselves across every surface in a living pavement, edge to edge, back to back, leaving no mineral visible except where they have recently mined it. And they are all working.

This is where the parameters stop contradicting and make sense: **Built: none. Tech: industrial.**

There are no hives. No nests. No constructed structures at all. No queen's chamber, no walls made by them. They do not build.

They *are* the factory.

Each one is a small engine. You hear it before you see it clearly — Sound 2.4/3, loud in a space this small. Not a chirp or a buzz. A clank and hiss and thump. As they crawl, incredibly slowly — Dynamic 0.8/3, patient to the point of geological — their backs vent. A tiny *tik-tik-tik* of legs on hot stone, then a *hiss* of pressure released, then a dull *thunk* as their mandibles strike the rock, chipping off a flake.

They eat the mountain as it is born. They scrape raw basalt with iron jaws, take it into the furnace of their gut — you can see the glow through the seams in their plates — and extrude it behind them as something else: a fine, glassy, black pellet, still hot, still ticking as it cools. The floor of the chamber is inches deep in these pellets, in this industrial slag. The air smells of hot iron and ozone and struck flint.

They do not pile it. They do not make anything with it. The pellet is waste. Or maybe it is spore, or egg, or message. It doesn't matter. The point is the process: intake, firing, exhaust, step forward one body-length per hour. Milling the embryonic rock as fast as the earth makes it. The chamber stays the same size because they mine it at exactly the rate it tries to close.

It is deafening, in aggregate. Thousands of tiny pistons firing out of sync. *Tik-hiss-thunk. Tik-hiss-thunk.* The rock conducts it. Your chest conducts it. There is no other sound. No drip. No wind. Only this low, dense, mechanical roar of insect industry in the dark.

If you stand still too long, they will tile over your boots. Not aggressively. Just as they would any other warm surface. They will test the leather with their glowing jaws, find it not basalt, and move on, leaving a faint soot print. They do not care about you. This is not a weird place. Weirdness 0.4 — it is shockingly normal, once you accept its rules. A hot, dark, dry kiln high in a young mountain, where life found that the best niche was to become the erosion that hasn't happened yet. To be the weather, indoors. To be the factory with no product, whose only purpose is to keep this one hidden room exactly as packed and exactly as empty as it is.

You cannot stay long. The dry heat bakes the water out of you, the noise fills your skull, and the faint, acrid steam of their exhaust makes the air hard to breathe. You back out through the shoulder-crack, back into the cold, thin, bright, silent alpine, with the sound of that tiny, relentless, packed industry still ticking in your ears, continuing its slow work in the dark, whether anyone is there to hear it or not.
