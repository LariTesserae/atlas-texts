The Stair is not a mountain anymore.

It was, once. You can tell in the way the air thins. At 2.8 you are high enough that sunrise hits you before it hits the lowlands, high enough that your lungs work for it, high enough that clouds come apart on you like surf. But there is no mountain here. There is only construction, contiguous from the foot to the crown, so thorough that no natural substrate is left exposed anywhere. Everything is made.

The Stair is built.

It is not a city on a mountain. It is a mountain made of city. A cone of terraces, each one a ring the height of a three-story house, stepping up and up until the upper rings disappear into brightness. Every ring is faced with its own material history: the lowest tiers in poured basalt-concrete, pitted and dark; the middle tiers in brick and riveted iron plate, green where copper has wept; the upper tiers in pale limestone block and enameled steel, still holding the glare of the high sun. You can read time in it. This is what erosion 2.1 means here — not raw, not crumbling, but settled. Established. The edges have been weathered soft. Corners are rounded where a century of wind has worried them. Iron has bled rust in long vertical drools down every face. The expansion joints have opened a finger's width and been filled with grout again and again until they look like veins.

And on every horizontal surface, vegetation.

Lush is too clean a word for it. The Stair is furred. Tame jungle grown over an industrial skeleton. Because the water is not wet, not oceanic, not rivered — it is damp. Intermittent. 1.4/3 means condensation and pocket and accident.

There is no watershed. There is collection.

Each terrace catches its own rain. The night at altitude is cold even when the day is warm — warm overall, 2.1, a greenhouse warmth — and so every metal surface sweats at dawn. The condensation runs down struts and cables and leaves and is guided by design into gutters, into cisterns built into the lip of every ring. Between rains, the Stair drinks itself. You hear it before you see it: the ticking of drip lines, the sigh of overflow pipes, the sudden clatter of a cistern gate opening somewhere above you and sending a sheet of stored water down a sluice to the next level.

In the pockets where water lingers — the sagged roof of an old rolling mill, the interior courtyard of a habitation block, the abandoned cooling basin on Tier 73 — the vegetation goes ecstatic. Broadleafed engineers' ivy with leaves as big as doors, engineered for shade tolerance, has mutated back into wildness. Bougainvillea in violent magenta pours over gantry cranes like a spill. On the sunward faces, terrace rice and terrace corn are grown in straight industrial rows in soil that was itself imported, made, composted from the city's own waste for generations until it is a meter deep wherever the concrete allows. On the shadow faces, ferns, mosses, black mold that looks like velvet from a distance.

There is nowhere that is not planted or paved or both. That is the density: 2.2. Layered, near-touching.

You stand on Tier 42 — mid-range, open scale, you can see at least fifteen tiers above and fifteen below, each curving away east and west until perspective flattens them into a wall — and you are in three ecosystems at once. At your feet, in the drainage cut between the walkway and the retaining wall, isopods and millipedes the length of your thumb churn through leaf litter. At eye level, finches — hundreds, thousands — move in nervous clouds between clotheslines and power lines and vine lines, no distinction to them. They nest in the hollow arms of decommissioned semaphore signals. Above you, on the overhang of Tier 43, swifts have built mud tubes along the underside of every I-beam, and hawk analogues — broad, copper-feathered, with the scream of bent metal — ride the thermals that the warm stone throws up all day long.

Fauna is abundant, but not wild in the old sense. It is city fauna, adapted to total building. Lizards with translucent bellies that live only in the expansion joints. Moths that eat enameled paint and have wings the exact flecked grey of basalt-concrete. Feral cats, lean and high-altitude, that walk the power conduits like tightropes. In the cisterns, carp, fat and orange, introduced a hundred years ago to keep larvae down, now sacred and untouchable. People — because you are fauna too — are everywhere at low density but constant presence. Not crowded, but never alone. A woman beating rugs over a railing. A crew in canvas masks scraping rust off a water tower. Children running along a pipe as wide as a road, shouting, their voices swallowed and thrown back by the hard surfaces.

Tech is industrial: 1.7. Not sleek. Engines, not networks.

The Stair moves, but slowly — dynamic 1.1, a patient process. Nothing flashes. Everything turns. Along the outer face of each retaining wall runs a chain-driven freight lift, open, clanking, carrying bales of taro root upward and ingots downward at walking speed all day and all night. Inside the walls, you can feel the thump of stamping presses from the fabrication floors, one thump per second, never varying. Electrified, yes, but with that early-electrified feel: big knife switches, humming transformers in cages, filaments in caged bulbs that buzz yellow-white. The air smells of hot oil and ozone and wet earth. Factories are not separate from habitat here; a habitation row might be stacked directly over a forging hall, so that the floor of your kitchen is warm from the furnaces below, and you grow tomatoes up there because they love it.

And it is bright. 2.6. At this elevation the atmosphere has thinned enough that the light has an edge. Noon is white and total, bouncing off pale block and water and leaf until even the shadows are full of green reflected light. You squint always. Laundry snaps like flags. The Stair was built to face the sun — every terrace lip is slightly canted to catch more — and the whole southwestern quadrant at midday is a blaze you can hardly look at. People wear wide hats of woven vine fiber, stained by sun. The plants orient visibly, turning. Even the lizards flatten to absorb it.

Sound: 1.8. Loud, but not chaotic. A regional chord.

Wind through girders. The industrial thump. Water dripping, always dripping, from tier to tier. Insects making a field of white noise that at this altitude and warmth never really stops. The chitter of birds echoing off hard parallel surfaces until a single call seems to come from six places. The distant claxon of the freight lifts changing shift, not urgent, just informational. If you are quiet, you can hear the Stair itself creak as it warms — concrete and steel expanding minutely, ticking, like an old house waking.

The weirdness is low — only 0.8 — so the world holds together. Physics as you know it. But it accumulates at the edges. There are places where the building has been so total for so long that the vegetation has started to build back. Fig roots, fed by constant condensation, have pried apart a retaining wall on Tier 58 and now hold the earth behind it in a net of roots instead of concrete, and no one repairs it because the roots hold better. On the highest tiers, where the air is thinnest and brightest, the iron has been left to rust completely through and the wind has carried soil into the rust flakes and seeds have lodged there, so there are gardens growing vertically on the sides of walls, without horizontal ground at all.

There is no summit. At the top, the rings get too narrow to farm, too steep to live. The last tier is a crown of water tanks and radio masts and maintenance shrines, all furred with lichen that at this height glows faintly orange, drinking only fog. From there you can look down and see what you live in: not a building, not a town, but a weathered, breathing topography made entirely by hands, now settled into its own balance. A made mountain, damp between rains and lush despite it, warm in the sun, loud with life and machines, slowly turning over its water and its ore and its people, tier to tier to tier, breathing condensation out at night and drawing it back in at dawn.
