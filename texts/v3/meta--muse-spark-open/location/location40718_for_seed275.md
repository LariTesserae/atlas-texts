It is a city that was not built on ground, but inside it.

Far below any sky, in the cold, fresh wound of the earth where the chamber is still coming into being. This is not a cave in the old sense — not a hollow worn smooth by water over millions of years. The stone here has no memory of rivers. The walls are raw, embryonic. Basalt and salt-white tuff still sharp with their first fractures, stepped in clean, unworn shelves where the continent shivered and pulled itself apart a thousand years ago. A new valley, still under construction, a kilometer across and kilometers long, its ceiling a single, low arch of unweathered rock that has never seen rain. The air inside is cool, a constant 8 or 9 degrees, the chill of deep cellar stone. It doesn't bite; it preserves.

And there should be nothing living here. There is no water.

Not in any form you would recognize. No riverbeds, no seep, no damp, no condensation on the walls. The floor is a dry mineral field, fine as cement dust, pale and alkaline, that hangs in the air for hours when disturbed and then settles again without a trace of mud. Any water that ever bled up from below was sealed off long ago by salt. The atmosphere is thin and dry and faintly metallic on the tongue, like breathing inside a sealed machine. It should be a void.

Instead, it is a forest.

Not a forest that grew — a forest that *is* the place. The vegetation does not cover the landscape; it replaces it.

They are not green. In the normal, steady light they are a pale, waxy ivory and bone-yellow, flushed in places with a bruised lavender where the light strikes them longest. They are not trees as surface people understand trees, because trees need water and these have never touched it. They are aeroflora — lithovores, gas-eaters. Their roots never sought a water table. Instead, colossal trunks, twenty to thirty meters around, rise directly out of the dry mineral floor or plunge from the unfinished ceiling like pillars holding the young earth apart. Their bark is not bark but layered felt, dry and warm to the touch despite the cold air, made of densely matted aerial fibers that comb moisture and hydrocarbons straight out of the atmosphere and split the stone for trace minerals.

Between these infrequent giants there is a great deal of emptiness — this is a sparse place, for all its totality. You can stand on the factory catwalk and see a hundred meters to the next trunk, across open, dusty air. The scale is regional; the chamber repeats for what feels like town after town, valley after valley, the same rhythm of pillar and void. But the emptiness is not barren. The space between is filled with a second, finer architecture: endless curtains and sails and scaffolds of living thread. From every pillar, smaller branches throw out horizontal limbs that never grew leaves, but instead extruded vast sheets of translucent membrane, thin as paper, veined like moth wings. They hang in tiers, overlapping, filtering the light, trembling slightly in the slow air currents. They are lungs. They are solar panels. They are walls and roofs.

And the people came and understood immediately.

The settlement is not cut into the plants. It is hung among them and grown with them. That is the only way to live below-surface when stone is still being born. There are no foundations to pour — the floor is too soft and too new. Instead, industrial platforms of black iron and reclaimed basalt are bolted directly into the living pillars. Factories, refineries, hab-blocks, strung along the trunks like bracket fungi, three or four stories high, connected by swaying bridges of cable and woven fiber that the plants obligingly thicken and tighten if you leave them alone for a season.

It is unmistakably urban and unmistakably industrial. On the largest trunk — called the First Pillar by those who live on it — a vertical town climbs six hundred meters. You can see the soot-marks where forges vent their heat, not from chimneys but through chimneys the plant has hollowed for them, the interior walls now glazed black and warm. Great toothed engines pulse slowly on the mid-levels, not loudly, pressing the living membranes to extract the waxy oils the flora produce instead of sap. That oil is everything here: fuel, food-stock, lamp oil, plastic, medicine. It beads on the undersides of the membranes like sweat and is harvested by mechanical scrapers that run day and night, their electric motors humming low. Conveyor belts carry dried fiber to looms. Cable-cars run on humming high-tension wires strung from pillar to pillar across the empty air.

The light is normal. That is the strangest thing, until you remember where you are. There is no sun. High above on every pillar, at the level of the stone ceiling, the settlers have mounted long, linear suns — industrial flood-bars that burn with a steady, neutral white, 10 hours on, 10 hours off. The plants have learned to face them. The membranes turn their faces to the bars and flush faintly pink with photosynthetic charge, even though the chemistry is not photosynthesis as the surface knows it. In that even, workmanlike daylight you can see clearly to the far wall of the chamber, kilometers away, where more lights are being mounted on raw rock that cracked open only last winter.

It is mostly quiet. For a city of engines, it is unnervingly quiet. The dry air and the vast curtains of fiber swallow sound. The engines hum, the scrapers tick, the cable-cars whine on their wires, and then it all falls away into open, cool silence. So quiet you can hear the plants themselves: a soft, periodic creak as a pillar grows a millimeter, a faint dry rustle as a membrane unfurls overnight to catch new light.

And everywhere, life flickers.

Fauna is abundant here, but not as you expect. Nothing drinks. Great pale moths, the size of coats, drift between pillars on powdery wings, never landing, sipping vapor through long proboscises coiled under their chins. Grazing beetles like polished rivets chew the dead outer felt of the pillars, excreting fine, dry pellets that the factory folk collect for fertilizer. Things like translucent lizards sprint along the fiber bridges, farming colonies of even smaller mites. In the dim understory between trunk-bases, flocks of blind, chirping bulb-birds — round, featherless, warm — nest in the dust, incubating their eggs with heat borrowed from the factory vents.

The whole system is active, in motion, even as it seems still. The chamber itself is still widening — you can hear, once or twice a year, a deep, clean *crack* from the far end as a new fissure opens, extending the valley another few dozen meters into raw stone, exposing fresh mineral for the hungry roots to find. The plants reach for it within weeks, sending out pale exploratory tendrils that harden into new pillars. The settlers follow, extending a light-bar, a platform, a bridge, letting the town grow not by planning but by tending.

It is a place that should not work — a dry forest underground, a city without groundwater, an industry without smoke, a farm without rain — and yet it does, cool and bright and patiently humming to itself in the quiet dark under the world.
