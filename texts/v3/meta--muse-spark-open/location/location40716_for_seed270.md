It is not a valley. It is not a plain.

It is a room that forgot it was a room.

You find it by accident, low to the ground - low everywhere, the whole world here is low, a near-flatness that has given up on ever being high again. To get inside you have to crouch where the concrete has buckled by a finger's width and slide sideways between a rust-swollen support strut and a wall. The gap is only as wide as your shoulders. Inside, the air changes.

### The Hollow

Think of a service pit. Or an inspection vault. Or the drained sump under a factory that was built, worked for fifty years, and then built over. No one drew this place on a plan; it happened in the negative space between things that were intentional.

It is about the size of a single-car garage, but lower - the ceiling is the underside of something massive and industrial above you. A slab, a press-bed, a generator housing. You can't stand upright; you move in a stoop, hands sometimes touching the floor. That's the scale of it: intimate, pocketed, contained. A hidden place embedded inside a larger, indifferent machine. From outside, you would never know it was here. From inside, it feels like the whole world.

The terrain of this world is not ground in any natural sense. It's a laminate of ancient surfaces.

The base layer is concrete, but concrete so old it has stopped being concrete. Windblown grit and fifty winters of condensation and fifty summers of dry heat baking out from the machinery above have eroded it until it is smooth as beach stone, shallowly pitted, the aggregate exposed like fossils. In the low spots the dust has lithified into a pale crust, a salt-and-lime pan a few millimeters thick that crackles under your palm. It is absolutely, profoundly dry. Not desert-dry where a plant might still know to look for water. This is sealed-environment dry, void-dry. Any water that ever was here has long since wicked away into the air or been drunk by rust. The crust tastes of iron and ozone if you get dust on your lips.

On top of the concrete is a second geology: industrial fallout, settled into its final form. A century of fine black scale shaken off the machine above, oil that turned to varnish and then to a hard amber skin, iron filings that rained down and rusted together into low, lumpy ridges. Everything is rounded. Nothing has an edge anymore. Even the bolts that hold the ceiling slab up - thick as your wrist - have had their threads smoothed away to dull nubs. Erosion here wasn't done by rivers. It was done by vibration, by dry air, by time. This place is primordial in the way an old tool is primordial: it has settled into the last shape it will ever have.

Light comes in normally, which is strange. It shouldn't, this deep. But the machine above leaks it. Two caged work-lamps, still hot, still wired, have been burning here on industrial timers for longer than anyone has been checking them. They are leaded glass, amber and humming, on a simple circuit: on for sixteen hours, off for eight. No one remembers to replace them, but no one has turned them off either. The light they throw is even, flat, factory-yellow, without shadow or glare. It makes the dust visible as a permanent haze, but you can see everything clearly - every pit in the concrete, every movement.

And there is so much movement.

### The Occupants

At first you think the floor is the floor. Then it resolves.

It is not empty. It is not *mostly* occupied. It is overwhelming. It is dense in a way that makes you hold your breath without meaning to. The ground, the walls, the low ceiling, the air an inch above the ground - every available surface is layered with life, near-touching, shifting.

There are no plants. Nothing green has ever taken root here; the air is too dry and too cool and the substrate offers nothing to a root. No moss, no lichen even. The mineral world stayed mineral.

Instead: fauna. An entire ecology built without photosynthesis.

The base layer - by far the most numerous - are the grazers. You could call them isopods, or beetles, or woodlice, but they aren't quite any of those. They are flat, slate-gray, the size of a thumbnail to the size of your palm, with overlapping plates that look pressed from the same scale that falls from the machine. They move slowly, endlessly, lapping the crust with soft mouthparts, scraping up iron oxide, dried oil varnish, salt, skin cells. Where they have passed, the crust is left a shade lighter, etched with faint paired trails. They cover the ground so completely that you can't put a hand down without touching three or four of them; they don't scatter. They flow around you, over your boot, patient.

On them, and among them, the second layer.

Thinner, faster, louder: crickets the length of your finger but with no wings, just long, filamented antennae that taste the air. Hissing roaches with translucent bellies. Something like a pale centipede, a hand-span long, that keeps to the edges where the concrete meets the wall, flickering. They are the middle predators, and they are also everywhere. You hear them before you see the detail - a constant dry rustle, a clicking, a ticking of chitin on concrete and chitin on chitin. It is loud, not in a sharp way, but in an enveloping way, like being inside a room full of people whispering at once. The sound of many small legs.

And then the top.

Hanging from the ceiling slab, clinging to the underside of the machine with hooked feet, are the big ones. They are cool to the touch and cool in the air they breathe out. They look at first like folded laundry or discarded tarps - until one shifts and you see the joint. They are bulkier, furred not with hair but with a dense, felted fiber that collects dust. They are about the size of rabbits, or small dogs, with blind, flat faces and wide, heat-sensing pits where eyes should be. They don't move much. They wait. Every few minutes one will drop, incredibly fast for its bulk, onto the seething carpet below, scoop a mouthful of crickets and isopods, and haul itself back up with a soft grunt and a whir of the loose ceiling plate it disturbs. Above you, their droppings fall as dry, odorless pellets that the grazers immediately attend to. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves.

The air is cool - 50, 55 degrees - and very still except for the vibration. The machine above cycles. You can feel it in your molars: a low industrial throb, *whump ... whump ... whump*, as a press or a compressor somewhere in the factory that no longer has workers does its job. With each pulse, a little more scale sifts down like snow. The animals pause, then resume. The lamps hum louder than they should, a 60-cycle electrical drone underneath the chittering.

The rule of this place is simple and complete, and it has been complete for a very long time:

*What falls from above is food. What lives below is food for what clings above. No water is needed because water is not part of the bargain.*

These creatures do not drink. They metabolize rust and hydrocarbon and each other, exhaling a faint, metallic, slightly sharp smell - like hot electronics and old pennies. Their bodies are dry to the touch. If you killed one, it would not bleed so much as leak a clear, oily fluid that would evaporate in minutes.

Scattered through all of this, almost invisible until you stare, are the built things. Small structures. Not for you. A few low walls, no higher than an inch, made of glued-together iron filings and varnish pellets - windbreaks and corrals the isopods build and abandon. A single, perfect cube of stacked concrete chips, the size of a die, that a cricket-like male has assembled as a display. Faint chalky marks on the ceiling where something has scraped again and again. The ghost of a stenciled number - **B-9** - on the underside of the machine, almost worn away.

No one tends this. No one needs to. It is an engine that fell out of the main engine and kept running on its own terms, ancient, flattened, dense, and furiously, loudly alive in its one small, cool, dry, ordinary-lit pocket of the world.

You realize you are breathing shallowly so as not to inhale anyone. If you stay still long enough, they begin to treat you as terrain again, and a gray plate-bug climbs, without hurry, over the toe of your shoe and continues on its endless, polishing circuit.
