This is the Low Lattice.

Not a desert the way Earth has deserts. There is no sand here, no dune-sea. It is a low place — a basin so wide and so shallow you only know it is a basin because the horizon all the way around is toothed, a faint, even rim of young mountains like the lip of a fresh impact. The elevation is almost nothing. You are walking at the bottom of a saucer the size of a county, and the air is still warm and heavy in it, trapped and held.

And it should be dead. There is no water.

Not in the human sense. There is no river, no lake, no sea, no spring, no ice, no rain. The sky is a clear, high, normal blue-white with a normal sun in it, warm on your shoulders — about body-temperature warm — but the sky never clouds. The ground gives no seep. If you dig, you find no dampness.

The ground is what holds the story of dryness.

It is sharp. This place is young. Geologically newborn. The floor of the basin is not eroded smooth, not weathered. It is a plain of fresh black glass and pale salt, broken as it cooled. Hexagons and pentagons of basaltic glass the size of houses, the size of fields, separated by upwells of white and faintly pink salt where the mineral brine extruded and flash-dried millennia ago and then stopped. The edges where glass meets salt are knife-edges, an inch high, a foot high, untouched by wind because there is almost no wind. Not a grain has been rounded. If you kneel you can see your reflection warped in the glass, and the tiny, perfect salt crystals like frost growing along the seams, still growing, but so slowly that no human watch would ever see them move.

The air is mostly quiet. Without water there is no rush, no lap, no drip. Without wind there is no hiss through grass. Your footsteps on the glass make a clean *tok, tok*, and the salt makes a faint *crunch* like sugar, and then the sound is gone, swallowed by the heaviness of the warm, low air.

And yet it is lush.

That is the first breakdown, the first place your brain refuses.

From every salt seam, from every fracture in the glass, grows a forest so dense you cannot walk in a straight line. It is not green. Not at first. Up close it is green-black and copper and deep wine-red, but from even a hundred meters away it hazes into a lush, impossible emerald because of the way it bends light.

It has no need for water. It never had it.

Each plant — though plant is a poor word — is a dry engine. The trunks are not wood but bundled cables of silica fiber, matte black, branching according to rules that are almost fractal and then deliberately violate them. They split into filaments as fine as hair, as thick as your wrist, and each filament ends in a leaf.

A leaf is not a leaf. It is a flat, manufactured thing. Thin as paper, rigid as mica, a millimeter thick, overlapping its neighbors so densely that the canopy is three, four, seven layers deep. The upper surface is a photovoltaic black so perfect it seems to drink the light; you can feel warmth coming off it in the cool of the shadow. The underside is a mirror. Not silver — a living interference mirror that shifts color as you pass, mother-of-pearl, oil-slick. The forests do not drink. They breathe the mineral air and eat the light directly, condensing carbon straight out of the atmosphere, building their bodies from airborne dust and trace salts that ride the thermal stillness.

When the sun is directly overhead the whole basin flashes. The mirrored undersides bounce light down and down and down through the layers until the understory glows, even though no direct sun reaches it. The whole forest is a light trap. From above it would look darker than shadow. From inside it is luminous, dappled with slow, liquid movements of light that have no source.

There are no flowers. There is no rot. That is the second breakdown.

Nothing here decays the way you expect. There are no microbes to turn leaf-litter to soil. Fallen leaves — flat, black-mirror shingles — lie where they fell, perfectly preserved between the glass plates, hundreds deep. They do not curl or brown. They stack like pages. The forest floor is a dry archive of itself, crackling faintly underfoot. The air smells of hot ceramic and ozone and faint iron, like the air next to a computer left in the sun.

This is dense life at equilibrium. Nothing is growing, nothing is dying, yet everything is present. The scale of it is disorienting — you came down off the sharp rim and expected scrub, a few tenacious stalks. Instead the green-black density runs to the other side of the basin and beyond, a town, a valley, a small country of silent, layered geometry, unbroken except for the black glass clearings where the salt plates are widest.

That is the technology. There are no buildings, no ruins, no artifacts, no roads. No hand has placed stone on stone here. But the forest *is* the artifact, or the afterimage of one.

You see it when you stare too long at a single leaf.

The leaf is not grown, it is assembled. Nanoscale text runs along its edge — not text, circuit — lines of structure smaller than a wavelength that diffract the sun into that normal light you see. Each leaf knows its neighbors. If you hold perfectly still for a long time, you will see a leaf twitch a fraction of a millimeter without wind, adjusting its angle by a calculation you can almost feel as a pressure in your teeth, a faint, low, inaudible thrumming that lives in the bone. The whole basin is in silent communication, rebalancing its light budget. A photon not captured here is bounced there.

And the weirdness — the reality breakdown the place is built on — is that the forest is not fully *here*.

At noon, when the light is vertical, you can see through a gap in the canopy straight down to salt and glass, and then a little further. There is depth where depth shouldn't be. The stack of fallen leaves below your feet is transparent in a way that is not transparency; you see three meters down, then thirty, then the leaves repeat. The same branching pattern tiles downward into an infinity that has no dust in it, no dimming. The forest is dense sideways, but dense *downwards* in a way that violates volume. It is folded. You could walk for a day and cover five kilometers, but the photosynthetic surface you passed over is larger than a continent if you count the folded layers.

Sometimes a leaf on the lowest visible layer flickers and is briefly somewhere else, edge-joined to a stem half a kilometer away. Nothing moves. The forest is static, frozen-equilibrium. Yet space leaks.

You thought you saw an animal once, which is rare here. Fauna is rare to the point of myth. There are no herds, no flocks. Only a single kind of thing, and you are not sure it is animal.

It was the size of your hand, a dry lattice of the same mirrored leaf-matter but folded into a dodecahedron that turned itself inside out without opening. It hung in the air between two trunks, motionless for minutes, then was six feet to the left without having crossed the distance, still motionless. It did not look at you. It reflected you, but delayed, as if the light taking your shape needed a second to calculate you.

It made no sound. When it vanished — not flew, vanished — the air did not stir.

The rules of this place are not Earth rules:

1. No consumption without replenishment. Light in equals light stored; dust in equals lattice out. Nothing is wasted, so nothing changes.
2. Sharpness is preserved. Young rock stays young. A broken edge never dulls. Time passes but does not erode.
3. What is dense is not crowded. You can always step between the trunks, even where there should be no room. The forest makes space as you need it, a courtesy of folded geometry.

It is warm. It is silent. It is untouched and it is beyond any making you understand, and it is not going anywhere. You can sit on the warm glass at the edge of a clearing and watch the light loop itself forever between the black tops and mirrored bottoms of leaves, a lush, dry, silent engine the size of a region, breathing without water, thinking without moving, holding itself in perfect, never-changing, sunlit equilibrium while the young salt crystals at your feet hold their sharp, perfect, unsmoothed points against a sky that will never rain.
