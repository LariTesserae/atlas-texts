It is not a valley and not quite a summit.

To get there you climb — a long, hot climb up a young mountain, the kind that hasn't learned to be gentle yet. The lower slopes are bare, sharp, black glass and raw basalt still cooling from the inside out, stair-stepped in fresh columns that haven't had time to round off. No scree, no softness. The rock is new enough that it still rings when you strike it, and the edges will cut your boots.

And then, abruptly, the climb stops. Not with a peak, but with a lip.

You step up and over and you are inside it.

A bowl, no larger than a hamlet — you could walk its rim in twenty minutes if you didn't stop every ten steps to stare. Perhaps three hundred meters across, an almost perfect oval bitten out of the top of the mountain like a thumbprint in wet clay. High, so high the air should be thin and cold, but it isn't. It is hot here, a wet, greenhouse heat that doesn't belong at this elevation. The light is normal, full-spectrum daylight, sun directly overhead, but the heat doesn't come from the sun. It comes from the stone itself, and from the air that won't leave.

This is a place that keeps its weather.

The atmosphere inside the bowl is thicker than it should be. It pools here, held in by something you can't see — past the scattered tech, past understanding. From outside, from the slopes below, the bowl looks empty, a dry crater. From inside, it is oppressively humid. Water condenses out of nothing, beads on the cool side of every shadow, weeps continuously down the interior walls. The rock sweats.

The walls of the bowl are vertical, twenty or thirty meters of that fresh, sharp basalt, and they weep. Not in streams but in a thousand slow seeps, dark veins where water forces its way out of the stone even though there has been no rain. It gathers in the lip's overhangs and drips, constantly, with a metronome patience: *pit... pit... pit...* onto broad leaves below. The sound is the loudest thing here, because mostly it is quiet.

The bottom of the bowl is not flat. The mountain tried to heal itself after whatever made this — impact, extraction, birth — and pushed up a low, broken floor of terraces and tilted plates. Between the plates are clefts and pans where water collects. There is no lake, no river to speak of. Instead water is everywhere thinly: a skin of warm water over black stone, ankle-deep marshes caught between rocks, shallow mirrors that reflect the sky and double the heat. You never have to search for it. It finds your feet.

Where the water sits, life is lush out of all proportion to the sparseness around it. Vegetation here doesn't carpet; it clumps. Each wet pan supports a single, emphatic burst of green — a grove of scale, cathedral scale. One terrace holds six or seven umbrella trees, their trunks pale and swollen with stored water, their leaves huge, waxy, cupped to catch the drip from above. The next terrace is empty stone and a single mat of emerald moss breathing steam. The next, a tangle of ferns taller than you, hissing faintly as they unfurl, and a congregation of pitcher plants the size of barrels, their mouths open, full of warm rainwater and slow, digesting darkness. Between these islands: bare, wet, black rock, warm to the touch, empty. Lush, but scattered. A garden planted by someone with a sense of negative space.

Nothing grows on the walls except a thin, orange lichen that seems to grow *into* the rock rather than on it, spelling faint, branching circuits that pulse dimly after sunset. Not bioluminescence. Something else.

The built things are like that too — sparse, quiet, almost missed if you're looking at the plants.

There are four of them. Maybe five, depending on whether you count the one that failed.

On the highest terrace, half-sunk in water, is a low pylon of matte white, about waist high, featureless except for a seam that never collects condensation. It hums at a pitch you feel in your teeth more than hear. From it, at noon, a distortion rises — a vertical lens of heat-haze about two meters wide, hanging unsupported in the air, showing the other side of the bowl slightly magnified, slightly delayed. If you toss a seed through it, the seed comes out the other side a few seconds slower, a little warmer. The pylon is not keeping the weather in. Or not only. It is keeping *something* in, or out.

A second structure is just markers: three slender rods of dark metal driven into the stone at the bowl's cardinal points, each no thicker than your finger, each perfectly straight, each vibrating faintly in wind that isn't blowing. They sweat water on one side only.

The third is the ruin — or the beginning — of a walkway that never finished: seven floating steps, slabs of the same white material, hanging in the air in a gentle arc from one tilted plate to another, about a meter apart, stationary, indifferent to gravity. They don't move when you step on them. They don't seem to know you are there. Under them, the water is completely still, reflecting them perfectly even when the other pools ripple.

The fourth is small, easy to miss among the pitcher plants: a cube, black, the size of a heart, that opens and closes like a slow lung, exhaling cool air that smells of iron and distant rain, even though you are standing at the hottest point for a hundred kilometers around. When it exhales, the ferns near it shiver. When it inhales, which it does for much longer, dust and gnats and loose water droplets drift toward it from every terrace, vanishing at its seam.

The rules here are slightly wrong in a way that accumulates.

Water here doesn't flow down, exactly. It flows *toward* — toward the lowest of the floating steps, toward the breathing cube. If you watch a cleft long enough you will see a rivulet run uphill for a handspan before remembering itself and dropping. Gravity is polite but negotiable.

Sounds carry too well and then stop abruptly, as if the air has rooms in it. A dragonfly — one of the only animals, rare, huge, with wings like mica sheets — will pass overhead and you will hear every beat of its wings with startling clarity, and then, two meters later, hear nothing at all. There are maybe three kinds of fauna. The dragonflies. Something small and white that dives in the pitcher pools and never surfaces on the same side. And a calling thing you never see that makes a single, low *coo* every ten minutes or so, from a different terrace each time, even though nothing could move that fast unseen.

Everything is active, but not busy. The clefts fill and spill, fill and spill. The pitcher plants slowly close and open on a cycle of hours. The orange lichen on the walls spreads a millimeter a day, visibly if you are patient, etching new branches. The humidity condenses, drips, evaporates straight off your skin, condenses again. The bowl is breathing, cycling water and heat in a closed loop that should have exhausted itself long ago.

It is hot, wet, quiet, enclosed. A small hothouse at the top of the world that shouldn't be wet, a high place that shouldn't be hot, a young rock that holds old water, a natural amphitheater with unnatural acoustics. If you stay too long, your sweat stops evaporating. The air is already full.

And if you stand in the center, on the one dry plate where the water avoids, and look straight up, you can see the distortion from all four pylons meet — a faint, normal-light shimmer like a soap film across the mouth of the bowl, sealing it. Dust motes rise to it and hang, suspended. Rain from outside, which you can see falling on the slopes below, never falls in.
