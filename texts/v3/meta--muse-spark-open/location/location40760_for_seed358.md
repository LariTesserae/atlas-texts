It is not a valley. It is a cut.

A long time ago, or not very long ago at all, someone decided that the ground here was more valuable than the ground itself, and they took it away. What is left is a basin the size of a small county set down into the world like a thumbprint in wet clay. From the rim it doesn't look deep at first because there is no horizon to judge against — the far wall is two or three miles off and hazed out — but the road down spirals for twenty minutes in low gear, past switchback after switchback cut raw into banded earth. You can feel the air get heavier as you descend.

The elevation is negative. Not dramatically — you wouldn't get a nosebleed — but enough that the world above ceases to matter. The wind up on the plain goes over the top of you. Down here the air is still and warm, a held breath. Warm, not hot. Like a greenhouse in late August, like the inside of a mouth. 85 degrees and damp enough that your shirt never really dries. The walls of the basin weep.

That is the first thing you notice about the water. It isn't a lake, not quite, and it isn't a river. At this depth you've gone below the water table and the earth regrets being opened. Every exposed stratum seeps. Dark sandstone bleeds in slow sheets. Shale sweats. Even the freshest cuts, the ones made last week by the bucket-wheel excavators where the rock is still sharp and unoxidized and the color of a fresh bruise, are already glossy with water finding its way out. It gathers in the lowest tier in a shallow, warm, rust-colored sea that was never designed to be there — an accidental lake a mile across and rarely more than four feet deep, turbid with rock flour and oil.

But "lake" implies stillness and this water is not still. It is worked. All around its marshy edge — and it is all edge, a fractal of inlets and settling ponds and borrow pits and trenches — are the pumps. Low, scattered, industrial pumps on stilts and pontoons and rusted catwalks, each one a different age, each one left by a different crew. None of them are ever turned off. They hammer and suck and cough, lifting water up one tier to a levee, where it immediately begins to leak back down through the gravel. The lowlands are a maze of dikes made of spoil, black fabric, and stacked sandbags that have melted in the heat. Between them are fields of reeds.

Vegetation should not be here. The rock is too raw, too salty, too freshly turned inside-out for soil to have formed. Erosion hasn't had time to make anything graceful. There are no gullies or hoodoos or smoothed hills, only tool-marks: vertical walls with the ridges of teeth still in them, spoil heaps like the spill of some colossal quarry-truck that haven't been weathered into hills yet, terraces that end abruptly in a ten-foot drop where a shift ended early. It is pre-landscape. Embryonic geology, still showing the trauma of its birth.

And yet in the wettest places, life has taken the invitation. Not forest, not meadow — sparse, stubborn things. Black-green algal mats that slick every standing surface below the splash line. Beds of cattail and phragmites so dense in the clumps they look planted, then nothing for a hundred yards of bare silt. On the drier terraces, tamarisk and saltbush in singletons, stunted, white with dust, their roots reaching down to that ever-present wetness a foot down. Everything beaded with condensation even at noon, because although the light is dim it is also diffuse and wet.

The light never gets strong down here. The sun has to clear the rim before it can reach you, and by then it has to come through the permanent haze that hangs in the basin — diesel exhaust and water vapor and fine rock dust churned by machines, held in place by the warm inversion. There is no direct glare, no sharp shadow. It's a big, pearlescent half-light, like being under a dirty skylight. Sodium lamps burn on the pump stations and on the high cranes all day and all night; you stop noticing which light is natural after a while. In that dimness, colors narrow to rust, to algae-green, to concrete grey, to the high-vis orange of workers' vests.

Because there are workers. And that is where the density comes in. For a place that is mapped as "scattered" — a few sheds, a maintenance prefab, a rusted water tower, a line of power pylons marching down the haul road — it is impossibly packed. The built things are few and far between, but the *occupants* are not.

Every tier, every catwalk, every warm shallow pool is occupied.

The fauna came for the water and stayed for the heat. This artificial wetland in the desert, this warm and predator-free sea below the world, has become a flyway stop that forgot how to be temporary. Clouds of midges rise at dusk so thick they show on radar. Swallows wheel over them in such numbers the haze flickers. In the reeds: rails, gallinules, red-winged blackbirds calling in thousands, their nests so close you could step between them if the mud would hold you. Frogs. An absurd, deafening number of frogs. Introduced bullfrogs the size of fists, and the native tree frogs that learned to live with them, and all of them calling at once. Turtles stacked three deep on every half-submerged tire and sheet of plywood. Herons standing like foremen over the settling ponds. And in the water itself — carp and tilapia and mosquito fish seeded originally to keep the midges down, now breeding in such warm superabundance that the shallows roil when you throw a stone.

And above and among them, the people and the machines. Three shifts. The mine still works the upper walls while the pumps work the bottom, so there are excavators moving with glacial slowness across the face, dragging their power cables behind them. Haul trucks the size of houses crawling up the spiral, so slow you can watch the heat shimmer off their engine decks for a long time before they are gone. Crews repairing levees with shovels, standing thigh-deep in warm water, shouting to be heard.

You have to shout, because of the sound.

It is deafening in a way that has nothing to do with suddenness. There is no bang, no crash. It is a saturated, built-up, unending industrial-natural chorus at the threshold of pain that you stop hearing as separate sounds after an hour and then start hearing again at night in your teeth.

The pumps are the foundation — a low, percussive *chud-chud-chud-chud* from dozens of stations slightly out of sync, so the beat never resolves. On top of that, the high whine of the powerlines and the cicada-like rattle of the sluice gates vibrating. The frogs and insects have learned to pitch their calls to fill the gaps, so the biological sound is not underneath the mechanical sound but *in* it — a shrill, reedy, endless mating trill that seems to come from the air itself. And periodically, every twenty or thirty minutes, one of the big excavators swings its boom and drops a load of overburden with a sighing roar that rolls around the basin walls for a long time before it can escape upward.

Nothing happens fast here. That is the rule of the place. Water seeps slowly. Rock weathers slowly. The trucks move at five miles an hour. The lake deepens by an inch a month, is pumped out by half an inch, deepens again. Even the frogs are slow in the heat, inflating their throats with a deliberation you can watch. Change is patient and accretive. A new spoil heap appears over a week. A new reed island anchors over a season. A pump rusts through over a year and is left where it dies because it's too much trouble to move, becoming another island for turtles.

If you stay down there for a day, your boots never dry. Your ears ring. Your clothes pick up a smell of warm mud and algae and hot oil. At night the temperature drops only a few degrees, the dimness goes from pearl-grey to a sodium-orange dusk, and the sound, if anything, gets louder as the frogs take over the chorus and the machines keep pounding water uphill against gravity, forever, in a basin that was never supposed to hold water at all.

On the rim, up where the wind moves again, you can turn around and the whole thing looks almost normal — a big, wet, humming, crowded construction site in a hole in the earth, going about its slow, loud, industrious business of existing.
