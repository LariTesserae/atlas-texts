It is not a cave. It is not a valley.

It is a wound.

Maybe a year ago, maybe ten — no one agrees and the rock doesn't keep that kind of time — the ground gave way. Not a slow collapse but a clean, sharp punch down through the limestone cap into the hot dark underneath. A cylinder, almost perfect, about three hundred meters across and sixty deep. The walls are still raw where they broke. You can see it in the strata: pale, flat beds of old seabed on top, then a sudden red band of ironstone, then the black basalt that still holds the heat of its making. All of it sheared vertical. Young rock. Unweathered edges that haven't learned to be soft yet. If you put your hand on the wall it will still cut you.

They call it The Kettle.

From the surface you would almost miss it. The plains above are dry and flat and white with heat. Then the air shivers a little, and there is a lip of rebar and warning paint and then — nothing under your feet. A mouth of shadow breathing warmer air than the desert.

Sun gets in for only about two hours at noon, straight down like a searchlight. The rest of the day the Kettle is dim. Not dark, exactly. Dim in a permanent, inhabited way. Work-light amber strung on every level, sodium lamps in cages, the low orange of forge-light from below, and everywhere the green.

That shouldn't be there, the green. It's too hot for this. The air inside the Kettle sits at a wet, heavy heat — 38, 40 degrees even at night — because the hole tapped something. A deep aquifer, a warm seam, a breath from below. It doesn't flood. It sweats.

Water condenses on everything. On the basalt it beads and runs in black trickles. On the undersides of pipes it gathers and drips, one drop every seven seconds, with patient regularity. On the corrugated roofs it patters after midnight when the upper air cools just enough. There is a single spring that found the sharpest corner of the break and now runs as a thin, warm river — never more than ankle deep — along the eastern wall, falling in three small, steaming waterfalls from level to level before it vanishes again into the rock where the drillers let it go. People don't pipe the water so much as guide it. Gutters, barrels, open cisterns. Every flat surface has a sheen. Damp, not drowned. Intermittent, but omnipresent.

And because of that damp and that heat and that two hours of brutal sun, everything grows.

This is a town that is packed — no, *grown shut*. The Kettle is only hamlet-sized, maybe four hundred souls could live here if they had room, but nearly a thousand do. So they built *inward* and *upward*. The walls of the sink are terraced with scaffolding that became street that became building. There is no empty space. Walkways made of welded grating hang over workshops that hang over homes that hang over gardens that hang over older workshops that were never dismantled, just built around. Corrugated iron, poured concrete, basalt block cut straight from the fall, all stitched together with black iron pipe and insulated cable as thick as your wrist.

It is undeniably urban. Not a camp. A vertical town with addresses: 3rd Ledge, Low Spur, River-Run. Laundry lines cross the open air. A municipal board nailed to the elevator shaft lists water rations and shift rotations. There are two main elevators, counterweighted and screamingly slow, and a hundred ladders and stairs cut into the rock itself, worn smooth in the middle already.

And it is industrial. This place was not settled, it was *installed*. When the hole opened, the company came first for the heat — geothermal. Then for the mineral water. Then they stayed because the Kettle makes its own weather and the basalt is easy to carve while it's still young.

Low down, near the bottom where the heat is worst, the geothermal plant breathes. Three big piston engines, painted a flaked cream and green, chugging on a cycle so slow you think they've stopped. *Chuff...... chuff...... chuff.* They drive fans the size of houses that never quite cool the air, just move it. Conduits sweat and hiss. Further up, machine shops turn all day, lathes and presses making parts for the engines that keep the shops alive. A bottling line — mostly idle now — still clinks brown glass bottles for the warm spring water every third day. Everything is electrified, everything hums at 50 hertz, a low tooth-buzz you feel in the railings.

But the vegetation is winning the argument.

Lush is too clean a word for it. It's *intrusive*. Vines with leaves as big as dinner plates pour over every abandoned cable. Tree-ferns have rooted in the crack between 2nd and 3rd Ledge and shot up twenty feet to drink the noon sun. Their trunks are wrapped in fairy lights and drying laundry. Morning glory strangles the bottling plant's smokestack and opens white every evening, huge and phosphorescent in the dim. Moss carpets the shady side of every wall so thick you can press a handprint into it. Banana trees, mango saplings, taro in standing water barrels — all planted, all now half-wild, all growing twice as fast as they should in this hot, still air.

No one weeds anymore. They prune. They negotiate.

Because there is a strangeness here that everyone has quietly agreed not to talk up too loudly. Things grow toward iron. Not light — iron. You can watch a new vine tendril over a day correct itself away from a work-light and curl instead around a rusty pipe, tightening there. The moss is thickest on the engine housings. A fig tree on Low Spur split a concrete wall to get to the rebar inside. The old-timers say the basalt is still a little magnetic from its birth, that the Kettle is a compass that points at itself. The engineers say it's just the condensation and the heat. Both keep their tools oiled either way.

Animals know better. Fauna is rare. A few geckos, flat and translucent, live on the high walls where the sun hits. Dragonflies hunt over the cisterns. Once a week someone sees the blind white catfish in the lower pools. That's mostly it. Birds don't come down. The air is too heavy, the walls too sheer. At night you can hear a single bat make its rounds above, but it never roosts inside.

Sound is the other thing that makes newcomers uneasy. For a packed industrial town, it is *mostly quiet*.

The engines are slow. The people have learned to be slow with them. Voices are low because sound carries brutally across the open cylinder and everyone can hear everyone. The waterfalls mutter. Metal pings as it expands and contracts. Leaves rustle without wind. Even the machine shops run dampened, with rags stuffed where they can be. When the noon sun finally spears down, the whole Kettle goes silent for a few minutes because the light is too bright and the heat is too perfect, and you can hear every drip hit every barrel across sixty meters of still air.

Change here is patient. Rust spreads over months. A vine thickens around a stair rail over a season until the rail is part of the vine. The terraces sag a millimeter a year under the weight. The water carves a groove in the basalt the width of a hair each flood. No one is in a hurry. No one can be — the elevator only goes so fast, the air only lets you work so long, the heat holds you in place.

At the bottom, past the engines, there is a place where the builders stopped. The original floor of the sink is not flat but tipped, a jumble of sharp, fresh boulders the size of houses that fell and never settled. Between them are pools of warm, perfectly clear water full of lime-green algae and the roots of everything above, hanging down like hair. It steams gently in the work-light. People throw coins in there, and the coins go dark within a week, furred over with mineral and moss.

If you stand on the lowest walkway — Low Spur, where the air is hottest and the leaves are biggest — and look up, you see the whole town at once. A single room. A grove. A cathedral made of scaffolding and living things, all crammed together under a hard white circle of sky that never gets bigger.

You understand then that no one is going to fill this hole in, or climb out for good. The desert above is empty and sharp and dry. Down here it is damp, dim, hot, crowded, humming, and alive in a way that holds you. The Kettle keeps what falls into it.
