This is not a valley. This is not a mountain. This is the place *inside* the mountain that the mountain was built to contain.

### The Cistern

High up where the air goes thin and mean — at 2.0 — there is a pocket cut into the crown of the range. From the outside you would never find it. A service door, half-swallowed by frost-rimed basalt, steel so cold it burns to touch, set flush into a cliff face that the wind has polished to a black glass. Inside is a room not much larger than a chapel's nave, maybe thirty meters end to end. That's all there is. Hidden-scale. A single-feature world, fully contained, like a lung held inside a chest of stone.

Everything in here is made. This is what *total built* means. There is no natural substrate left. The floor is poured concrete with a radial drain pattern cut into it. The walls are concrete, then steel plate, then concrete again. The vaulted ceiling is riveted iron ribs, each rivet head fat as a fist and sweating ice. Pipes run everywhere. Not along the walls — they *are* the walls. Bundles of them, dense, layered, near-touching. Twelve-inch mains, two-inch feeders, steam jackets, overflow runnels, valve wheels the size of cartwheels, pressure gauges with cracked glass. Industrial — electrified, mechanized, scaled — but not modern. It is all engines without elegance: a 1930s hydrostation built by a dead empire and never decommissioned.

It is pre-formation. Raw-geology, embryonic. The erosion value here is 0.1 — nothing has had time to wear. The concrete was poured sharp and is still sharp. The rebar shows fresh where it was never properly covered. The iron has not rusted so much as it was *made* textured. Every edge is 90 degrees. Water has not yet had time to carve anything. Instead, water *arrives*.

### Water in the Cold

The temperature is the kind of cold that does not read as temperature at all but as a property of the metal. 0.1/3. Deep alpine cold, the cold of water at 2°C that refuses to freeze because it never stops moving. The air is wet to suffocation, 2.2/3 — not a flood, but saturation. The room weeps.

Condensation forms on every pipe and rock surface and never evaporates. It beads, runs, drips, joins. The ceiling weeps into a permanent low rain that never gets heavier or lighter. The weeping rocks of the mountain itself — the one patch of natural basalt left exposed behind a burst inspection panel — seep continuously, a slow dark stain that births a trickling sheet down the made wall. The floor is not flooded, but it is never dry: a half-centimeter skin of black water flows silently and perfectly evenly toward the central drain, a glassy, oil-sheened flow over concrete. The gutters along the pipe bundles gurgle full. The air tastes like cold iron and wet stone.

Light is dim, 0.6/3. There are two caged industrial bulbs on the vault, one flickering at a frequency you feel in your molars but never actually goes out, and one that has burned steady for forty years. They throw a sodium-orange light that does not reach the corners. The rest is shadow between pipes, and in that shadow, the water shines.

Vegetation is sparse — 0.6/3 — and it knows it doesn't belong. There are no forests here. Only the things that can grow on made things in the wet cold: a crust of black-green algae slick on the floor where the light hits, a beard of white-grey lichen hanging from a valve wheel like wet wool, a few clumps of hard alpine moss squeezed into the bolt-holes of the steel plate, each one stunted, frozen in place. They do not spread. They have been exactly this size for decades.

### The Density

Because the room is dense, 1.8/3. Many presences per volume, layered, near-touching. And the presences are not human.

Fauna: 2.7/3 — overwhelming.

In every other way this place is too hostile for life, but in the one way that matters it is perfect: it is stable, warm *just enough* in the pipe heat, wet always, sheltered from wind and predator, and it is LOUD in a way that keeps everything else out.

The walls are alive. Not metaphorically.

Thousands — tens of thousands — of mountain moths, each the size of your palm, the color of wet concrete, cling to the ceiling ribs. Their wings do not move. They shiver to stay warm, a collective, almost imperceptible tremor. Between them, in the pipe bundles, roost the bats — small, white-furred alpine bats with translucent ears, packed shoulder to shoulder like seeds in a pod, hanging in dense curtains. Below, on the warmest pipe of all, a slow river of black isopods, each as long as a finger, flows in both directions, antennae touching, shells clicking faintly against steel. And the cave swallows — too many to count — have built their mud nests on every flat rivet plate, every pipe elbow, lined up in brutalist terraces. They stare with unblinking eyes. None of them call. None of them fly.

They just *are*, packed in, breathing. The air smells faintly of guano and ozone and wet feather. You cannot put your hand on any surface without touching something living. You cannot stand anywhere without being within inches of a hundred eyes.

It is strange — weirdness 2.0/3. Wrong in the quiet, biological way. These species should not cohabitate. This density should not be sustainable. The moths should be eaten by the bats. The bats should be eaten by something else. But no one eats. Or rather, no one needs to. The system feeds on something else: the condensation itself, thick with mineral and algae and airborne plankton drawn up from the valley far below by the pressure engines. Open your mouth and the damp *feeds* you a little. The food chain here is the air itself.

### The Sound That Never Changes

Sound: 2.7/3 — deafening.

And Dynamic: 0.1/3 — never changing.

This is the rule of this pocket world, the contradiction that defines it. The turbines below this cistern — three stories down, you never see them — are turning. They have always been turning. Water from the high melt is pulled, pressured, released. The overflow valves in this room vent the excess pressure in a continuous, absolute scream of white-noise steam and rattling pipe-shudder and thrumming iron resonance. It is not loud like an explosion. It is loud like being inside a bell that is being struck forever. 110 decibels, maybe 120, of pure, static industrial roar. The roar of water through a too-small pipe, the whine of a dynamo under load, the judder of a loose plate that vibrates at exactly the same frequency forever.

You feel it in your molars and your sternum before you hear it. And then you cannot hear anything else.

But it never changes. Not in volume, not in pitch, not in rhythm. There are no gusts. No sputters. No day-night cycle. The gauges hold at the same numbers they held when your grandfather was born. The water flow never increases, never decreases. The moths never take flight *en masse*. The bats never wake screaming. The light never brightens. Time has reached an equilibrium here. A frozen equilibrium. The system was built, started, and left to run in a perfect, deafening, wet, cold stasis. It is under construction and already complete. Raw geology poured yesterday that will look exactly the same in ten thousand years.

If you stay more than a few minutes, your brain does the strangest thing: it edits the sound out. The deafening becomes silence. And in that silence, you can hear the thousand tiny breaths of the dense, packed life around you, and the ceaseless ticking of water dripping from steel into steel, in a room made entirely by hands, high in a mountain where no hands live anymore.
