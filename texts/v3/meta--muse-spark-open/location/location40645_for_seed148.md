It's a town that was built before the ground finished forming.

You come down to it — because it's always down from wherever you are — along a soft, unfinished slope of cold clay and basalt that hasn't decided if it wants to be rock yet. Low country. Cool air pools here. The elevation never rises more than a few meters, but the air feels pressed, weighted, like the bottom of a bowl. The geology is embryonic: everything is raw and rounded, surfaces without sharpness, hills that are more like swellings, streets that dip and rise over what were clearly root-bulges and half-born stones lifting themselves slowly up through the soil. There is no dust. Nothing has had time to erode.

And then the grove-city.

From the outside, it looks empty — devastatingly empty, for something so dense with meaning. A hamlet-scale urbanism, maybe thirty or forty structures, bounded on all sides by a dark, damp perimeter where the light gives out. You could walk its circumference in ten minutes if the streets would let you. They sometimes won't. The scale is small, intimate as a cathedral's nave or a single clearing in an old forest, a room made for one purpose.

But every building is plant.

Not overgrown. Not reclaimed. *Constructed* of plant, from plant, as plant. The vegetation here is total. It doesn't decorate the architecture; it *is* the architecture, the substrate, the infrastructure. Walls are living cambium, tight-woven ivy trunks thick as pillars that have learned right angles. Roofs are broad, shingled leaves the size of doors, overlapping in dark green and veined black, exhaling condensation in the cool air. Factories are hollow strangler figs the size of chapels, their aerial roots pulled taut into colonnades and pipework. Gutters are unfurled fern-fronds. Balconies grow themselves overnight and curl back by morning if no one uses them.

It's urban — legibly, bureaucratically urban. There's a grid, or there was. Street signs nailed into bark that has since healed around the nails and lifted them. Power lines, but they are thick green vines strung with porcelain insulators that hum with a low-voltage industrial current. Streetlamps with glass shades full of amber filament light, but most are cold now, because the town keeps the dark.

Light here is 0.3 — not night, but the permanent pre-dawn that lives under deep canopy. A darkness that isn't absence, but a medium. The sky is there, somewhere far above, but the ceiling of interlocked canopy and steam closes it out. What illumination exists is local and alive: the sodium-orange of the few working factory windows, the sickly yellow of a train-signal that clicks over for no train, and, everywhere, bioluminescence. Pale fungal seams tracing the mortar-less joints between living bricks. Clusters of fruit like paper lanterns, unpicked, glowing white-green in alleys. Your hands look blue in it.

It's damp, but not wet. Water 1.1 — interstitial water. There is no river, no lake, though the ground remembers rivers and holds their shape as shallow, plant-choked ditches that only run after a rain. For the most part water arrives as condensation. The factories — still running, somehow, though no one tends them — breathe out huge, cool plumes of steam through stoma-stacks on their roofs. The steam hits the cold air and the cold leaves and becomes dew. Dew becomes drops. Drops run down leaf-gutters in the streets and collect in axil-pools where the wall meets the sidewalk. Every morning the town waters itself. You hear it ticking. If you put your hand to a wall, it is slick. If you stay still too long, moss tries your shoes.

Cool, 0.9. The air is 10 or 11 degrees Celsius and never moves fast. Breath shows. The living walls like it. They exhale that green, wet-wood smell, compost-sweet, iron-bitter. It keeps everything from rotting too fast, and from drying out.

And it is empty. That is the first wrongness your body feels before your mind catches up. Density 0.1 — a near-void. For a town, for a jungle, it is hollow. The streets are too wide. The plazas are too large for the number of things in them. You can stand at the central intersection — where four avenues of root-asphalt meet, each lined with three-story tenements of woven hazel and reinforced kudzu — and see straight through three buildings because their winter-bare lattices are translucent. There's almost nothing per volume: just air, damp air, and widely spaced presences. No crowds. No cars. No piles.

But look closer and the abundance is elsewhere. Fauna 2.4.

It is *loud* with life, just not human life. The emptiness is what lets you see it. Moths the size of your hand drift between streetlamps like slow snow, dusting everything with scale-powder. Beetles with mirrored backs click industrially along the power-vines. In the axil-pools, salamanders with translucent throats pulse. The upper canopy — thirty feet up, where the factory crowns tangle — seethes with birds you've never seen and starlings you have, all calling in short, quiet phrases. Vines move not with wind but with the weight of possums and raccoons and things thinner and longer, things with too many joints, commuting. Spiders have taken the empty air as their own, stringing webs the size of billboards in the void between density, between buildings, from factory steeple to apartment fig. They are not intrusive. They are the town's telephone wire now. And everywhere, under every leaf, the rustle of a million mites, larvae, sow bugs, doing the town's slow digestive work.

It's mostly quiet — 1.1 — which makes the abundance more uncanny. The industrial thrum is backgrounded, dampened by leaf and fog. A generator chugs somewhere behind a wall of living wood, steady as a heart. A loose shutter of bark taps once, twice, then stops. Otherwise the sound is biological and close: drip of condensation into pool, soft pop of a seed pod opening, skitter, flutter that stops as soon as you look for it. The town is not silent; it is *holding its breath* to let you hear the small things move. To speak loudly here feels rude.

Dynamic 2.3 — active — and that is the second wrongness. Because despite the quiet and the emptiness and the darkness, nothing is still.

The town grows visibly. You can watch it. A tendril on the second floor of the post office — yes, there's a post office, its sorting boxes are seed pods, its mail long rotted — will inch forward while you stare, tasting the air. A new leaf unfurls over an alley with a soft hydraulic hiss, big as a blanket. Roots shoulder up through the already-soft streets, cracking the root-asphalt, seeking the dew-pools. Predation happens at street level: a pitcher-plant storefront, its awning a lolling lip of red-veined flesh, snaps shut on a pigeon that got too curious, and the whole building shudders as it digests. Across the street, a barber's shop made of bristle-brush branches combs itself.

The industrial and the organic are not separate systems. They are one metabolism. The factories are not buildings that contain machines; they are machines that have become buildings. On the north edge, the largest structure — four stories if stories were a fixed height — is a textile mill. Its looms are still working. They are not powered by electricity anymore but by growth; vines pull the shuttles, swell and contract. They weave not cloth but more vine, more leaf, spooling it out into the street where it takes root and becomes sidewalk. Its chimney doesn't smoke now; it transpires. Workers are gone but their aprons hang on hooks made of thorn and still twitch when the steam valves sigh.

This is where the 3.0 weirdness lives — the reality breakdown — not as spectacle but as municipal ordinance:

Rules here are horticultural.

1. **Topology is seasonal.** The town is small, walkable, but you cannot map it. Two streets that were parallel yesterday will be braided tomorrow because the trunks that define them have grown toward each other and fused. The hamlet-size area holds more interior space than its exterior should allow because interiors are hollow trunks, gourd-rooms bigger than the buildings that contain them. You walk into what should be a closet and find a long, empty hall of greenish pith with windows looking out onto a different street than the one you entered from.

2. **Inheritance is vegetative.** Anything left still for more than a day roots. Leave your backpack on a bench — a bench which is itself a low, patient branch — and by morning fine root-hairs will have stitched it to the grain. Leave longer and the town will incorporate it. There are bicycles half-absorbed into walls, a telephone booth that is now mostly leaf, its ringing a beetle trapped in its glass. The town does not steal; it *adopts*.

3. **Time is photosynthetic.** At true dark — which comes for about an hour when the factories cycle down and even the fungal seams dim — the growth stops and the town *revises*. You hear it if you're still there: a soft, collective creak, as if all the plants are shifting weight in sleep. When light returns, a door will be where a wall was. The bar you were in last night is two blocks west and one block inward. Not hidden — moved. The residents, if there ever were any, would have understood this as normal maintenance.

There are no people currently visible. That's part of the emptiness, and part of the industrial tech that outlived its operators. But the infrastructure is tended — not by custodians, but by fauna and by the town itself. Leaf-cutter ants march in perfect lines along the old trolley rails — the rails are now two parallel taproots, polished by use — carrying not crumbs but tiny screws, flakes of rust, which they pile at the base of the mill like offerings. A fox, thin in the cool, sleeps in the doorway of the town hall and wakes to prune a sapling growing over the threshold with precise bites. The spiders repair the power vines when they sag.

If you stay — and the cool and the dark make you want to curl up in one of those hollow, warm, pith-rooms — you'll notice the final, quiet horror/beauty: the town is under construction in the geological sense. Erosion 0.3, emerging. This whole low bowl of soft rock and soft wood is still being born. The ground under your feet gives slightly, not like mud but like cartilage. New hillocks press up overnight at the edge of town, bald, steaming, studded with pebble-like seeds. The town sends out runner-roots to meet them, to climb them. It is not an old, ruined place being taken back by nature. It is a very young place, raw and embryonic, that invented city-ness early and is figuring out how to be both.

Stand in the center at the dampest hour, when the factories exhale hardest and the cool air is full of floating dew like glass beads, and you can see the whole small system at once: the green architecture breathing, the abundant animals commuting through the empty volumes, the quiet broken only by drip and rustle and the deep industrial sigh, the darkness made gentle by scattered glow. The town does not need you to witness it. It will keep growing its streets, weaving its sidewalks, digesting what it catches, revising its layout while you sleep, regardless.

It has plenty of room.
