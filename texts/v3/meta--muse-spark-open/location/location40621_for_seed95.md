On the top of the world, there is a plain that remembers being mountains.

You don't climb to it so much as you surface onto it. The air thins and warms at the same time, a dry, high sun-heat that makes the thin atmosphere ring like glass. Elevation here doesn't mean peaks - not anymore. This is what is left after peaks have lived their entire lives and died and settled. An ancient peneplain, three thousand meters up, where the continent has been sanded down to its bedrock bone.

The rock is everywhere. Not jagged, not dramatic. That kind of drama was ground away a billion years ago. Everything is round-shouldered, humped, exfoliated in vast onion-skins the size of houses. Low domes of pink-grey gneiss rise and fall like the backs of buried whales, separated by shallow pans and runnels polished to a desert varnish, dark as tea-stain. There are no cliffs to cast shadow, no ridges to catch the eye. Only undulation, on and on, until distance swallows detail and the domes dissolve into sky. It is open in the way an ocean is open — valley-sized, town-sized, but without walls — you can see weather coming an hour before it arrives.

It is warm. Not hot. A high, steady, basking warmth that soaks into the stone and stays there. The stone itself radiates it back at night. At noon the light is normal, unfiltered, too direct. No haze, no cloud-filter most days, just a pale, washed-out blue pressing straight down, making every grain of quartz glitter, making every shadow sharp-edged and black underneath a rock lip.

There is almost nothing green, and then, suddenly, there is.

Vegetation is a rumor. A lichen crust — mustard-yellow, ash-green, rust-black — stretched tight over the north sides of the domes, so flat it seems painted on. In the pans where wind has dropped a palmful of grit, a few things have taken hold: cushion plants, hard as stone themselves, forming perfect low discs a foot across, covered in tiny waxy leaves. Widely spaced, one every twenty or thirty feet, each alone in its pan like an island. And bunchgrasses, straw-colored, shivering even when there is no wind. No trees. No shade but rock-shade.

Water does not live here permanently. It visits.

It comes as condensation, as sudden cold rain that drums on hot rock for ten minutes and vanishes. As morning dew that beads heavy on the cushion plants and runs down the smooth gneiss domes in temporary silver threads. As seeps. Every shallow pan holds a secret: under its skin of cracked mud, there is dampness. If you lift a flake of rock — and there are millions of flakes, curling off the domes like dead skin — the underside is cool and damp and smells of iron. Narrow, disconnected rills cut only an inch deep wander between domes, holding strings of clear pools linked by damp sand, never quite a stream, never quite dry. Dragonflies patrol them, though there is nowhere for a dragonfly to have come from.

Because this place is overwhelmingly full.

At first, in the quiet, you think it is empty. Sound here is a held breath. The wind, when it moves, moves high and thin. No birdcall carries far. The dominant sound is the faint tick of stone expanding in heat.

Then your eyes adjust to the scale of what is living.

The rock is carpeted. The air is thickened. Every dome, every pan, every damp crevice is occupied to saturation. Not large animals — there is nothing here to support them — but dense, near-touching life layered upon life.

Colonies of small, pale moths cling to the undersides of every exfoliation flake, shoulder to shoulder, breathing together so that if you approach, the whole sheet of them lifts at once in silence and resettles a foot away. Armored pill-bugs the size of thumbs march in endless, slow processions along invisible highways worn shiny in the rock. In the damp pans, the mud itself moves: a writhing mat of translucent tadpoles or nymphs or something between, mouths open, waiting for the next rain. And above it all, the air.

The air is strangely heavy. Midges, gnats, mayflies, creatures with no proper name hatch in clouds from the intermittent pools, rising in vertical pillars that hang motionless in the warm stillness. They do not buzz. Their wings make a sound below hearing, a felt pressure more than a heard one. Swallows should be here to feast. Instead, other things feast —

Perched on every cushion plant, on every raised quartz vein, are the watchers. Lizards, but not quite. Or insects grown large. Pale, long-limbed, mantis-like animals the color of the rock, each utterly still, each facing outward, each occupying its own dome or plant or pool-edge with exact spacing, as if placed by design. Their heads track without movement. When a pillar of midges drifts too close, a tongue too fast to see flicks out, and the pillar thins by one. No struggle. No cry.

And there are the builders.

This high plain has no technology. No wire, no cut stone, no road. But it is not untouched. Scattered across the openness, always at the center of a pan or at the high point of a dome, are structures. Small. One at a time, out of sight of the next until you crest a rise and find another.

Cairns of balanced flakes, three or four high, that cannot be natural — wind would topple them. Shallow spirals raked in the grit, a meter across, perfect as fingerprint whorls, with a single white pebble at the center. Hollow domes made of cemented sand, the size of beehives, with a single round entrance facing east, cool breath coming from within. They are not human. No hand made them. They are made by the dense inhabitants, in slow, patient cycles. Termite cathedrals on a world with no termites. Prayer wheels made by creatures with no gods that we would recognize.

Weirdness is not an event here. It is the law of the place. The longer you stand, the more wrong it becomes in a quiet way: the mica flecks in the gneiss all catch the light at the same instant, as if blinking. The cushion plants are all exactly the same size. The processions of pill-bugs never collide, never deviate, flowing around your boot as water flows around a stone, rejoining on the other side without pause. The pillars of insects hanging in the air do not drift with the wind.

Everything moves slowly, if it moves at all. This is old life. End-of-life life. Species that have had a hundred million years to negotiate boundaries and have settled into a final form where nothing wastes motion, where sound is unnecessary because everyone already knows where everyone else is. Change happens across seasons, across wettings and dryings. A spiral in the grit might take a generation to complete. A midge pillar might hang in the same place for three days.

If you stay until late afternoon, when the sun lowers and throws the low domes into long relief, you will see the plain reveal its true density. Every shadow under every flake hides eyes. Every damp crack glitters with eggs. Every dome has its watcher, its cairn, its silent column of floating prey. It is crowded beyond bearing and completely quiet, a high, warm, ancient table set for a feast that eats itself forever, politely, without hurry, under normal light.
