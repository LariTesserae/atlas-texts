It is a high place that has forgotten how to be a mountain.

You are on a peneplain at the top of the world — 4, 5, maybe 7 kilometers up, but you would not know it from steepness. There is no steepness left. This is what extreme elevation looks like after it has been eroded for so long that elevation stopped meaning height. The world here was thrust up once, in some hot, violent youth, and then was left alone for a geological eternity to be sanded down by wind without water. What remains is not a peak but a table — a vast, gently-domed shield of stone so old its own weight has settled it into stillness.

The light is normal. That is the first wrong thing. A high, thin, cool sunlight falls straight and clean at about midday-strength, with soft, short shadows. It has no visible source. The sky is not blue. It is a pale, flat, mineral white, like the inside of a shell, bright without a sun. The light simply *is*, even and cool on your skin, about 8 or 10 degrees Celsius — the temperature of shade in late October. The air is so dry it feels like no air at all. It does not move.

**The Ground**

There is no water here, and there has not been for longer than rock can remember. The ground is not sand. It is salt and basalt and chert polished into a skin.

Underfoot is a pavement of interlocking stones — each one rounded to an almost perfect pillow-shape, fitted together like the back of a turtle the size of a country. They are dark iron-red and ash-black, but coated in a thin evaporite crust, a white-pink salt varnish that squeaks faintly under your boot and comes off on your fingers as fine, cool powder. In the shallow pans between the pillows, the salt has thickened into paper-thin rafts, curled at the edges, chiming if you brush them. They break and do not heal. Nothing grows over the break.

Erosion here does not carve canyons. It erases. Every edge has been gone over until it forgot it was an edge. Boulders are eggs. Ridges are long, sleepy swells you only realize you are climbing when the horizon shifts. The world looks flat until you walk it and find you are constantly going up and down over whale-backs of stone ten meters high and a kilometer long — the ghosts of mountains, pressed nearly flat but still holding all that old altitude.

Sparse vegetation survives by cheating. It does not need soil or water. Low to the stone, in the lee of every swell where dust collects, there are patches of something like lichen, but thick and black-red, like dried scabs of rust. They are hard as enamel. If you kneel and look close, each patch is a dense rosette of tiny, folded cups, all angled to catch the sourceless light. They do not grow. They persist. A few inches high, no more, spaced fifty or a hundred meters apart, so from a distance the plain looks bare. Up close, you realize each one is old. The edges are chipped like pottery. Some have hairline fractures filled with salt. They have been the same size for a very long time.

And across all of it: marks.

**The Built and the Dense**

Someone was here. Or something. Scattered, primitive, never a town. You walk for twenty minutes and you find a low wall, three courses high, dry-stacked from flat salt-plates, enclosing nothing — a rectangle about the size of a sleeping mat, open on one side. Another hour: a cairn, but not piled — the stones are knapped, flaked to points, leaning against each other like a tiny, empty house with no roof. There are flakes everywhere if you learn to see them: chert debitage, hand-struck, the bulb of percussion still visible, dusted white. Scrapers. A few small, lens-shaped hand axes, too small for a human hand, left where they were dropped.

There are also hollows pecked into the pillow-stones — shallow bowls, just big enough to hold a handful of salt powder. Hundreds of them, sometimes in clusters, sometimes alone on a rise. They catch the light. None of them hold anything now.

The builders are still here. This is why the plain feels crowded even though you are alone for kilometers in every direction.

**The Fauna**

You notice them first as texture. Then as faces.

They cover everything, and they do not move.

They are dense — overwhelmingly so — one body per square meter, sometimes two or three layered where the stone dips. From a standing height the plain looks speckled, mottled, as if the rock itself has a nap. When you crouch, the speckles become *them*.

Call them rooks, for lack of a better word. Each one is about the size of a folded hare, or a large crow with its wings furled. They have six or eight limbs, jointed wrong, tucked tight against a body that is not fur or feather but a matte, overlapping scale of the same salt-pavement material — iron-black edged in white. Their heads are blunt, with no eyes you can see, only a faint seam where a face should be, and a frill of fine, translucent filaments that spread against the stone like frost.

They are everywhere. Perched on the lichen-rosettes without harming them. Packed shoulder to shoulder in the shallow salt pans. Lining the little built walls as if the walls were made for them to sit on. You could not walk ten steps without stepping carefully between them, and you learn quickly not to step on them at all. Not out of kindness — they don't react — but because the sole of your boot makes no impression on them. They are heavy. Stone-heavy.

They are alive. You know it because the air, silent as it is, is *full* of their quiet. If you hold your breath and press your ear close to one, you hear — almost below hearing — a faint, dry tick, like a cooling ember, or the *tik* of a fingernail on glass. Not breathing. Something slower. A lattice flexing. All of them together make the faintest field of quiet sound, a held chord so soft you think you imagined it until you move away from a dense cluster and the silence gets deeper.

They never change. That is the law here. Nothing does. The light does not migrate. Your shadow stays exactly where it falls, crisp-edged, no matter how long you stand. The air does not gust. The salt rafts you break stay broken. The rooks do not eat the lichen, the lichen does not spread, the knapped chert does not weather another micron. You can watch a rook for an hour and its filaments will not twitch. You can come back — though you have no way to mark time — and it will be in the precise same attitude, the same speck of pink salt on its back where you left it.

This is not hibernation. It is equilibrium. The world has settled into its final form. Energy still exists — light, mineral, form — but gradients have flattened out. There is nowhere for anything to go. So everything stays, densely packed and perfectly still, like figures in a frieze that has no wall.

The weirdness is not that they are still. It is that when you look at them from the corner of your eye, there are more of them than when you look directly. When you turn your head fast, the plain flickers. For a split second you see the rooks *unfolded* — limbs extended impossibly long, filaments raised like sails, bodies translucent and full of a slow-moving light, standing on the air a few inches above the stone. Then you focus and they are small and dark and tucked again. Reality here is thin and has been rubbed thin, like the stone. Underneath is something that does not agree on how many dimensions a body should have, or whether distance means anything. Walking a kilometer in one direction sometimes puts the cairn you passed an hour ago back in front of you, just slightly offset, with the same arrangement of rooks around it, but mirrored.

You are not in danger. Hunger does not come quickly here, nor thirst in the way you expect — your mouth dries to paper, but your body does not seem to use water the way it should. The cool holds you. The silence holds you. The rooks make room for you without moving. If you sit down and stay long enough, very still, the plain will start to include you in its density. Dust will settle on your shoulders and not blow off. A rook nearby, who has been facing away for eons, will not turn, but you will suddenly understand its attention has been on you the entire time.

The place is not dead. A dead place is a place where things have stopped. This is a place where things have *finished*.
