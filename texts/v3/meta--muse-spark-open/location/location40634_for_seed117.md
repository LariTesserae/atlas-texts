It is a low place, and it knows it.

Not a valley so much as a sink — a broad, shallow dish pressed into cool rock just barely above sea level, if there is a sea anymore. You can see its edges from wherever you stand inside it. A low rim on all sides, maybe thirty or forty feet high at its tallest, irregular as a broken bowl. That is the entire world here. Hamlet-sized, room-sized, you could walk its diameter in twenty minutes if you could walk in a straight line, which you can't.

The light never gets high. The sky over the rim is a uniform cool white, like daylight through frosted glass, and by the time it filters down to the basin floor it has lost most of its strength. Dim, even at noon. A perpetual post-rain dimness. The air is cool enough that your breath shows faintly, cool enough that the water holds its cold.

And there is so much water for a place with no plants.

It doesn't come as a river or a lake. It comes as everything else. It weeps. The rim-rock — a soft, old, weathered limestone and concrete aggregate that has settled into its final shape — sweats constantly. Beads form on the underside of every overhang, grow heavy, fall. Seams in the stone bleed in slow, dark trickles that leave behind ribbons of rust and travertine. The ground itself is saturated to the touch; step on the black grit and water wells up around your boot, clear and cold. Condensation forms in the air a few feet above the floor and drifts down like a second, slower rain even when it isn't raining above. The depressions in the basin never dry. Shallow pans, tire-ruts, footprints, and the deliberate industrial sumps all hold water to the brim, black-glass still except for the dimple of the next drip.

There is no green here. Not a blade, not a leaf, not moss as you would recognize it.

This is a mineral world. What looks like vegetation from a distance resolves into something else up close. The stone is coated in a dense, felted skin of biofilm and iron-bacteria — sheets of ochre, burnt orange, and deep matte black that grow in velvet bands along the waterline. In places it bubbles up into low, spongy mounds that quiver when water runs over them, fungal or pre-fungal, but not plant. In the drier cracks, salt and mineral efflorescence has grown into dense thickets of needle-crystals, white and glassy, so thick you could lose a hand in them. They do the work that leaves were supposed to do, without ever being alive in that way.

The basin is old, and it has stopped trying to become something else. Erosion here is not dramatic. The channels are settled, polished, in-balance. Water has found its routes and now just rehearses them — over the lip of a sluice, down a grooved chute, through a culvert half-choked with smooth stone. The rock is rounded, pitted, lipped with centuries of calcite curtains no thicker than paper, now brown with age. Everything that could be smoothed has been smoothed.

Scattered through this wet stonework are the built things. Not a town. Not a factory complex. Just the remains of one small industrial process scaled for this single depression.

A pump house, concrete and much-patched steel, its door rusted open. Inside, a single diesel generator still runs — not loudly, just a low, even *thrum* you feel more in your sternum than hear, as reliable as a heart. It powers nothing grand. A string of amber work-lights on iron stalks, half of them shattered and still somehow glowing faintly under water. A chain-driven screw that still endlessly lifts slurry from the lowest sump and deposits it two feet to the side where it slides right back. An ore washer drum, tilted and still turning once every minute with a wet screech, washing nothing.

Faint marks are everywhere else: rebar stumps cut flush with the floor, anchor bolts in precise rows now holding only puddles, a collapsed corrugated awning under a skin of water and bacterial leather, rail ties going nowhere. The machines were not removed. They were just left to do their small, pointless jobs in the wet.

And they are crowded out.

The density of animal life here is absurd for a place with no plants. The water is *thick* with it, layer on layer, near-touching.

In every pan and pool, thousands of pale, translucent amphipods and isopods the size of rice grains and the size of your thumb flick and tumble over each other, their backs dotted with carried mineral grains. Newt-things, cool-water newts with frilled, feathery gills like rust-colored ferns, lie stacked three-deep in the shallows, absolutely still until a midge cloud passes low, then snapping in perfect, rippling unison. The bacterial mats pulse with the movement of blind, white worms threading through them in such numbers the mat seems to crawl.

Above the water, it continues. The air a foot above the pools shivers with midges and gnats in dense, slow vortices, so many that they make a faint stippled shadow on the dim sky even though they make almost no sound. On the crystal thickets and the pump-house walls, colonies of shell-things — not snails, something flatter, with lacquered black shells like overlapping coins — cover every vertical surface completely, each one breathing with a tiny synchronized sigh that beads water on its lip.

It is strange, because it should not work. There are mouths everywhere and no visible food. But the food is the water itself, and the stone, and each other. The generator's heat keeps one sump a few degrees warmer; bacteria bloom there in a soupy claret, and everything grazes it. The midges lay rafts of eggs that the newts eat; the newts shed their gills that the isopods eat; the isopods die and dissolve into the film that the midge larvae filter. A closed loop in an open bowl. The whole basin respires slowly, wetly, with no need for leaves.

The sound of all this activity is almost nothing. For all its motion, the place is mostly quiet. The generator hums. Water ticks from stone to stone: *plip... plip... plip...* from a hundred different heights, unsynchronized. Sometimes a shell pops off the wall and drops into a pool. Sometimes the washer drum groans on its turn. The midge clouds move without buzzing, or at a frequency just below hearing. You become aware of your own breathing, your own boots squelching, as the loudest things in it.

The rule of the place is this: what falls here, stays here. Water that weeps from the rim has nowhere to go. The floor is sealed with clay and old industrial tailings. So it circulates. It evaporates from the warm sump, condenses on the cool rim, weeps back down. The animals disperse as far as the bacterial mat goes and no farther; the strange ones near the old transformer — pale, elongated things with too many legs that stand motionless facing the hum — never leave its vibration. The crystals grow until they crowd themselves, then dissolve in the next flood and regrow in the same pattern.

It is a complete world, hamlet-small and densely packed, dim and cool and endlessly wet, a mineral marsh humming quietly to itself in its stone dish while the white sky holds its breath above the rim and never quite rains.
