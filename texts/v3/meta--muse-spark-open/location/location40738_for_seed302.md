It is high.

Not a peak, but a place that was once peaks and has settled into being a table left in the sky. A plateau shouldered out of the surrounding earth by almost a kilometer of lift, and then planed by time until it forgot it was ever otherwise. You come up over its lip and the air thins and brightens at once — not darkening toward blue, but a thin, scoured, high-altitude light that has very little atmosphere left to soften it. The sun in that light is not warm. It is white and absolute and it strikes everything without shadow for long; stone throws back glare until your eyes water.

On that table, there is no soil.

That is the first thing you understand, because you expect at this wetness there must be bog and moss, peat, sedge. But there is nothing green. Not a blade, not a lichen crust, not even the black-green slick of algae. The world here is mineral and water only. A pre-life or post-life condition, kept sterile by cold and altitude and the clean violence of the light — ultraviolet poured unfiltered onto stone that never learned to hold a root.

And yet it is wet. Impossibly wet for a stone desert.

The plateau is packed.

From horizon to horizon, edge to white edge, it is a pavement of stone figures so close they touch shoulders. Not boulders strewn loosely — but columns, fins, teeth, and broken hexagonal pillars, like a basalt forest shattered and then weathered back into standing. Columnar joints on the scale of houses, and between them, narrower shards, splinters, plates stood on edge, shards fallen crosswise but never leaving a gap larger than a body. There is no room. You cannot see ground, because ground does not exist between them; the stones are the ground, fitted side to side, seam to seam, a tessellation of basalt and pale granite and bands of iron-red chert polished to a glassy sheen.

Erosion did not carve valleys here. It polished what was already crowded. Every edge is rounded just enough — not softened to dunes, but eased, fluted, lipped. Wind and water have been working the same channels for so long that each joint has a perfect throat, each pillar a perfect lean. It is an old old place in absolute equilibrium. Nothing moves forward. Nothing is falling further apart. It has found its shape and holds it.

Because through every seam, water weeps.

It comes from nowhere you can source and falls nowhere you can collect. The air itself condenses on the cold stone before noon — bright light, cool air, rock still holding night's temperature — and gathers and runs. It beads on the flat tops of the columns and sheets down their sides in black films. It collects in the tight V-clefts and pours in threads, in ribbons, in sudden little chutes no wider than your wrist. And because there are tens of thousands of these seams — millions across the visible bowl of the plateau — the whole plain sings.

No — not sings. Roars.

The combined voice of water falling two feet, three feet, six feet, over stone lip after stone lip, is deafening. Not a single waterfall to fix your attention on, but the aggregate hiss and crash and hollow booming of all of them at once. Water slapping stone, water drumming in potholes the size of cups and kettles, water undercutting and gurgling through slots too tight to see, wind dragging across wet flutes and making them organ-pipe. You have to raise your voice to unnatural volume to hear yourself think, and even then the sound presses on the ears, on the chest.

Light makes it louder, somehow — the brightness reflecting off every wet face so that each trickle flashes as it falls, strobing white on black, so the whole plain appears to flicker while it thunders.

If you crouch and look into a joint, you see how the rules work here:

Stone, cold to the touch even in full sun. Light, unrelenting. Water, constant but never pooling — there is no lake, no marsh in the vegetable sense, only transit. Potholes fill and spill in seconds, never stagnating, never warming. Rills run clear as air because there is nothing to stain them, no tannin, no soil. They taste of rock alone. On drier worlds this would be desert pavement. Here it is a wet desert — a lithic tide-flat lifted two miles into the sky and left to weep forever.

And because there is no change, the water has made permanent companions of the minerals. Each cleft is lined with precipitates — pale silica frills like frozen foam, bands of travertine white as bone, orange iron varnish where a seep persists for centuries in the same line. Not living growth, but stone remembering water.

Life — animal life — has almost no purchase. At midday you might see one of the pale things that lives here and hear nothing of it for the greater roar: a transluscent midge born and drowned in the same minute, a high-altitude mite the color of the basalt picking its way across a seep-line, a startled ash-white moth that looks less like an insect than a flake of chert caught in wind. Birds do not cross often; the air above the plateau is too loud, too bright, too empty of updrafts that smell of anything edible. When one does, it passes high and fast and does not land. There is nothing to eat. There is nowhere soft to land.

You walk, and your boots find stone, and immediately water finds your boots. Not deep — ankle-deep at most where three channels braid behind a leaning pillar — but insistent. It wants to keep moving. You cannot dam it. You cannot divert it. Crowded as the stone is, water has made its own ten thousand equal roads, and each road is exactly as wide as it needs to be to keep moving without eroding further. That is the equilibrium. Light keeps making more water out of the thin air; cold keeps condensing it; stone keeps channeling it; noise keeps announcing it. Nothing accelerates. Nothing dries.

Farther out, toward where the plateau seems to dissolve into glare, the density only increases. The pillars grow shorter, tighter, until they are a set of knuckles barely rising above the sheet-wash, and the water there is a single continuous film catching the sky, so that for a half hour of staring you can't tell if you are looking at flooded stone or stone poured from water.

The sky does not change. The sound does not change. The cold bright wet crowd of stone does not change. You stand in the middle of it, soaked to the knees though it never rained, deafened in brilliant sun, surrounded on all sides by more presences than you can count — all stone, all wet, all alike, all exactly in place — and you understand why nothing has been built here.

There is no room.
