There is no ground here. Not anymore.

What there is instead is Floor. A low, continental Floor, nearly flat on the curvature of the world, stretching to every horizon without hill or ridge to break it — a basin so vast it has its own weather, but the weather is dry and bright and cool. The light is total. It comes from above through a sky that is not sky but Roof, a vault of dimmed glass and white-painted steel a mile up, or ten miles, distance fails here. The Roof diffuses the sun into a shadowless, overexposed noon that never fully ends. You can go for days and your shadow will only ever be a faint grey smudge directly beneath your feet.

Below the Roof, there is the Forest. But Forest is the wrong word because it implies earth. This is the Built Green.

Everything that grows here grows on, through, and as architecture.

Long ago — erosion will tell you it's long ago, everything is rounded and settled and patched — someone laid down the lattice. A planet-scale industrial floor of grating and girder and nutrient canal and cable tray, low and level, designed to be expanded forever sideways rather than up. Factories the size of countries, but factories without roofs of their own because the Roof is the roof. Then they planted it.

Or they built it. The difference has weathered away.

The vegetation here scores 2.9 out of 3 on any scale that pretends to measure such things. It is not coverage. It is substrate. The plants *are* the place.

They do not need water. There is almost none. No rivers, no lakes, no soil damp. The air is cool, 8 or 10 Celsius, and so dry your lips crack in an hour and static jumps from every surface. What little moisture exists never pools; it is snatched from the air before it can fall. This is a forest of drought-perfectionists, of non-aqueous chemistry.

The base layer is felt. A dense, springy mat of rhizome-wool a foot thick over the Floor, brown-black and threaded with coppery veins that are not veins but the original irrigation capillaries, now colonized. From it rise trunks that are not trunks but columns — fluted, load-bearing, grown around scaffolding that rusts somewhere inside. They branch at precise industrial intervals, as if they remember being pruned by machines that are still working somewhere far off.

Higher, everything tangles. Liana-cables as thick as your body, but light as paper, strung between the column-trees in catenaries. Sheet-leaves the size of doors, and sheet-leaves the size of hands, and needle-leaves like fiberglass, all overlapping until the light that filters down has been used ten times over. Nothing is wasted. The topmost canopy presses almost flat against the Roof, a continuous photosynthetic ceiling of pale jade and dusty silver, drinking that brutal, bright, cool light and converting it into more mass, more structure.

Touch one. It is dry. It is not succulent or waxy. It is papery, mineral, faintly warm from industry. Some fronds shatter like mica. Others are tougher than canvas, woven deliberately. If you tear one — don't — you find no sap, but a puff of fine, dry spores and a smell of ozone and cinnamon. These plants run on light and air and the dust of the Floor. Their roots crack stone into dust and eat the dust. Their leaves condense the barest atmospheric trace onto trichomes of glass hair and split it with electricity piped up from below.

Because it is still industrial. Walk long enough and you find the looms.

In clearings where the density thins just enough to walk upright, the Forest reveals that it is tended. Huge, silent gantry frames straddle rows of column-trees, moving a few millimeters per century on tracks now overgrown. Extruder arms dangle, crusted with lichen-steel, occasionally twitching to lay down a new filament of growth-medium, or to braid a young vine into a load-bearing arch. Further off, stamping presses the size of apartment blocks open and close with hydraulic sighs, shaping living panels that will become walls, then floors, then more forest. There is no crew. Or the crew is the forest now.

It is very, very quiet for an industrial continent. Sound is rated 0.4. The density — leaves on leaves on felt on grating — eats it. Sound doesn't carry more than a few meters before being swallowed by that dry, porous mass. What you hear is close and small: the tick of a cooling strut, the faint creak of a cable-vine tightening in the cool air, the almost inaudible fizz of static discharge jumping between leaf-tips. Every so often a deep, subsonic thrum passes through the Floor itself, from a generator farm a hundred kilometers away, felt more in the soles of your feet than in your ears.

There are almost no animals. Once in a great while a pollinator passes — not bird, not insect. A small, tetra-winged drone of folded leaf and wire, grown rather than built, humming on no fuel but light, dusting glass-hair flowers that never wilt because they were never alive in the way flowers are supposed to be alive. It will inspect you with indifference, log your heat, and go. Nothing eats you. Nothing needs to.

The weirdness is not that this place exists. The weirdness is that it persists without contradiction, and that geometry is tired here.

Because of the scale and the light and the repetition, perspective breaks. Stand under a colonnade of column-trees and look down its length. At first it recedes normally. Then, a kilometer or two out, the bright haze and the precise spacing make it seem to curve upward, as if the Floor is bowing to meet the Roof. Walk toward that curve and it retreats. Stay still and you can see, out of the corner of your eye, another identical colonnade where there shouldn't be one — light leaking around a fold. The Forest is so vast and so regular it has started to tile itself. Radio pings come back a second too early, as if the distance folded in half while you weren't looking. Your footsteps sometimes make a duplicate echo a few meters to the left, where you haven't stepped yet.

The old maintenance markings help. Arrows stenciled on living bark point in directions that no longer quite correspond to north. Some point straight up, into canopy that is now floor. Some point at each other in loops. The plants grew over them, then grew the arrows themselves, faithfully reproducing the pigment pattern as part of their leaf variegation long after the paint flaked away.

It is established. Eroded, but not by water. By time, by dry wind sifting through a billion leaves, by the slow stretch and sag of living cable, by light bleaching everything to pastel. Edges are soft. Metal is pitted to lace. Living buttresses have slumped into graceful, settled arches. Nothing is new here, but nothing is dead either. Growth and fabrication have reached an equilibrium: the gantry lays down a gram of medium, the felt eats a gram of dust, a leaf falls and is immediately reabsorbed as felt. Active, multiple, biological and mechanical at once, ticking forward at the pace of cool-season growth.

You can live here, if you have good shoes and learn not to expect water. The air will keep you alive. The light will keep you awake. The Floor will hold you no matter how far you walk, because there is nowhere else to fall — only more Floor, more green, more bright, cool, humming silence under the Roof that has become a sky.

And if you walk long enough, you may find where the Roof is thin, and look up and see not stars but the underside of more Floor, with another forest hanging inverted above you, growing down toward this one, leaf by silent leaf.
