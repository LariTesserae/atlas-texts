This is a low place.

Not low like a pit or a basin. Low like a coast that has not yet decided to be a coast. A plain of warm black stone that is still so new it hasn't learned how to be smooth.

You are standing a few feet above sea level, if there is a sea — there is, somewhere out beyond the haze, but you can't see it. The land goes on and on to a blurred horizon, flat and then faintly stepped, regional, town-and-beyond sized. It is all the same rock: raw basalt poured recently enough in geologic time that it has never been weathered. Nothing has been rounded. The flows are ropy, glassy, sharp. Pressure ridges split into plates the size of houses. Lava blisters stand intact, their hollow roofs thin as eggshell. There are channels where rivers should be but aren't yet — shallow troughs with knife-edges no flood has ever softened. This is a world under construction, still hot in its bones.

It is also wet.

Not flooded. Wet in the other way — saturated. Every surface weeps. The warm stone sweats. Steam or sea-spray or just the heavy air itself condenses the moment it touches the dark rock and runs in beads, in sheets, in slow continuous strings. The troughs between the flows are not rivers but long marsh-threads, shin-deep, tea-colored water crawling between banks of unbroken basalt. Puddles the size of plazas collect in the collapsed blisters. In the lower streets the water stands inches deep and does not seem to drain or evaporate; the stone is too dense, the air too close to dewpoint. It just sits there, warm as blood, reflecting.

Because there are streets.

The stone would be empty if not for that. But it is crowded — impossibly crowded for a place with no soil. This is a city, not a camp. A low, dense, sprawling urban fabric that has made a compromise with the raw geology instead of conquering it.

The buildings are all hand-made. Piled block and stacked slab, quarried right from the flow with stone mauls and fire-setting. Walls of black glassy basalt, mortared with a pale lime that must be brought from somewhere else, because there is nothing to burn here. Roofs are flat slabs or carefully corbelled domes, low to keep them out of the damp wind, all of them dark and streaming with condensation that runs off their eaves in unending threads. There are no timbers because there are no trees. Doors are hung with stretched hides. Ladders are bound reed — also imported — or carved stone.

It is a city built in the cracks. Where the lava rose it left walkways; where it fell it left plazas. The people built where the water wasn't, and then the water moved a foot to the left and they built with it. Causeways of laid stone bridge the permanent marsh-threads. Cisterns are just deepened blisters, lidded with slabs. Everything is near-touching, layered, second stories over alleys over waterways, courtyards the size of blankets, walls shared wall to wall to wall for blocks and blocks until the dimness swallows them.

And there are no plants.

This is the strange thing first, before the noise hits you. No grass. No trees. No weeds pushing through the cracks. On a world this warm and wet you expect it to be choked green, but there is nothing. The stone is black and wet and bare. If you crouch, you can see why: the rock is still too new, too salty, too hot from below. On a few older surfaces there is a film — not moss, but something before moss, a dull rust-orange bacterial felt, a slick of lichen like dried blood — but nothing you could call vegetation. No shade. No fields. The city must import every fiber, every calorie, in baskets and on backs.

What it has instead is animals.

They are everywhere. Abundant past counting, and this is what makes the density something more than architecture.

The wet warm stone is perfect for them. The puddles and marsh-threads boil with small fish, translucent shrimp, tadpoles, newts. Crabs the size of fists climb vertically up weeping walls on needle legs. On every flat surface there are clusters of barnacle-like things that aren't barnacles, opening and closing. Clouds of midges hang in still columns over the water, so thick they look like smoke. Swallows — or something very like swallows, soot-black with forked tails — knife through them in numbers that darken the already dim air. They nest by the thousands in the crevices of every wall, in the eaves, in the quarried holes, shoulder to shoulder.

Higher up, bats pour out of the larger blister-caves at all hours — the light never gets strong enough for day or night to matter — and wheel. Herons stand like statues in every cistern. Geckos chirp from every crack. There are dogs, thin and wet, everywhere underfoot. There are people, of course, dense as the swallows, moving shoulder-to-shoulder through water-filled alleys with baskets on their heads, children shouting, mortars pounding dried fish and imported grain, hammers ringing stone on stone.

All of this makes a sound.

God, the sound.

It is deafening, not loud. Loud implies a peak. This is a wall of noise that never varies, never drops.

First there is water: the ceaseless drip and trickle and sheeting from every roof, every ridge, every overhang. A thousand weeping rocks, none large, all together a hiss like static.

Then there is life: the midges humming in a single high note so steady your teeth ache; the swallows and bats screaming without pause; the frogs in the marsh-threads — and there are millions of frogs — calling in one unbroken chorus that never takes a breath. Dogs bark, babies cry, hammers clack, feet splash, voices call, and none of it stops because the night never comes and the work never ends.

You have to open your mouth to equalize the pressure. The dim light vibrates with it.

The light is always dim. The sun, if it is a sun, is somewhere far above a perpetual low stratus deck that traps the warmth and the wet. Or perhaps it is the steam from the stone itself. Everything is lit as if through oiled paper: no shadows, no glare, just an even, warm, amber-gray luminescence that reflects off wet basalt until the whole city glows faintly with its own damp light. Colors are muted to black, wet-gray, lime-white, skin-brown, the orange film.

And nothing changes.

That is the last and most unsettling thing. For all the noise, all the water, all the teeming, this is a system in equilibrium. An equilibrium that has held.

The water level in the alleys never rises, never falls. It just sheets and drips and sits. The frogs never get louder or quieter. The construction never advances into a new district nor collapses into ruin; men chip at the same kind of block they chipped at yesterday and will tomorrow, building the same wall a little higher, repairing the same cistern. The basalt does not erode — there has not been time. Edges stay sharp. The midge columns stay exactly where they are. The swallows wheel in the same pattern.

You can live your whole life here and the place will look identical when you die. A warm, low, wet, fresh-made world, paved shoulder-to-shoulder with hand-cut stone, crawling and screaming with life that needs no plants to feed it, weeping endlessly under a dim sky, roaring so loud and so constant you eventually stop hearing it at all — until you notice your own heart has synced to its unchanging beat.
