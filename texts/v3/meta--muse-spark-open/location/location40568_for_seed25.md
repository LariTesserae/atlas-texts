It is not night. It has never been day.

The sky, if you could call it a sky, is a low roof of cloud and stone-dust held too close to the ground — a dark so dense it has weight. No sun gets through it, no moon. The light that exists is borrowed and brief: a cold, milk-blue starlight that leaks through when the cloud thins, and the light that the land makes for itself.

You are standing in a low place. Not a pit, not a crater — a vast, shallow basin, a valley so wide its far rim is lost in haze and dark, so low you can feel the air settle here and pool. The elevation is humble; the world has sagged here. Cool air collects. It smells of wet basalt and iron and salt without a sea.

This is a mineral world. There is nothing green.

### The Plain of Wet Glass

The ground is not soil. There was never soil. It is a pavement of weathered stone, old enough that all its sharpness has been negotiated away. Long, low ribs of basalt run for kilometers like the vertebrae of something buried, their backs polished smooth by wind and by water that comes and goes. Between them are flats of siltstone and shale, cracked in perfect, patient hexagons, and fields of black volcanic glass where an ancient flow cooled and then waited a few million years to be lapped dull.

It is damp. Not wet. The water here does not believe in rivers, not really. It believes in condensation.

During the cool hours — which is all hours — mist forms out of nothing, beading on every cold surface. It gathers on the leeward side of every stone rib, drips, vanishes into cracks, reappears a hundred meters away as a seep. Intermittent rain comes without clouds breaking: a sudden cold drizzle that lasts seven minutes and then stops, as if the basin is breathing out. You find water in pockets: a palm-deep pan in the basalt holding perfectly clear, sky-colored water for a day before the stone drinks it; a slow trickle that runs only in the dark between stones, following a fissure you could follow for miles; the walls of shallow caves weeping continuously, their floors covered in a slick of mineral slime that never dries.

Everything shines faintly. Everything is cool to the touch. The stone sweats. Your own skin does too. Breath makes a small cloud that is swallowed immediately by the larger one.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a moss that would be called a plant. What grows grows *on* the stone, not *from* it.

A thin, leathery skin coats much of the basin — not plant, not fungus quite, but something older than both. In the dry hours it is dull and tight as dried varnish, rust-red and black. When the mist beads, it swells and softens into sheets of translucent amber jelly, pulsing faintly. It does not photosynthesize; there is nothing to photosynthesize with. It eats the stone directly, exhaling a faint metallic tang, and in turn it is eaten.

And then there are the crystals.

In the hollows where water sits longest, the seep has left gardens of salt and silica: white needles as fine as hair, low rosettes of smoky quartz, nests of hollow tubes that chime when the wind passes. They are not alive, but they grow, millimeter by millimeter, and they are farmed. You can see the scrape-marks where something has grazed them clean.

### The Inhabitants

You hear them before you understand them. Actually, you hear them before you see *anything*.

The sound is deafening. Not loud like an explosion — loud like being inside a bell that never stops ringing.

It is a dense, layered, high-saturation roar made of a billion small sounds stacked until they become a texture, a weather. Clicking. Chittering. Stridulation. The *tik-tik-tik* of chitin on stone, the wet *pop* of suction cups releasing, the glassy *skitter* of claws on basalt, the low, resonant *thrumming* that seems to come from the stones themselves. There is no silence to be found, not even between sounds; the quietest moment is still full.

Because the basin is *covered*.

The rocks are not rocks. They move.

Every surface, every crevice, every damp pan, is occupied. Layered, shoulder to shoulder, back over back. The fauna here needed no meadows, no forests. They are lithovores and detritivores and predators of lithovores, a complete ecology built on stone-eating slime and on each other.

Low, flat things the size of your hand, like pale isopods made of polished slate, graze in herds thousands-strong, their mouths leaving bright scrape-trails on the varnish-skin. Over them scuttle things like long-legged crabs, blind, translucent white, their legs absurdly jointed to pick over the herd. Bundled in the cracks are colonies of soft, sack-like filterers that inflate with mist-water and then wring themselves out, sighing. And above — always above, in short, frantic bursts — are the fliers.

They are not birds. They are not insects, though they sound like insects scaled up to the size of gulls. Leathery, four-winged, eyeless, they navigate by sound, screaming in ultrasound that bleeds down into your hearing as a constant, painful whine. They wheel in clouds so thick in the low air that they seem to darken the darkness further, thousands at a time, snapping up the crab-things, snapping each other. When one dies, it falls and is gone in minutes, dismantled by the ground itself.

If you stand still, they will walk over your boots. Not around. Over. You are not interesting to them as food — you are warm, and wrong-textured — but you are terrain. A crab-thing pauses on your toe, antennae tasting the salt of you, disappointed, moving on. A grazer tries the rubber of your sole and leaves a pale scratch.

This is what dense means here. Not population. *Presence*. The air vibrates with wingbeats. The ground is a carpet that breathes. You cannot put a hand down without displacing a dozen lives. You cannot take a clean breath without inhaling the faint ammonia of their exhalations.

And strange —

Because this much life should not be possible on stone and mist. The rules bend slightly here.

Some of the stones hum. Basalt ribs, when the wind is right, resonate at a frequency that makes the fliers go still mid-air and cling, stunned. The varnish-skin, when disturbed, ripples with bioluminescence for many meters beyond the touch, a slow blue-green flame that travels without heat, calling predators from far off to the site of a wound. Water here sometimes flows uphill for a handspan along a magnetic vein before remembering gravity. The fliers navigate not only by scream but by following lines of faint static that crawl across the basin after rain, visible only in the absolute dark as pale threads — the plain discharging.

It is not magic. It is just a place where mineral and animal never learned to be separate categories.

### At Ground Level

If you crouch, the cool hits you more fully. The air is a degree colder a foot above the stone. The mist is thicker. You can see, in the trembling light made by the disturbed skin and by the occasional glow of a dying flier's belly, how established it all is.

Nothing here is new. No landslide scar is fresh; every fall of rock has been rounded, grazed smooth, incorporated. The hexagonal cracks in the siltstone hold generations of shed carapaces that have become part of the sediment. The basalt ribs have grooves worn not by water but by the ceaseless passage of millions of tiny feet taking the same route for ten thousand years. This place is in its own state, weathered into balance. It erodes, yes, but it erodes the way an old animal breathes — slowly, without crisis.

A rain begins. No warning. Just a fine, cold spatter that darkens every stone a shade deeper. The sound of the fauna *shifts* to accommodate it — the clicks become wetter, the thrumming intensifies as wings seek shelter under overhangs. The varnish-skin swells visibly before your eyes. Pans fill. Trickles start in cracks that were dry a minute ago. You can hear the water moving under the sound of the animals, and the animals moving to drink it.

You are in a wide, open room with no ceiling and no walls that matter, cool and dripping and dark, and you are the only thing in it that is not entirely certain what it is for. The basin does not need you, did not make you, will not mark your passing except for the brief, glowing outline of your footprints in the living varnish, which will fade as the grazers come to smooth them away.

The cloud of fliers lifts again as the rain stops, deafening again, and the plain, damp and dense and dark, goes on being exactly what it is.
