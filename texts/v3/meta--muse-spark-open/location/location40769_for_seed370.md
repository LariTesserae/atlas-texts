It is not a mountain. It is what is left of one.

High up where the air thins and should have gone cold, the air stays warm. Not hot — warm the way stone is warm after holding sun all day, even though there is no direct sun here to speak of. The warmth comes up from below, from the mass itself. The whole mesa exhales it, a low, dry heat that never breaks, never spikes, never cools at night. It has been the same temperature for longer than any record.

You are standing on a summit that is no longer a summit. Erosion should have taken it. Time did take it — took the peaks, took the ridges, took the rivers and the scree slopes and the cliffs, ground it all down over an age that makes geology feel nervous. What remains is not natural ground at all. Or rather, there is no natural ground left to be found.

Every surface is worked.

The top of this high place is a bowl, or a courtyard, or a cell — depending on how you try to measure it. Perhaps three hundred paces across, oval and slightly off-true, rimmed by a wall that was cut from the mountain and then built back up out of the mountain until you can't tell where quarry ended and construction began. The floor is flagstone, but not laid. *Carved in place*, each slab still joined to the bedrock at its root if you could cut deep enough to see. The seams between them are straight enough to run a finger along, tight enough that dust doesn't settle in them, worn shallow by feet and hands and more feet over a time so long the stone has gone soft at the edges, polished to a dark, amber slick.

There are no empty places.

That is the first thing your body understands before your eyes do. The density is wrong. In a village there are houses and then spaces between houses. Here there is no between. The entire bowl is filled — not crowded, *filled* — with structures that are still somehow one structure. Low walls, chest-high, then shoulder-high, then over your head, all knapped and stacked from the same honey-colored stone. Terraces that are altars that are benches that are sleeping platforms that are work tables. Niches cut into every vertical face, and in every niche, a thing. A stone bowl. A stone hammer. A stone needle. A stone cup with a stone lid. A stone figure worn down to a lump with two dimples for eyes. There is no room to say "this is a street" because the negative space left for moving is exactly the width of one person sideways. You have to turn your shoulders. You have to choose a line and commit to it. Children could not run here. Nothing ever has.

Nothing grows here. Not really. On the leeward faces of some of the carvings there is a black-green stain that might be lichen if lichen learned to eat rock and exhale dust, but it does not leaf, does not flower, does not seed. It just darkens the stone a half-shade, like a bruise under the surface. Nothing moves in it. There are no insects to live on it, no birds to eat the insects. No animals have ever found purchase in this dryness. The air is too dry for that kind of bargain. You breathe and your mouth is parched in seconds. There is no water. Not a cistern, not a well, not a stain where a spring once was. The builders — if they can be called that — left no channel for rain, and rain has honored that absence. The sky over the mesa is high, pale, and empty, a washed-out violet-grey that gives light without giving brightness. It's dim the way late afternoon is dim when dust has eaten the sun, except this dimness has no source to get brighter or darker from. It just *is*, a flat, warm luminescence that casts almost no shadows. You can see everything, but nothing gleams.

All of it was made with hands. You can see it in the tool marks, still there after eons because nothing has come to erode them away except other hands. Every block was split with wedges, every curve pecked out with a harder stone, every face ground down with sand and patience. There is no metal here, or if there was once, it has long been taken and hammered into something else and then back into stone-tools again. The work is primitive in the oldest sense — not crude, but *first*. The kind of work that assumes you have all the time in the world and only your own strength. And so the place has the feel of something that was never finished because finishing would mean stopping, and stopping was never intended. Another course of stones on a wall that already has enough courses. Another niche carved beside the last niche. Another bowl to sit in the niche. A world of pure accretion without progress.

And it is deafening.

That is the thing that does not seem possible until you are inside it. The place never changes — wind does not gust, temperature does not shift, light does not flicker — and yet the sound is enormous, physical, a pressure on the molars and the sternum. The entire bowl is an instrument. Along the rim wall, at intervals of an arm's length, stand tall, thin slabs of stone set upright like reeds, each one pierced with one, two, three irregular holes, each one a slightly different height, a slightly different thickness. They are not decorative. They are the reason the wall was built to that exact height, at that exact oval. In the high, constant, warm stream of air that moves over the mesa — not wind, but the planet's own slow exhalation moving uphill — those stones sing.

Each one drones, a single, unwavering tone. Together they make a chord so dense it stops being music and becomes weight. Low notes you feel in your gut, high notes that make your teeth itch. There is no melody, no rhythm, no change. The holes were cut so that the tone would hold, stable, without wavering, forever. Just on the edge of hearing there's a grinding, too — not loud, but *present* — the sound of dust, impossibly fine, passing through holes too small to see, polishing the tone smooth.

You understand, after a minute, why everything is packed so tight. The sound needs the enclosure. The maze of low walls and terraces and niches is not architecture for living. It is baffling. It breaks the drone into a thousand smaller drones that meet and cancel and reinforce until the air itself feels solid and carved. Standing in one of the narrow slots, the sound can be so loud you cannot hear your own breath, even though your mouth is open. Move two feet to the left, into the shadow of a different pierced slab, and a different harmonic hammers you.

No one lives here now. Perhaps no one ever needed to. The cups have never held water. The benches have never held sleep. The hammers are too worn to be held. It is a fully constructed world at hamlet-scale that has settled into its final form — not ruined, not abandoned, *completed* in a way that has nothing to do with usefulness. Erosion gave up. Weather gave up. Biology never auditioned. Only the warm, dry air passing over stone remains, making the same deafening, perfect, unchanging note it made when the last hand set the last pierced slab upright and stepped away into the tight, dry, warm dark between walls that left no room for departure.
