It isn't on the mountain. It is *in* it.

Near the summit of the White Horn — so high the air has gone thin and mean — the windward face split. Not a valley, not a crater. A fissure. A fresh wound from when the peak was still pushing itself up out of the world, so young the rock hasn't learned to be round yet.

From the outside you wouldn't see it. From five meters away you wouldn't see it. The basalt up here is black glass, sharp as broken bottles, folded into knives and teeth that never weathered. The wind screams over them and around them, but the crack runs vertical, maybe thirty meters deep, three meters wide at its widest, narrowing at the top to a jagged seam where the sky shows through like a torn sheet. A pocket cut into extreme elevation, hidden even from the hawks that circle below.

And inside that pocket is a city.

Not a metaphor. A town compressed until it had to learn to grow into itself.

---

The walls are the terrain. Sheer, un-eroded basalt on both sides, still showing the conchoidal fracture where the mountain tore open — glossy, soot-black, veined with dull iron red and sulfur yellow. No soil, no softening. Every edge is fresh enough to cut. Builders didn't level anything; they anchored into it. Catwalks of riveted iron, black with cold, driven straight into the rock with pitons the size of your arm. Houses, if you can call them that, are stacked like wasp cells: one room deep, two rooms high, perched on scaffolds, hung from chains, wedged into natural alcoves. Corrugated tin and reclaimed turbine housing and poured ash-concrete, all rusted to the same orange-black as the stone. There is no ground level. There is only *up and down* - ladders, chain lifts, rope bridges strung so densely the air between the two walls is a lattice.

It's dim in here by physics. The sun passes overhead for an hour at noon and throws a single hard stripe of white light down to the fissure floor, where it catches the dust. The rest of the day is blue dusk, high-altitude twilight. The thin air holds no moisture. The light that does get in is filtered through the perpetual haze of the town itself — not smoke, there isn't enough to burn, but mineral dust and iron filings and cold exhaust from the engines.

Because it is industrial all the way down.

You feel it before you see it: a low, bone-deep thrum in the stone. Every surface vibrates faintly. Along the eastern wall, the Great Furnace is set into the rock like an organ pipe, a vertical kiln that never goes out because at this height, at this cold, if it goes out everything dies. It chews powdered basalt and air, spitting slag and heat and a trickle of electricity that lights the amber caged bulbs strung on every cable. Below it are workshops, each no bigger than a closet: a man with soot-black hands turning a lathe powered by a belt drive that runs the entire length of the fissure. A woman winding copper coils while her children sort screws by size. Forges the size of kitchen tables. Assembly frames where half-built weather-drones hang like gutted birds. Nothing leaves here except ideas and, sometimes, the drones themselves. Everything else is needed to keep the crack habitable.

It is cold. Not killing cold — not the open wind outside that would strip you to frostbite in minutes — but a persistent, dry, mineral cool that lives in the iron. Maybe 4°C. Your breath never shows because there is no water to show. This is a dry world. No river, no seep, no ice. The rock sweats nothing. The air is so desiccated it peels your lips. The town survives on what the air gives: great sheets of black aerogel netting are strung across the top seam at night, catching static, catching the high-altitude frost that forms not from water but from trace hydrocarbons that snow down from the stratosphere — a faint, glittering, kerosene-smelling dust. It is scraped each morning, boiled down in sealed condensers, rationed by the cup. Nobody wastes it. The rules here are written by aridity: no open containers. No steam without a hood. Spill nothing.

And yet there is life everywhere. Abundant life.

No plants. Nothing green has ever taken hold here; the wind is too young, the rock too sharp, the water too absent. Instead — fauna. A crawling, fluttering, scuttling density that makes up for the emptiness.

First you think it's debris. Then it moves.

The rock doves are the largest: pale, almost translucent gray, with too many feathers to stay warm, huddled by the dozens on every warm pipe, shoulder to shoulder. They have learned to be silent to keep the heat. Below them, in the layered dark, are the eaters. Long, flat basalt-skaters with six legs and iron-colored carapaces that drink heat directly through their bellies, clicking quietly across the walls. Clouds of glass-moths — no bigger than ash flakes, with wings of mica — that live for an hour and die in swarms around the amber bulbs, where the children collect them by the handful and render them for oil. Cable-rats, blind and whiskered, that run the power lines in endless lines, gnawing mineral crusts off the insulation. And deep in the lowest wedge of the fissure, where the heat pools, things that are not quite insects and not quite machines: hives of builder-wasps that have incorporated iron filings into their paper, building tiered gray nests that look exactly like the human houses above them.

They are everywhere, layered, near-touching. Life here does not eat green. It eats heat, eats dust, eats each other, eats the town's waste. Droppings become paste that becomes wasp paper that becomes insulation. A closed, frantic loop.

And it's turbulent despite the quiet.

This is the strange part: for all the density, for all the motion, there is very little sound. The thin air carries poorly, and the rock absorbs. What you hear is muffled — a padded hammering somewhere above, a chain creaking as a lift jerks upward under the weight of a dozen doves that refuse to move, the soft *tik* of skater legs. But you *see* the turbulence.

Nothing in the fissure is still. Nothing is finished. Because the mountain is still young and its walls still shift — a millimeter a year, enough to pop a rivet, to crack a weld — the town must always move. A house is being unbolted on the north side even as you watch, its residents carrying corrugated sheets two bridges over where a new anchor has just been drilled. A drone half-assembled yesterday is cannibalized today for parts to shore up a walkway. The wasps tear down last week's nest to build it a foot higher where it's warmer. Children chase rats from one cable to another, and the rats return by a different route seconds later. It's an anthill pinned vertical, a bazaar turned on its side, chaotic change in a space that should be still as a tomb.

Stand on the central bridge — a piece of old gantry that sways a hand's width each time the furnace cycles — and look up. The two black walls narrow to a bright seam of impossible sky, cold blue and depthless. Look down, where the walls meet in a V, and you see the true bottom: not rock, but a dense weave of discarded things — cables, slag, bones, nests, bent scaffolding — compacted by years of falling, all shivering faintly with life.

You understand the rule of this world then: at the extreme top of everything, where there should be nothing, life doesn't spread out.

It squeezes in.
