There is a place high up where a mountain forgot how to become a mountain.

You don't climb to it so much as you level out into it — one last switchback through thin, cold air and the ground simply stops rising. It's not a peak. It's a taken-out bite, a small room cut into the ridge just under the cloud ceiling. Cathedral-scale in the truest sense: if a cathedral had no roof and no builders and was made to hold weather instead of people. You could walk its length in a few minutes if you wanted to, and you won't want to. The far end is always a little too far, even though you can see it clearly.

Everything here is young. That is the first thing the rock tells you.

This is not an old, worn-down place. The basalt came up fast and cooled fast and broke fast. Its edges have not learned to be soft yet. The bowl of the cirque is all sharp teeth and fresh fractures, black glass and iron-red seams still showing the heat they were born in, though they are cool to the touch now — cool as everything is here. The air is cool the way water running over stone is cool, even at midday. Your breath ghosts. The light never gets high. It's dim, not with night but with thickness; the clouds sit low and close over the open top of the bowl like a lid that doesn't quite close, filtering the sun to a flat, pearled grey that makes no shadows. It's bright enough to see every lichen, every bead of water, and dim enough that nothing casts a shadow at all.

Water is everywhere and nowhere it should be.

There is no lake, no river. The cirque doesn't collect water — it sweats it. The young rock is still weeping. Every vertical face, every sharp fin and newly-split boulder is seamed with it; it beads up out of the stone itself, runs in slow, clear threads down glassy planes, gathers in the moss, drips without a source. The air condenses on your eyelashes. At your feet, the ground is not ground but a thin skin of peat and living mat floating over stone, soaked through, marshy, giving slightly under your boot and then springing back. You hear it squelch and you see the water table shiver just under the moss, right at the surface. Small pools — perfectly round, perfectly still — sit between tussocks like open eyes. They don't ripple. They don't drain. They are simply full, always, to the lip.

And it is lush. Absurdly, impossibly lush for this altitude, for this cold, for this light.

Not forest-lush. There are no trees. The bowl is too small and too high and too raw for trees, and nothing here was ever planted or tended. But the stone is furred. Every horizontal and many of the verticals are taken over by moss so deep and green it looks poured, by liverworts like sheets of oiled leather, by tiny ferns that unfurl black and then bright green in the damp. The moss grows in mounds and hanging curtains, in beard-like tangles from the sharp ridges. Between them, alpine flowers have gone excessive — gentians the size of your fist, white starflowers in clusters that should not survive the cold, cushion plants that pulse faintly with trapped rain. Everything is wet and everything is growing on everything else, but sparsely. That's the strangeness: it's not a jungle. It's spare. A great green cushion here, then ten feet of bare, wet black rock with nothing on it. A hanging garden there, then emptiness. Life is scattered like listening posts, each perfect and self-contained and far from the next, with a reverent amount of nothing between them. The bowl is lush and empty at once.

Then the animals.

You hear them before you see them, and you see them everywhere once you do, and there is nothing built for them to live in. No dens, no burrows, no hives.

They are abundant to the point of crowding the air and the moss, but they keep the sparse rule of the place. Nothing clusters. Moths the size of your hand — grey as the rock, with eyespots like wet stone — hang singly in the air, beating their wings so slowly you can see each down-stroke, each one alone, spaced precisely from the others as if on an invisible grid. Pale salamander-things, translucent with orange gills fanned wide, sit motionless in every second pool, one per pool, not looking at each other. High on the basalt fins, alpine ibex-like creatures with coats too thick and too long stand utterly still, one per outcrop, beards dripping, watching.

And none of them move the way animals should. Or rather — they all move, constantly, loudly, and never get anywhere.

This is the industrial heart of the place, and why there are no structures.

There is no factory here. No one built anything. No wall, no chimney, no road, no artifact at all. The cirque is untouched, undeveloped in the strictest sense. And yet it is industrial.

You understand it when you look at the far wall.

The rock there is not rock. Or it was rock and decided to be something else without ever being made. A seam of iron-dark basalt runs the length of the bowl, and along it the stone has organized itself into forms that have no business being natural: a row of vast, cylindrical pistons, each as wide as a house, emerging halfway from the cliff and stopping. Their heads are wet with condensation. They bear the marks of casting, of rivets, of machined grooves — but if you touch them they are warm stone, not metal, seamless with the mountain. Steam, or something like steam — white, scentless, cold — sighs endlessly from valves that are not valves but folds of rock that open and close a fraction. Below them, the peat-moss floor hums. You can feel it through your boots.

Higher up, sheets of thin black glass vibrate like speaker cones, each one taut between two sharp spires. The wind does not move them. They move themselves. They thrum with a low, electrified drone. At intervals along the rim, water that has been weeping down the rock suddenly jerks sideways in mid-air, as if caught by an invisible belt or conveyor, runs horizontally for several feet while hanging perfectly level, dripping all the while, and then drops straight down again.

The whole bowl is a machine that was never built. Geological industry. The mountain manufacturing itself.

And it is loud. Not deafening, but loud the way a power station heard from the next valley is loud — a composite of deep thuds, hisses, high electrical whines, the wet slap of water being moved by force. Each piston gives off its own low knock. Each vibrating glass sheet holds its own note. The moths' wings add a papery percussion. The water-conveyors hiss. It should be cacophony and somehow it is a chord, broad and unchanging. You can pick out layers but none of them ever rise or fall.

Because nothing here ever changes.

This is the violation that makes your stomach drop after a minute of watching.

That piston halfway out of the wall — it is in motion. You can see the blur at its edge, feel the pressure wave. But it never extends. It never retracts. The water caught mid-air is flowing sideways, visibly flowing, but it never progresses along its path. The salamander in the pool flicks its gills — and holds the flick. The moth beats its wings down — and stays in the down-beat, vibrating there, loud, alive, utterly frozen in progress. The steam sighs out of the rock-valve continuously, but the cloud never grows or dissipates; it hangs in the exact same shape, hissing.

Time has not stopped here. It has stalled in equilibrium. Everything is happening and nothing is going anywhere. A factory floor caught in a photograph that still makes noise. You can walk through it and you are the only thing that casts a sense of before and after; your footprints fill with water behind you at a normal speed that feels wrong, obscene in this place.

If you stay long enough — and the cold will make you want to — you notice the small reality breakdowns that let this be possible. Moss grows downward as well as up, away from gravity, into the air. A pool reflects not the sky above it but a slightly different arrangement of moths. When you breathe out, your fog hangs a second too long next to your face, as if the air has to decide to accept it. The pistons are not quite in phase with their own sound; the knock comes a moment after the shudder.

There are no rules posted, because there are no builders. But the place has rules the way a frozen waterfall has rules:

1. Nothing arrives, nothing leaves. What is here is here.
2. Everything works, nothing is produced.
3. You may look as long as you like, but the bowl does not record you. When you leave, your warmth will go with you and the peat will spring back without memory.

It is wet, and green, and cold, and sharply new, and very small against the huge ridge, and very loud in its perfect, never-changing drone.
