You are standing on the roof of the world after the world has finished becoming itself.

This is the High Salt - not salt as in sea-salt, because there was never a sea here. A mineral salt. A pale, warm, chemical desert lifted two miles into the sky on the back of a continent so old it has forgotten how to be a mountain range.

### The Ground

Under your feet is not stone, exactly. It is a pavement.

Beneath the cities, the ancient highlands have been eroded down to an end-state: a peneplain. The wind and the dry chemistry of ten million rainless years have ground granite and basalt into a flatness so vast and so perfect it feels intentional. There are no peaks anymore. No ridges. Only a faint, ocean-like swell to the horizon, where the old roots of mountains show as low, dark ribs in the salt, like the bones of whales under a sheet.

The surface itself is a crust - beige and bone-white and rust-pink - kilometers thick. Evaporite without evaporation. A dry mineral skin made of alkali flats and compacted dust and glassy slag where old industry melted it. It pings softly under your boots. Warm. Always warm. The air up here should be freezing at this elevation, thin and biting. Instead it holds a steady, bath-warm heat coming up from the crust itself, a low geothermal exhale with no vent and no volcano. The stone breathes heat without light.

If you kneel and brush your hand across it, it comes away with fine, dry powder that smells faintly of hot iron and ozone. Nothing grows from it. Nothing has ever grown from it. There is no soil. There is no lichen, no crack-weed, no bacterial stain. Where the crust is broken - by a foundation piling or an ancient stress fracture - you see it goes down clean and dry and empty for meters, layer on translucent layer, like dirty ice that never melted.

There is no water here to erode anything anymore. What little moisture exists is locked as a vapor pressure, a warm dry haze that never condenses. No rivers. No springs. No wells. The chemistry that runs this place does not use water at all.

### The City That Is Not Crowded

It extends in every direction until the darkness swallows it, and even then you know it continues. This is what URBAN means when there are no people to crowd it.

It is not a dense hive. It is a Sparse City - an industrial archipelago spread across the white plain, sparse by design so that the emptiness shows between things. Low factories the size of cathedrals. Rows of worker housing without workers. Refineries like pipe organs. Freight yards where a thousand black iron cars sit on a thousand parallel tracks, none moving. Chimneys that never smoke anymore but still hold their heat.

Everything is built close to the ground, as if the builders were afraid of the sky. One, two, three stories maximum. Flat roofs, thick walls, small windows that are all dark. Brick made from baked salt-clay. Steel gone a uniform matte brown. Concrete bleached almost luminous. The style is layered - you can see generations of construction simply added on top of each other without ever demolishing what was underneath: a rail depot built over a coking plant built over a salt-temple, all fused into one low, sprawling complex.

It was electrified once. It is still electrified. Wires sag between pylons for miles, humming too low to hear until you stand directly under them and feel it in your teeth. Lamps hang over every intersection and every loading dock, unlit or lit at a bare filament glow - a dull orange coal-light that doesn't so much illuminate as define the darkness, making pools of visibility ten meters wide with absolute night between them. From any rooftop, you see constellations of these dim lights stretching to the horizon, like a ground-level galaxy reflected. Not enough to see by. Enough to know you are not alone in the pattern.

Nothing here is ruined. That is the strange part. There is no collapse, no rubble, no looted shell. Paint fades but does not peel. Metal rusts to a point and then stops. The great rolling doors of the machine shops are all closed, or all open the same six inches. It is preserved in operational equilibrium, as if the entire regional industry was paused mid-shift and told to wait. Belts remain taut on flywheels. Boilers stay warm to the touch, ticking. But nothing moves.

The sound reflects this. At first you think it is silent. Then you learn the quiet.

Mostly quiet means: the wind is rare and when it comes it is not wind as you know it, but a slow, warm pressure shift that carries salt-dust in a ground-hugging sheet a foot high, with a sighing like sandpaper on paper. You can hear your own heartbeat. Then underneath that, the city's held-breath noises emerge: the 60-cycle thrum of transformers idling forever. The ping of cooling metal that never cools. The creak of a gantry crane settling a millimeter per century. Far off, always at the edge of hearing, a stamping press cycles once - *thud* - and then not again for an hour. Or a day.

People could live here. People did. The infrastructure is still waiting for them: waterless toilets, sealed cisterns for some other solvent, mess halls with tin cups hung in rows. But now - rarely.

### Ecology Without Biology

In the long darkness between streetlamps, something crosses the salt.

Fauna 0.8: you will not see it unless you stay very still and let the dark adapt.

They are not animals as you understand animals. They drink no water, eat no plants because there are no plants. They are spare, dry, strider-things that seem assembled from the same industrial logic as the city - jointed limbs of dusty chitin or chitin-like plate, bodies like bent rebar wrapped in heat-shrink membrane. Some are the size of dogs, stalking slow across the flats between factories, pausing to press a proboscis to the warm crust and sip minerals directly. Some are smaller, beetle-bright, gathering in the lee of a warm wall and vibrating together for heat.

They make no calls. They navigate by vibration through the pavement, feeling your footsteps a kilometer away and simply altering course to keep the distance. They are not hostile. They are not tame. They are just the residents that remain when all other biology has opted out. You might see more eyeshine than forms - brief emerald pinpricks at the limit of a lamp's glow, there and gone.

Above, there is no moon large enough to matter. No aurora. If you look straight up, the darkness here is a different quality than night at sea level. Because you are high - so very high - the atmosphere above you is thin, almost gone, pressed down to a shallow lens. The stars do not twinkle. They stare, hard and close and motionless. You can see the structure of dust lanes that should only be visible from orbit. Yet they give no light to the ground. The light stays in the city, in its dying filaments.

### The Rules of a Finished Place

1. **Nothing dries out, because nothing was wet.** Things do not rot. Paper left on a desk stays crisp. Corpses would mummify before they could bloat, and then stop.
2. **Heat without fire.** The warmth is ambient and sourceless. You never find the furnace room that feeds it. It's the planet's own retained memory of formation, perfectly insulated by that immense salt blanket.
3. **Time has flattened, like the landscape.** Things happen, but they have happened at this exact rate for so long that change is indistinguishable from stasis. A conveyor belt that moves an inch a year. A warning buzzer that takes a decade to complete its tone. You would have to live a lifetime to see a window finally fail.
4. **Light is expensive.** Darkness is not the absence of light here; it is the default state of the atmosphere. Light has to be manufactured, coaxed from wire and filament, and hoarded in small pools. To step out of a pool is to cease to exist visually.
5. **Weirdness is structural.** Now and then geometry slips. Between two identical warehouses you will see a third warehouse that is taller on the inside than the outside, its far wall receding further than the block allows. Or you walk a straight service road and realize you have returned to your starting gantry from the opposite direction without turning. The ancient plain is so settled it has begun to fold slightly, like paper left in heat. Nobody maintains these folds. The city is built around them, doorways set askew to accommodate them.

Standing here on a warm roof in total darkness, with only the sparse amber lights below and the hard stars above, with that endless, empty, industrially-perfect plain breathing its dry mineral breath around you, you understand why it was built high up: not to be closer to heaven, but to be as far as possible from anything that could make it change.

It has succeeded.
