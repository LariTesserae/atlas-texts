You are standing on a mountain that is no longer a mountain.

Not a peak, not a ridge. A height that has forgotten how to be high. At 1.9 on the scale of elevation and 2.8 on the scale of age, this is what happens: the world thrust it up a very long time ago and then spent the next eon sanding it down. It is a tableland now, a high, cold palm open to the sky. The wind reaches it first and leaves last.

The rock here does not look like rock anymore. It is ancient-erosion rock, primordial in the way an old bone is primordial. Everything sharp has been persuaded otherwise. Domes and bowls and broad, shallow valleys without rivers, polished hollows like giant thumbprints. The horizon in every direction is a soft lip, the rim of the world, low and round and unquestionable. You can see where the plateau ends simply because the cloud begins. Beyond the rim there is only cloud, and below that, unguessed distance. This is a small place — hamlet-small, grove-small, room-small though the room is open to heaven — perhaps a half-mile across in its flatness, bounded entirely by that smooth stone rim. You could walk its circumference before lunch, if the cold would let you.

And yet it is a town.

That is the shock of it, and the strangeness. From the rim inward, the stone is not empty. It is *organized*. Terraces step down toward the center in perfect, too-regular rings. Aisles run between them. Plazas — unmistakable plazas — open where the hollows deepen. Towers, if you can call them towers when erosion has melted them into blunt knuckles, still keep a vertical faith, pocked with doorways and window-slits and niches. A street grid, softened to a labyrinth of U-shaped canals, all carved or grown or coaxed from the same living bedrock.

No one built it with tools. There are no toolmarks. No quarries, no mortar, no straight lines that hold under scrutiny. On approach every wall seems to breathe a little too much. The edges are grown over, grown *through*. This is urbanism without technology, settlement without settlement — a city that occurred rather than was constructed. Whether lichen excreted it, or wind learned to carve habitations, or the plateau itself remembers being a city and dreamed it back, the rules here are simple: what looks like architecture is biology, and what looks like biology is architecture. The distinction is the first thing to erode.

It is cold. Not killing-cold, but old, high cold — 0.3/3, the cold that lives in stone. Air temp hovers just above freezing even at noon. Your breath makes brief, startled ghosts. Your hands ache quickly without gloves. And yet, impossibly, it is lush.

The vegetation should not be here by that logic, but lushness has found a loophole. Not trees — wind would never permit trees — but a deep, insistent carpet. Mosses in violent greens, lichen in chartreuse and rust and sulfur-yellow, liverworts black-green and glossy as wet leather, cushion-plants forming tight, bright domes that you want to press your palm into. Every surface not vertical is furred. Every vertical surface weeps with it. In the center, where the terraces are deepest and most sheltered, the moss has become deep enough to swallow sound, ankle-high and springy, studded with tiny, star-like flowers that close when you step near and open when you leave. The town is upholstered.

That is because of the damp. Water here is intermittent, scarce in volume but everywhere in behavior. There is no lake, no river. Pockets. 1.4/3. Condensation is the climate. Morning comes with a cloud that *arrives* — it rolls over the rim like a slow tide and sits, exhaling on every stone. Droplets bead on every moss hair, on every rock pore, on your eyelashes. It gathers and runs in silver threads down carved gutters that were once, perhaps, streets, collecting in cisterns that were once, perhaps, foundations. By midday the cloud lifts and the pockets remain: shallow amber pools in plazas, dark wells under overhangs, the constant *drip... drip... drip* from lip to lip of terrace. Occasionally it rains, a short, cold, sideways rain that does not fall so much as scrape across the plateau and is gone. Then for days only the sweating of the stones.

And the cold keeps it active. This is not a dead place. Dynamic, biological pace: things move. You hear ice form at night with a bright, glassy *tink* in the high cisterns, and melt by noon with a rush. Moss creeps visibly over a season — you can see last year's growth as a paler ring on a dome. Water finds a new route through the town every few days, abandoning one gutter to carve another. The cloud comes and goes. The wind never stops negotiating.

What makes it loud — 2.4/3 loud in a place that should be silent — is the way emptiness carries.

Density is 0.4/3. Almost nothing per volume. You are almost certainly alone. There may be no other person within hours of vertical distance below, and up here, there is vast space between presences. The moss is dense; everything else is hollow. One raven, perhaps, in a whole day. One moth-like thing clinging to a wall. Fauna is rare to the point of eventhood — when something alive besides plants moves, the town notices.

And so sound has room. The wind hits the rim and organ-pipes through all those empty doorways and water-chutes and window-slits. Each street is a flute, each tower a throat. It hums, then moans, then on a bad gust it howls in chorus, a chord made of stone. Water drums on water. Drips echo like struck bells in the cisterns. Under it all, deeper than you expect from moss, is a creak: the ancient plateau settling a fraction, or the moss city respiring, or ice heaving. You have to raise your voice to be heard by yourself.

Light is ordinary. That is the relief. 2.1/3, normal light, high-altitude daylight with no theatrics. The sun is small and white, the sky a hard, washed blue between clouds. Shadows are crisp at noon, long and blue in afternoon, pooling in the plazas. It is the kind of honest light that makes the strange things stranger because it refuses to hide them.

The strange things: The terraces on the north side are warm. Not warm enough to melt ice, but warm enough that snow never settles there, and the moss there is a different species, redder, that grows in spirals, always clockwise. The pools in the central plaza reflect stars at midday if you look straight down — not the sky above you now, but a different night, slow-moving. The doorways — none of them lead inside. Every one is shallow, a niche two feet deep, as if the idea of *inside* was abandoned. When the wind is loudest, the niches seem to inhale.

If you stay until evening, when the cloud returns to sleep in the town, you will see the rule most clearly. The condensation thickens. The moss releases its spores in little silent puffs that hang in the damp air like dust in a cathedral. The empty streets fill with mist and, for a minute, the town looks inhabited after all — shapes moving between towers, not fauna, not people, just mist taking the form the architecture suggests, crowding the empty place with suggestion. Then the wind turns and empties it again.

There is room here for a hamlet. There is structure for a city. There is food enough, barely, for a hermit. There is sound enough for a crowd. It waits, ancient and flattened and small and hollow and loud and lush and cold, not ruined, not built — simply arrived at its final form, a town-sized room with no roof, waiting for whatever life knows how to live damp and cold and strange without needing a door that opens.
