It is not under the water. It *is* water.

You are on a high place that should be dry. A long time ago, before the flooding, this was a ridge - a bald stone spine of basalt that ran high enough to catch snow. Now the ocean has risen over it, or the world has sunk, no one remembers which, and the ridge remains high. Not high as in mountains; high as in *shallow*. The seamount plateau is only forty or fifty meters below the surface, a table of old, wind-scoured stone that now makes a false seafloor hanging impossibly far above the true abyss. If you fell off its edge you would fall for a long time through blue into black before you hit any real bottom.

Because it is high, the light still finds it. Normal, late-morning light, filtered through three atmospheres of moving water. It doesn't come in shafts so much as it comes in a slow, breathing dappling - the sun broken by surface chop a long way above, by the slow swing of the canopy, by particulate snow. Everything here is cool to the touch, a steady cool that never quite becomes cold, the kind of cool that makes color seem sharper. The stone of the ridge is still there, weathered smooth before the drowning, now settled into its second life - pitted, dark, colonized, no longer eroding so much as *held*. Established. In balance.

And on that stone, the forest grew.

Not a forest like you know. This is vegetation at 2.9 out of 3, which is to say vegetation has stopped being a thing *in* the landscape and has become the landscape. A single species, mostly - the old ones call it Mother Kelp, though it isn't kelp - has taken the entire plateau. It rooted in the cracks of the basalt and then kept going. Trunks as thick as houses rise from the rock, but they are not wood. They are dense, fibrous columns of layered frond and stipe, hollow and semi-translucent, green-black and amber when the light hits them. From the trunks, curtains of bladed leaves fifty meters long lift toward the sun, braiding and unbraiding with the slow current. Between them, where branches should be, there are bladders the size of rooms, taut spheres of living membrane filled with trapped oxygen and sweet water, humming faintly with gas exchange. The plant *is* the ground, the walls, the ceiling. You walk - swim - through its interior architecture.

And people live there.

This is a town, though from a distance you wouldn't know it. Tech here never got past stone and shell and clever hands, but urbanism doesn't need metal. It needs intention. The hamlet - it is small, hamlet-scale; you could swim its length in ten minutes if you didn't stop to talk - is scattered sparsely across the plant-city, which is why it feels both built and empty. There are perhaps thirty households, and much water between them.

Each household has hollowed out or coaxed a bladder to grow to their liking. The living bladders are entered from below through a puckered sphincter-mouth; inside is a pocket of breathable, humid air that smells of green sap and iodine. The membranes filter, slowly, endlessly, turning seawater into air the way leaves do. You can live in one for days if you tend it. Outside the bladders, the rest of life happens in water.

The infrastructure is all weaving. Primitive, patient work. Kelp-fiber rope, thicker than your wrist, lashes walkways of stretched hide and bone between trunks. Ladders of knotted stipe descend the colossal boles. Nets the size of houses hang slack between leaves to catch drift. Farming terraces have been cut directly into the living columns - not built *on* the plant, cut *into* it, where the plant obligingly heals around the wound and grows new, edible shoots in the troughs. The wounds are old, smooth-lipped, patinated with generations of hand-oil and algae. Nothing here is new. Everything has existed long enough to find its shape and stay there. Even the erosion has stopped; the current moves slow, a planetary breath, back and forth, back and forth.

It should be quiet. Slow places are quiet. This place is loud.

Water carries sound like air never could, and the forest makes sound constantly. The great trunks groan as they flex. The bladders pop and sigh as pressure equalizes. The leaves - acres of them overhead - rub together with a vast, leathery creak, like a fleet of ships straining at anchor. And the fauna. God, the fauna.

The vegetation that fills every cubic meter would be oppressive if it weren't eaten so thoroughly. Schools of silver, plate-sized grazers move through the leaves in rivers, mouths working, scraping the plant clean and keeping it from choking itself. They move as one mind and then scatter. Anemone shrimp the length of your forearm tend the bladder walls, picking parasites. Cuttle-birds - not birds, not cuttlefish, something in between - jet from bladder to bladder, flashing language on their skins, nesting in woven kelp baskets the children make for them. Deeper in the trunks, where the light thins to green gloom, big, slow things live - blind kelp-oxen with mossy backs who chew the old growth and exhale streams of pearl-like bubbles that the whole town harvests for lime. You hear them before you see them: clicks, croaks, grinding beaks, the sudden whip-crack of a hunting fin breaking the leaf-canopy.

People here have rules for moving through all that life. You never cut a main stipe. You never close a bladder-mouth all the way; the plant must breathe. You sing or tap while you swim so the kelp-oxen know you are not a predator. You do not swim over the edge at night, when the deep water rises and things from the true bottom come up to look at the lighted garden. Children are taught to read the pressure in their ears the way mountain children read wind.

At midday, when the sun is straight overhead, the whole hamlet goes still and you can see it for what it is: a small, scattered city of air-pockets and rope-bridges hanging in a sunlit cathedral of living green, set like a jewel on a high, drowned table above an abyss, primitive and complete and humming. The water above it shivers with boat-light. The water around it holds. The forest creaks. Somewhere a bladder births air with a soft, tremendous sigh, and a dozen human voices answer it, calling to each other through water that has, for them, always been the sky.
