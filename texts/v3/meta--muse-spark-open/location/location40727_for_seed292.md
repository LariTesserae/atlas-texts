This is not a sea at sea level.

This is a sea on top of the world.

### The Drowned Heights

A long time ago - before this water came - these mountains were already old. That is the first thing you understand when you get down in it. They do not look like young mountains, all teeth and knives and avalanches. They have been ground down for so many millions of years that even at their most extreme, they are soft.

From the air, if the weather ever clears enough to see it, the range looks like the back of some vast animal sleeping under a shallow, trembling skin of ocean. A high plateau, three or four kilometers above where any ocean should be, now overtopped entirely. The water didn't carve these peaks. Time did. Ice did. Wind did, when wind still had somewhere to fall. Now water just sits on them, heavy and cold, filling every ancient valley to the brim. The elevation is still there - you can feel it in your ears when you dive, the terrible pressure of a mountain's height turned into depth. But nothing is sheer anymore. Ridges are broad and rounded like whales. Summits are flattened into mesa-tables the size of towns. Passes between them are not narrow cuts, but wide, U-shaped sounds, their floors scoured to smooth stone bowls.

You are submerged for almost all of it.

The air is just a thin layer on top, maybe ten or twenty meters of wind and rain between storm fronts, and then you are back under. The water is cool - not polar, not killing in minutes, but a deep mountain-lake cold that gets into your bones and never leaves. Five, maybe seven degrees Celsius at depth. It makes your hands ache on the cables. It keeps the water clear, carries light down a long way.

And light does come down. This is not the abyss. The sun is normal here, a white cool sun at high altitude, and because the peaks themselves are so high, the water above them is only tens of meters deep in many places. Light columns slant down through the current-haze, bright enough to work by, bright enough for forest.

### The Forest

Because it is a forest. Lush, impossibly lush for how cold and pressured it is.

Everything that was once alpine scrub and thin larch on these high slopes has been replaced. Giant kelp and black feather-weed root in the old soils and in the cracks of the primordial granite. They grow from the valley floors in the dark, hundreds of meters tall, straining toward that shallow sun-roof. Their stipes are as thick as tree trunks, their bladders big as drums. Higher up, where the old treeline was, there are meadows of cold-water coral and anemone-fields so dense you can't see the rock - pinks and bone-whites and deep reds waving in unison. And then on the flat summit-tables themselves, in the brightest water, there is a kind of drowned prairie: short, dense eelgrass and luminous green mosses, thick as carpet, grazed down by herds of grazing chitons and urchins that move across it like slow livestock.

You can't see far for life getting in the way. The density is startling. The water column is never empty. Krill clouds turn the water to snow. Shoals of silver-sided charr and pale mountain trout - descendants of lake fish trapped when the flood came and made huge - move as single bodies, millions strong, darkening the light when they pass. Behind them, things hunt: long, seal-like amphibians barking through the water, squid with arms in cold colors, and everywhere crustaceans, leggy and armored, clinging to every surface.

It is loud. Water transmits everything here, and everything is happening at once.

The kelp creaks and knocks. Tree-sized trunks grinding together in the surge. Boulders - old glacial erratics the size of houses - being rolled a centimeter at a time by the bottom current, rumbling. The constant click and snap of shrimp and crabs so dense it sounds like static. The whistles and booms of the big vertebrates. And underneath it all, the industrial throb.

### The Works

People could not let a place like this stay empty. Too much surface, too close to light, too rich.

The built things are scattered, but you see them everywhere because the scale is open - you can look across a submerged valley ten kilometers wide and count them. On the highest of the high tables, where the rock almost breaks the surface at low surge, they have driven pylons. Iron caissons, rust-red, streaked white with guano from the birds that still try to use them as islands. Pumping stations, winch houses, refineries. Industrial, not post-industrial - riveted steel, black smoke, diesel smell that somehow carries even over water. Gantries lower dredge-heads and kelp-harvester combs the size of houses down into the meadows. Cableways strung on floating pontoons zip back and forth between peaks, carrying ore-bags and meat-bales and crews in yellow pressure suits.

Lower down, where it was once monasteries and mining villages, there are remains. Stone walls, perfectly preserved by the cold, running for hundreds of meters along a ridge before disappearing into kelp. A stupa half-buried in coral, its prayer flags now replaced by long white tunicates streaming in the current. Roof beams. Terraces, ancient, so ancient they have settled into the mountain's shape and look natural until you see the right angle. No one lives in them now - you only dive them to strip copper wire or to anchor a net.

Nothing is still. That is the rule of the Drowned Heights.

The weather on the surface is violent because there is nothing to stop it at this altitude - storm cells build in an hour and tear across the shallow cap, turning the whole top twenty meters into white aerated chaos, pulling even the great kelps sideways. And under the surface, the currents that eddy between the broad old peaks are turbulent and fickle. A valley that was clear and workable in the morning will be running like a river by noon, carrying a wall of silt and detached kelp that buries your equipment. A factory raft moored over a table will drag its anchors in the night and wake up two kilometers away, tangled. The fish come and go not in seasons but in storms.

So everything is made to move, to give, to be rebuilt. The platforms are on flexing legs. The cableways have slack. The harvesters cut and the forest regrows behind them in weeks, impossibly fast in the cold light. The crustaceans swarm over any idle machine and have to be hosed off with steam. People shout over engines and water and wind to be heard, and their voices carry strangely far through the hulls.

You live in a suit or on a deck that is always wet. You check your pressure, you check your line, you drop down off the edge of a summit that was once a place where monks watched the sunrise over Tibet, and now you fall down into green-gold light through a forest taller than any forest that ever grew there before, while the engine above you chugs and the water roars past your hood, and far below in the ancient, smoothed-out valley, the dredge lights are coming on like a town.
