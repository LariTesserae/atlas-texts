This place shouldn't exist yet.

That's the first thing you understand when you get your footing on the high spine. The rock is too new.

You are on a plateau so high the air has thinned to a cool, blue-black glass. Dim light is the law here - not night, never night, but a perpetual pre-dawn. A small, distant sun hangs low and still behind a permanent haze, like a coal seen through frosted glass. It gives light without heat, a flat, even luminescence that casts no hard shadows. Everything is visible and nothing is illuminated. The coolness is constant, dry to the point of ache - the kind of cold that lives in stone, not in wind. 4 degrees, maybe 5, forever.

Below you in every direction, the world drops away. Not in slopes but in sheers. Extreme elevation doesn't cover it - you are on the Roof. This is a continent-scale mesa of raw basalt, lifted too fast and too recently. The plateau stretches for miles, a town-sized, valley-sized openness that you can see the curve of, and then it simply ends in absolute cliffs that fall into a lightless void where no weather ever comes. You cannot see the bottom. The stone here isn't old. Erosion hasn't learned how to touch it yet.

That is the eeriness of it. There are no riverbeds. No gullies. No talus fans at the base of things. The basalt columns are still hexagonal and perfect where they thrust up, like the world was poured from a mold yesterday and left to cool. The ground is undeveloped - dustless, sharp-edged rubble and vast, flat sheets of mineral pavement that ring faintly underfoot. Potholes where bubbles burst when the rock was still molten. Ridges where tectonic pressure is still *in progress*, the stone groaning and slowly buckling upward a millimeter a year. You can watch it. You can hear it. The whole plateau is under construction, embryonic, raw geology fresh from the interior with its edges still unsmoothed. It is a desert, not because it is hot, but because the hydrological cycle never started here. No rain has ever fallen. No sea ever existed. Salt flats of pale, dry silica stretch white and blinding in the dim, not made from evaporation but from exhalation - mineral frost sublimated directly out of the upwelling rock.

And it is lush.

That is the contradiction that keeps your eyes from focusing at first. In the absence of any water, the plateau is forested.

Not with green. Not with anything that knows what water is.

The vegetation here drinks something else entirely.

Across the open, sparse reaches - because nothing crowds here, everything holds its distance like it is listening - stand groves of structure that your mind insists on calling plants. They are sparse enough that you can see for a long time between them, each one isolated in its own circle of cleared stone. And they are enormous and abundant in a way that reads as lush: thick, riotous, layered canopies of form.

Low to the ground, there are mats of what looks like black glass moss, but up close it is a dense felt of hair-fine mineral filaments, vibrating. It feeds on wind-scour and static. Higher, there are the Reeds - stands of translucent, hollow chitin-crystal stalks ten, fifteen feet tall, banded in amber and bruise-purple, swaying though there is no breeze. They tick and chime against each other. They grow visibly where the stone is still warmest, rootless, their bases fused directly into the basalt. They condense their mass out of the atmosphere itself - out of the dry, cold, mineral-rich gas that breathes very slowly off the cooling plateau. At their crowns they unfurl not leaves but vast, feathered lattices of aerogel, so fine they are almost not there, fraying into the air to filter silica, iron vapor, suspended salt. When the dim light catches them right, the whole grove lights up from within, a faint bioluminescent blush, photosynthesizing not sunlight but the planet's own faint geothermal infrared and the ambient charge.

In the valleys that are not valleys yet - shallow, emerging undulations in the new stone - the growth becomes almost jungle-thick. Great cabbage-like rosettes of slate-grey lithophytes, their leaves thick as leather, layered like shale, opening and closing on a cycle of days. Fields of filamentous "grasses" that are not grass at all but colonies of vertical, vibrating quartz threads, each one humming.

Because it is deafening here.

You expected silence from a lifeless high desert. Instead the sound is physical. With no water to soften it, with no animals to add irregular noise, the world amplifies its own slow becoming. The wind is constant but slow, a vast, patient pressure gradient moving across the open plateau at walking speed, and when it hits the Reeds, the crystal stalks become instruments. Each hollow stalk is a different length, a different tone. The groves sing - not metaphorically. They howl, drone, moan in chords so low you feel them in your molars and so high your ears ring. The basalt itself is a sounding board; the new rock expanding and contracting in the cool thrums like a struck gong the size of a county. The mats of filament moss buzz with accumulated charge. And underneath it all, the deep creak of the plateau still rising, a tectonic groan that never stops, that you thought at first was your own blood.

It is loud enough that you have to open your mouth to equalize pressure. It never varies. It is patient, glacial, inexorable - dynamic on a geological clock. A leaf unfurls over an hour. A reed grows an inch a decade. The sound does not spike or fade. It simply *is*.

There is no fauna. Nothing moves under its own will. No insect, no bird, no scavenger. Pollination, if it happens, is done by resonance - a low note shatters a ripe crystal pod and its mineral spores ride the slow wind to the next grove. That is why the built things are so startling.

Scattered, very occasionally, you find them - and you understand primitive, pre-metal tech not as ruin, but as attempt.

A low wall of unmortared basalt slabs, waist-high, encircling nothing, with a gap that faces the dead sun. A cairn of stacked hexagonal columns, carefully balanced, far too precise to be natural, though no chisel marks show - perhaps pried and levered when the rock was still slightly pliant. A lone, hut-like dome made of overlapping lithophyte leaves, dried and stitched with filament, now empty and collapsed inward, its doorway aimed at the largest grove. Small structures, faint marks, single buildings. No villages. No roads. Whoever was here came sparsely, worked by hand, left little, and either left or was erased by the weirdness.

Because the weirdness here is not an accent. It is the foundation.

Reality is thin at this elevation. Or perhaps the elevation is thin.

Light pools wrong. In some places on the flat, the dim luminescence gathers in shallow basins as if it were liquid, a knee-high lake of glowing haze you can wade through that leaves no wetness. Shadows fall *toward* the light source. If you stand still for a long time between two large Reeds, you hear your own breath arrive a second before you exhale it. Gravity stutters. Pebbles lifted by the slow wind sometimes do not fall, but hang for hours, slowly rotating, until a certain chord from the singing forest knocks them loose and they drop straight down, faster than they should. And the horizon - if you look toward the cliff edge long enough, you can see the plateau repeated above itself, inverted, another dim plateau hanging in the sky with its own groves of hanging Reeds growing downward, and you cannot tell which is reflection and which is real because there is no sky to reflect in, only that frosted haze.

The farthest groves flicker. Not like light flickering. Like existence flickering. There, then slightly to the left, then there again, as if the slow dynamic of the universe hasn't decided where to put them yet. Erosion will never have time to work here if the place keeps unmaking itself.

The rules, as you piece them together while your ears ring and the cold seeps into the seams of your clothes, are these: Water was never invited. Growth does not need it. Stone is still being born. Sound is a force like weather. And height brings you close enough to the ceiling of the world that you can see the seams where it was badly sewn together.

You crouch beside one of the small walls. Someone sat here and listened to the deafening hymn of the Reeds. They stacked stone against the slow wind. They watched the light-pool gather. They probably thought, as you do, that if they stayed long enough, the dim sun might finally rise.

It never does. The plateau just keeps very, very slowly rising to meet it instead.
