This place is a room that thinks it is a town.

Not a town spread across a valley — you can walk its length in seven minutes if you don't stop, and you will stop. It is small enough to have edges. Low, flat ground, barely above the water table, in a basin where the air settles and does not leave. From the outer levee wall you can see the whole of it at once: a shallow bowl, maybe three blocks by four, cradled in dark earth and concrete, with a haze for a ceiling. Cathedral-scale is the right word. It feels roofed, even though there is no roof.

It is hot in the way deep lowlands are hot after rain. 2.6/3 hot. Not dry heat, not desert heat. A wet-bulb heat that sits on your skin immediately. The air is 95 degrees and 95 percent water. You breathe and drink it at the same time. Condensate beads on every surface, runs down every vertical line, gathers in every horizontal one. The town sweats.

Water is everywhere but there is no river here. That's the first strange thing. Water 2.3/3 — post-rain, marsh-edge, weeping-rock, water-near-surface. This place manufactures its own wetness. The low ground weeps. The industrial structures exhale.

You came in through the south sluice, where the levee door stands open and rusted in place. Outside is flat, fallow high-grass plain baking under white sky. Inside, the light drops. Light 1.0/3, dim. Not night. Perpetual late-afternoon under cloud and steam. The sun is a hot coin somewhere above the haze, diffused to a general glow. Everything is illuminated as if through oiled paper — no hard shadows, no glare, only damp amber light filtered through vapor and smoke.

And noise. Sound 2.3/3, loud, active. You expected quiet from a place this small and sparse, but it is unrelenting.

Here is the town:

The streets are not streets. They are grates and catwalks and poured channels. Black iron and green-black concrete, all long-settled, long-weathered. Erosion 1.6/3 — established, in-balance. Nothing here is new, nothing is crumbling either. The rust has a depth to it, layered, stable. The concrete has been worn by decades of bare feet and rubber wheels and sheet-flow into soft, rounded lips. Corners are moss-round. Bolts have swollen into their holes. This place has found its shape and holds it.

Built 2.0/3 — urban. Tech 1.8/3 — industrial. But Density 1.4/3 — sparse. Scale 1.1/3 — small. That contradiction resolves when you see it: this is not a city thinned out, but a single machine that became a settlement. A hamlet-sized factory-town where the buildings are parts of one organism.

At the center stands the Mill. No one calls it anything else. A low, broad brick and riveted-steel building, two stories at most, but sprawling, with a sawtooth roof that vanishes into steam. Its smokestack does not smoke — it breathes. White plumes, rhythmic. Not coal, something wetter. From inside comes a deep, arrhythmic thudding: piston, press, flywheel. The floor trembles faintly under the grates even when you are a street away. Outbuildings cluster around it like shy children: a washhouse, a barracks-row, a cistern tower, a switching house, all connected by overhead pipes fat as torsos, wrapped in asbestos and vine.

The pipes are important. They sweat continuously. Needles of condensation form, fatten, fall, hiss on hotter pipes below. The air is full of falling water that never came from the sky. Drip, tick, splash, sizzle. The whole hamlet is a condenser. Hot vapor from the Mill hits the cooler levee walls, beads, runs down. Gutters run full even though it hasn't rained in a week.

And the vegetation. Vegetation 2.0/3 — lush. In this dim, scalding soup, plants have seized every non-moving surface. Not trees — there's no room or light for canopy. Instead: a low jungle. Ferns as big as doors thrust from cracks in the brick. Elephant ear taro crowds the cistern base, leaves black-green and shining with water, each leaf holding a trembling pool. Pillars of rust are furred in emerald moss so thick you could press your palm into it to the knuckle. Vines — kudzu-thick, fig-strong — have stitched themselves through chainlink, through the sawtooth windows, through the teeth of idle gears. They hang down in curtains and drip.

Everything that can be green, is green. Everything that can't, is still trying. The grates between the buildings are choked with watercress and duckweed in the standing channels. The water in the channels is hot — bathwater hot — and perfectly clear except where it is black with peat. It moves slowly, clockwise around the Mill, driven by some hidden paddle or temperature differential. Steam lifts off it in sheets.

It is dynamic. Dynamic 2.4/3 — active. Growth here is audible. You can hear vines creak as they swell. Fern fronds unfurl with a papery rustle. The water hyacinth in the cistern channel doubles visibly while you watch, budding daughters, jostling. Seed pods pop in the heat with soft artillery thumps, spattering wet seeds onto hot metal where they immediately germinate, sending out white root-hairs. The town is not still. Even the rust grows.

Which makes the absence louder. Fauna 0.3/3 — no animal life.

There are no birds in the vines. No rats in the Mill. No mosquitoes over the hot channels, no flies on the moss. No dogs, no pigeons, no people even, right now. At first you think you must be missing them. Then you realize the sound you are hearing — which is loud, industrial, vegetal, hydrological — has no peep, no chitter, no buzz. The fern trembles and no insect lifts off it. The watercress mats are un-nibbled, perfect.

The water is lifeless. You kneel and look into the hot channel — you expect minnows, tadpoles, larvae. Nothing. Just your reflection warped by heat shimmer, and the white sun overhead bleeding through haze. The moss is un-grazed. The great taro leaves are un-chewed. It's lushness in a vacuum, growth without food web, production without consumer. That is the weirdness. Weirdness 1.6/3 — strange, but quietly so. Not surreal. Uncanny-biological.

The rule here, unspoken, is: plants and machines can live, animals cannot.

Perhaps it was the heat, or something vented from the Mill's stack long ago — a sterilant, a heavy metal sigh, a frequency from the flywheel that scrambles any nervous system more complex than a root's. The workers left — the barracks are empty but made-up, each with a folded blanket now spotted with mold that grows in the exact shape of the blanket — because staying meant nausea, tinnitus, miscarriage. Now only the automatic processes remain. The Mill makes something that no longer needs to be shipped. The vegetation grows because no mouth eats it. The water condenses and falls and runs and steams again.

You stand on the central grate between the Mill and the cistern tower. The heat presses on you. The pistons THUD — pause — THUD THUD inside the brick. Water ticks on tin and hisses on steam pipes. A seed pod pops like a knuckle behind you. The taro leaves dump their collected condensation all at once with a splash. The flywheel whines up to a scream then subsides. The levee walls weep.

If you stay long enough, the lushness will take you too, not as predator but as substrate. Moss is already softening the edges of your shoes.
