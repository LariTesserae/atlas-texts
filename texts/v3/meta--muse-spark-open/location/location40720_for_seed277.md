It is not night. Night is something that happens inside a day.

This is darkness as a permanent condition.

You are high up. Not on a mountain — on the world after mountains have finished becoming what they were going to become. A continental tableland, so vast it has lost any memory of edge or base. The air should be thin and cold at this elevation and it is thin, but it is not cold. It is warm. A low, close warmth that never breaks into heat, that sits on your skin like breath held just after exhaling.

2,000 meters? 4,000? The numbers stop mattering. You can feel the height in the pressure behind your ears, in the way sound doesn't carry quite right, in the way the weather has to climb for a long time to get to you and arrives tired.

The plateau is eroded, but not raw. This is not a young, sharp, collapsing place. Wind and water have had their argument here for so long they have come to an agreement. Everything is settled-form. Broad, scooped basins a hundred kilometers across, smooth as old bone. Mesa-tops flattened to perfect tables. Canyons that are not gorges but long, patient folds, their walls striated in muted reds and ash-greys and sulfur-yellows, weathered soft at the lip where the wind has been lipping at them for epochs. Scree slopes at the angle of repose that haven't moved in a thousand years. Salt flats cracked into hexagons the size of houses, the cracks filled with a dust so fine it feels damp even when dry.

Water is here, but it does not rule. This is not a desert and not a wetland. It is damp in the way an old stone church is damp.

There is no rain coming now. There might be tomorrow, a sudden warm squall that blows in from the lowlands and spends itself in an hour against the high air, leaving shallow, steaming pans in the stone basins that will be gone by evening. Mostly the water is what condenses. As the warm air from the deep mantle-vents cools against the night-sky, it sweats. Every dawn - if you could call it dawn - the undersides of overhangs bead with droplets. The leeward faces of boulders weep. In the deepest folds of the canyon system, where the air pools, you find the riverine pockets: narrow, dark threads of water that never seem to flow and never seem to stagnate, flanked by a few desperate meters of green, then gone again into dry. Potholes in the mesa top hold sky-mirrors a foot deep, warm to the touch, alkaline, buzzing with larvae for three days until they evaporate.

And yet, for all this dryness, the place is not empty.

Vegetation is sparse, but stubborn. It does not carpet. It punctuates. From a distance you could think the plateau is barren, but as you walk the scale corrects itself. The black brush - not really brush, but a low, hard, carbonized-looking shrub that grows in perfect hemispheres - dots the flats at intervals of thirty, fifty meters, each one exactly alone, as if it cannot tolerate a neighbor. In the canyon pockets, ribbons of reed-grass shiver, pale white in the dark. On the windward cliffs, lichen spreads in huge, slow blooms, orange and rust, glowing faintly with their own stored chemistry, not light enough to see by but enough to make the rock seem diseased. Nothing is lush. Everything is spaced. Balanced with emptiness.

What there is a lot of, incomprehensibly a lot of, is animals. Fauna: 2.1/3 abundant, but density: 1.0/3 sparse. Both are true. You walk for an hour and see nothing, and then you realize the nothing was full.

Because nothing here is small.

On the open flats, the herds move. They are kilometers apart from each other, so you register them as landscape features first. Low, black shapes against blacker air. But as one passes within a few hundred meters, you hear it: a deep, contained throb. Not a heart. A piston.

There are no built things here. No walls, no roads, no ruins, no artifacts. Nothing was ever made. And yet everything is industrial.

This is the weirdness, mild but absolute, the rule that makes the place hold together: biology went industrial without ever inventing construction.

The great grazers - call them Forgebacks - are the size of locomotives, low-slung, six-legged, their backs broad and flat. Their skin is not skin but overlapping plates of black iron-bloom, warm to the touch, freckled with vents. They do not eat the sparse brush so much as process it. They shoulder through a hemisphere of black brush, grinding it in a forward maw that sparks, and as they walk they exhale. From lateral stacks along their flanks, they vent excess heat in shuddering, rhythmic sighs: *huhh-ssh, huhh-ssh,* far too regular to be breathing. Their bellies glow. Inside them, you can hear tumbling, the churn of a kiln. At night - which is always - you can see the dull cherry-red of their interiors through the seams in their plates when they open to cool. They leave behind not dung but neatly extruded cinders, still hot, black glass that fractures as it cools. In the morning, smaller things come to pick those cinders for half-burned minerals.

There are others. Spinnerets the size of dogs that drag behind them a skein of hot wire, laying it across the dust where it cools into brittle filaments that sing in the wind. Filterers that plant themselves in the alkaline pans and unfold vast, soot-black sails - not wings, but electrostatic precipitators - humming faintly as they pull condensation and ash straight from the air. Predators, of course: lean, stilt-legged things with ceramic jaws that do not roar but cut the air with a high, lathe-whine when they strike, clamping onto a Forgeback's vent and drinking the superheated steam of its blood.

None of them were designed. None of them build. They mate, they rust, they calve, they seize. They are animal and engine at once, not cyborgs - there is no seam where meat was joined to machine. Their ancestors were animals and the planet asked industrial work of them, and they became it.

Because of that, the sound is wrong for an industrial place. It is mostly quiet. 0.9/3 quiet, an enormous, high-altitude quiet where you can hear the blood in your ears. The machines do not clang or hammer. They thrum, low and contained. The Forgeback's piston-heart is felt more than heard through the soles of your feet from half a kilometer away. The precipitator sails make a faint crackle, like distant power lines after rain. The wind over the weathered mesas is louder than any of them. If you stand still long enough, you realize the quiet is not absence, but scale: sound here dissipates into so much space it starves.

Light is almost nothing. The sky is not black but a deep, warm indigo, cloudless, star-pricked but with stars that seem dimmed by the thin, warm haze that perpetually hangs at this height. There is no moon, or it never rises. The only reliable light is bioluminescent and industrial at once: the vent-glow of the herds moving like slow furnaces across the plain, the brief flare when a predator's cutting jaw sparks against plate, the vast, faint orange lichen-fields that exhale a glow you can only see out of the corner of your eye. Enough to avoid walking off a cliff. Not enough to cast a shadow.

And the air moves. Dynamic, active. Weather does happen here, just slowly and hugely. You can watch a condensation front form a hundred kilometers away, a low, dark bruise dragging over the plateau, precipitating nothing but a heavy dew that the precipitators drink. The herds migrate not for food but for cool air, seeking the thermal lanes where the planet's deep heat wells up less strongly. Cinder-piles are broken down by wind-borne bacteria that oxidize iron. New shrubs split the cinder to root. A calf-Forgeback, smaller than a truck, stumbles after its mother, its vents still whistling, learning to regulate its internal pressure with sharp, embarrassed toots.

The rules are simple and unbroken:

1. Nothing is built, everything is grown.
2. Heat is food, stone is slow food, water is occasional mercy.
3. Do not stand too close to anything large when it vents.

If you stay long enough, your own skin begins to bead with the same condensation as the rocks. Your breath fogs even though the air is warm. The vastness stops being frightening and becomes a kind of weather inside you — a sparse, thrumming, patient place that has reached equilibrium between engine and animal, between damp and dry, between darkness and the dull red glow that lives under every shell.

Far off, across a basin so wide you could not cross it in a day, another herd's vents open in unison, and for a moment the darkness is dotted with a constellation of cooling, breathing lights, rising and dimming, rising and dimming.
