They call it a city because there is no other word for something with streets.

Not a city built. A city accreted.

You are very high up. Not high like a hill or a mountain, high like the afterthought of a mountain range - a plateau that kept rising after the rest of the world stopped. The air here is thin and cool, the kind of cool that lives in stone. Not biting, not frost, just a permanent late-October evening cool at 0.7. You breathe and your breath doesn't fog; the dryness drinks it first.

Light is dim, 1.4, not because it's night but because the sky is wrong. It's a deep, high, dustless vault the color of old slate and faded denim, without sun or stars visible - just a diffuse luminance from everywhere and nowhere, as if the atmosphere itself glows faintly and has forgotten why. Shadows are soft-edged and fall in slightly different directions depending on where you stand. At noon, you can have two.

Below that light extends the Expanse.

### The Mineral Town

At first from the rim you think you see ruins. Then you walk down into it and understand it has never been inhabited.

For kilometers - regional, valley-scale - the plateau is interrupted by structures. Towers, arches, colonnades, terraces, avenues, cul-de-sacs, amphitheaters, ziggurats. All stone. Pale, cool stone: ash-white tuff and dove-grey basalt and a rose-veined salt rock that flakes like mica. Everything is weathered. Erosion here is not violent but settled, 2.1/3. This place has been here long enough to find its equilibrium.

Wind has had millennia to work without rain to help it. So the erosion is aeolian, patient. Corners are rounded to a perfect radius, as if sanded by invisible hands. Lintel edges droop like wax. Doorways gape everywhere, but many are too tall and thin, or too low and wide, or they lead three feet into solid rock. Staircases climb the sides of towers and then end in mid-air. Balconies overlook other balconies. It is urban in density and organization - blocks, districts, thoroughfares - but empty, 0.2, in a way that makes a desert feel crowded. Between one monolithic "building" and the next there can be a hundred meters of flat, scoured pavement where nothing at all exists.

It was not built with technology. There is no tech here, 0.1 - no tool marks, no mortar, no quarries, no cut blocks. Nothing was ever hammered or welded or fired.

It grew.

Or it was exuded.

The prevailing theory among those few who come up here and return is that it is a colony-thing. Not plants - there are no plants, 0.5, nothing photosynthetic has ever taken hold in this dry, mineral air. No lichen on the towers, no moss in the cracks. Just stone. But somewhere under the plateau, a fungal-or-simpler organism, a lithovore mycelium as large as a county, digests basalt and exhales it. Slowly, grain by grain, it has pushed up this city like a salt sculpture or a termite mound scaled to incomprehensible size. The "buildings" are its lungs, its spires, its fruiting bodies. Its infrastructure. Layer upon layer, hollow and inhabited only by air.

You can find its seams if you look: faint concentric growth rings in a broken column, like a tree with no leaves. Pores in the pavement that breathe cool, odorless drafts upward. Channels under the avenues that hum when the wind passes over them, low and organ-like.

### The Quiet Motion

For a place so silent, 0.3, it is surprisingly active, 2.5.

Silence does not mean stillness. The wind is constant but too thin and too laminar at this elevation to howl. It moves as a cool, dry river you can barely hear - a whisper through a thousand empty doorways, a sigh down a boulevard that has never had feet. Dust devils, pale as ghosts, spin up between towers, live for an hour, and collapse, having moved a few ounces of grit from one empty plaza to another.

And then there is the breakdown, 2.9.

This high up, with so little water to anchor reality, physics has become tired and inattentive.

Distance misbehaves. You can see a tower that should be three blocks away and walk toward it for twenty minutes without getting closer, until you look away and look back and it is beside you. Alleys sometimes are longer on the inside than the outside. If you stand in the central plaza - a perfect circle a half-kilometer across, paved in flawless hexagonal tiles that no one laid - you can see the entire city at once, and it looks smaller than it did from the rim. Like a model.

Gravity has opinions. In certain courtyards dust falls up, slowly, settling on the underside of arches. In one district, every loose pebble hangs about a foot off the ground, vibrating gently, unwilling to commit to falling. You learn quickly not to linger under the inverted-fall zones; every few days, for no reason, they remember which way is down and everything drops at once with a soft, simultaneous patter.

Time slips. Shadows move when you aren't watching them. A crack that was hairline yesterday is a hand-span wide today, not because it grew but because it was always that wide and you remembered it wrong.

The rules here, insofar as they can be stated:

1. Nothing rots, because there is nothing wet enough to rot. Nothing rusts.
2. No right angle is perfectly true. If you try to measure one, your instrument will be slightly off until you leave.
3. The city is not getting bigger or smaller. It is re-arranging when you blink.

### Rare Inhabitants

Fauna is rare, 1.4, but not absent. Whatever lives here lives without drinking.

High above, paper-thin things ride the laminar wind. They look like ash flakes or translucent kites, a meter across, rippling. They never flap. They are probably not animals in any Earth sense, but colonies of electrostatic fibrils that feed on the charge differential between plateau and sky. They make no sound.

On the ground, the lithovore's gardeners move: slow, low, beetle-shaped things the size of tortoises, made of the same rose-veined salt as the towers. They have no eyes you can see. They crawl along the avenues at glacial pace, smoothing a bump here, filling a pore there with a paste they secrete from their undersides. They are silent except for the faint grit of their passage, like a palm on sandpaper. If you block one's path, it will wait. For days, if necessary. Eventually you move.

You don't see predator and prey, but you see evidence. A salt-beetle overturned, its underside hollowed clean. Long, stilt-like footprints - three toes, a meter apart - that appear around a corner and vanish mid-stride, as if the walker stepped out of this layer of reality and into another.

Everything is dry, mineral, cool to the touch. If you stay too long your lips crack, your eyes ache. Water - 0.2 - does not exist here in any usable form. The air will take what you have. The stone will take the rest. There are no pools, no springs, no ice. Once, in the deepest cistern-like hollow, an expedition found a perfectly spherical nodule of black glass that might have been ancient brine, sublimated and sealed. They broke it open. Inside was only dust that smelled faintly of iron.

You do not live here. You pass through, quietly, because quiet is the only sound the city allows. And when you leave, climbing back down from that extreme elevation into thicker, wetter, louder air, you will have the unsettling feeling that the plateau behind you was not high at all.

It was empty. And the empty places are always higher than the full ones, pressing up against the sky until the sky gives way.
