You are on the high bench.

Not a peak, not a lowland — a long, broad, wind-scoured table set into the mountains high enough that the air thins and never really warms. Below you, the world falls away in white-gray steps; above you, there is only more sky. The scale of it is too large to take in at once and too close to feel distant: a single valley-basin, maybe fifteen kilometers across, walled on three sides by old, softened escarpments. No sharp teeth. No young rock thrust. Everything here has been weathered into endurance — long-existed, settled into its own shape. The sandstone shoulders are rounded, pitted with lichen, carved by millennia of ice and thaw into low ripples and amphitheaters that hold the bench like a palm.

Nothing is built here. No wall, no path, no ruin, no posthole. No sign that a hand ever made a place to live.

And yet it is industrial.

The basin is not empty. It is not even open in the way bare rock is open. It is filled — totally — with plant.

Not a forest over ground. The plant *is* ground. A cold-climate hyperjungle of bryophytes scaled up past any temperate reason: peat-mosses, haircap mosses, liverworts, dwarf willows and arctic birch that never learned to be trees, all grown monstrous and structural on the high cold. Everything is alive to depth. You stand on ten meters of living mat, springy and saturated and cold as a carcass. Under your boots — if you wore them — the sphagnum gives, sighs, and seals again. The fronds are the size of sails. Pillars of old sphagnum have lignified into spongy trunks as thick as houses, not wood but compressed, living fiber still breathing. Between them, curtains of hanging moss drape a hundred feet down, forming halls and naves and tangled colonnades. Leaves bigger than doors overlap and interleave until light has to filter through four, five, six layers of green-black translucence to reach lower levels.

It is dense in the way a city is dense — many presences per volume, near-touching, layered. And it is empty of animals. You might wait an hour, a day, for a beetle to pick across a frond, or a white-browed moss-mite bird to flicker between pillars and vanish. Fauna here is an afterthought, a rare punctuation. The plants have taken all the niches for themselves. They photosynthesize, they predigest, they move.

Because they do move. That's the industry.

In normal light — a pale, high-altitude daylight, thin sun through thin overcast, shadowless and even — you can watch the basin work. This is a turbulent place held in a strange, profound quiet.

There is little free water. It is not a swamp, not a lake. Water: 1.4 in a world of green. It's damp, marginal, cunningly held. The air itself sweats. Condensation gathers on the underside of every frond the size of a roof and drips slowly, endlessly, into felted reservoirs higher up. Fog pools in the colonnades for an hour then is wrung out. In shallow depressions on the upper canopy, perched bogs appear after a rain — black tea-colored mirrors a hundred feet across, floating on moss — then drain over days through living spillways. In the deep understory, pockets of meltwater hang suspended in sponge-chambers, held aloft by capillary tension, glowing amber where light catches them. Rivers don't cut here; they are carried.

And through this damp, cold engine, the dynamic never stops.

You came expecting silence to mean stillness. It doesn't. The basin is hyperactive and nearly soundless — an anthill the size of a town, conducting its bazaar in felt.

Watch one of the pillar trunks: its crown, a frayed head of haircap a hundred feet across, shudders. Slowly, with a hydraulic patience, it extends — a new growth pulse surging up through its core like pressure in a piston. The cellulose swells, steams faintly in the cold, and thrusts another meter into the air before locking. Elsewhere, another pillar has overextended. Its fibers whiten, exhale a puff of spores that hangs like factory smoke in the cold still air, and then the whole upper third sloughs. Not falls — *sloughs* — a controlled collapse where living mats delaminate, slide down the sides of their neighbors, and are caught, folded, and reabsorbed by the understory. Growth and destruction at the same pace, everywhere. Gears made of leaf.

The valley floor churns without wind. Liverwort sheets the size of fields creep visibly at their edges, rucking up into new ridges then flattening again. Tendrils as thick as your waist, tipped with sticky, lamp-like buds, probe the air, taste the cold, recoil. When two moss-colonnades grow into each other's space, they don't tangle; they compete as machines do. One will suddenly desiccate a swath of fronds — a rapid, deliberate browning — shedding them in a dry rustle too soft to hear ten meters away, to let light through to its lower intakes. The shed leaves are already being digested by white fungal wefts that run like conveyor belts along the lower trunks.

It smells like cold iron and wet paper. It sounds like nothing. Even the collapses are muffled to a hush by the endless absorbency of the mat. The only true sound is intermittent: a soft *tick* of a seed pod firing, a sigh as water shifts its weight in an aerial pool, a low creak as a living arch adjusts. You have to hold your breath to hear it. The cold enforces quiet. Snow never quite leaves this elevation — it lingers as rime on the highest fronds, as granular drifts tucked into the lee of trunks where sun never hits, melting not into streams but directly into the plant.

The light is normal but strange for it. There are no shadows cast by buildings or cliffs at midday; shadows are made only by leaves on leaves, a fractal dimming. Everything glows faintly from within, water and chlorophyll diffusing the sun into a green, even illumination like light through a factory's frosted windows.

Stay long enough and you understand the rules:

1. Nothing is substrate except plant. Rock is only the bowl that holds it. Soil is dead plant, living plant, plant becoming plant.
2. Water is not given, it is husbanded. Held, lifted, passed hand-to-hand by capillary and fold. Drought is not lack of rain but a failure of retention.
3. Cold does not stop work, it times it. Metabolism is slow, vast, high-torque. Things happen at the speed of glaciers that have learned to be ambitious.
4. There is no builder, but everything is built. The forest is a factory without a foreman, a machine with no exterior, electrified not by wires but by gradients — ion potentials flickering through the wet mat like signals, telling a frond fifty meters away to swell, to release, to die.

You can walk here — if walking is the word for stepping carefully from one springy buttress to another, sinking ankle-deep in living felt that closes over your print as you lift your foot — for a full day and never leave the same organism, in a sense. Valley-sized, regional, open and yet full. Unusual in the way a place is unusual when it has solved a problem you didn't know existed: how to be a dense city with no inhabitants, an industry with no masters, a storm that makes no noise, staying exactly itself for ten thousand years while never staying still for a minute.
