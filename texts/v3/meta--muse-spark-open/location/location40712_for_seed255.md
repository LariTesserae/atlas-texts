This place is low.

Not low like a valley between mountains, because there are no mountains here, no geology that wasn't put here by a hand. Low like a floor. Like the bottom of something very large that was hollowed out or laid flat on purpose, and you are standing on it, and it keeps going until the dark takes it.

The air sits cool against your skin. Not freezing, not biting — the cool of stone that has never been warmed by sun. The cool of breath in an unheated hall. You can see your exhale sometimes. It lingers and is gone.

### The Built Floor

There is no ground here underneath what was built. Nothing natural holds this up. For as far as you can see, which is not far, every surface has been worked.

The floor is a plain of fitted basalt and limestone blocks, hand-sawn, uneven at the lip, each one roughly the size of a doorway laid flat. They go on for a town's worth of distance in every direction, a low, faintly undular pavement cut with seams where a chisel slipped. In places the blocks are still rough-quarried, in others they have been worn smooth by feet. Between them, thin lines of lime mortar squeeze up white. In other places, there is no mortar yet — just the dark crack where one stone does not quite meet the next.

And it is not just the floor. Rising out of the floor are walls. Pillars. Foundations that never became houses. Low partitions chest-high and then shoulder-high and then suddenly three stories high, all unfinished, all close together. Alley mouths that lead to alley mouths. Scaffolds lashed from green wood and leather cord, still damp. Stacks of unlaid stone waiting with quarry dust still on them. Half-arches propped up by timber frames. Steps that go up three stairs and stop in air.

Everything is crowded and everything is handmade. There are no machines to have done this, only hammers and wedges and ropes and hands, thousands of hands over a long time, working in the dark.

The density is tactile. You cannot see far because there is always something in the way — another wall, another pillar, another leaning lattice of poles hung with stone hammers and wet cloaks. You turn sideways to pass through most gaps. The city — if you can call it a city and not just *the construction of* a city — presses in, layered. Roofless rooms overlap half-built roofless rooms. You look down a street and see its far end is already filled by the backside of another structure being raised inside it. It's not planned, but it is everywhere.

And it is all new, geologically speaking. Raw. There has not been time for rain to round these corners, for talus to pile, for rivers to cut. Where a corner chips, the fracture is sharp and bright. Where a block is split, you can still see the line of drilled holes where wooden pegs were swollen to break it. The few channels where water runs are not valleys; they are ruts left by dragging sleds, now briefly repurposed as drains. This place is embryonic. It is still becoming itself. Dust hangs in the air from cutting done an hour ago, a mile over.

### Water and Darkness

There is no sky. Or rather, there is, but it is so far above or so completely roofed over by more unfinished levels that it gives no light. What light there is comes from below and beside — small things.

Fire pots set on pillars, burning seal oil or rendered fat with a low smoky flame, each throwing a pool of orange no wider than a room before the dark swallows it again. Glowing tallow dips carried by workers. Lines of phosphorescent lichen paste smeared on wall edges to mark walkways where you shouldn't fall — except it isn't lichen, it doesn't grow, someone made it and pasted it there, and it fades unless reapplied.

Beyond the pools, the dark is complete. Cool, damp dark.

It is damp because water lives here intermittently, without ever becoming a river or a sea. It condenses. It weeps. The cool air touches the warmer stone around a fire pot and beads collect instantly on the uncut faces of rock. Everywhere you hear the metronome of dripping: *tik, tik, tak* off scaffolding into puddles. In the low places, black shallow sheets of water lie trapped between foundation stones, perfectly still, reflecting the tiny flames upside-down until a foot breaks them. In the cracks, thin films shine. On vertical walls, runnels darken downward where last hour's rain found its way through some fissure far above and fell as a sudden cold shower, then stopped.

Twice while you stand here you feel a cold drop hit your neck.

No plants grow. The damp does not help: there is no soil to hold it, only stone dust and lime slurry. A few strange analogues try — a flat fungal crust pressed white-grey onto shaded blocks, a rust-colored slime in the puddles that might be mineral or microbe but is hard to call alive in any familiar sense. Mostly it is bare shaped mineral. Hand-polished mineral. Stored mineral. The air smells of wet stone, smoke, cold ash, and sweat.

### Sound and Motion

For all its darkness and its emptiness of life, the place is brutally loud and restlessly active.

Sound carries off all those close hard surfaces. A hammer falls three alleys away and it rings like it's next to your ear, echoed twice off the half-walls. Chanting — work cadence — starts somewhere to keep a rope-pull together; other groups pick it up out of sync. Stone grinds on stone as blocks are levered inch by inch with timber pry-bars that creak and shriek under load. Water drips by the thousands, unsynchronized, onto different depths, making a rain that isn't rain. Fires crackle. Feet splash. Someone drops a bucket and curses.

It is a night-shift that has never ended. Movements flicker at the edge of your light cone. Figures pass — people, wrapped in felt and hide against the cool damp, faces streaked with dust, carrying baskets of rubble on their heads, dragging sleds, whispering. Their numbers are impossible to estimate because the walls keep breaking sightlines; there could be hundreds within shouting distance and you'd think there were ten. Then a procession of them turns a corner at once, fifteen strong hauling a single lintel stone on rollers, breathing hard together, and they're gone down a gap barely wider than the stone itself.

Life that isn't human is rare. A pale, eyeless cave-rat freezes under a scaffold and is gone. Something with too many legs scuttles across a ceiling beam. In a puddle, blind white fish the size of a thumb dart away from your step — introduced, perhaps, or wandered in through seepage, not native. Predator-prey does not mean wolves chasing deer here; it means competing crews quietly undoing each other's string lines when the foremen aren't looking, pilfering good chisels, fighting over dry spaces to sleep.

It is strange not because it violates physics but because it violates expectation. The scale refuses to stay put. What feels like a cramped cellar from within, tight and choked with stored material and near-touching walls, unrolls if you climb a scaffold and look out over the dark: it is a plain. A basin kilometers across, terraced faintly downward toward a center you cannot see, completely tessellated with this mad human honeycomb of unfinish. From ground level: claustrophobic. From four meters up: vertiginous in its extent. It is both dense and open. Both a room and a region.

If you listen, you can feel the rule of this world: **nothing here existed before it was placed, and nothing will remain unless someone keeps working.** Erosion has barely begun, but neglect would still kill it. Without constant tamping, mortaring, pumping of the seep-water, propping up of timber, the cuts would silt, the pools would spread, the fires would drown in condensation. This place lives in the act of being made. Stop, and the cool damp darkness reclaims it one layer at a time.

Somewhere out beyond your little orange light, another stone is being set. You hear the scrape, the collective grunt, the dull thud as it finds home.

Then dripwater fills the silence again.
