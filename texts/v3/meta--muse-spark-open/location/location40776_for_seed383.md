It is a city that should not be able to exist where it is, and that is the first thing you understand about it.

You are below the surface. Not in a cave exactly — in a wound.

The world above is gone, or irrelevant. Here, the crust has sagged and split along an ancient seam, a basin kilometers long and hundreds of meters deep, with walls so old they no longer look like walls. Erosion 2.6/3 does not mean weather. It means time has finished with this place. The basalt and limestone have been smoothed by a million years of cool, still air until every edge is round-shouldered, every fall is a slope. The terraces of the chasm are not sharp steps but long, tired swells, like the bedding of a very old animal. Boulders the size of houses lie half-sunk in the floor, their tops polished to a dark gloss by nothing more violent than condensation and dust. This is primordial, oldest-stable-state country; the rock is not crumbling, it is *settled*. Nothing more will ever fall.

And yet it is bright.

The light comes down in solid columns from the fissure in the ceiling of the world far above you — a white, cool sun that never quite reaches the bottom directly, but is caught and thrown and caught again. The walls are veined with salt-crystal and selenite seams that act as light-pipes, as mirrors. A diffuse, shadowless brightness fills the whole basin, bright enough to hurt if you look at the salt flats straight on. 2.6/3 bright, but not warm. The air itself is cool — 1.3/3 — a constant 10 or 11 degrees Celsius, spring-water cold, that makes your breath faintly visible if you stand still. It smells of wet stone and mineral.

Water is not absent, but it is shy. 0.8/3 damp. There is no river here, no lake. Instead, the whole basin *weeps*. At night — or what counts for night when the fissure light dims to a deep indigo — condensation forms on every stone surface in beads fat as grapes. It gathers in pitted hollows in the rock, in carved cisterns, in the cupped palms of fallen pillars. By midday it has mostly evaporated again, leaving rings of white and rust mineral. You hear it nowhere, but you see it everywhere: the intermittent glisten on a wall, a shallow pocket-pool reflecting the selenite veins, black with depth and perfectly still. The city lives on what the air gives up. Every roof is slightly concave. Every street has a runnel down its center, dry at noon, trickling at dawn.

There has never been anything green here. Vegetation 0.3/3.

Not a blade, not a lichen. The rock is too salt, the light too mineral, the air too thin on organics. This is a mineral world. Where you expect moss, there is instead a thin, leathery film of black and ochre biofilm — not plant, not fungus, something simpler and older — that grows in the damp shadows and tastes the stone itself. It slicks the undersides of rocks and the walls of cisterns with a matte, slightly iridescent sheen. In the darker side-caverns, pale fungal fans the size of dinner plates flare out from cracks, not to photosynthesize but to filter condensation. They are the only soft thing in the landscape, and they are not soft for long; they stiffen with gypsum as they age and become part of the wall.

And because there are no plants, you notice the animals immediately. Fauna 1.5/3 — abundant, impossibly so.

They own the emptiness.

They are not the animals of a forest. They are the animals of stone and air. Pale, long-limbed isopods as big as dogs roll salt balls ahead of them. Clouds of translucent gnats hang in the light shafts like dust motes, never landing. Something like a blind, featherless bird — all throat and hollow bone — runs on two legs along the terraced streets, tapping the walls with its beak to taste the biofilm. In the pools, blind white fish turn in slow circles. On the salt flats, colonies of glassy, shrimp-like things flicker and vanish as condensation evaporates.

And then there are the builders.

Built 2.2/3: urban. Tech 0.7/3: primitive. Density 0.7/3: sparse.

The city sprawls across the basin floor for what must be two or three kilometers — regional, open scale 1.9/3, a town-sized thing that you can see from end to end if you climb one of the old boulders. But it is not dense. That is its strangeness. It is urban but hollow, like a ribcage. Structures stand far apart with great stretches of bare, polished rock between them. Low, thick-walled houses of stacked, unmortared limestone and basalt, each one slightly different, each one roofed with a shallow bowl to catch water. Terraced workshops cut into the basin walls. Causeways paved with flat salt slabs that ring when you walk on them. Tombs. Cisterns. A plaza marked with standing stones worn to stumps. All of it hand-made, all of it primitive — no metal you can see beyond hammered copper staples and chisels left on ledges, no kilns hot enough for glass. Everything is quarried, carried, stacked by hand.

No one has built anything new in a very long time. The erosion has gotten to the city, too. Corners are rounded. Doorways are half-filled with wind-blown salt sand. But the city is not dead.

Dynamic 2.8/3: turbulent. Sound 0.2/3: silence.

This is the paradox that defines the place.

From a distance the basin looks still as a salt flat. But when you sit and watch, it *seethes*. The turbulence is not storm or wind; it is motion. Pure, quiet, anthill motion. Thousands of small, pale humanoids — or perhaps not human at all, but something that learned to use hands — pour over the rocks in constant, chaotic flow. They are slight, hairless, with wide, light-gathering eyes and hands blackened with stone dust. They carry water-jars on their heads, perfect hemispheres of fired clay. They carry slabs. They disassemble one house while assembling another three streets over. They gather in sudden, chattering bazaars around a newly filled pool, trading blind-fish and salt-cakes and copper tools, then dissolve in minutes and scatter. A funeral procession crosses a salt flat at a run. Children chase the feathered runners and scream without sound carrying.

Because sound does not carry here. The porous salt and the cool, heavy air swallow it. You hear your own footsteps as a dull tap. A hundred creatures can be running fifty meters away and you hear only the faintest patter, like rain on stone, and the soft clink of jar against jar. The bazaar is a bazaar of gestures, of hands flashing. Even the wind, when it squeezes down through the fissure, makes no howl; it just stirs the gnat-clouds.

The weirdness — 1.1/3, unusual, but not nightmare — is in how this all holds together. The bright, cold light that should mean open sky but means you are buried. The city that is both ruin and hive, ancient in its stones and frantic in its inhabitants. The ecology that runs without a single leaf, where every animal drinks stone-sweat and eats stone-film, and every person drinks the same. The silence that makes frenzy feel solemn.

If you stay long enough, you learn the rule of the basin:

Nothing grows. Everything moves.

The rock will be here forever, round and bright and weeping once a day. The city will never be finished, never be twice the same shape when the light returns. They will take it apart and put it together again somewhere else on the dry, shining floor, and the water will gather in the new bowls, and the fish will find them, and the silence will keep it all, undisturbed.
