High up, where the air should be thin and cold, there is a warm place that does not breathe.

You have to know where to look. The plateau itself is raw and wrong in its newness — a broad lift of dark stone that has only just arrived. It hasn't learned to be a mountain yet. There are no valleys cut into it, no scree slopes sorted by age, no riverbeds. The basalt is still close to its birth-shape: pillow-swells and rope-coils frozen mid-flow, glassy where it cooled too fast, still holding the blistered texture of gas escaping. It is pre-erosion geology, embryonic. Wind has not yet had time to sand it, water has not yet had time to carve it. You walk over it and it feels under construction, edges too sharp, hollows too perfectly round, as if the earth made a first draft and forgot to revise.

And then one of those hollows holds the pocket.

It is not a cave as caves are usually made, by water dissolving or wearing stone away. It is a kept bubble, a single-feature containment, an uplift blister that never popped. Perhaps ten strides across at its widest, less than two tall in the middle - you can touch both walls if you stretch, you have to stoop near the edges. A small, hidden room embedded within the high rock, entered through a tight, off-center fissure where two cooling lobes failed to meet. From outside there is no sign unless you put your hand to the stone and feel the heat sighing out.

Inside, it is absolute dark. Not dim, not twilight. The kind of darkness that exists only under tons of unbroken rock at elevation, a darkness that has weight and temperature. No light reaches here and none is invited. If you bring a flame it sweats and gutters in the saturated air before it can throw more than a hand's breadth of amber.

And yet it is warm. Unreasonably warm for this height. The stone itself is warm, the air is warm, the water is warm. Not hot-spring scalding, but the steady, blood-warm of a body holding its heat. Outside on the plateau, night would bring frost even in summer. In here, the season never changes. That is the rule of the place: equilibrium. Nothing gradients, nothing flows away. It is a frozen system, not in ice but in stasis.

Water is everywhere without going anywhere.

It weeps. The ceiling - low, close - is dense with presences. Not stalactites in the old, tapered sense, but a crowded field of embryonic growths. Nubs and fingers and plates of dark mineral, each still forming, each beaded with water. They are packed near-touching, layered, a dense thatch of unfinished stone hanging just above your head. Between them, the rock constantly condenses. The atmosphere is at saturation; warm, wet air given off by the stone itself meets the slightly cooler stone and beads. Droplets form, swell to heaviness, and hang.

They do not fall often.

Most of the water in this pocket is in suspension: droplets clinging to every surface, films running glossily over every curve, shallow pans of water held in rimstone lips on the floor that never overflow. The air is full of water near the surface of itself, thick enough to drink by breathing. When a drop does finally let go, it falls straight into a pool with a soft, distinct *tok* that takes a long time to fade, and the ripples travel outward until they gently meet the many careful edges and stop. There is no outlet stream. The water is just held here, present, dense, present again.

The floor is not flat. It is that same raw basalt, but here it has taken on a second texture under the wet: a skin of warm, fine clay-mud and mineral milk, and over that, a dense gathering of what people have left.

The built things are scattered but intentional. This place has been found before.

There are perhaps a dozen small structures, if you can call them that. No walls, no roof - the pocket is the roof. Instead: hand-stacked cairns no taller than your knee, made of the sharp plateau stone carried in one by one, their balance precise despite their primitiveness. Shallow bowls pinched from the pale clay that collects in the lowest dimple of the floor, fired right here until you can see the fire-clouding on their rims. Some still hold water, perfectly still, filmed with dust that has not settled because no air moves. Woven reed mats, now dark and sodden, laid to make a dry-ish place to kneel. A string of wooden beads, swollen and blackened, hanging from a low mineral finger. A fire-ring of stones, but not for heat - for light, once. The charcoal inside it is cold and damp and intact, as if left yesterday and as if it will be left forever, because decay here is slow to the point of stopping.

Everything is hand-made, early-tool-made. Chipped edges. Twine wrapped and knotted. Nothing with a nail, nothing with a pane, nothing that needed a forge beyond a small pit. Whoever comes here comes lightly and leaves lightly, and the pocket keeps what is left without rusting it or rotting it.

Life is sparse, but insistent.

There is almost no green - no soil to speak of, no sun to feed it. Yet in the densest wet, where condensation runs thickest down the walls, there is a pale, sparse vegetation: mats of lichen the color of wet ash, thin and flat against the stone, and here and there a single fern, bleached and translucent, unfurling one frond at a time in the dark as if it does not know it should need light. They do not spread. Each holds its small, exact place. The lichen drinks from condensation. The fern drinks from the warm film. Neither grows large; they just persist, warm and wet and unchanging.

Fauna is rarer still. You will not see movement at first and you will think the place empty. Then, after long quiet, you will notice: a white, slow woodlouse-like thing curled in the rim of a clay bowl, antennae twitching once an hour. Blind cave-springtails that make the surface of the still pans seem to shiver without wind. Perhaps a single salamander, pigmentless, gills feathery and enormous, resting in the largest pan with its eyes closed, breathing so slowly you have to watch its throat for a full minute to catch it. Nothing hunts. Nothing needs to. They are layered into the dense, quiet system like the droplets, each in its niche, not multiplying, not dying back.

The weirdness is quiet too, until you notice it violates the rest of the world.

The water hangs too long. Droplets on the ceiling grow to the size of grapes, of small plums, trembling with surface tension, reflecting nothing because there is no light, and they do not fall. The air is too warm for the elevation and too still. Sounds do not echo as they should in stone; the dense thatch above and the saturated air swallow them. And time - time has thinned. The clay bowls do not crack. The woven mats do not mold through. The fern does not quite grow or die. The droplet that you watched for ten minutes is still there when you blink. The place is in equilibrium so perfect it feels like held breath. It is not dead. It is never changing.

The rule here is stasis. What is put here stays. Heat does not leave. Water does not leave. The stone is still being born but has paused mid-birth to hold this warm, wet, dark pocket for a little infinity.

You sit on the sodden mat, in the warm dark, listening to the mostly-quiet. A *tok*. A long pause. Another *tok*. Your own breathing, loud at first, then lost in the thickness. The salamander does not move. The fern does not move. The great clinging droplets above your head hold their place in the dark, warm as skin, waiting without urgency to fall.
