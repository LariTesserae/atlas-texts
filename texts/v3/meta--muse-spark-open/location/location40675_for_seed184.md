This is not an ocean. Not anymore.

This is a Room of Water that has learned how to stay put.

You are below surface — not deep enough for pressure to crush, not shallow enough to breathe. Elevation 0.5/3: A sunken place. A drowned courtyard. A single hollow scooped out of the limestone and then left behind when the water level rose and forgot to fall again. From above, on dry land, you might have seen it as a perfect oval of dark green, a quarry or a cistern or a collapsed dome. Now you are inside it.

The scale is small. Hamlet-small. Cathedral-small. You could swim from one wall to the other in thirty slow kicks if there was anything like open water to swim through. There isn't.

Because everything is packed.

**The Town That Kept Working After Drowning**

The water here is not empty water. It is 2.8/3 water — atmospheric-water. Wall-water. Submerged to the point that "submerged" stops being an event and becomes the condition.

It does not lap. It holds. Warm — 1.7/3 — bath-warm, skin-warm, amniotic at about 29 Celsius. The warmth keeps it still. No currents worth naming. No tides. Only a slow thermal turnover, a patient breathing once every few hours when the warmer top layer trades places with the cooler bottom. You can feel it if you stay motionless long enough: a faint silk-shift across your shoulders, and then nothing again.

And inside this warm, still, dim vat — a town.

Not ruins. A town *in use*. Built 1.6/3, urban, full of intent: terraced balconies cut into the bowl's inner lip, catwalks of riveted steel bridging the gap, narrow alleys that were once streets and are now canals with no surface. Three, maybe four stories of occupation stacked down toward the bottom, layer after layer of construction that never had a master plan but arrived at order anyway. Pipe runs become handrails. Handrails become conduit. You see corrugated warehouse walls bolted directly onto older brick tenements bolted onto the original quarried rock.

And all of it saturated with industry. Tech: 2.4/3. Industrial, mechanized, electrified — but adapted.

Nothing roars. Nothing churns fast. This is post-flood industry; slow industry; underwater industry. Great drum-boilers the size of houses sit half-buried in the silt floor, their fireboxes long cold, their function converted. They now gently electrolyze, or distill, or breathe. Huge flywheels turn so slowly you can't see them move unless you fix your eye on a rusted mark and wait a minute. Chains drift rather than drive, carrying baskets of kelp-steel and cultivated coral up one shaft and down another at the pace of a sleeping clock. Everywhere: the soft amber glow of sealed filament bulbs in wire cages, the fizz of small anodes keeping the rust at bay, the patient tick...tick...tick of pressure gauges that have been ticking for fifty years and will tick for fifty more.

Dynamic: 0.9/3. Slow. Everything happens on the timescale of accretion.

**What Grows When Nothing Leaves**

Vegetation: 1.7/3. Lush, for where we are. Warm water and dim light and iron everywhere makes a greenhouse without sun.

This is not seaweed as you know it. This is freshwater jungle co-opting the machine.

Engine-block moss — a black-green felt that grows only on hot metal, even though the metal hasn't been hot in decades. It remembers. Rust-lilies, whose pads spread wide as dinner tables on the still surface far above you, sending down taproots as thick as your wrist that anchor directly into fuse boxes, splitting them open to flower with copper filaments inside the blossom. Beard-algae hangs from every catwalk in curtains ten feet long, swaying with the impossible slowness of hair in honey. Tomatoes — actual tomatoes — have gone aquatic here, tended in net-hung gardens that float mid-water. Their skins have thickened to hold against osmosis; they glow faintly orange in the gloom like low lanterns.

Because the light is dim. 0.6/3. Dusk at noon. The water column above is perhaps twelve meters thick, and it acts as a lens of suspended lime and pollen and iron flake. Sunlight reaches you as a diffuse, green-gold bruise, never direct. Mostly you see by the town's own light: every window still lit from within, all glass kept miraculously clear. Warm yellow squares spilling into warm water, showing kitchens where someone boiled something an hour ago, workshops where brass tools hang exactly in place.

No shadows. Only attenuation. Further than three buildings away, everything softens into silt-haze. The far wall of the bowl is always impressionist, always just beyond resolving.

And everything touches everything else. Density: 2.8/3. Packed. Saturated. There is no vacancy.

You cannot extend your arm in any direction without brushing life or manufacture. Algae on pipe on laundry line on vine on hull plating on brick. Barnacle-coral knits itself into rebar in nodules tight as cauliflower. Air-hoses and vine-ropes and power cables are braided together because space forced them to share the same run. Doors open into rooms that open into tanks that open into living quarters with no intervening alley — people cut passages directly through each other's walls generations ago. The water itself is half-material: thick with spores, leaf detritus, iron snow falling eternally at millimeters per minute. To move, you wade-float-push through a medium closer to broth than sea.

And yet it is not choking. Erosion: 1.6/3 — established. Settled-form. Weathered, in-balance.

This has existed long enough to stop decaying. The initial panic-rust of the flood years is over. Now each surface has found its equilibrium. Steel exhales a slow crust of rust only to have that crust immediately colonized, sealed, and hardened into something new by bacterial iron-shells and rootlets. Paint peels and is replaced by mineral varnish. Brick softens at the edges, rounds like river stone, but does not collapse. You get the feeling this whole drowned hamlet could stay exactly like this for a thousand years without getting any more ruined or any less alive. It has negotiated terms with water.

**Quiet**

Sound: 0.5/3. Silence, or near it.

Water steals almost everything. What remains is intimate and close-miked.

The ping of metal contracting one tick to the left.
Your own heart, too loud.
A distant, arrhythmic thud — some piston taking its half-hour stroke at the bottom of the bowl. You feel it in your sternum more than hear it.
A frayed belt slipping, not squealing, just sighing, on a wheel the size of a house.
Somewhere above, a child laughing, filtered through twelve feet of water and two walls — you can't tell if the child is swimming or walking in a pocket of air you haven't found yet.

There are supposed to be fish. Fauna: 1.3/3. Rare.

They made almost no accommodation for animals. Occasionally a pale carp the length of your forearm hangs motionless in what used to be a stairwell, staring with eyes grown large for the dim. Blind white crayfish pick at the beard-algae, moving backwards in confused fits. Up near the light, water boatmen dimple the underside of the surface-film, which hangs like a ceiling of mercury. That's it. Almost everyone who lives here eats plant, or machine-filtered paste. Animals are guests who came down with the flood and never quite committed to staying.

Instead, the movement belongs to people.

You see them infrequently, because your vision is short and they are excellent at being still. A woman in waxed canvas and a copper nose-clip hovering at her tomato nets, pruning with iron scissors, breathing from a hose that trails off to a gurgling condenser box. She doesn't startle at you. An old man sitting on a balcony railing — which is insane until you remember falling has no meaning here — mending a net while his feet are tethered by ankle weights. His bubbles, few and silver, rise in a lazy corkscrew and flatten against the surface-film above, where they join a vast, trapped archipelago of air pockets clinging to ceilings and inverted bowls, shivering in place for weeks.

**The Strange Logic**

Weirdness: 1.7/3. Strange, in the way warm closed systems get strange.

This place remembers gravity, but negotiates.

Dust does not settle downwards here. The silt is neutrally buoyant most days, hanging in strata according to temperature. At about midday, for reasons no one can articulate, all the loose tea-leaves and skin flakes in the bowl will suddenly align north-south for twenty minutes, then release.

Water pools in air and air pools in water. You'll pass an open doorway that looks flooded, swim through, and find yourself falling briefly onto a dry rug in a bubble of trapped nitrogen where a family has kept a sofa perfectly dry for years. Next door, a room that should be dry is filled brim-full with clear water that refuses to leak out despite having no door at all. Surface tension has become architectural.

If you stay still too long, the beard-algae will try to root on you — not aggressively, just speculatively, with the patience of something that experiences time as accumulation. The locals let it happen. Many of them have living cuffs and collars they never remove.

The industrial relics at the bottom — the big boilers and wheels — are tended like shrines, though no one remembers what product they originally made. Now they make "the Balance." They warm, they spin, they oxygenate, they keep the dim lights on with hydropower bled from the inflow trickle that still comes in through the old aqueduct gate, a spill no wider than your body, bringing fresh silt from somewhere miles away uphill. Without that slow turning, the algae would bloom and crash and consume itself. With it, the hamlet holds.

Small — just this one bowl, bounded — packed — no inch unclaimed — dim — warm — humming slower than breath — both jungle and factory and town meeting in warm water, agreeing to become something else entirely that has not yet found a name outside this drowned room.

You tread very gently here, not to disturb the filings hanging in the water. Your wake will be visible for hours.
