It is a basin, not a valley — something smaller and more contained than that.

Low ground ringed on all sides by a smooth, weathered lip of stone maybe eight or ten feet high. You could walk its circumference in a few minutes. From above it would look like a shallow bowl pressed into flat country, but from inside you don't see any outside at all. Just the rim, dark against the sky, and the close, warm air held inside.

The light never really gets in. The sky over it is always the color of wet paper, or the rim itself cuts the sun, or there is steam — enough that the day stays in a permanent late dusk. Not dark. Dim. You can see everything clearly for twenty or thirty feet, and then color thins out and distance dissolves into a pale, warm haze. Shadows are soft or not there at all. Everything looks damp, even when it isn't directly wet, because everything *is* damp.

There are no plants. Not one.

That is the first strange thing about it, because there is water everywhere. This is a wet place the way a cave with a roof that weeps is a wet place, or a hot spring terrace after rain. The rock is black basalt below and then stained, coated, crusted over in creams and rusts and bone-whites where the minerals have come out of solution. The whole floor of the basin is terraced — not cut, but grown that way, century after century of water laying down paper-thin sheets of silica and travertine.

The erosion here is old, settled. Nothing is sharp. The rims of the pools are rounded and lipped over like pottery, smoothed by water that has run the same way for longer than anyone could remember. The stone is pocked and scalloped where bubbles have formed and popped, and polished where water sheets over it. You get the feeling this place found its shape a long time ago and has just been keeping it, perfectly, ever since.

Each little terrace holds water. That's why it feels so packed. There is almost no dry floor. From the lowest point in the center out to the wall, it's pool after pool after pool, edge to edge, some no bigger than a washbasin, some big enough to lie down in, all shallow, all warm. They touch each other at the lips. They spill into each other constantly in a slow, almost invisible overflow — a glassy skin of water sliding over a mineral rim without a ripple, falling a thumb's width into the next pool. The water is clear to the point of invisibility. You see it mostly by the way it quivers, or by the shimmer of heat coming off it.

It is warm. Hot, in places. Not scalding, but bath-warm, blood-warm, the air itself warm and heavy with it. The water doesn't steam violently; it breathes. A low, even veil of condensation hangs at chest height that never gets thicker or thinner. It condenses on the cool underside of the basin rim and runs back down in dark beads, cutting tiny vertical gullies in the mineral crust. It condenses on any surface you leave out. Your skin stays damp even if you don't touch the pools. The smell is clean mineral — salt and iron and warm stone — no rot, no green.

Because there is no green. No algae slick, no reeds at the edges, no moss. The water is too mineral, or too warm, or too something. The only thing that lives on the crust is a thin, dark film in a few shaded corners, not quite plant, not quite stone — slick to the touch — but otherwise the world here is entirely mineral. White, cream, yellow, rust, and the black rock underneath where the crust has chipped. When a flake breaks off it shows how thick the deposit is, layered like pages.

It is a static place. Equilibrium.

The water level never changes. The pools never flood or dry. Even after the rare heavy rain that patters warm on the surface for an hour, by morning every pool is exactly as full as it was, spilling exactly as much over exactly the same notch. The crust grows so slowly a lifetime would not show it. There's no current, no tide, no season. Sounds don't carry because the close air and the soft, porous stone swallow them.

And it is quiet. The perfect quiet of a place where nothing moves except water at its slowest. If you stand still you hear a faint, endless ticking: drip from rim, spill from lip to pool, a heavier plink where a larger sheet detaches. Your own breathing sounds loud. Far off, perhaps once an hour, a pale cave moth or a stone-colored cricket — the only animals — will move between shadows and stop again. Nothing hunts here. Nothing calls.

Someone has been here before, or comes here still, but lightly.

On the few dryish places — the tops of the thickest rims, the small islands of built-up travertine that rose just a little higher than the water could claim — there are small, scattered human marks. They are not a village. Not even ruins. They are the kind of things people make when they use a place but do not live in it.

A low windbreak of stacked, unmortared stone, only three courses high, curving around a naturally dry hollow to keep the condensation drip off. Inside, a fire-blackened stone, cold for a long time, surrounded by smooth, palm-sized river stones that were clearly carried up and in from somewhere else — used as seats, or as warmth-holders put in the pools to heat. A few rough clay bowls, thick-walled and fired poorly, left inverted on a ledge so they stay dry. One is cracked clean in half and never mended.

Closer to the center, where the water is warmest, someone has taken a flake of that black basalt and chipped it to an edge — a simple scraper or knife — and left it on the crust beside a pool where the mineral is softer and can be scraped up. The pile of fine white paste is still there, dried hard again. Used, perhaps, to seal those clay bowls, or as pigment, or as medicine. The workmanship is simple, hand-tool, careful but not expert. No metal. No cut wood — there is no wood to cut.

You find a path, too, if you look. Not a built path, but a wear-path: where feet have stepped, for years, on the same broad rims, the crust is worn through to a smoother, darker track. It goes from the low break in the basin wall — the only easy way in and out — in a loop from pool to pool, past each small shelter, without ever having to wade.

Everything about the path says: we come, we sit, we soak, we scrape a little mineral, we leave. We have done this for a very long time. The place lets us.

If you sit on one of those warm stones that is just barely above the waterline, with your feet in a pool that comes exactly to your ankles and stays there, and watch the next pool over spill its invisible sheet into the next, you get a sense of why no one ever changed it or built it up. To dam it or drain it would break the balance that makes it what it is. It is already packed full — full of water, full of stone, full of quiet — saturated, with no room for anything more. It doesn't need anything else.

And it will be exactly like this tomorrow.
