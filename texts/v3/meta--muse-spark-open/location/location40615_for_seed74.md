It is not a forest that swallowed a town. It is a town that learned how to be a forest.

You come down into it — not up, never up, because this is low country. Elevation 1.1: a broad, cool basin where the air settles and stays. Not a valley with dramatic walls, just a long shallow declination in the land, like a palm held open. The rivers here gave up carving canyons a long time ago. Erosion is established, 2.5/3 — everything is settled into its own state, weathered soft at the edges. Curbs are rounded. Bricks are pitted. Even the rust has rusted into a kind of soil.

The town is small. You can walk its length in twenty minutes if you stay on the ironwork catwalks, forty if you go by the root-stairs. Scale 0.9/3 — hamlet-size, grove-size, room-size. A single bowl of land with boundaries you can feel: where the basin lip rises, the plant architecture thins, and beyond that is just cool, damp field. Inside the bowl, everything that happens, happens.

And inside the bowl, vegetation is total. 2.6/3. Not decorative. Not parks. Plant-as-substrate.

It began as an industrial river town — built 2.0/3 urban, tech 1.9/3 industrial — and it never stopped being that. The grid is still there. You can see it in the way the load-bearing vines grow. They were planted — a century ago, two? — as bio-infrastructure for insulation and carbon capture, a municipal project: engineered ivy, black-bark kudzu, structural moss. The factories were supposed to be wrapped in a living skin to keep the cool in and the smoke in check.

Then the skin became the body.

Now the streets are not lined with trees; they are made of them. The trunks have grown together into colonnades, their canopies grafted into vaulted roofs. What were once brick tenements are now hollowed burls, floors and walls still brick underneath but faced thickly with living bark that the residents prune into doors and windows. The windows are not cut; they are coaxed open in spring and shuttered with leaves in fall. The factories still stand — sawmill-basilicas, brick and soot and glass — but their walls sweat green. Their smokestacks are breathing organ pipes now, lined inside with filter-lichen so thick it hums when the draft pulls.

You can read the urban plan in the plants. The avenues are all running water and light: long, straight tunnels of woven willow and alder where the tramlines used to be. The blocks are perfect squares of root-mat housing, each building a single vast organism that has swallowed four or five old houses and made them into one connected hollow tree-house. Balconies are shelf fungi, hard as concrete. Plumbing is hollow vine, tapped and valved.

And yet — density 0.9/3, sparse.

For all that mass of living architecture, there are not many people. Not many presences at all. The town was built for ten thousand and holds maybe three hundred now. That is the quiet rule of this place: much space between presences. You walk a whole pillared avenue of living wood and corrugated factory echo and pass one old woman tending a condensation collector, one kid asleep in a hammock of live cable-vine. The buildings are huge but mostly empty. The factories run at quarter capacity, tended as much by growth as by hands. Doors stand open to green-lit rooms with no one in them.

Fauna 0.7/3 — rare. The plants did not invite animals. Insects in carefully managed numbers — pollinator drones, actual drones with gossamer wings, and a few true bees that work the flower-facades. No birds flocks, just a single heron sometimes, standing in a pocket of water like a statue. Rats are gone; the roots exude something they dislike. People are the most common animal, and even they are sparse.

Water 0.9/3 — damp, not wet.

There is no river in the town center anymore, just its memory. The water here is intermittent, pocketed, economical. It comes as condensation. The whole basin is cool — 0.8/3, early-October cool even in summer — and normal light, 1.6/3, a flat, overcast-bright that filters through layers of leaf-roof and factory glass without ever being bright or dark. In that cool and light, everything sweats.

Every surface drips without rain. The leaves exhale, the brick exhales, the still-warm skin of the boilerhouse down by the old canal exhales. By morning, the catwalks are beaded. By noon, the beads have run together into threads that run down bark-gutters into cisterns. On the outer leaves, it gathers and falls as proper rain every three or four days — a brief, polite shower that drums on the canopy-roof and vanishes into gutter-roots. The only standing water is intentional: round, dark pools set into plazas where a building was removed to let the sky in; rain barrels the size of rooms; the Canal itself, a straight, black, still cut that still carries one barge, back and forth, back and forth, for no reason anyone can remember except that the mechanism still works.

Erosion says long-existed. This place is in balance. Nothing is collapsing, nothing is new. The roots do not crack the foundations anymore; they *are* the foundations. The iron does not rust away; the lichen arrests it at orange-black and holds it there, weatherproofed. The town has reached a state where it can continue indefinitely like this, with pruning.

Weirdness 1.1/3 — unusual, but not impossible. It is just enough to make you tilt your head.

Because the plants keep industrial time. That is the active part — dynamic 2.4/3. This is not a sleepy ruin. Multiple processes are running at once, visibly.

The factories breathe. The filter-stacks inhale cool air with a slow sigh all afternoon — the lichen inside expanding. The sap pumps tick. At 3pm the western avenue-flowers — huge, engineered, pale as paper — all open at once with an audible pop-pop-pop and turn their faces a fraction toward the light, and the light shifts in the whole western quarter. Down in the lower workshops, the vine-looms keep weaving insulation quilts out of air-root fiber whether anyone is there to collect them or not; you can see the finished bolts piling up, green-black, untouched. On the roofs, the moss charges its weak electric field — tech 1.9 industrial but now photosynthetic — and the streetlamps, old copper and glass, come on early, not because of a switch but because the plant-battery has reached voltage.

It is active the way a beehive is active, or a tidal pool. Quietly busy. Quiet is the rule — sound 0.8/3, mostly quiet — because all that living mass is insulation. The engines are still running underneath — low, huge diesels wrapped in root felt, turning over slowly to pump water and sap — but you hear them as you would hear your own heartbeat when lying on moss: thud ... thud ... thud ... muffled, felt in the feet more than the ears. Most of the time the loudest thing is water moving: drip to gutter, gutter to cistern, cistern valve clicking open, the soft sigh of a leaf-door opening.

The rules of the world here are simple, and learned by children without being taught:

1. Do not cut, prune.
2. Collect the water where it gathers; do not try to make it gather elsewhere.
3. Keep to the sparse paths. The plant will fill anything you leave alone for more than a week, and it is impolite to make it withdraw again.
4. Tend your machine — the boiler, the pump, the cutter, the stitcher — even if you do not need what it makes. The machines and the plants have an agreement, and you are the mediator.

You leave your shoes at the edge of a root-mat floor. Inside, the living walls are warm despite the cool air, and damp to the touch in a way that is not unpleasant. Light comes through leaf windows green and even, normal as day. Somewhere far above, a factory whistle blows — not loud, not urgent, just marking the hour for no one — and the sound doesn't echo. The leaves take it.
