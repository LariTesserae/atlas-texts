This place has no horizon.

Not because the light fails, though it does, but because there is no distance in which a horizon could form.

You are high. High enough that the air has thinned and cooled to a permanent early-morning chill, that clean, thin cold that makes your breath hang and then be swallowed. The elevation here is continental — a plateau the size of a country that is still rising. This is not an old mountain, worn down and thoughtful. This is a new place. Raw geology.

The stone beneath everything is black and glassy, still warm in its deep seams. Pillars of basalt are pushing up through themselves like teeth coming in, hexagonal and steaming where they meet the air. The ground cracks audibly as it cools. Everywhere water finds those cracks.

### A Wet Lung at Altitude

It never really rains here; it condenses.

The air is saturated. You are inside a cloud that never quite decided to be a cloud. Water beads on every surface, runs down every vertical, gathers in every cupped leaf and overflows. The rocks weep continuously. Low cliffs of new stone sweat cold, clear water that falls a thousand feet as a slow, white-threaded veil before it ever hits a pool. There are no rivers yet — rivers require erosion, require time — only sheets and seeps and tarns, shallow pans of black water perched on warm rock, steaming faintly in the cool air.

The sound of it is impossible to get away from. 

Water dripping, trickling, sluicing, pattering on leaves as broad as roofs, splatting into pools. Water exhaled and inhaled by the plants themselves. A low-frequency groan as stone shifts under hydraulic pressure. And underneath it all —

Growth.

### The Plant That Is The Place

There is no ground here, not really. There is plant.

Vegetation on a 2.6/3 is not “forest.” Forests have ground with trees on it. This has plant on plant on plant, a living mass so packed there is no room left for anything else. Think of it as architecture that grew itself.

The base layer is a dense, springy mat of moss and liverwort feet thick — black-green, rust-red, yellow where it catches the dim light — that holds water like a sponge and sighs when you step on it, releasing a puff of cold mist. From that mat rise trunks, but not like trees. They are columns, buttressed and fused together, walls of photosynthesizing wood with no bark, only more leaves. Their leaves are vast, leathery, overlapping like shingles on a roof that goes on for a hundred miles. The canopy is not a layer — it is a depth. Fifty, a hundred meters deep of wet, interleaving leaf, vine, aerophyte, epiphyte growing on epiphyte. 

Nothing ever falls here. A dead leaf is caught by ten living arms before it can drop. Seeds germinate in mid-air, in the crook of a larger leaf, sending white root-threads questing down through the humid dark for kilometers until they find stone.

There are no animals. Nothing eats with a mouth. The ecology is entirely botanical and mineral, but it is not still.

Vines as thick as your torso constrict and release, pumping water up from the stone fields below to the dim upper reaches. Sap moves with audible pressure, ticking and creaking inside translucent stems big as subway tunnels. Flowers — if you can call them that — open with a hydraulic hiss, a meter wide, dark purple and infrared-black, exhaling a heat-fog of spores into the cool air, then snap shut seconds later, trapping the warmth for their own metabolism. The whole continent breathes. Pollination is done by wind and by water-drip and by ballistic launch: certain seed-pods build pressure for days and then detonate in the dark with a sharp crack, flinging their contents through walls of wet leaf.

And there is light, but not much. A cool, overcast, high-altitude gloom filters down through endless wet layers. Everything glows faintly at the edges. Not bioluminescence — refraction. The leaves are full of engineered silica lattices, like opal, that catch the weak, diffuse sun and pipe it inward, down stems, along roots, deep into places the sun has never touched. At night you can see it: a faint, deep-green tracery of light moving through the walls of the plant-city, like blood.

### The Technology Without Tools

You will not find a city here. No roads, no towers, no cuts. The parameters say built: none, and it’s true in the human sense. No one built anything.

And yet nothing here is natural. Or rather, nature has been replaced by something that does the same work better.

Look closer at a leaf. On its underside, the stomata open and close not randomly but in waves, rippling across kilometers in coordinated pulses, pumping atmosphere. The water that beads on them is not pure; each droplet is positioned, sized. The moss is doped with nanostructures that assemble themselves, breaking down the fresh basalt as it rises, turning raw glass into soil that never existed before at a speed geology cannot explain. The roots excrete acids that are also circuit etchants, laying down conductive filaments of carbon and iron through stone, chatting to each other through ion exchange at what must be gigabits per breath.

The whole vast, packed, wet, cool, half-dark continent is a machine that does not look like a machine because the substrate it chose was plant. It is terraforming itself, in real time. The embryonic stone — that 0.1/3 erosion, pre-formation — is being laced and held and weathered and hydrated by intention. The deafening sound is not just water and growth. If you stand perfectly still in a hollow between two fused leaf-columns, where the water is momentarily quieter, you can hear the other sound:

A high, clean thrumming. Not mechanical. Computational. Trillions of microscopic switches opening and closing inside lignin. The forest thinking about how to be a forest.

It is cold enough that you shiver. It is wet enough that you are soaked through in minutes. It is dim enough that you lose depth perception. It is so densely alive that there is nowhere to put your hands that is not alive, and so vast that the weather above the upper leaves is different from the weather inside them — thunderstorms crawling across a green sky while, fifty meters below, there is only ever a patient, endless drizzle of condensation.

It is still being born. The stone is still warm. The plants are still learning to be the shape that will hold this much water on such a high, cold roof.

And it makes no room for you. It isn’t hostile. It simply has no empty spaces where an animal could fit.
