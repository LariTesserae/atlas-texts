This is the Hollow Hamlet.

You don't find it so much as you step down into it. The land around it is low and flat and tired-looking — old warm lowland, the kind that has been warm for so long it has forgotten how to be anything else. Alluvial plain, silt and clay settled into its final shape, not eroding anymore so much as *being eroded*, in the perfected, weathered way a riverbed is. The banks are smooth. The channels know where they go. Everything is established, in-balance, old.

And then there is a place where the ground simply gives way, not as a collapse, but as an opening.

### The Architecture

It is about the size of a hamlet. You could walk its circumference in ten minutes if there wasn't so much in the way — though there isn't. That's the first wrongness.

The vegetation here is total. Not "lush." Not "overgrown." Total as in substrate. As in there is nothing else. The walls, the floors, the roofs — all are plant, all are alive. But not dense.

Think of a cathedral grown instead of built.

Three, maybe five, immense living structures root in the low silty floor — they are trees the way an apartment block is a pile of bricks: technically true, uselessly reductive. Their trunks are ribbed and flanged like buttresses, pale green-grey bark stretched over wood that sighs when the warm air moves. Their lower branches have fused and flattened into canopies, into eaves and terraces. Their aerial roots have come down thick as columns, twenty feet across at the base, and re-entered the ground, becoming walls. Between one root-column and the next is a doorway, an alley, a nave.

Inside, it is empty.

That is the principle of this place. The plant mass encloses vast hollow volume. The Hamlet is mostly air. You stand in a vaulted room the size of a town square, its ceiling a living weave of leaves a hundred feet overhead, light coming through as normal, dappled daylight — not too bright, not dim — filtered green-gold through layers of transpiring leaves. The floor is a springy mat of interwoven rootlets over damp earth. There is nothing in the room. The next room, formed in the axil of a second great plant, is also empty. And the next. Urban in plan — cross-streets of root, plazas of clear ground under leaf-domes, obvious second stories made of broad flat limbs — but with almost no objects per volume. The city is here, but the inhabitants have left the architecture hollow.

It's not abandoned. It's grown that way on purpose.

Damp lives here in pockets. This is not a swamp. The greater air is warm and dryish, humid only in its breath. But condensation runs down the interior of every vast leaf-ceiling all night and drips from the tips all day. Grooves in the root-floors channel it. In every low point there is a basin, a cup, a cistern — the plant's own gutters and cups and folded leaves, some large enough to bathe in, some small enough to hold in two hands. Clear, rain-warm water that trembles with larvae and light. Narrow riverine threads cut between the buttress roots, there for an hour after the midday cloudburst, gone by evening, leaving dark mud that smells like iron.

### The Sound

You hear it before you descend.

Deafening. Not loud — *deafening* — a physical weight. The hamlet is an instrument built to amplify.

The air itself roars with insects. Not a hum, a roar. Cicada-analogues the size of your forearm cling to the column-roots by the thousands, their abdomens pulsing, each one hitting a note that rattles your teeth, and together they make a chord so solid it feels like architecture. Under that, birds — or what fill the bird niche — scream in mimicry, in chorus. Frogs in the leaf-cups boom like drums, so low your sternum vibrates. The leaves, huge as sails, applaud and shudder against each other in the thermal wind, a dry rustling raised to the scale of a forest collapsing.

And underneath all that, the plants themselves sound. The sap moves with an audible hydraulic sigh and creak. The great limbs grow, and you can hear them: a slow, constant micro-cracking, the sound of wood laying itself down cell by cell, at predator-prey speed, at weather-cycle speed. It never stops.

You have to open your mouth to equalize the pressure.

### The Life and the Breakdown

It is active. Everything is in motion. Vines throw themselves across a plaza overnight. A water-cup tree flowers between your blinks, white trumpets opening with a pop. Things eat things. Bright beetles strip a leaf dome in an hour, and the dome regrows it by dusk. Small monkey-things with leaf-green fur chase flicker-flies through empty air. Serpents that look like hanging vines drop onto them and constrict. Fungi balloon and burst and send sweet rot through a terrace, which sinks and is shored up again by a new root in a day. This is an ecosystem operating at city pace.

There is no technology here. No devices, no tools, no cut marks. And yet the built-ness is undeniable. The root-streets are too regular. The plazas too round. The leaf-gutters too well-placed to be accident. The plants are building a town in the only way plants can — by becoming it. Or they are remembering a town that was here before the erosion settled, and rehearsing its shape.

This is where the weirdness lives.

Because emptiness at this scale shouldn't be empty. In the second square, your shadow falls the wrong way. The light is normal, but it comes from a sun that is not where the sun should be for that shadow. In the third square, the sound doubles, echoes before it is made. You throw a seed-stone into a water cup and the ripples move inward first, then outward. The hollow rooms are bigger inside than they should be — you can see one column-root through another, as if space is thinned. Warm air hangs still when the leaves overhead thrash; wind blows when nothing moves.

The hamlet breathes with you, then out of time with you. The plants notice you and don't. A new root-wall will grow overnight to block the way you came, and open a new arch where there was solid trunk yesterday. The city rearranges for traffic that isn't there. The fauna act as if crowded streets exist — ants march in highways across empty air, birds nest in densities that would suit a metropolis, all of them singing at deafening volume to fill a volume that is already full of sound.

At the center, where the lowland dips lowest, all the root-gutters meet in a single, wide, shallow pool. It never dries. It is warm as blood. Its surface is completely still despite the roar, reflecting the leaf-cathedral perfectly, and in its reflection there are lights in the windows of the plant-buildings — warm yellow lights — though there are no windows and no lights when you look up.

If you stay until condensation hour, the water rises a finger's breadth and the whole hamlet exhales at once, and for a second the emptiness is not empty at all.
