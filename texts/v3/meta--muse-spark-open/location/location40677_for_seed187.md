This place is a flat.

Not flat like a plain, flat like something that has finished being anything else. The land here is low, only a few feet above its own water table, and so old it has forgotten how to have hills. Glaciers came and went and came again a long time ago and left it scoured down to a broad, shallow dish of stone - not dramatic bedrock, but old shield rock, pink-gray granite gone soft at the edges, rounded, pitted, lichen-stained, sunk into itself. There is no slope to speak of, no ridge, no valley. You could walk for an hour and gain or lose three feet of elevation and not know it except by which way the water is seeping.

It is a grove, if a grove can be a whole country. The boundaries are not walls but a change in breath. You step into it and the air changes. The scale is small, hamlet-sized, room-sized on the scale of the earth - maybe three hundred yards across, cupped by that imperceptible dish. You can see its edges from anywhere inside it, a low dark rim of older trees where the ground finally firms up and the world resumes. Inside, there are no structures. No cabin, no fencepost, no cairn. Nothing was ever built here, or if it was it has been completely taken.

And yet it is industrial.

Not in ruins. In operation.

Long before this hollow grew shut, someone brought machines here to work the low ground. Not a factory - there was never a building, just engines left to do a job. Squat, riveted iron things on sled runners, peat harrowers and sphagnum presses, condensation collectors, low-pressure steam pumps meant to drain and bale and process. They were supposed to be attended. They were left running.

They still are.

There are five of them, spaced absurdly far apart in the emptiness of the grove. That is the rule here: everything is far from everything else. The vegetation is total - it is not that plants *grow* here, it is that *here is made of plants* - but it is not dense. It is empty, vaulted, cathedral-scale. Each plant has been given leave to become architecture.

The dominant form is a kind of giant moss, or clubmoss, or something between moss and fern that has no proper name anymore. It grows as pillars. A single stem as thick as a man's thigh rises twenty, thirty feet straight up, then explodes at the top into a broad, flat crown of feathery fronds that meet and interlace with the crowns of its neighbors to form a living ceiling. The trunks are shaggy with epiphytes - smaller mosses, liverworts, filament ferns that hang like damp green felt. Between the pillars there is vast, open air. You can see a long way through the grove, from one moss-pillar to the next, thirty or forty feet of clear, cool air, the floor entirely carpeted but the volume mostly empty. Near-void, hollow, quiet in its spacing.

The floor is what holds the water. It is not a swamp, not a marsh. It is damp in the old, deep way. The ground itself is a mattress, a meter thick, of living peat and sphagnum and root-mat that gives under your boot and sighs. It is never flooded, but it is never dry. Water hangs in it the way sweat hangs in wool. In the cool air - cool enough that your breath shows faintly in the morning, cool enough that the peat never rots quickly - condensation beads constantly on the undersides of the big fronds and drips, tick tick tick, down onto the mat. The machines help with this. Their condensers are still cold.

Each engine sits in its own shallow, perfectly round pool, a working hollow it has maintained for a century. The pool is not deep - six inches of dark tea-colored water, clear enough to see the white granite bottom - fed by seepage and by the drip from above, drained infinitely slowly by the pump's puttering. The engine in the center of the pool sits on its sled, rusted the color of dried blood but intact, its flywheel turning, turning, turning at the same unhurried speed it turned a hundred years ago. A piston sighs. A belt, made of some rubber that never perished, squeaks around a drum that winds sphagnum onto a spool, compresses it, bales it, and then - with no one to take the bale - drops it wetly back into the pool to unravel and grow again.

That is the equilibrium. That is why nothing changes.

The grove is in balance to a degree that feels intentional, though no one intends it. The machines take exactly as much as grows back. The water that is pumped out seeps back in. The peat that is cut swells again. The temperature stays cool, the light stays normal, diffuse, overcast-bright, filtered green through the high crowns but not dim. This is not a place that progresses. It has reached its oldest stable state. Erosion had already finished its work before the machines arrived; now biology and iron have formed a loop so tight that time has nowhere to catch.

And it is loud.

That is the first surprise, after the emptiness. You expect silence in a place so hollow and so still. Instead the grove roars, gently, constantly, without ever peaking or fading.

Part of it is the engines. Five of them, out of sync but never varying, making a low, rolling industrial chorus: chuff-chuff-chuff of the steam pumps, the whine of flywheels, the creak of the bale-presses, the hiss of relief valves letting off white ghost-puffs that vanish instantly in the cool air. The sound carries perfectly in the vaulted space between the pillars, echoing faintly off living wood.

Part of it is the fauna, which should not be able to be abundant in a place so empty, and yet is. Abundance here does not mean crowding. It means every niche is filled and singing at once.

The pools around the engines are alive with amphibians. Small black newts with orange bellies crowd the water by the hundreds, completely silent, completely still. The sound comes from above them. Cloud-like midges rise and fall in columns between the pillars, and the birds that feed on them never stop. They are small, moss-colored warblers with absurdly loud, clear, repeating calls - *tsee-tsee-tsee-TCHEW* - one per pillar, each claiming its trunk, answered distantly by another, and another, around the rim. On the mat itself, rail-like birds with long toes run, flicking, rustling constantly through the sphagnum. Crickets, though it is too cool for crickets, saw away in the root-mat without pause. Tree frogs - impossible this far north, but here they are - inflate their throats in the crowns and make a sound like two smooth stones struck together, *tok, tok*, in a rhythm that meshes, accidentally, with the piston chuff.

Nothing is alarmed. Nothing flees when you walk. The animals have lived their whole lives with the engines and have incorporated that throb into their baseline the way ocean animals incorporate surf. A warbler will land on a hot flywheel to sing louder.

There are rules here, though no one wrote them:

1. Nothing rots fast. The cool, the damp, the acidity of the sphagnum preserve everything. A fallen frond from a crown will lie for years, bleached, still recognizable, slowly pressed into the mat.
2. Nothing grows beyond its allotment. The moss-pillars do not crowd each other. They maintain their thirty-foot spacing as rigorously as trees in an orchard, chemical signals in the peat keeping them apart.
3. Nothing stops. If an engine were to stop, the pool would overtop and the mat would close over it in a season. But the engines do not stop. They have been oiled by rain and by the fatty spores of the moss itself, which drift like rust-colored snow and settle in every joint.

You can walk the whole grove in twenty minutes if you go straight, but you won't. You will have to pick your way around pools, duck under hanging ferns that have grown to the size of blankets, pause because a pillar trunk in front of you is suddenly revealed to be not a trunk at all but a solid column of living roots braided together, with beetles as big as your thumb moving slowly up it, carrying mites that carry smaller mites.

The light at noon is white and even. At evening it goes amber for an hour and then it is night, with the engines' small fireboxes glowing dull red under their boilers, never stoked, never dark, fed on compressed peat that feeds itself.

It does not feel haunted. It does not feel weird. It feels normal in the way an old field feels normal - a place that has settled into exactly what it is and asks nothing of you. The water seeps. The pillars drip. The flywheels turn. The birds call. The mat breathes under your feet and then, very slowly, erases your footprint after you have gone.
