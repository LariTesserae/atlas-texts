The city was not built. It was extruded.

This is a low country - not quite sea level, but close enough that you can feel the weight of the air settling. The horizon is wide and flat in every direction, a young basin ringed by sharp things. The valley itself is only a few million years old, torn open by a fault that hasn't had time to learn how to erode. The walls are still sheer, saw-toothed, black basalt and pale limestone in clean, vertical lifts where the earth cracked and pulled apart. No soft hills, no rounded river stones. Every edge is fresh, as if the world was cut with a knife and hasn't yet healed. Scree slopes at the base of the scarps are short and angular, piles of broken plates leaning against each other.

Between those distant walls, the land is open. You can see weather coming from an hour away, even in the dim.

The light never gets bright here. It isn't night. It's a perpetual late-afternoon under a high, warm haze - a layer of stratocumulus that never quite breaks, diffusing the sun into a uniform amber-gray. The sky is luminous without being light. Shadows are soft or absent. At noon you can look directly at where the sun should be and see only a brighter bruise in the cloud. The air itself is warm, 28 or 30 degrees, humid but not wet. It holds you like breath.

And yet there is not much water.

There are no lakes, no wide rivers. The great basin has no outlet to the sea and no inlet from the mountains. What water there is arrives sideways - on the wind, as night condensation, as a sudden, warm rain that drums for twenty minutes and vanishes before puddles can form. The city drinks the air.

That is what the city is for, perhaps.

From a distance, it looks sparse. Not a dense metropolitan smear, but a scattering of verticals across a broad, green emptiness. Ten, twelve, maybe twenty major structures in a valley that could hold a hundred. Each one stands alone, kilometers from its neighbor, connected by nothing you can see at ground level. Between them: open ground, lush and uninterrupted.

The vegetation is everywhere that concrete is not - and in many places where it is. Because water is scarce on the ground but abundant in the air, life has gone epiphytic, aerial. The ground cover is a dense, springy mat of low ferns and black-green mosses that need no roots, only dew. Above that, on every flat surface, on the windward faces of buildings, on the cables that shouldn't be there, hang gardens. Long curtains of bromeliads and staghorns, vines with waxy leaves designed to comb moisture from fog, pitcher plants the size of barrels that fill overnight with condensation and slowly release it during the day. The buildings themselves are furred with it. You cannot tell where architecture ends and botany begins, because the architects didn't want you to.

The buildings - towers is the wrong word, though they are tall. They are narrow at the base and wider as they go up, like inverted deltas or mushrooms that have forgotten gravity is supposed to work. Their skins are not glass, not metal. It's a matte, dark material that drinks the dim light and returns it slightly shifted, a faint polarization at the edge of vision. Up close you can see it move. Not moving like a machine - moving like skin healing. A nanoscale weave that tightens and loosens, opening pores the size of pinpricks to exhale warm air, closing them to keep water in. When it rains, the entire flank of a structure will go dull and pitted, collecting every drop, channeling it in invisible capillaries to the cisterns held in its crown.

No roads. No cars. The space between the towers is supposed to be empty. The infrastructure is not on the ground. Look up on a clear-hazed evening and you can see it: faint lines of heat and bent light connecting tower to tower, high up, hundreds of meters in the air. Transit threads - not bridges, but held places. Corridors of altered air where gravity is told, locally and politely, to lean sideways. A pod, or a person, steps off a platform and simply falls horizontally, silently, along an invisible slope. The air doesn't ripple. The light does, just a little, like heat above asphalt.

That is the strangest thing about this place, more than the floating transit or the breathing walls. The physics is... negotiated. In the center of the valley, between the four largest structures, there is a column of space about half a kilometer wide where rain never falls straight down. It falls slowly, then stops, then rises a few meters before evaporating, caught in a standing low-gravity well that the city maintains whether it needs to or not - an old utility field, perhaps, or an accident of overlapping systems that no one has bothered to correct. Dust hangs there permanently, a vertical river of golden motes that never settles. At night it glows faintly with captured charge. The locals call it the Stillness and walk around it without comment.

You don't hear the city. That is the second strangest thing. For something so large, so active, it is mostly quiet. The towers make no hum. The transit makes no whine. The sound is biological, low, intermittent. The rustle of the hanging gardens shifting to face the wind. The soft pop of pitcher plants opening at dusk. The tick of contraction as basalt cools. Far off, a tower will inhale - a long, slow intake of air through its pores - and you will feel it as a pressure change in your ears more than hear it.

Animals are rare. Not absent, but thinly spread. In the warm, damp dim, you expect insects in clouds, birds in flocks. There are none. Something about the field, about the engineered atmosphere, discourages them. Instead there are large, solitary things that move seldom. A six-legged glider with translucent, leaf-wide wings that lives its entire life in the canopy of a single tower's garden, drinking from the pitchers. Pale salamanders as long as your arm in the moss mat, who only move after those brief rains. High above, in the Stillness, something like a manta - not a machine, not an animal, or both - will sometimes turn a slow circle, filtering the suspended dust, and then be gone for weeks.

The rules here are simple and unspoken:

1. Do not build on the ground. The ground is for growing.
2. Take water only from the air.
3. Keep the city sparse, so the wind can pass through.
4. Keep the light dim, so the heat doesn't build.

People live here. You see them rarely, which makes the scale feel even larger. A figure on a high balcony tending a vine wall. Two silhouettes falling silently sideways from one tower to the next, feet not touching anything. They wear dark, loose clothing that collects dew on its shoulders. They are not loud, either.

At ground level, between two towers, you can stand in moss up to your ankles. The air is warm and smells of wet stone and green. Above you, the towers rise like dark trees, their crowns lost in haze, their sides bearded with hanging forests that drip once a minute with collected water. Droplets fall straight down here, except in the Stillness. The young cliffs on the horizon are a jagged, blue-black cutout against the glowing sky. The whole valley breathes, in and out, condensation forming and evaporating, gardens opening and closing, gravity leaning invisibly from tower to tower.

It feels less like a city you inhabit and more like a machine for making a climate that a city can inhabit - and then, almost as an afterthought, letting the jungle back in to cover it.
