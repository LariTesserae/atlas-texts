This is the bottom of the world, or what feels like it.

Not a basin exactly, because a basin implies rims. Not a valley either, because there are no sides to be a valley between. It is a low country that has been low for so long it has forgotten any other shape. The continent here has been sitting still since before memory — no uplift, no folding, no volcano has troubled it in eons. Whatever mountains once stood here have been ground down to a rumor.

What remains is a plain so old it is almost perfectly level. Not flat like a table — flat like a palm left open. You can see for a town's distance in every direction and still not find a true hill. The horizon is a straight, clean line softened only by heat. If you walk a mile, your feet will rise and fall perhaps six inches. The land swells and dips in breath-shallow undulations, broad as sleeping whales under the skin of the earth.

The rock is ancient underneath, but you never see it fresh. It is all deep-weathered saprolite, kaolin clay turned white and red, laterite pisoliths like rusted marbles, sandstone gone soft and porous as stale bread. In the few places the stone does outcrop — low, rounded whalebacks no higher than your chest, polished bald by wind and rain for a hundred million years — it weeps. Water beads out of the rock itself, even when it hasn't rained for days, seeping from a thousand tiny pores, running in dark trickles down lichen-black flanks to be swallowed immediately by the ground at its feet.

Because the water is everywhere, but not deep.

The water table sits right at the surface here. It doesn't need rain, though rain comes often — warm, heavy, short-lived downpours in the early afternoon that pass and leave the air steaming. Between rains, the plain simply stays wet. The soil is saturated to the top inch. Every footstep sinks slightly and fills with tea-colored water. Shallow pans, miles wide and only inches deep, stretch between the low swells. Meandering creeks have no banks to speak of; they are just slightly more definite threads of flow within the general wet, tea-stained channels that braid and rejoin and lose themselves in reed beds without ever deciding to be a river.

The light is white and direct. High sun in a pale, washed-out blue sky, thinned by humidity. Heat hangs without being brutal — 28, 30 degrees, the kind of warmth that makes air hold water and makes everything grow soft. The atmosphere condenses on everything. Leaves drip even when it hasn't rained. Your skin never quite dries. Distant things shimmer, not from desert mirage but from evaporation, the whole plain quietly exhaling.

Vegetation is sparse, and that is what makes the openness visible.

This is not a jungle. Not a forest. Trees exist as individuals, not as a canopy. Widely spaced melaleucas and paperbarks, their trunks swollen at the base, bark peeling in wet white sheets. A kind of stunted eucalypt with a wind-bent crown. Clumps of pandanus. Between them, the world is sedge and reed and spike-rush, tussocks sitting on their own pedestals of peat above the water, their roots knotted like fists. Floating mats of fern and water-lettuce cover the stiller pans, green and unbroken until a bird's legs punch through. There is a lot of bare ground — not bare like desert, but bare like wet clay and dark peaty mud, slick, pocked by crab holes and worm casts, reflecting the sky.

No plant crowds another. Each one has its generous allotment of sky and water. And because of that, you can see how the plain works. The pattern is hydrological, not botanical: slightly higher ground — a four-inch rise — is drier, with tougher grasses and scattered trees; slightly lower ground is open water and reeds. The vegetation maps the micro-topography like contour lines drawn in green.

No humans have built here. No posts driven, no ditch cut, no wall raised. Nothing is planted or cleared. If people ever passed through, the wet has taken the trace. Fallen trunks lie where they fell and slowly melt into peat. No smoke, no road, no straight line anywhere that nature did not draw herself in a meander.

And yet it is not empty. It is loud.

The sound hits you first as a single wall, then separates into layers as you listen. The base note is insects — a solid, shimmering, almost electric drone of cicadas and crickets and midges that never stops, only dips slightly and swells again. Over that, amphibian sound: the deep bottle-knock of one kind of frog, the high repeating peep of another, the rolling croak of a third, tens of thousands of them spaced out across the reeds, calling without hurry. Birds punctuate — the harsh staccato of a heron taking off, the liquid trill of unseen rails inside the sedge, the occasional far-carrying whoop of a crane. Water makes its own quiet noises against that: the plop of a fish surfacing in a pan, the trickle off a weeping rock, the rustle of a floating mat nudged by wind.

It is loud the way a warm rain is loud — not violent, but present everywhere, impossible to ignore, coming from no single source.

Life is abundant here precisely because it is so wet and warm and so thinly spread. Dragonflies the size of small birds hawk in slow circuits, wings amber in the sun. Ibis and spoonbills stand solitary, each one a half mile from the next, perfectly still, watching. Turtles slide off peat banks. Tiny fish, dark-backed, flicker in every puddle. Insect-eating plants hide in the grass. At night the eyes come out — reflected — but even in the day, you sense movement constantly at the edge of vision: a ripple where something entered water, a reed stem shaking where something climbed.

Everything moves slowly.

Water moves slowest of all. Tilt a leaf and watch a droplet travel: it creeps across the plain at walking pace, then less than walking pace, diverted around a sedge hummock, pooling behind a crab mound, losing itself to evaporation before it ever finds the sea, which is far away and almost as flat as this. The wind moves slowly through the reeds, making a long hushing wave that takes minutes to cross a single pan. Clouds build slowly, tall and white, rain slowly, dissipate slowly. Even decay is slow and waterlogged — wood does not rot quickly here so much as it soaks and sinks and becomes ground.

Time here is not measured in events but in saturation. Wet season, wetter season, drying-but-never-dry season. The plain has been in this final, settled state for so long that change has become patient to the point of stillness. It deepens its peat by a millimeter a year. It rounds its last few stones by a grain a decade. It does not need to do anything else.

If you stand still long enough, the sound seems to organize itself around you. The plain is so open you feel you could be seen from miles away, and so level you feel you could hear for miles too. The heat holds you. The water holds your reflection and then gently erases it.
