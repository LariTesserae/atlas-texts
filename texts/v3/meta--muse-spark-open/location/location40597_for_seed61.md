It is not a city you arrive at. It is a city you notice you are already inside.

### The Pocket

Far below the surface - not deep in the mythic sense, just *under* - beneath the cool weight of fieldstone and clay and rootless earth, there is a bubble. Not a cavern system. Not a mine. A single room.

About the size of a town square swallowed whole, or a cathedral cistern, or a lung. An ovoid blister in the bedrock where the basalt cooled wrong a long time ago and never collapsed. The walls are that basalt, but old. Not jagged. Established. Everything here has been in its own state for long enough to stop pretending it will become something else. The stone is smoothed, bellied, licked by water until it shines even in the dark. Pillars are not pillars - they are the places where the ceiling sagged closest to the floor and decided to stay, thick waists of rock rubbed glossy by a thousand years of shoulders brushing past.

This is the whole world. There is no outside visible from here. The scale is intimate, hidden, pocketed. If you lie on your back in the center you can see every edge. That is the unsettling part. You can comprehend it all at once.

And yet it is crowded.

### A Dense, Wet Mineral Town

They built here because the bubble never drains and never floods.

Water is everything and it does not behave like water should. The walls weep. Not in streams, but uniformly, constantly - a cold sweat on every surface. It beads on the ceiling and swells and falls. The air itself is close to condensing; your breath does not plume, it just makes the air heavier. The floor is not a floor. It is a shallow, perpetual sheet, ankle-deep at its deepest, skin-deep at its shallowest, spread over flagstones that were laid by hand.

Someone leveled this place. Someone carried flat grey limestone down through a fissure too narrow to remember now, and set each slab with primitive tools, with hammers of harder stone and levers and patience. The grout is gone, replaced by a black mineral vein that grew itself in. Between the stones, water finds its level and holds it. It never rises. It never recedes. You can watch a drop fall and make its circle and vanish, and the level does not change. This is the rule of the place: equilibrium. It is loud, and it is frozen.

The town is dense because there is nowhere else to build. So they built up and in.

Houses of stacked slate and salvaged basalt rubble, dry-stacked without mortar, chinked with wool that never rots because nothing rots here. Two stories tall at most, because the ceiling is low - cool enough that you can feel it on your scalp when you stand straight. Roofs are flat and also floors; ladders of lashed wood lean everywhere. Cloth lines strung from wall to pillar that hold nothing because there is nothing vegetal to wash, only felt and leather and stone. Every dwelling touches the next. You do not walk through streets, you squeeze through gaps left between outer walls, sideways, one shoulder forward. Doors are low curtains of cured hide, dark with damp. Windows are just holes, because light is communal.

There are maybe sixty structures in a space that should hold ten. Layered construction - a second generation built atop the first's roofs, now using the older walls as foundation. You can see where that happened: a change in stone color, a line of soot.

No plants. Not a blade, not a moss. This is a mineral world. What looks like growth from a distance is mineral accretion - thin fans of white calcite spreading from every drip point, like frozen lichen. Black manganese blooms. In the corners where hands do not often brush, the water leaves behind tiny terraced dams of travertine, empty and white as bone. The only softness that might be biology is in the corners of the cisterns where the water is deepest: a slick of grey-pink biofilm, bacterial, simple beyond naming. It is not vegetation. It does not photosynthesize. It just exists where water touches stone, and it is scraped for food in lean times.

Because there is nothing else alive to speak of. Fauna is rare to the point of superstition. Sometimes a pale salamander, blind and translucent, paddles through the floor-sheet and hides under a flagstone. Sometimes a cave shrimp flicks in a collected pool. The people keep no animals - nothing could be fed. Once in a generation, a rat finds its way down the fissure and is an event. They speak of it for years after.

### Dim, Loud, Unchanging

Light comes from fat-stone lamps. They mine a seam of dull amber rock that catches flame reluctantly in the ceiling's lowest dome - a vein of poor oil shale. They chip it into bowls with those same primitive hand-tools, and set a wick of twisted fiber from below - not plant fiber, but hair and fungal thread that grows on damp leather. The light is dim, 0.7 dim, the color of weak tea. It does not flicker so much as sigh. Each lamp makes a pool of visibility about an arm's length wide, and then gives up. There are dozens of them, always burning, because they are never allowed to go out. To relight one is hard labor in damp air. So the city glows with scattered amber freckles, reflecting endlessly in the wet stone and the floor-sheet. You see the town twice: once in stone, once in its rippling mirror.

And the sound.

God, the sound. It is impossible that something so stable should be so loud.

Because the space is enclosed, pocket-scale, with hard, wet, curved walls, every drop is an event. Not a gentle plink. A sharp *tok* like a knuckle on a bowl. And there are hundreds falling at any given second from a ceiling that is always beaded. Tok. Tok-tok. Tok. An arrhythmic, dense percussion that never resolves into pattern and never ceases. It echoes and re-echoes until you cannot tell which is the drop and which is its ghost. Under that, the lower hush of water sheeting down walls in invisible films, and the occasional louder splash when someone steps and displaces the floor-water and it slaps against a house foundation.

The people have stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your heart. For a visitor, it is deafening. You have to lean close to speak. They speak loudly without knowing it, and their voices are hoarse.

It is cool down here. Not cold enough to ache, cool enough to preserve. Cool enough that the water that touches you feels like a second, truer skin. You never quite dry. Your fingertips prune and stay pruned. The stone never warms.

And it never changes. That is the weirdness. Not a monster, not a floating stone. A thermodynamic impossibility made mundane.

The water level does not rise even after you watch a thousand drops fall. The air does not get more humid. The lamps do not dim as oil is consumed - the stone lamps sweat oil as fast as it burns, a slow weeping, like the walls. The people age, but the city does not erode further, does not fill with silt. Tools chip but the amount of rubble stays the same. You get the sense that if you left and came back in a hundred years, every house would be in the same place, every curtain of hide in the same fold, the same salamander under the same stone. It is weathered to perfection and has stopped weathering. It is in balance in a way open-air places never are.

They have a story that the bubble breathes once a day, inhaling through the fissure somewhere in the dark ceiling where no ladder reaches, exhaling nothing. This is probably not true. But at certain times the condensing atmosphere thickens and for a moment you see your breath after all, a cloud that hangs too long before dissolving.

There is no governance visible, no square, no palace. Urban but not citified. Settlement-scale infrastructure is all water management - small hand-built runnels chipped into flagstones to keep the floor-water moving in a slow, pointless circle around the central pillars and back again. Tiny bridges of flat stone where the flow is deepest. Pots set to catch ceiling-drip for drinking, because floor-water tastes of feet and stone. Everyone tends their own lamp.

You realize after a while that the quietest thing in the loud room is the people. They move slowly, economically, as if conserving a different kind of energy. They have hammered this impossibly dense, wet, dim town into a space that should have held nothing, with nothing but hands and early tools, and then learned to live inside a moment that never passes.

If you stay long enough, the sound stops being loud and starts being pressure, like depth. And the cool stops being cool and starts being normal. And you begin to understand why they never left. There is nowhere to go where equilibrium is so kind.
