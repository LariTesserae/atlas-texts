It is called The Hollow, though no one remembers who hollowed it.

You find it not by looking for it but by falling into it — a low place. Not a valley, not a basin. A low place in the way a held breath is low. The desert above is a hammered shield of salt and basalt, dead flat to the horizon, hot enough to make the air itself seem to thin. There is no water there; not a well, not a seep, not a ghost of a riverbed. The salt has grown together in plates that ring underfoot like cold pottery. The wind has polished the stone until it shines.

And then a crack. A lip. A stair.

The town is inside a blister in the earth.

Long ago — long enough that the wound has healed — gas rose through the cooling world and got trapped and made a bubble. Not a cave. A vesicle. An intact, oblate pocket perhaps three hundred strides across at its widest, half that high, buried twenty meters down. The roof is one smooth piece of dark vesicular basalt, swirled as it cooled, still holding the frozen flow-lines like a thumbprint. It is low. You can see the whole of it at once if you stand in the center, and still feel hidden. It is not a landscape you walk across; it is a room you are inside.

The heat that bakes the salt above is trapped here and concentrated. It wells up from the floor itself. The lower basalt is warm to the touch, almost hot. At noon the air near the ceiling shivers. The light is dim and indirect — only what falls through the Throat, the single ragged skylight where the roof split, and what the town makes for itself. The sun up there is white and pitiless, but down here it comes as a single angled column, thick with suspended dust, that crawls across the floor as the day turns. It never reaches the edges. The rest of the light is ember-light: clay lamps, coal pans, the dull red glow that lives in warm stone. Everything is amber, umber, bruise-purple in shadow.

There is no water here. This is the law the place repeats to you until you believe it.

Your lips will crack in an hour if you do not cover them. Your sweat vanishes before it beads. Wood left untended cures and splits with a sound like a gunshot. And yet the Hollow is lush.

It has no other word. It is a jungle contained in a bowl.

The vegetation does not drink. There are no roots searching for a water table because there is no table to find. What grows here grows *from* dryness. The dominant forms are not green, exactly — green is a water-color. These are soot-black, rust-red, asbestos-white, a glossy deep violet where the light catches. They are lithophytes and aeroliths, a botany built on other chemistries.

Along the warmest seams of the floor, the Glass Reeds grow in loose, empty stands. Each is a hollow tube of silica three meters tall, thin-walled as a lamp chimney, faintly translucent. They do not photosynthesize; they thermosynthesize, flexing minutely to pump the searing air through their bodies. In the heat they chime. The temperature gradient between the floor and the roof makes a slow, permanent convection — hot air rising in the center, falling along the cold wall — and the reeds sing with it constantly. Not rustling. A low, loud, organ chord, each reed tuned to a slightly different length. When the wind above shifts and changes the pressure in the Throat, the whole grove groans and pings, loud enough you have to raise your voice.

Between them sprawl the Blanket Trees. They look like upturned coral, or like vast black lichen grown to the size of a house. A single individual is a low, spreading dome of overlapping waxy plates, each plate hard as horn and slick with exuded oils. There is no soil, so they roof over the basalt and etch it directly, dissolving mineral with acid vapor and precipitating it as structure. Under a plate you can see the stone gone soft and pitted, breathing. Their tops are covered in fine, black velvet hairs that comb the air for dust, for sulfur, for iron oxides. Once a month the hairs slough at once in a dry, rustling snow that never gets wet and is swept up by the town for tinder and fiber.

And in the deepest dim at the bubble's edge, where the roof meets the floor in a crawlspace, hang the Lantern Vines. They are not vines. They are long, rope-like colonies of bladder-units that dangle from the ceiling, swaying in the convection, pulsing faintly with stored heat. Each bladder swells and tautens and then relaxes, forcing hot air through internal valves, making a soft, loud *whuff, whuff, whuff* like a sleeper breathing. At maturity the end-bladders detach and roll across the floor like tumbleweeds of parchment, scattering spores that smell of hot iron.

No insects pollinate them. No birds eat them. There are no animals here at all — a profound, unnatural silence beneath the plant-noise. No buzzing, no scuttling in the thatch. You notice it after an hour: nothing looks back. The Blanket Trees open their plates slightly in the dim morning without being eaten. The Glass Reeds shatter and are not scavenged. Things fall and lie and are slowly dissolved by the next generation of plants. It is a plant town, entirely. The air is sterile and hot and smells of ozone and warm clay.

Because the people came.

The town is built in and under the plants. It is not ruins; it is inhabited, in balance, weathered into its niche. The builders had not much more than hands, stone adzes, copper that they still treat as half-sacred because it comes rarely in nodules sweated out of the basalt. They did not carve the Hollow — it was already here, already the right size — they *furnished* it.

Houses are low, circular walls of dry-stacked scoria, roofed with cured Blanket plates that have been pried up, flattened, and lashed. A roof will last twenty years before it curls. Streets are not streets but the natural alleys between reed stands and tree-domes, worn smooth by bare feet. The whole settlement is layered — first floors built on the basalt, second floors built on the broad backs of old Blanket Trees after the colony underneath died and left a hard, domed platform. Children run across living roofs and the plant does not mind; its waxy surface bears their weight. Hammocks are strung between reeds, though you learn quickly not to tie too tight or the silica will sing louder and crack.

It is urban in density of intention, not in crowd. The Hollow could hold three thousand and holds four hundred, so that it feels both planned and empty. Houses are close, walls touch, cisterns — not for water — stand at every corner. They are tall clay jars for collecting night-dust, and oil pressed from reed joints, and the fine black hair of the Blanket. There are workshops, a temple cut into a thickened pillar where two walls of the bubble met and left a buttress, a market circle where nothing is sold that came from outside because outside has nothing to offer but glare. There is a lot of empty space between presences: a reed grove with a single bench in it, a house wall fifty feet long with one window, a plaza that is only warm stone and a circle of chimes. Sound carries. The loudness of the place is not human loudness. It is the reeds resonating, the vines sighing, the plates ticking as they expand and contract, copper hammers ringing in a forge that never slakes, voices echoing under a stone sky.

Everything is active. This is not a preserved diorama. The Hollow breathes.

In the hot peak, the convection speeds and the whole forest leans inward. Hairs lift. Reeds scream, almost painfully loud, and the people stop work and sit in their doorways with cloth over their ears until it passes. At dusk the column of sun slips off the floor and the temperature drops just enough — still hot, but less hot — that the Blanket Trees exhale. They open their shingled plates a fraction and release a hot, dry puff of spores and volatile oils that hangs in the still air like incense and then settles. Men and women walk with brooms and sweep it before it can root in unwanted cracks.

At night, the wind above howls over the Throat and the pressure change makes the vines inflate taut and drum. The night-watch climbs to the highest Blanket dome with a pole to tap the reeds and keep them from locking into resonance and shattering. You can hear the tapping wander across the dome, *tok tok tok*, under the greater *hmmmm* of the air engine that has run for ten thousand years without stopping.

The erosion here is finished. Nothing is being cut anew. The basalt bubble has found its shape and now merely polishes itself. The floor is pitted where the plants have eaten, the roof is smooth where heat has spalled, the Throat is rimmed with softened, rounded lips. The town repairs the same walls its great-grandparents built with the same stones. It has reached equilibrium: hot enough, dry enough, lush enough, loud enough. A pocket ecosystem sealed against the void above, needing no rain, wanting no animals, growing from stone and heat and air alone, with people tucked into its interstices like seeds in a pod that have learned to tend the pod itself.

If you stay too long you forget thirst as a need for water and remember it as a need for shade.
