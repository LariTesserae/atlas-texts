You are on the Roof.

Not on top of a mountain. On *the* top. The high, final table of the world, where the land rose a long time ago and then simply stopped trying.

### The Table

Elevation here is not a slope. It is a fact. Three or four thousand meters of empty, amber air below you, and then one last lift — a sheer skirt of ancient stone that has been wind-scoured into softness. Up close the cliff isn't jagged. Nothing here is jagged anymore. Erosion has had eons to work on it. This is 3.0/3 eroded: not canyons and teeth, but a primordial smoothness. The basalt and sandstone have been rounded down to the texture of old bone, of river stones the size of counties. The edges of the mesa are buffed, undercut in long, patient scoops, stained black and rust-red where iron and lichen have bled for a million years.

On top, it is almost perfectly flat.

The Roof is not large. You can walk its length in twenty minutes if you don't stop, and you will stop every few steps because there is too much in the way. This is a small place — scale 0.9, a hamlet-scale world with edges you can see. On all sides, the dim air thins out over the lip and falls away into haze. There is no beyond. There is only here, this one high room with the sky for a ceiling.

The stone of the table itself is pitted and shallowly cupped, not into pools but into dry basins, polishing hollows, grinding mortars left by wind. The dust that collects in them is fine as flour, pink-grey, warm to the touch. It holds the day's heat. At midday the surface is sun-warm — 1.6/3 warm, not scorching, but a present, bodily warmth that radiates up through your sandals and the soles of your feet even as the air stays thin and cool in your lungs.

There is no water. Not in any way you would recognize.

No streams. No springs. No wells. No rain that reaches the ground as liquid. At this height the clouds come low and heavy, a dim, pearl-grey sea that drags its belly across the Roof most afternoons, but it never rains. It condenses. It thickens. And then it passes. The stone never puddles. If you leave a bowl out overnight, by morning it will be dry again, filmed only with dust.

And yet —

### The Thicket Town

It is lush. Improbably, claustrophobically lush.

Because what grows here doesn't drink. Not like that.

The Roof is covered, edge to edge, in a dense, low forest-thicket that is more felt than seen. The dominant plants are not trees in the normal sense. They are massed succulent rosettes, barrel-stackers, and air-ivy — things you could mistake for stone until they move slightly in the wind. Their leaves are thick, waxy, grey-green and dusty purple, swollen with stored fog. The largest — the Old Barrels — are as big around as huts, ribbed and furrowed, their skins tight and glossy. Smaller rosettes cluster at their feet like a litter of overlapping plates. Between them, everything is laced together with the Veil: a dryland Tillandsia that hangs in beards and curtains, silver-grey, rootless, living entirely on air. It drapes from every stone lintel and plant-shoulder, it nets the alleys, it softens every hard edge.

Nothing has roots deeper than a handspan. There is no soil to root in, just that warm stone dust and the slow compost of their own fallen leaves, which never quite rot, just crumble into more dust. They make their own soil as they go, a centimeter per century. Slow — 0.8/3 slow. This whole place changes on a timescale you have to squint to see.

And in and through and over and under this thicket, the town is built.

Built is not quite the right word. Grown and wedged might be better. This is 1.8/3 urban: not a scatter of huts, but a genuine, organized hamlet, dense with intention. But 1.4/3 primitive: every wall is dry-stacked from the flat, flaked slabs that the mesa itself sheds. No mortar. No metal nails. Stone fitted to stone, chocked with hardwood pegs cut from the skeletal trunks of dead Barrels. Roofs are woven Veil-mats stretched over rib-frames, weighted with stones.

The town doesn't clear the thicket; it *is* the thicket. A house is a barrel-plant with a door cut into its side and a stone lean-to extending its shadow. An alley is a gap between two rosettes where someone laid down flat stones so you can pass without crushing the pups. Terraces follow the faintest undulations in the table, two courses of stone high, holding back dust and fallen leaves to make a level for drying racks and sleeping platforms. Everything is within arm's reach of everything else. Density 2.2/3 — layered, near-touching presences. You brush Veil with your shoulder as you walk. You duck under a curing rack strung with strips of succulent leaf. A child's handprint is pressed into the dust on a barrel's side at eye level.

It looks like it has always been here. No foundations, no straight avenues. Just accretion. The oldest walls at the center are sunk an inch into the stone, their edges rounded by wind to the same smoothness as the mesa lip. The newest walls at the edge are sharp and still show quarry marks.

### The Light and the Air

Light here is dim, 0.6/3. Not dark. Dim, like late afternoon perpetually.

The sun at this elevation should be brutal, but it isn't. The air is full of fine mineral dust, suspended permanently — the mesa slowly exhaling itself grain by grain — and the near-daily fog-sea diffuses everything. Light arrives flat and directionless, filtered through haze and through the translucent leaves of the canopy. Under the Veil-curtains between houses, it is twilight even at noon. The succulents seem to glow from within, their waxy leaves catching what light there is and holding it. At the edge of the Roof, the world falls away into a luminous void; you can see the shadow of the mesa projected onto the haze below like a dark ship.

Warm, thin, dry, dusty air. Breathing takes a little extra work. Sounds carry.

### The Sound

For such a slow place, it is loud. 1.7/3 loud.

Not with wind or water. With life.

Fauna: 2.3/3 abundant. The aridity does not stop them because the plants do not stop them.

Everything drinks from leaves.

At dawn — a dimming up rather than a sunrise — the Barrels open. Not flowers, but their crown pores: a ring of pale, wet-looking mouths at the top of each plant that exhale stored moisture as a faint, sweet vapor. For an hour the thicket steams. And everything comes to drink.

The air fills. Small, soot-black finches with heavy, nutcracker beaks hammer at the waxy leaves to chew out watery pulp, calling in sharp, stacked chips. Larger gliders — skinny, bat-like things with translucent membranes — launch from the mesa edge, catch the updraft, and snag bearded clumps of Veil to wring them out mid-air, squeaking. On the stone and on the leaves, beetles like polished stones, thumb-sized and copper-green, trundle in lines towards the basal pools where dew has beaded. Lizards, fat and mottled to look exactly like Barrel bark, flick tongues at the beetles. Underfoot, in the dust, things rustle constantly: pale, blind crickets with legs like eyelashes that feed on shed Veil and sing — this is the backbone of the loudness — a high, metallic, almost electrical trill that never stops, day and night, rising and falling in waves as one patch picks up when another pauses.

And over it all, the people. The town is dense, and sound has nowhere to go. You hear children shouting two alleys over, a stone being shaped with a stone hammer — *tok tok tok* — the soft, endless thump of someone pounding leaf-strips to fiber on a slab, the low murmur of old people talking on a roofterrace, the clatter of beetle-shell wind chimes that every doorway has to warn the gliders away.

No one raises their voice much. They don't need to. The thicket itself amplifies, the close stone throws sound back at you, warm and near.

### The Rule of the Place

The rule is simple and understood by every child before they can speak properly: Do Not Waste Pulp.

You never cut a living Barrel down. You take only the outer leaves, and you close the wound with dust paste. You wring Veil gently, and you hang it back. You catch your own moisture: every roof mat funnels its tiny night-dew into a gourd, and a gourd may take a week to fill a hand's breadth. You chew leaf for water; you don't swallow the fiber. You learn the taste of the three edible beetles and the two that will sour your gut for days.

The mesa gives no water because it needs none. It has settled into its final form. It is not becoming a mountain or a valley. It is just this table, warm, high, ancient, patiently holding up its one small, crowded, whispering garden in the dim while the world turns far below in haze, and the plants breathe in the clouds one particle at a time.
