It is an ocean, but not an ocean as a place you go into.

It is an ocean as a place that *is*.

The water has no surface here. Not one you can see. Far above, somewhere beyond cold and pressure and a gradient of thinning dark, there is supposed to be air and sky, but no light from there reaches down intact. What reaches you is a memory of light — a uniform, bruised dusk without direction. It does not cast shadows. It does not warm. At noon it is the color of wet slate; at midnight it is simply less. You learn to tell depth not by brightness but by weight.

The cold is total and even. Not a biting cold, not a wind cold. A soaking, patient cold just above freezing that has had ten thousand years to work itself into everything. Stone holds it. Water holds it. Your bones would hold it if you stayed long enough. Nothing freezes; everything is preserved.

And under it all, flat beyond comprehension, is the Floor.

---

You expect a seafloor to be rock or mud. This one isn't. This one was made.

A long time ago — so long ago that the word "ruin" no longer fits — someone leveled a continent and built on it. Low country, basin-country, a plain that was already ancient and tired and near-flat when they found it, and they finished the job. They paved it. Terracotta brick and quarried limestone and fitted basalt, mile after mile after mile, not as a city with streets but as a city that *is* the ground. Terraces one course high, retaining walls the height of your knee, vast plazas with hairline seams, shallow cisterns and canals and foundations for houses that were never meant to be tall. The work is everywhere. There is no natural substrate left exposed. If you brush away the silt in any random square meter of this buried continent, you will find cut stone underneath.

The technology that did it was simple. You can see it in the tool marks where the silt has slipped away: hand-chiseled faces, wedge-split blocks, rope-worn mooring eyes, stone pounding stone. No poured concrete, no rebar, no glass. Primitive work, but endless work, and precise in the way only endless work can be. Everything is right-angled or gently curved, human-scaled even though its extent is inhuman. A stair of three steps running for a hundred miles until it disappears into gloom. A colonnade with every column broken off at exactly the same height, their stumps softened to domes.

Because what has happened since is erosion, but not the quick violence of rivers. This is old erosion, final-form erosion. For millennia under still, cold, heavy water, with no tides to speak of and only the slowest contour currents dragging their feet across the plain, every sharp edge has been worried away. Corners are round as elbows. Carvings are ghosts, shallow ripples in stone where letters used to be. The mortar between bricks has swollen and gone soft, then sealed again with mineral crust. Silt — fine as flour, grey-white — has settled into every joint and every sunken courtyard until the whole built plain looks half-buried in snow that never melts. The city has settled into itself. It is not collapsing. It has already collapsed, slowly, into a stable shape that will not change again except to get smoother.

It is vast. That is the first thing your mind fails at. There is no hill to climb for perspective, no tower left standing. Visibility in the cold water is thirty, maybe fifty feet before darkness simply absorbs the rest. But you can walk — or swim, heavy and slow — for days in a straight line and the pattern never ends. Plaza becomes terrace becomes canal becomes plaza. Occasionally the floor drops by a single step, eighteen inches, marking some ancient district boundary that now extends further than any modern nation. Weather happens down here, despite the lack of sky. Where currents brush over square miles of slightly darker stone that holds a fraction more heat, the water shivers and turns over, and a pale turbidity blooms for hours, a kind of underwater fog. Storms are just a change in the clarity.

And it is dense. Not crowded — there are no people — but saturated. Every surface within reach is occupied.

Because the city did not stay dead.

Vegetation took it.

Without fish, without whales, without even insects, without anything that moves under its own will except the drift of water itself, the plants have had it all to themselves. Lush does not mean colorful here. In this darkness, in this cold, photosynthesis is a marginal, desperate trickle. The forests that grow from the Floor are black-green and deep olive and rust-brown, colors that look black until you bring your face close. Kelp analogs thicker than your wrist rise from the mortar lines, their holdfasts splitting brick with patient, century-long pressure. Sheet-algae coat the plazas in a fur a foot thick, rippling when a current passes like wind through grass. Filamentous curtains hang from every former lintel and doorway, swaying vertical, ten or fifteen feet long, cold to the touch and slightly oily.

It is a managed lushness, if that makes sense. The plants do not grow wild. They follow the geometry that was given to them. They prefer edges. A field of stone is edged with a hedge of fronds that exactly traces the old property line. A rectangular cistern is filled to the brim with a single bulbous kelp mass, its bladders rising just above the rim like a flooded garden bed. In the avenues — those impossibly long, straight canals now without flow — the vegetation grows in dense rows down the center, as if planted. Of course, no one planted it. It is simply that silt collects deepest there, and light — what little there is — funnels a fraction better along those open cuts.

There are no animals to eat it, to prune it, to carry its spores. And so it propagates by shedding and drifting. The water is full of fines: spores, cuttings, translucent leaf-bits that never rot quickly in the cold. They hang in the water like dust in a cathedral, moving so slowly you have to stare to see them move at all.

That is the sound of this place.

Mostly quiet.

Not silent — liquid never is — but quiet in the way a deep vault is quiet. Your breath, if you have breath, sounds enormous. Beyond it: the creak of a holdfast contracting, a low groan as water temperature shifts by a tenth of a degree and a million stones tick in unison, the soft *pat* of a bladder popping far off and releasing a bead of gas that rises for minutes before it is gone. Sometimes a sheet of algae sloughs off a wall under its own weight and settles with a sigh of silt that takes an hour to clear. No clicks of shrimp. No whale song. No darting. The absence of animal life is not felt as emptiness; it is felt as attention. The forest is listening to itself grow.

The rules here are simple and unbreakable, and you understand them quickly:

1. Light comes only from above, weak and diffuse, and lessens by the fathom. Nothing shines of its own.
2. Warmth does not exist at depth. Movement must be slow or you will spend heat you cannot replace.
3. All ground is floor. There is no wilderness to wander into beyond the city, because the city *was* the leveling of the wilderness. To leave the built plain you would have to walk past its edge, and its edge is an ocean away.
4. Growth is faster than decay. In cold, dark, still water with no grazers, even a little light is enough, given time. Given enough time — and this place has had nothing but time — a little becomes everything.
5. Nothing sudden happens. Currents take months to turn. Seeds take years to root. Stone takes ten thousand years to round. If you wait, you will see the forest breathe once, and that is enough.

If you kneel on the Floor and press your palm to a brick — its surface pitted, its arris gone, warm only in contrast to water — you can feel the nap of algae that has claimed it, and under that, the unmistakable, stubborn squareness of something made by hands. Primitive hands. Patient hands. Hands that thought they were building for forever, and in a way, got it right.

Their forever is just cold, dark, flat, green, and drowned.
