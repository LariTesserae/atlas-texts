This is a high place that shouldn't be wet.

You are standing at about nine thousand feet, though the air does not feel thin so much as *heavy*. The heat sits on the plateau like a held breath — not desert heat, not dry, but a wet, mineral heat that makes the light swim. At this elevation the sun is mercilessly bright, a white coin in a thin blue sky, and everything is lit too clearly. No haze to soften it. The stone, the steam, the metal — all overexposed.

The plateau is a shallow bowl of pale rock, kilometers across, rimmed by low, old mountains that have been weathered down to smooth knuckles. The ground itself is not ground as you think of it. It is accretion. Centuries of water have built it upwards in terraces, lips and curtains of travertine and silica, bone-white and sulfur-yellow and, in the deeper pools, a rusty iron orange. The rock weeps. It sweats. Water is everywhere, but not in rivers — in sheets. In films that slide endlessly over the rimstone edges. In springs that open suddenly as glass-clear eyes in the white floor and spill over without banks. In condensation that beads on every surface and ticks down to rejoin the flow. You can hear it if you stand still enough — not as rushing, but as a collective trickle, a sip, a drip from a million tiny spears of cooling mineral.

Nothing green grows here. The water is too hot, too alkaline, too thick with dissolved silica to permit it. This is a mineral world. What looks like vegetation from a distance resolves into crystal gardens: dumb forests of white and ochre chimneys where steam has left its skeleton behind. Needles. Cauliflowers of bubbling carbonate. Fronds of salt that grow an inch a year and shatter under a boot. The only living carpet is simpler and stranger — mats of thermophilic bacteria and fungal slime that bloom in the warm flows. Bands of impossible color in the shallows: viridian, copper-green, bruised purple, eye-burning pink. They throb faintly. They are alive, and they stink of hot iron and ozone. They are the only "plants" this place allows, and nothing eats them casually.

Instead, the town has grown with the terraces, not on them.

It is an open city, low and spread and deeply honest about itself. No towers. No wall. It had to negotiate with water, so it is built on stilts and arches, on catwalks of black steel and poured concrete that have themselves been half-swallowed by new white stone. Pipes as thick as a man's body run everywhere, humming not with sound but with heat-shimmer. Factories — broad, flat-roofed sheds with slatted vents — sit directly over the hottest springs. They don't pump water; they farm the *precipitation*. The steam turns turbines that you never hear. The cooling flows leave behind lithium, silica lace, sulfur cakes, rare salts that harden on racks like laundry. Conveyor belts creep, crusted white, carrying slabs of mineral glass to cutting houses. Everything is electrified and wet at the same time, every wire sheathed, every bulb behind clouded glass. And everything is quiet.

That is the first strange thing. An industrial town should roar. This one just breathes. The steam muffles. The high, wet air devours sound. Engines turn with an oil-smooth, submerged hum. Water slides without splashing. Even your own footsteps are padded by the constant thin film underfoot. Men and women in pale work clothes and rubber boots move along the causeways and speak with their hands, or in low, close voices. Noise would be disrespectful. It would be useless.

The second strange thing is the abundance.

The city is sparsely populated for its size — whole terrace-fields lie empty but for their mineral flowers, whole factory floors are tended by three people and a lattice of automatic arms. There is so much space between presences: a kilometer of glittering white between one habitation cluster and the next, a vast shallow pool reflecting the sky perfectly, broken only by steam. Yet the place *teems*, just not with us.

Fauna owns the interval.

They are not animals that need leaves or grass. They drink the mineral soup and graze the bacterial mats and eat each other, and they do it in utter, disciplined silence. Over every pool hangs a cloud of glasswings — dragonfly-like things the length of your finger, with wings that do not flap so much as *glide* on the hot updrafts, utterly silent. Their abdomens are clear tubing full of glowing water. Puddle-skimmers, white as the rock, run on legs so long and thin they dent the meniscus without breaking it, dozens per square meter, cleaning the mats with surgical mouths. In the deeper, hotter basins you see backs breaking the surface — something smooth, pink-grey and limbless, rolling slowly, breathing steam. They are the size of pigs. They crowd the edges, dozens of them, lolling, completely still unless touched. Children from the city are taught not to step near them.

And above it all, in the bright, brutal light, the soarers. Pale, almost translucent things like manta rays of paper skin, riding thermals over the bowl. No feathers, no fur. You can see the sun through their wings, and the shadows they cast on the white flats below are crisp and black. They never call. They drop occasionally, with terrifying precision, to skim a glasswing cloud in one silent, open-mouthed sweep, then climb again without a beat. There are hundreds at noon, circling the industrial steam plumes as if the town were just another hot spring.

This is an active place, but not a violent one. Everything moves at its own metabolic speed. Water deposits. Bacteria spreads. Crystal grows. Glasswings hatch. The soarers circle. The factories excrete another slab of white glass. Erosion here is not destruction. The landforms are settled, established — the terraces have been building and dissolving and rebuilding in the same shapes for so long that they are in balance, growing as fast as they are worn. Even the city has learned to be in balance: when a terrace grows too fast it quietly claims a walkway and the workers simply build a new one a meter higher, leaving the old one entombed like a fossil of rebar and pipe inside white stone.

If you stand long enough at the edge of one of the larger pools, light scorching the crown of your head, heat cooking you gently from the feet up, you can watch all the rules at once. The water sheeting endlessly over a lip that never erodes away. The pink bacterial velvet creeping a millimeter a day. A line of glasswings moving as one mind. A maintenance crew in the distance, silent on their catwalk, harvesting salt. And above them, the vast silence of the plateau, held down by bright sun, holding in its steam.

It feels less like a town was built here and more like this particular kind of high, hot, wet, mineral-rich nothingness *invented* a town to tend it, and invented quiet, pale fauna to keep it company.
