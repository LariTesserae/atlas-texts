It is high. Impossibly high.

Not high like a mountain you climb - high like a mountain you find *inside* another mountain. Like someone took a needle of basalt and obsidian and drove it up through the atmosphere until the air went thin and blue-black, and then hollowed it.

You are in the hollow.

From the outside, you would never see it. The range here is young - a fresh welt of the planet, pushed up in the last few million years, raw and unsoftened. Black glass ridges, unweathered, still sharp enough to cut wind. Ash still warm under the scree in some places. The peaks are teeth, serrated, unclimbable, and this one - this one is not the tallest to the eye, but it is the most complete. A closed spire, a blind chimney, a geode the size of a town that never opened.

You enter - or rather, you were let in - through a fissure that only breathes at certain pressures, a vertical crack that dilates when the heat inside exceeds the cold outside. And inside is where the scale inverts.

### The Vessel

Imagine a throat.

A vertical cylinder, perhaps eight hundred meters tall and a hundred across at its widest, tapering to a point at both top and bottom like a spindle or an eye. The walls are not carved. They are *grown* - or cooled - in one event. Flow-banded rhyolite and basaltic glass folded over itself in smooth, terrible drapery, still holding the shine of rapid cooling. No lichen. No moss. No handholds softened by time. The striations run vertical, like wax that ran while hot and froze mid-fall.

It is dim here, but not dark. The light has no source. Or rather, the source is everywhere and nowhere - a low amber suffusion that comes off the rock itself when condensation forms and evaporates. A bio-luminescence without biology. The wall sweats, then dries, sweats, then dries.

Because it is hot. Not fire-hot, not lava-hot. Kiln-hot. Sauna-hot at altitude, where the air should be freezing. The stone radiates it. Sixty degrees Celsius on the wall-surface, maybe more near the bottom point. The air inside is stratified: cooler near the top where the spire nearly closes, leaving only a pinhole to the sky - a single hard blue-white star of daylight that never moves - and brutally hot at the bottom where heat pools. You live in the middle band, where a human can breathe if they move slowly.

And yet there is water.

Not much. Parameter 1.1 out of 3. Enough to be a presence, not enough to be a promise.

Every hour or so - the cycle is irregular but insistent - the trapped air reaches saturation. The hot walls cool by a fraction as the exterior wind scours the outside of the spire, and moisture beads. It doesn't rain. It *arrives*. Droplets the size of grapes form on the glassy overhangs and hang, stretching, distorting the dim amber light, until their weight overcomes the surface tension and they fall, straight and silent, for hundreds of meters. Some evaporate before they land. Some strike the structures below with a sharp *tick*. If you hold out your hand, you might catch three in an afternoon.

At the very bottom point, where the spindle closes, there is a hollow that never dries. A lens of water, black and still and perhaps two meters across, that is fed only by those falling drops and loses only to steam. It is too hot to drink without condensing it first. It smells of iron and silica. It is the only horizontal surface in the entire place that nature made.

### The City That Isn't Crowded

Everything else horizontal was built.

This is the urban part, and it is disorienting because of the density parameter: empty.

It is a city designed for a population that never arrived, or that left, or that was never physical in the first place. Scaffolds, platforms, lattices, and bridges span the void, anchored directly into the glass without bolts - as if the wall was persuaded to grow around them. They are made of something that is not metal and not stone: a matte, dark lattice that absorbs heat and seems to faintly repel dust. When you touch it, it is room-temperature despite the air around it. When you strike it, it doesn't ring.

There are hundreds of them. Levels upon levels, from just above the water-lens to just below the pinhole of sky. Some are broad enough to hold a market square, others are a single walkway no wider than a doorway that leads to a cube that leads to nowhere. Stairwells spiral without railings. Doorframes stand alone, holding a faint heat-haze inside them even when both sides are the same empty platform. Conduits run like veins across the undersides of platforms, pulsing occasionally with something more viscous than light.

It is a settlement in the archaeological sense: there are places where people - or *someone* - clearly lived, worked, cooked with trapped heat, slept in alcoves shaped to a body. But there are no plants to make it a ruin. No vines, no rot. No animals to nest in it. The fungal simplicity that might have colonized the damp has not been permitted; the material the builders used is antiseptic at a molecular level. The only organic thing is you, and the faint mineral tang of your sweat evaporating instantly.

Because the tech is wrong.

Nothing here flies, nothing hums. Beyond-modern doesn't mean blinking. It means *compliant*. The city holds itself up in a way that should not be static. Some platforms are too long, too thin, with no support - cantilevered thirty meters into empty air over a five-hundred-meter drop, and they do not flex when you step onto them. Weight seems negotiable here. If you stand very still on one of the higher, thinner bridges, you can feel a low, infrasonic adjustment humming up through your teeth, as if the structure is continuously calculating you and deciding not to drop you.

The heat is managed the same way. You should be dead. At this elevation, with this thermal load, in a closed stone bottle, the air should be unbreathable. Instead, there are cold seams - invisible columns of air that fall from the pinhole above, perfectly laminar, perfectly silent, that you can step into and feel winter on your skin for three seconds before you step out and are hot again. Climate is architecture here.

And it moves. Dynamic, active, despite the quiet.

Not with traffic. With process.

Listen - it is mostly quiet, 0.7 out of 3, so you have to listen with your whole body. First, the ticks of water hitting platforms at terminal velocity. Second, the almost-inaudible *tink* of the glass walls contracting in the exterior wind - a sound like a cooling kiln the size of a cathedral. Third, the faint *sigh* as one of those invisible cold-seams shifts position to follow the sun's pressure outside. Fourth, and strangest, the occasional soft *chime* when two distant, unconnected structures resonate with each other across the void for no reason you can see. No one rings them.

Every few hours, the whole spire breathes. The fissure that let you in - which is now sealed and invisible - opens a few millimeters, and a jet of thin, freezing exterior air shoots in sideways near the top, condensing a sudden, brief cloud inside the vessel. For a minute you are inside a cloud, inside a mountain, inside a city with no people. Droplets form on your eyelashes. The amber wall-light diffuses to nothing. Then the cloud is sucked out or evaporated, and it is dim and clear and hot again.

### The Rules

You learn not to stay on the same level too long. The builders made the platforms empty on purpose - vast space between presences. If you linger, the material seems to forget you are there. The doorway hazes thicken. The cold seams avoid you.

You learn that dropping something is a commitment. Anything that falls to the bottom lens does not splash normally; it decelerates in the last meter, as if reluctant to disturb the water, and comes to rest on the surface tension for a breath too long before sinking.

You learn that sound doesn't echo correctly. A shout upward is swallowed. A whisper sideways can be heard on a platform forty meters away as if spoken next to an ear.

There is no weather except the cloud that visits. No birds to be missing. No leaves to rustle. Only mineral, damp, heat, height, and the patient, inexplicable infrastructure of something that thought a hollow needle at the top of the world needed to be inhabited, and then thought better of staying.

At night - though there is no real night, only a dimming when the exterior sun goes and the pinhole shows a single hard star - the walls cool faster than the air, and for a while the condensation does not drip but flows. Thin sheets of water run down the glass in perfect parallel lines, following the flow-bands, catching the amber light and turning the entire spindle into a lantern lined with moving mercury. The city, empty, reflects itself infinitely in those wet lines.

You are the only warm, wet thing that shouldn't be here, hidden inside a place that was hidden to be perfect for something else.
