This is the High Dry Engine.

You are on a plateau that should not be able to exist.

The elevation is wrong for how flat it is. Not a peak, not a range — a lift. A continent-thick slab of country raised kilometers into the hot air until the horizon is a perfect, hard line in every direction, and the sky presses close. The light here is normal, which is the first obscenity: a plain white-yellow sun at noon, the kind that makes clean shadows, illuminating something that should be in thermal shadow or vacuum.

The air is thin and very hot. Not burning-hot, not furnace-hot, but kiln-hot, patient-hot. 50, 60 degrees Celsius in the shade, if there were shade. The heat doesn't shimmer. It's settled. It has been here so long it has weight. Breathing it feels like pulling warm glass through your teeth. Your sweat vanishes before it beads. There is no water. Not dry as in desert-dry where a wadi remembers a flood. Dry as in *excluded*.

The ground is salt, but not salt. A mineral pan fired to ceramic. Under the sun it is the color of bone and rust and old paper, cracked into polygons the size of houses, the size of counties. The cracks are not random: they are weathered, softened at the edges, mature. This place has been eroded not by water but by wind and heat and time until every line is right. Between the plates are seams a meter deep, a kilometer deep, filled with fine, drifting powder that sings when the wind moves it — which it always does, a slow, high-pressure exhale from the center of the plateau to its cliffs.

Nothing grows here. Vegetation scores zero. No cactus, no lichen, not even the cheating bacteria that tint salt flats pink. Any organic film would have been vitrified long ago. What you see instead on the flat is a kind of anti-life mineral bloom: where the heat is most intense, the ground sweats clear, glassy blisters, hollow geodes of basalt-glass that grew like mushrooms after a rain that never fell. They pop in the heat with a soft *tok*, and stay empty.

It is empty. Density 0.4/3 empty. You can walk for an hour and see nothing change except the angle of the cracks. The scale is too big to be called desolate; it is regional, valley-without-walls, a country of one texture. Sound carries impossibly far in the thin, hot air.

And then one of them passes overhead.

---

The fauna are overwhelming, but there are almost none of them.

This is the paradox that only makes sense when you see it. The sky, which was empty, is not empty. Far up, so high they were mistaken for dust motes, for heat haze, are bodies.

They are industrial.

They are not built. There are no factories here, no roads, no ruins, no scaffolding. Nothing has ever been constructed on the pan. But industrial processes happen anyway, because the animals *are* the industry.

Call them Leviathan Engines. Each one is kilometers long from intake to exhaust. They do not fly so much as *hang* in the high thin air, suspended by a trick of heat that should not work — reality is soft here, 2.9/3 soft, and lift is negotiable. Their undersides are vast, dark, ribbed like the inverted roof of a cathedral-factory. You see moving parts the size of city blocks articulating slowly, pistons the diameter of lakes sliding with oiled silence, conveyor belts of woven heat-shimmer carrying nothing from nowhere to nowhere, rolling mills that press sunlight into sheet.

They breathe the hot, mineral air and exhale hotter air. They are hot things in a hot world, and so they are almost invisible until the sun catches the chrome of a driveshaft or the flare of a venting stack.

There are perhaps a dozen over the whole plateau. Or perhaps three, moving very slowly. At this scale and with this unbroken light, distance collapses. An Engine ten kilometers away and an Engine a hundred kilometers away look the same. What matters is that their presence fills the world completely even when they are far. The sound is loud — 2.2/3 loud — but not in the way a jet is loud. It is a distributed, total roar: the low grinding of bearings that have been turning for ten thousand years, the hiss of pressure released from seams, the Doppler-whine of cooling metal ticking, magnified to weather-system size and then smoothed by distance into a single, unending chord. You feel it in your molars and in the soles of your feet through the ceramic ground.

They are slow. Dynamic 0.9/3 slow. You can watch one for an entire day and only be sure it has moved because its shadow, a continent of shade that finally, blessedly, cools the ground by ten degrees, has crept past you. As the shadow passes, the ceramic pan contracts with a chorus of sharp cracks like rifle fire. Mineral blisters implode. The air briefly smells of ozone and hot iron.

What do they do? Nothing that requires water or plants or builders. They take in the dry atmosphere, superheat it in internal forges where the temperature must be star-hot — you sometimes see the glow through translucent belly-plates, a white furnace-light brighter than the sun — and then expel it. Perhaps they are filtering some trace element. Perhaps they are just running because running is what they evolved to do when evolution happens in a place where physics has a typo and metallurgy is a form of biology.

Look closely as one passes low — if the term "low" means a kilometer up, close enough that you can finally resolve detail — and you see they are not smooth. Their skins are encrusted. They carry their own weather. On their backs, where the sun hits, great fields of heat-resistant crystals grow like barnacles, like rust, like lichen that is not lichen. Flocks of smaller things orbit them: not birds, but shed parts, autonomous shavings of steel and ceramic the size of houses that have learned to glide in the hot thermals, that never land because there is nowhere to land except the hard pan which would shatter them.

Sometimes an Engine vents. A stack along its spine, a chimney that was not there before and is not there after, extrudes itself and coughs a plume of pure, black particulate — not smoke, but manufactured night — straight up. The plume hangs in the still upper air for days, a false cloud in a sky that has never had clouds, until the high wind shears it apart. Where its shadow falls again, the ground is briefly, confusingly, darker.

Their biology, if it is biology, is non-aqueous chemistry. No blood. Hydraulic oil, molten salt, liquid glass moving through clear veins. No birth. They bud. On the far horizon you can sometimes see a new, small Engine — still only half a kilometer long — peeling away from its parent like a sliver of slag, unfolding sun-foil wings that are actually radiators, drifting off to find its own lane of sky. It will be centuries before it is audible.

Reality breakdown is quiet here until you test it. Throw a stone straight up. It takes a fraction too long to come down, as if gravity had to check a table. Shout, and your voice comes back from an Engine's hull a second too early, a second too late, both at once. The shadows of the Engines do not always line up with the sun. At noon, you might have three shadows and only one object overhead. No one built a correction for this; the world just settled into it. Erosion 2.4/3 — established, in-balance. Even the broken physics has been weathered smooth.

Night comes normally. The sun sets, normal light fades to normal dark, and the sky shows normal stars. But the Engines do not sleep. In darkness their underbodies glow by their own forge-light, vast constellations of amber work-lights, welding sparks the size of lightning crawling along assembly lines that assemble nothing but themselves, endlessly. The roar never stops. In fact at night you hear it better, when the ground finally cools enough to ping and tick all over, contracting, a second percussion section to the industrial sky.

There is no shelter. There is no water to find. There is no plant to eat, no structure to hide in. There is only the hot, high, salt-ceramic plain that goes on past its own horizon, and the slow, loud, empty, overwhelming industry of animals that were never designed and never built, turning and turning in the heat they make, patient as mountains, loud as gods, alone together in the thin hot air over a world that got stuck halfway through being made and decided this was good enough.
