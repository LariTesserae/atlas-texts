This is the Basin.

Not a valley exactly — it is too low and too flat for that word, a shallow concavity in the world about thirty kilometers across where the land has settled and decided to stay. From any roof you can see the rim, a long, low, weathered lip of limestone and brick-colored chert that holds the weather in. Everything inside has had a long time to find its equilibrium, and it has. The slopes are softened, the channels are deepened, the streets are slightly sunken where a hundred years of foot traffic and water have worn them down.

It is hot. Not desert hot, not sharp-sun hot. A low, wet, body-temperature heat that never quite breaks. The air is too thick for its own weight. Low elevation and high temperature mean the atmosphere here is a press, a warm hand. Light comes through diffused, dim even at noon, because the Basin breathes.

There are no trees. There is no grass. Nothing green grows here.

A long time ago there might have been, but the water put an end to it. It comes up everywhere — not in lakes, not in rivers, but as seep, as sweat. The water table is so high it kisses the undersides of the foundations. It weeps out of the cut rock faces at the Basin's edge, it condenses on every metal surface, it beads and runs down the inside of your collar. The factories did not bring the water, they followed it. Geothermal heat rides with it: hot springs that were once baths and are now capped and piped, artesian seeps that keep the storm drains running even in drought, warm marshes that were drained and paved over but still try to be marshes underneath the concrete.

So the ground is mineral. Where the pavement has cracked, what shows is not soil but travertine, salt-caked silt, iron-black mud that steams faintly in the morning, pale expanses of evaporite crust that crunch underfoot like thin ice. The buildings are stained for three stories up with water marks — white, rust, yellow-gray. Their lower bricks are softened, their mortar re-pointed again and again. In the outer districts, where the industrial lots were never quite completed, there are flats of hot mineral pan, miles of it between structures, shallow as a mirror, warm as blood, skimmed with a bacterial film that is not, strictly speaking, a plant, and shivers with chemical color when the wind moves.

It is a city, undeniably. Urban in the truest sense: organized, layered, infrastructural. Not a dense downtown but a sprawling, low, industrial town scaled to its basin. Row houses of softened red brick, four and five stories tall with laundry strung across the alleys, give way to mill complexes and powerhouses, to brick chimneys that no longer smoke so much as vent steam, to waterworks and bottling plants and tanneries and machine shops. Elevated pipes run like arteries for blocks, sweating, ticking with expansion and contraction, dripping constantly onto the street below with a rhythm that has become part of how time is kept. Gantries cross over canals. The canals are slow, straight, hot, lined with concrete now smoothed by flow, carrying that mineral-heavy water from one end of the Basin to the other.

It is also, impossibly, empty.

That is the first thing you feel walking it. For a city, there is too much space. The streets are too wide. The warehouses are too vast, their interiors hollow, their high windows filmed with condensation so only a dim, sour light gets in. Lots stand vacant between buildings — not overgrown, because nothing will grow, but bare, puddled, salted, sometimes with a single rusted engine block left to settle. Sound does not carry the way it should in a city. The wet air, the low cloud deck that hangs just above the rim, the sheer volume of damp brick and porous stone — they swallow it. You can be two blocks from a stamping mill and hear only a distant, felt thud, as if through water.

The emptiness is not absence. It is spacing.

Because the Basin is overwhelmingly, almost unbearably, full of animals.

Not tucked away. Not in the way a forest is full of animals. They are here the way citizens are here. They have taken the hollow spaces as their own.

There are no plants to feed them — no seeds, no fruit, no leaves. The food web here is built from the bottom up on other things: on the warm bacterial mats in the mineral pans, on the midges that rise off them in clouds so thick they look like smoke, on the refuse of industry, on fat, on starch, on blood. The city feeds them and they have agreed to stay.

Pigeons cover every ledge, every pipe, every roofline — not in flocks but in a continuous, shifting carpet of gray and iridescent green, cooing constantly at low volume, a sound like murmuring water. Their guano streaks the buildings white and feeds white worms that feed black beetles the size of thumbs. Rats move in the walls in such numbers you can hear them as a single organism at night, a tide inside the brick. Gulls — impossibly far from any sea — wheel in the dim haze, crying once, then going silent to conserve moisture. In the canals live things that should not be in a canal: pale crabs with backs crusted in mineral, long whiskered catfish fat on slaughterhouse runoff, herons standing statue-still in the hot shallows, their legs stained orange.

And the insects. Primarily, the insects. Roaches that have evolved to be matte black and dry and fast, boiling out of drain covers when the pressure shifts. Dragonflies as long as your forearm hunting in straight lines down the alleys. Clouds of moths at every dim streetlamp, not fluttering but clinging, covering the bulbs until the light is a brown glow. Mosquitoes, of course, in the marsh-breath air — but even they are hunted, by swifts, by bats at dusk that pour out of the empty upper floors in a silent ribbon.

Nothing is rare. That is the strange part. You would expect, in a place with no vegetation, to find a few specialists eking out a life. Instead you find abundance without diversity’s drama. Everything is common, and everything is many. The herons do not fight. The rats do not scatter. They have their routes, their hours. The city is active, dynamically so — predator and prey moving past each other in the same street — but it is quiet, because a system in balance does not need to be loud.

At shift change, the factory doors open and workers step out into the damp heat, bicycles hissing on wet asphalt, and the pigeons lift all at once without panic, resettle, and resume. A tannery sluices its vats and within minutes the canal runs pink, and within minutes more the catfish are at it, and the herons are at the catfish, and a man hoses down the loading bay without looking up. The air cycles: morning steam thick enough to hide the rim, afternoon condensation dripping from every eave like rain that never fell, evening cool that is not cool at all but just a slightly less hot wetness.

The rules here are simple and long-established, weathered like the brick:

1. Water wins. Build around it, pipe it, pave it, and it will still come up through your floor.
2. Do not expect shade from a tree. Expect shade from a wall, from a pipe bridge, from a cloud of wings.
3. Make yourself small and plentiful if you want to live. The Basin has no room for anything large and solitary, but infinite room for anything that can live in a crack, under a tank, in a hollow.

It is not a dead city. It is not a ruined city. It is a city that has figured out a different partnership. Mineral, steam, brick, flesh, in that order, endlessly cycling in the warm dim light, going about its business so steadily you could mistake the quiet for stillness — until you stand on a canal bridge at dusk and feel the whole hollow, wet, hot, teeming bowl of it breathe around you.
