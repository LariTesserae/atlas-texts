The Top of the World That Breathes

You are high. So high the air thins until it is a bright, clear glass you have to chew to breathe, and the sky is not blue but *white* — a hard, polished brightness that never seems to dim. There is no sun in the way you expect a sun; the light is everywhere, scattered off everything. It bounces off the frost, off the cloud decks a mile below you, off the breath of ten million throats. Even at midnight it does not go away. The plateau holds the light.

This is a continent sized like a thought experiment. A single shield-mesa the size of Australia lifted into the stratosphere, its edges sheer and young. The rock here has not had time to soften. The world made this place recently — shoved it up in a hurry — and you can tell. The basalt is still black and glassy where it broke. Ridges run for hundreds of miles without a curve, knife-straight fault scarps that have never been rounded by rivers. Cones of loose cinder, still sharp enough to cut your boots, steam faintly from within though there is no volcano. The ground rings hollow when you step because it is cooling still, contracting, ticking like a cooling stove.

It is cold the way deep space is cold: absolute, patient, dry. -30, -50, it hardly matters. The wind that moves across the vastness has had all its moisture wrung out of it on the long climb up. And yet it is not a desert. That's the trick.

The water is here, but not as oceans or lakes. It is intermittent. Stolen. Hoarded.

Everywhere the air touches stone, it condenses. The plateau is a condenser plate. Each sharp ridge, each frozen spire, weeps. In the mornings — if morning means anything under the unblinking light — the entire landscape sweats. Beads gather on leeward cliffs and freeze, then melt, then freeze again, forming sleeves of clear ice around every rock. In the crevasses and in the shadow of the young scarps, pockets of water persist like secrets: melt-pools no bigger than a room, black and impossibly clear, steaming faintly even as a skin of ice tries to form across them. Rivers do not run here. Water appears, sits for a while, and is taken.

Because everything here is packed.

Nothing is empty. From orbit you might think this white plateau was barren, but step down onto it and you realize the whiteness is not snow. It is cover.

Vegetation — if you can call it that — is everywhere, but it does not grow *on* the rock so much as it *is* the softening on the rock. A low, dense, knee-high felt of life that covers every horizontal surface not actively steaming or being walked upon. In the cold it should not be lush, but it is. Fronds like frozen seaweed, fleshy black-green and deep violet, tipped with tiny, hard beads of anti-freeze sap that glitter like glass. They are not plants; they are closer to lichen made ambitious. They grow in interlocking mats so tight you cannot see the basalt beneath, each plant braided into its neighbor for warmth. When you push through them they do not rustle — they squeak, like wet wool. Underneath, the air is suddenly still and humid, smelling of iron and compost. The mat exhales. Condensation drips constantly from its underside, feeding those secret pools.

And on top of the mat, over it, through it, *as* it — the fauna.

There are no structures here. No walls, no roads, no ruins, no tools left behind. Nothing was ever built. And yet the plateau is industrial.

The herds are the factories.

You hear them before you see them, because sound carries forever in the thin bright air. A low, continent-wide thrum, a clanking and wheezing and deep bellows-sigh that vibrates the ice in your teeth. It is loud, not sharp but pervasive, like standing inside a boiler.

The animals are vast and slow. That is their rule: nothing hurries in the cold. To hurry is to die.

The smallest are the size of dogs, six-legged grazers with translucent backs, moving through the felt-mats and leaving trenches behind them, chewing the violet fronds and venting excess heat as plumes of steam from vents along their flanks. The largest — the foundations of the ecology — are the size of houses, of ships. They drift across the plateau on dozens of pillar legs, their bodies low and wide and covered in a shag of that same felt-plant that grows symbiotically on their backs. They are not grazing so much as processing.

You watch one stop over a condensation pocket. It lowers its entire underside, and from its belly a curtain of fleshy tubes descends, drinking. You can hear the pumping from a quarter mile away: *chuff-CHUFF, chuff-CHUFF*, a slow piston rhythm. Its back splits along the spine with a hiss and a cloud of bright steam billows thirty feet into the frigid air, instantly snowing out as glittering ice crystals that fall like sparks. Inside the briefly open vent you see it: not meat, but chambers. Compartments like kilns, glowing dull red with contained heat, bellows-lungs the size of rooms, conveyors of chitin and sinew moving pulp from chamber to chamber. It is smelting the mat. It is smelting *itself*. It takes in the cold water and the poor vegetation and the thin air and runs it through a slow, relentless, biological Haber-Bosch, a gut that functions as a refinery. What comes out is heat, and steam, and a black, tar-like wax it excretes behind it that the smaller grazers swarm to eat and seal over the felt with.

There is no artifice here. No one forged these engines. Evolution did, under the constraint of cold and vastness and too much light with too little heat. On this young, sharp world, the only way to stay warm was to become a furnace. The fauna are all furnaces, of different sizes and pressures, burning the meager fuel of the plateau with supercharged efficiency. They clang. They rattle. They whistle as pressure escapes through imperfect valves evolved over a million patient winters.

And they are packed. Saturated. The term "herd" does not do it — they are a moving terrain. From a high ridge you can see nothing but backs for twenty miles in every direction, a shaggy, steaming, clanking carpet that *is* the horizon. They move inches at a time, then stand for hours, steaming. Their sound merges into a kind of weather. The small ones dart between the legs of the big ones. Parasites the size of your hand, bright white and crab-like, cling to the bellies of the giants, tapping pipes, drinking heat. Predators do not chase. They attach, slowly, over days, and melt through.

No part of this is untouched in the sense of pristine. It is thoroughly used. Every drop of intermittent water is accounted for before it even condenses. Every frond of vegetation grows in the heat-shadow of a living engine. The rock itself is being reamed out underneath — you can see where a herd has passed and left behind warm, rounded hollows in the basalt where their communal heat pooled, young caves not yet collapsed, still smoking.

And the strangeness — beyond the biological industry — is that the place remembers being made.

Because the uplift is so recent, gravity is still confused here. The plateau is still rebounding. In certain valleys between the fault scarps, loose stones drift for a heartbeat longer than they should before falling. The steam from the herds does not always rise; sometimes it hangs in perfect, flat, trembling sheets ten feet off the ground, as if the air has layers of invisible glass. Light, so bright it hurts, sometimes arrives with no shadow, diffused through those hanging sheets until you cannot tell if you are looking at a hillside a mile away or the side of a grazing colossus ten feet in front of you. Sound folds oddly around the fresh cliffs, so the chuffing you hear may have happened hours ago on the other side of the continent, finally arriving after bouncing between scarp-walls.

Everything happens slowly, but on a scale so large that even slow is deafening. A migration shifts direction over the course of a year, a great curve in the living carpet visible only from the cliffs below. A mat of vegetation dies back, acres at a time, over a decade as the herd that warmed it moves on. A new pocket-melt forms in a fresh fracture, steams for a century, then freezes solid when the fracture seals.

You stand on young, sharp rock that has never known a hammer, under a light that never dims, in air so cold your nostrils stick together, with dampness condensing on your eyelashes and freezing there. The lush, squeaking mat crunches under your feet. The air is full of snow that was breath five minutes ago. And all around you, packed shoulder to chitinous shoulder to furnace-wall, the continent breathes through its engines — chuff-CHUFF, chuff-CHUFF — patient, loud, bright, and utterly uninterested in you.
