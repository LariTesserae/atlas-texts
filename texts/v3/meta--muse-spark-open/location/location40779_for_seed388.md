This place is a single room that thinks it's a city.

You come down to it - because you always come *down* to it. The land here is low and has been low for a very long time, a broad, shallow bowl in warm country where the air doesn't so much circulate as settle. The hills around it are not high, but they are complete. They ring the valley like the rim of a basin left out after rain, worn soft and round at the shoulders, their old slopes forested to a dark line that marks the edge of the sky. Inside, everything is still and close.

The town occupies the entire bottom.

It was not built *in* the marsh. It was built *with* it, until you can't separate one from the other. The ground is not ground anymore. It's a raft.

The streets - if you can call them streets - are a dense lattice of brick and iron and poured concrete, all at slightly different heights, all corrected and re-corrected over generations. Brick vaults and factory floors, cisterns and culverts, boiler-houses and tenements stacked shoulder-to-shoulder, wall-sharing, pipe-sharing, roof-touching-roof. Nothing stands alone. Corrugated iron leans on handmade brick leans on rebar-threaded concrete that has been patched with tar and then with more brick. Balconies overhang canals that were once alleys. Alleyways run under buildings that have grown together overhead. Laundry lines, power lines, steam lines, chain drives - a cat's cradle of crossings so thick in places it makes a second, lower sky.

And through it all, water.

Not a river. Not a lake. Water as a condition. It is at the surface everywhere, just under pressure. It weeps from the walls. It sweats from the underside of every pipe. It beads on the iron and gathers in the mortar and finds the lowest course of brick and stains it black-green. The warm air is saturated and carries a fine, perpetual mist; you can see it in the amber light from the factory windows as a slow drift. Condensation never stops forming. It runs down every vertical in bright, silent threads and gathers in the gutters, in the seams between cobbles, in the deep, still canals that have replaced roads.

The canals are not clean. They are warm. They are opaque with silt and iron and algae, and they reflect almost nothing. Lily pads and water lettuce crowd them so densely you could lose a wrench in them and never hear it hit bottom. From the stagnant water, mangrove-like roots - though these are no mangroves you know - have adapted to brick. Trees grow straight out of walls.

That is the vegetation here: lush to the point of argumentative.

There are no animals to eat it. No birds, no insects humming, no rats in the walls. None. And without anything to prune it, the green has learned to be industrial itself.

Moss upholsters every north-facing surface in a felt two inches thick, so wet you could wring it. Ferns the size of your forearm sprout from cracks in smokestacks, their roots finding purchase in soot. Vines - glossy, rubber-leafed, warm to the touch - have discovered how to follow steam pipes for heat, wrapping them in living insulation that drips and steams in turn. In the old dye-works courtyard, a fig has lifted an entire loading dock three feet and held it there, its trunk grown around an I-beam, bark swallowing rust. On rooftops, small gardens have gone feral and become meadows, waist-high elephant grass shivering each time a hammer falls below.

The people here tend it the way you tend a machine you can't turn off. They cut it back from intake vents with machetes. They peel it off flywheels. They train it up trellises to shade the vats.

Because the machines never really stop.

This is an industrial town scaled to a single, enclosed hollow - a hamlet the size of a cathedral complex, but with the layering and infrastructure of a city that forgot to sprawl. Everything is here, compressed. The foundry, the press hall, the dynamo house, the wire mill, the waterworks that is now also the greenhouse, the habitation blocks above the assembly floors. Power is everywhere, low and humming. Not sleek. Industrial in the soot-and-rivet sense: big belt-driven engines turning slowly behind brick arches, boilers sighing, transformers buzzing behind chainlink that has itself gone rust-velvet and soft.

Light is scarce for all that power. The sky is usually a pale, warm haze, bright somewhere above but filtered through vapor and the low cloud that catches on the bowl's rim. Down inside, the buildings block what remains. What light there is comes from within - the amber sodium glow from high factory windows, bare bulbs strung under gantries, greenish fluorescents over workbenches, the orange pulse of a furnace seen through a half-open door. It reflects off wet brick, off canal water, off the leaves. Everything glistens dimly. You learn to see by sheen.

And it is loud.

Not sharp loud. Deep loud. A held chord made of many slow parts. The thump-thump-thump of a beam engine that has been thumping since your grandfather's grandfather oiled it, so slow you can count between thumps and so deep you feel it in the soles of your boots. The hiss and tick of pressure bleeding from a hundred rivets. The groan of chain on sprocket. The constant, overlapping drip and trickle and pour of water moving one inch at a time from wall to gutter to canal to cistern to boiler and back again. Somewhere, metal is always expanding. Somewhere, water is always boiling. The sound has no start point. It comes from the density itself, from too many things occupying too little air.

Its strangeness is not dramatic. It accumulates.

The water, for instance, does not quite behave. In the heat and saturation, it clings. Drops form on ceilings and hang far too long, growing large and heavy and glassy before they fall straight down without swaying, even when steam is pushing past them. In the quietest hours - between shifts, when the big hammers are paused - you can hear the canals breathing: a slow inhale as water seeps in through the brick from the marsh outside, a slow exhale as it is pumped up to the roof tanks. The level never changes more than a finger's width.

The plants, too, have learned the wrong lessons. There is a vine in the south quarter that only grows along live electrical conduit, its leaves neatly avoiding the insulators, its tendrils blackened at the tips where it has tested the current and learned. In the old cooling pond, lotus leaves have grown a foot across and hard as lacquer, strong enough that the children - for there are children, human life is dense here, shoulder to shoulder on walkways - lay planks across them to cross.

Nothing here is new and nothing is ruined. That is the most unsettling part. Rust is present, but it is stable, a skin. Brick is softened at the corners, but still square. The erosion that shaped this bowl happened ages ago and then stopped, as if the land reached an agreement with water and industry and green and decided to maintain this exact degree of wet, this exact degree of wear. Things weather, but they do not collapse. Paint peels to a certain point and holds. A wall that should have fallen a century ago is still standing because roots and rust and mineral crust have made a new kind of mortar.

The rule of the place is this: No one leaves the bowl quickly, and nothing enters it quickly either. Water arrives as seep and mist. People arrive and stay and build one more walkway between two windows. Machines turn at the pace of breathing. Even decay is patient.

You stand on a narrow iron bridge over a canal choked with flowering water hyacinth so dense it looks like a lawn. Below you, a flywheel twenty feet across turns once every eight seconds, its axle weeping warm oil into a catch pan where a fern is growing. Above you, three stories of tenement windows glow dim amber through the steam, each sill crowded with potted taro and chipped enamel basins catching rain that isn't rain. The air smells of wet iron, of hot oil, of bruised leaves, of brick that has never fully dried.

Somewhere to your left, a press stamps. Somewhere to your right, water finds a new crack and begins, very slowly, to make it wider.

No bird calls. No dog barks. Only the low thump, the hiss, the drip, the dense, warm, green, wet weight of a whole city that learned how to live inside a single, endless puddle - and decided to keep the engines running while it did.
