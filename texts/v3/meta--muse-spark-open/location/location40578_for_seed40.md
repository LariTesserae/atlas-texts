This place is high.

Not a mountain with a city on it. A mountain that was *finished* — capped, terraced, and built over until there is no mountain left underneath, only the idea of one. Elevation as architecture.

You stand on the Upper Tier and the air is cool and thin enough that bright light has an edge to it. It is always bright here. The sun is closer and the construction is all pale — poured concrete the color of bone, aluminum cladding, glass that never quite got dirty, endless white pipework — so the light bounces. It doesn't warm you. It exposes you. Shadows are hard and precise, falling straight down through scaffolding as if cut with a ruler. Even at dusk the place holds light, the whole mass of it glowing faintly from within, from the sodium lamps and work lights left on in empty industrial bays.

There is no ground. Not really.

If you drill down you hit more floor. The whole valley is floored over, roofed over, and floored again. What looks like terrain from a distance — ridges, bowls, slopes — resolves up close into structure: mega-terraces stepping down toward the haze, each one a district wide enough to get lost in. Retaining walls a hundred meters tall, still sharp at the lip where they were cast. Stairs that go nowhere yet. Rebar ends capped in red plastic where the next pour will be, waiting. The erosion here is young, not in the geological sense but in the constructed sense: everything is fresh. You can tell the wind hasn't had time to round a single corner. Water hasn't had time to carve. The drainage channels are still square-cut, their grates still show casting burrs. When the builders left — or where they are still building — they left clean lines.

And it is dense. Improbably so.

Scale tricks you. From the observation cantilevers, the Construct looks open — a regional thing, a man-made valley you can see across — broad air avenues between plate-blocks, factory crowns in the distance shrouded in high cloud. You think: *open.* Then you try to move through it and realize every cubic meter is accounted for.

The air is full. Catwalks over catwalks. Pipe bundles thick as tree trunks, sweating. Cable trays, monorails, ventilation chimneys, laundry lines, prayer strings, bird nets, and between them, nests. Scaffolding that became permanent. Balconies that became rooms that became alleys. Factory floors stacked vertically with only chain-link between them, so that a lathe on Level 41 rains sparks down onto an assembly line on Level 40. There are no empty lots because emptiness was never poured. People — or whatever lived here before the people — filled in the gaps as fast as they were made, then filled the gaps between the gaps. You walk sideways through intervals where hanging gardens, hanging workshops, hanging cages just brush your shoulders.

It should be loud. It isn't.

That is the first rule of this place: industry without noise. The massive things still move — slow presses stamping out identical housing panels once every hour, freight elevators the size of houses ascending on oiled rails with a sigh you feel in your teeth rather than hear, dynamos turning somewhere deep beneath your feet — but they have been muffled. Perhaps by the thin air. Perhaps by the sheer mass of concrete that swallows vibration. The dominant sounds are small and intermittent. Condensation ticking off a pipe elbow. A loose sheet of cladding tapping in wind. The soft, collective cooing of pigeons from inside a duct. Your own breathing, a little too fast for the altitude until you acclimate.

Water exists here as an afterthought. There is no river, no lake. The dampness arrives sideways.

Clouds come over the rim of the Construct and snag. At 1.4 cool, they don't break so much as condense. One moment the upper walkways are dry and glaring, the next a fine rain is falling that never hits a natural watershed — it hits deck, runs six meters in a sharp new gutter, vanishes down a scupper, reappears three levels down as a sheen on a ceiling, then drips. Pockets of water collect where intention allowed them: in cooling basins left uncovered, in cisterns that breed mosquitoes, in low-grade sags between slabs where algae has found purchase. You learn to track water by smell: rust, wet concrete, cold iron. The city sweats. In the early morning, every vertical surface is beaded. By noon it is bright and dry again. The cycle is slow and patient.

Which is true of everything here. Nothing happens fast. Rust creeps. Concrete cures. The freight platforms take twenty minutes to traverse one terrace. Even decay is gradual — paint peels in year-long curls, holding on.

Vegetation is sparse, but stubborn in the way life is in fully-made environments. There is no soil, but there is dust. Lithops of wind-blown silt collect in expansion joints and suddenly there is grass, a single determined shrub with its roots in powdered concrete, a line of moss tracing the path of a chronic drip down a wall. On the windward escarpment, the maintenance crews — or someone — stretched long felt planters, now mostly dead, but sparrows have pulled the fibers for nests. The green is so rare that each instance feels tended, even when it isn't.

The animals more than make up for it.

With so much built surface and so few predators, the Construct is infested with abundance. Not forest abundance, but urban abundance perfected. Swifts cut the bright air between towers in screaming, silent arcs. Feral cats watch from pipe chases with eyes like ball bearings. Moths the size of your hand thump softly against work-lights at night and never leave because there is no outside for them to leave to. Pigeons, of course, and rats, and things that look like pigeons and rats but have extra joints. Starlings roost in such numbers on the undersides of walkways that their weight bends the mesh. Bees have colonized an abandoned ventilation stack and turned it into a low, resonant organ when they swarm. And in the deeper service voids, where the light from above filters down as a milky white, you hear scuttling that doesn't resolve to any shape you know — fauna adapting not to wilderness, but to *building*. To ducts and voids and insulation.

And then — the wrongness.

Because this place is too total, too bright, too sharp, too quiet, it has started to come apart at the conceptual level.

Gravity is negotiable near the center of the valley. Not absent, just... undecided. A dropped bolt will fall, hesitate, then drift sideways to cling to a wall as if the wall has become the ground. In certain plazas, your shadow falls the wrong way, toward the sun. If you look too long at the repetition of identical gantry cranes stepping into the distance, perspective inverts and the farthest one feels closer than the nearest. There are maintenance corridors that loop: you walk for ten minutes, turn left four times, and arrive one level *above* where you started without using stairs. The bright light sometimes pools. It collects in corners like water, humming faintly, and will not be dispersed when you wave your hand through it.

No one seems to find this alarming. Or perhaps they do, and that is why the pace is so slow. The inhabitants move carefully, as if sudden movements might tear something.

This is a cool, bright, high place, damp where it can be, sharp where it hasn't yet been softened, built to totality, dense to saturation, industrial but patient, quiet despite its crowds, green only in the cracks but alive in every pipe and eave — a young terrain made of old intentions, already starting to forget how physics is supposed to work.

If you stay until evening, the sun cuts horizontally through the lattice and turns every edge to gold. The condensation cycle starts again. Drip, tick, hush. Somewhere, a press stamps. Once. The sound takes a long time to fade.
