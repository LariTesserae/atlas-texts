It is low here.

Not low like a valley you descend into — low like the world has barely lifted itself out of the water table. A broad, shallow bowl of land at something close to sea level, though there is no sea. The horizon is a long, even line in every direction, broken only by weather. You can see for miles under the dim light and still see nothing that rises very high. The sky sits close. It is the color of cold iron, of wet newspaper, never quite day or night, and the light that filters through is diffuse and sourceless, as if it comes from the air itself rather than a sun.

This place is young.

You can tell by the edges. Nothing here has been rounded or forgiven yet. A few thousand years ago — maybe less — this was something molten or under pressure, and then it was not. It heaved up, cracked, cooled all at once. The ground is fresh black glass and basalt, step-fractured and still sharp enough to cut. There are no soft hills, no river-worn meanders. There are plates that have hinged up at angles, pressure ridges like frozen waves, and fields of columnar stone that burst upward in tight, hexagonal packs and stopped.

And it is crowded.

This is not an empty lava plain. It is dense in a way that makes your proprioception itch. For as far as you can see, out to the town-sized extent of the bowl, the ground bristles.

Think of a mineral forest with no trunks, no leaves. Ten thousand, a hundred thousand close-set formations per acre, shoulder to shoulder: slender needles of dark basalt and pale chert, twisted spires no thicker than your wrist, bulbous botryoidal domes that cluster like grapes, thin fluted fins that stand on edge. Some are only knee-high, some twice your height, but none of them have room to fall. They lean against each other, interleave, grow through each other. Walking here is not walking across a landscape — it is sidling through it, turning sideways, finding the narrow capillary gaps where water has found a way.

Because it is wet. Not with lakes or rivers. With nearness.

The water table is right there, an inch or a foot below the glass. The rock weeps. Every spire, every fin, every dome is slick and streaming, even when it hasn't rained. Condensation forms on the cool stone and beads and runs in constant threads down to join the shallow sheet that covers the ground. You never quite see the ground itself. It is under a skin of clear, cold water only a few centimeters deep that reflects the dim sky and doubles the forest of stone. Where the plates have buckled down a little deeper, the water pools to ankle, to calf — black mirrors full of perfect stone teeth. Your boots are always wet. Every step splashes, and the sound is wrong, too sharp, too loud between the close walls.

The air is cool. Not freezing. The kind of cool that sinks and stays. Fifty degrees, maybe less. Cool enough that your breath smokes a little when you exhale, and mixes with the other mists. Because the whole bowl exhales. Cool air over cool water over cool stone makes a perpetual low fog that clings to your knees and swirls when you move. It beads on your skin, on the stone, on everything. Nothing ever dries here.

There is no green. No plant life at all.

No moss softening the edges, no grass in the cracks, no tree. It is pre-life or post-life, a mineral world. Where you expect lichen there is only a thinner mineral: crusts of mustard-yellow sulfur, felted white needles of zeolite growing like frost, rust-red iron blooms that pulse slightly after the rock gets wet. In the shallow pools, a slick that is not algae but silica — opaline, pearlescent, forming delicate terraces and shelves at the waterline as the dissolved stone precipitates out. If you hold still, you can watch it grow, a millimeter a day. That is fast for here.

This is a slow place. Its dynamic is patient to the point of stillness. The water moves, but there is no current to speak of. The fog drifts inches per minute. The stones grow by accretion, by mineral sweat. A fin that is leaning today will be leaning tomorrow, and the year after. Change happens as stalactite-time, as the slow flaking of glass under perpetual damp. But you feel that something *is* happening, accumulating, that if you came back in a century the gaps would be narrower.

And it is loud.

That is the first strange thing, before you see the strangeness of the stones. For a place with no wind to speak of, no birds, no insects, no machinery, it should be silent. Instead it roars, softly and at multiple pitches.

The density makes an instrument of it. The constant trickle of water off a hundred thousand points becomes a chorus: drips, ticks, patters, high ringing pings when a drop hits taut shallow water, deeper *ploks* when it lands in a pool. Each formation has its own resonance, and the close packing amplifies and bounces it. Then there is the other sound, underneath — a low, continuous hum that you feel in your molars. It is the stone itself, or the water inside it. The young rock is porous, full of trapped bubbles and vertical tubes, and water wicks through it under capillary pressure and sings as it goes. When you put your hand on a larger column, you can feel it vibrating very faintly, like a throat.

In places the sound focuses. The fins are set almost parallel in places, corridors that act as waveguides. The dripping becomes rhythmic, almost intentional. You round a cluster of domes and find that the air is beating — *wuh-WUH, wuh-WUH* — as condensation drips in perfect sync from a row of needles onto a shelf of silica below. No one set it to do that. It emerged.

There is almost no animal life. Rare, as the world counts it. You can walk for an hour and see nothing move but water. Then, in a pool, a flicker: something flat and translucent and the size of your palm, not fish or amphibian but a kind of free-swimming sheet, a silica animal that paddles with undulating edges and feeds on the precipitated mineral. It is gone before you can kneel properly. Higher up, in the fog, something else — a pale, spindly thing on six stilt legs that picks its way across the tops of spires, never touching the water. It freezes when it sees you, and you freeze, and you both listen to the dripping for a long time before it decides you are also a stone and continues.

You are not the first thing here that thought it was a stone, or wanted to be.

Scattered through the mineral crowd are the faint marks. Built things, but not technology. No wires, no metal, no cut lumber. Small structures, all low, all trying to be as dense and wet as everything else.

A low wall of stacked basalt shards, only three courses high, enclosing nothing, already being incorporated by silica crust. A causeway of flat stones laid across a deeper pool, slick and mostly drowned, leading from nowhere to a larger, flat-topped dome that might have been used as a table or an altar. Further on, a shelter: three leaning fins propped together to make a tent just big enough for one person to sit out the condensation. Inside, on dry-ish stone, a circle of smoother pebbles. No bones, no ash. Whoever was here left nothing that would rot.

They are not ruins. They are too slight, too recent. They look like attempts to live in a place that does not want living — trial camps, pilgrimages, markers left by people who came to listen to the hum and found they had to make *some* kind of human sign to not be swallowed by the density, and then left.

And the strangeness, which is not in any one thing but in the whole.

If you stay long enough your eyes adjust to the dim and you see that the water is not only on the rock. In the clearest pools, in the thinnest fins of chert, there are inclusions — perfect, branching inclusions of water trapped when the glass cooled, now like little illuminated trees frozen inside the stone. And those trees of water move. Not much. But if you stare, you will swear you see a trapped bubble shift from one branch to another when no one is touching it.

And if you stay longer, until the fog thickens and the light goes even dimmer, which it does without sunset, you notice that the loudness is not quite random. The hum has harmonics. The drips have phase. The whole open, crowded, cold, wet bowl breathes in and out on a period of minutes, a tide of sound that swells and fades. The water rises a fraction of a millimeter, the vapor thickens, the stones weep faster, the roar peaks, then it recedes, sighs, and the pools go glassy and almost silent for a breath before it begins again.

No instrument measures it. No animal calls to it. It was doing it before the little walls were built, and it will be doing it when the little walls are gone and the gaps between the spires have finally closed and the sheet of water has become water inside rock, waiting for the next time this young land cracks open fresh and sharp somewhere else.
