It is not a forest on a mountain. It is a mountain that is forest that is building.

At first from a distance you would think it was a range. It has that kind of scale — a high country that makes its own weather, that you can see curving away with the planet. White at the crown where the cloud cap sits, green-black down the flanks, ribbed with shadow. But as you get closer, as you climb up out of the lowland haze and into the colder thinner air, you realize the ribbing is too regular. The ridgelines are too even. The valleys are too evenly spaced, like the pleats of a cloth.

You are looking at terracing. Not the small, human kind that steps a hillside for rice. This is continental terracing. Each step is a cliff a thousand feet high and a plateau a mile deep, and there are hundreds of them, rising and rising. The wall of one terrace is the foundation of the next. The whole massif was made.

No stone was quarried for it. That is the second thing you understand when you walk it. There is no natural substrate left. There is no bedrock to hit if you dig. The footpath under your bare feet — where the moss has been worn thin by generations of other bare feet — is not soil over rock. It is braid. It is root-mat, trunk, living beam and dead beam laminated together, roots grown together and trained, over centuries, into bulk.

A primitive craft, in the truest sense. Hand tools. Stone adzes. Fire. The people — or whatever built this, whatever still tends it — did not smelt, did not forge. They wove. They bent saplings the thickness of your wrist when the world was younger and coaxed them to become arches. Then they bent saplings around those arches. Then trees around that. The structure is basketwork at the scale of geography. You can see it where a terrace wall has slumped, weathered and settled into its own long balance: the cross-section exposed like a broken beehive, a lattice of wood still living and wood long dead, layer after layer of lignified lattice, hollow in places, choked with black humus in others. Established. It has been here long enough to be geology.

And it is alive. That is not decoration. Vegetation 2.9 is not "covered in forest." The plant *is* the mass. Leaves roof every horizontal, vines droop in curtains off every vertical, aerial roots as thick as houses dangle through the empty dark between terraces and take hold a thousand feet down to make the next wall. There are trees that germinated on the upper tiers whose roots are now the pillars of three tiers below. Flowers the size of doors unfurl in the warm, wet air and close again at a breath. Fruit grows not on branches but directly out of the structural columns, as if the mountain is offering it. Fungi span the undersides of plateaus like cities of shelves, pale and luminous in the dim.

It is always dim here. Not dark — dim. The light at this elevation should be fierce, but the mountain makes its own sky. Warm air forced up its impossible stair forces out its water. The cloud deck never leaves the summit, a low, thick, amber-gray wool that filters the sun to a diffuse glow with no source. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere. Shadows are soft or absent. Colors are saturated, as if seen through water. At midday the light is the color of late evening; at evening it hardly changes. Things glow faintly that should not — pollen hanging in the still air, the undersides of leaves, the sap itself. 

Water is everywhere but rarely as open water. This is a high, warm rain country without rain. Instead, it seeps. It weeps out of every vertical surface, beads on every leaf tip, runs in slow sheets down every root-column. You hear it before you see it: a constant, quiet ticking, dripping from tier to tier, a thousand small waterfalls that never gather into a river because the sponge of the structure drinks them again before they can. Condensation forms on your skin, on your arms, even as you stand still. The air is saturated, near-surface water, stone-fruit wet. Pools collect in the axils of leaves big enough to bathe in. In the morning, which is not brighter than the evening, the whole mountain steams.

And yet it is empty. That is the strangest part to hold in your head alongside the choking green. For all that it is vast and built and totally overgrown, there is almost nothing *in* any given volume. Stand on a plateau that is a mile wide and you might see two trees that truly matter — two motherpillars that uphold the sky — and between them a meadow of ferns and hanging moss so widely spaced you can see for hundreds of yards through clear, humid air to the next great pillar. The mountain is a hollow cathedral, again and again. The density is that of a lung: immense surface area, mostly air. You can walk for a day and only pass the presence of one other permanent thing — perhaps a hut, perhaps a colony of epiphytes that has decided to be a hut — because everything is so far apart. The built vastness is mostly void. The terraces are not solid. They are scaffold. Under the green skin you walked on, there are caverns of interwoven dark as big as nations, where roots hang like stalactites and do not touch the floor ten thousand feet down. The wind moves through them and comes out changed.

That is where the fauna are. Abundant, but quiet. You expect a jungle to scream. This one does not. Sound is muffled by the moss, by the cloud, by the sheer amount of wet wood and leaf. Everything moves carefully, with reverence for the lattice.

There are pollinators like kites, translucent and silent, a yard across, that ride the warm updrafts between terraces without beating a wing. There are beetles the size of dogs that bore slowly through living beams, their backs thatched with gardens of their own. There are small, soft-footed things that live entirely on vertical surfaces, grazing on algae that grows on bark. There are gliders — lemur-like, human-faced — that leap from the edge of one tier and fall for a long, long time, arms outspread on skin flaps, before catching the next curtain of vines a half mile down. You rarely hear them call. When they do it is a single, clear note that hangs in the damp air and is gone.

And at night, when the dim does not get dimmer but the air cools by a fraction, the mountain exhales. Spores lift off in clouds and hang, bioluminescent, marking air currents you could not otherwise see. That is when you see the weirdness plainly, because the spores should drift down, but some drift sideways and then up to make a perfect corner in mid-air. Some orbit a point where nothing is. The terraces themselves — you can feel it if you sit still long enough — are not quite where they were yesterday. Not that they move. The space between them does. A path you took this morning that led straight across a plateau will now, taken in reverse, take you slightly longer, or deposit you on the tier above without any climb. The builders did not master geometry; they outgrew it. When you weave living wood at planetary scale for ten thousand years, in warm wet dim silence, the weave begins to weave the rules that contain it. Distances stretch like sap. Parallels meet in the mist. You can drop a weighted vine off a terrace edge and watch the weight fall slower the further it falls, as if gravity is losing interest. You can see, through a gap in the lattice on a clearish hour, a lower terrace that should be hidden by curvature, hanging faintly *above* you in the haze, mirrored. The mountain remembers being a seed. It still thinks it is small. So space inside it is small and folded and patient.

There are people. Or there were. Their villages are not built *on* the mountain. They are knots in it. A dozen huts of wicker and living leaf, each hut a single leaf folded and propped, with fire pits that never go out because the wood here is too wet to burn quickly and so smolders for weeks, low and fragrant. They tend. With simple knives of chipped stone and bone, they prune. They redirect a root there, splice a shoot here, keeping a wall from closing, keeping a path from healing shut. The work is endless and immediate and active — the dynamic of a reef, not a city. No one commands it. Everything grows, and so everything must be tended, always. The predators are not wolves but stranglers: a vine that learns to mimic the shape of a handrail, a flower that smells like your dead kin. The prey is not deer but balance.

The inhabitants do not think of this as built. For them there is no word for natural. There is only *grown* and *ungrown*. What they have made and what grows by itself are the same thing, only more or less attended to. They will tell you, if you can understand them over the drip and hush, that the mountain is not yet finished. That it will never be finished. That it is still reaching higher, still trying to weave itself into the clouds and become weather entire.

If you stay long enough your boots rot. Your pack sprouts. The water beads on your eyelashes and does not fall. The silence gets inside your ears until the infrequent, soft sounds — drip, sigh, the creak of living wood adjusting, the single note of a glider — feel like intrusions. You start to understand the scale not as bigness but as *room*. There is so much room between presences. So much warm, wet, dim, empty room held up by green.
