It is not on the mountain. It is *in* the mountain.

You find it by mistake, because there is no path to it. High on the wind-scoured eastern face, where the air thins to a pale blue ache at 6,000 meters and every breath should be ice, there is a fissure no wider than your shoulders. The rock outside is dead and winter-blasted, lichen-crusted, snow-shorn. You squeeze through because the wind pushes you, or because something else does — and the world inverts.

Inside is a pocket. A single room. No larger than a chapel, no smaller than a throat.

The elevation hasn't changed — you can feel it in your ears, in the low pressure behind your eyes — but the temperature has. It should be freezing. It is not. It is hot. A wet, close, bathhouse heat that hits you all at once and sits on your skin like a hand. 45 degrees, maybe more, saturated, panting. The air outside is alpine sub-zero; the air inside is lowland monsoon boiled in stone. The contradiction doesn't resolve. It just hangs there, humming.

This is a young place. The mountain made it yesterday, geologically speaking. The walls are sharp in a way that mountains are not supposed to be. There has been no time to soften them. Fresh black basalt and volcanic glass, still with the conchoidal snap of a broken bottle, still with the chemical bite of born-rock. The caldera-bell of the chamber curves overhead in a single, unbroken pour of once-molten stone that cooled too fast to learn how to be round. Edges jut. Ledges knife out at right angles. A fallen ceiling slab, still steaming where it cracked, leans against one wall with its rebar of cooling columns intact, like a honeycomb of dark hexagonal prisms. There is no soil. No sediment. Nothing has had time to become sand.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a moss, not an algal film you would recognize as plant. The mineral world is too new and too hot and too wrong. In their place is something simpler and older — or perhaps something that comes after. Where water weeps down the walls in constant, sooty sheets, the rock itself blushes. Thin, flat, mineral-lichens of rust-red and sulfur-yellow and iron-black spread in perfect concentric rings, not growing *on* the rock but *as* the rock, a discoloration that pulses faintly when you aren't looking directly at it. The pools on the floor have skins — not scum, but a taut, translucent fungal membrane that shivers and tightens like a nostril.

Because water is everywhere, despite the heat, despite the altitude, despite the lack of any river to feed it.

It doesn't fall from the sky. There is no sky here. It *exhales* from the stone.

The walls weep. Every surface sweats and runs. Condensation beads instantaneously on the glassy rock, swells, and courses down in braided rivulets that never dry. The air is so supersaturated that breathing feels like drinking warm mist. Droplets form on your eyelashes, on the hairs of your arms. In the center of the chamber, the floor dips into a knee-deep basin of clear, scalding water fed by a hundred seeps, and the surface of that basin is never still — not from wind, but from below, where heat sighs up and turns the bottom stones over and over in slow convection. On the ceiling, water gathers into pendulous, trembling lenses that hang for a long time without falling, defying gravity until they become too heavy with their own light and drop straight up for a heartbeat before correcting themselves and plunging down.

The light is normal. That is the most unsettling thing. A soft, white daylight fills the pocket with no source. No sun shaft, no glow-moss. Just light, as if the idea of illumination had been placed in the room and left there. It casts almost no shadows, or it casts shadows that fall slightly to the left of where they should.

It is quiet.

This should be pandemonium. It is *dense* with fauna. Overwhelmingly, impossibly dense for a room with no food chain you can see.

The water holds them. The walls hold them. The air holds them.

They are not birds or fish or insects as you know them. They are a single kind of thing expressing itself in thousands of crowded variations — translucent, crab-like, caddisfly-like, shrimp-like, fetus-like — the size of a thumb to the size of a cat, all soft chitin and exposed gill and mineral shell. They cling to every wet surface in layers, shoulder to shoulder, leg over leg, stacked three deep in the pools where there is no room to stack. They do not chirp. They do not buzz. They move with a terrible, muffled industry, and the sound of them is the sound of wet fingers on glass: soft clicks, ticks, the lap of water displaced by too many bodies.

What are they eating? Each other, in part. The membranes on the pools. The weeping minerals. Each other again. You watch one peel a flake of sulfur-yellow stone-growth from the wall with delicate, almost human hands — for many have hands, tiny, six-jointed, black-nailed — and place it in its mouth-purse. Another beside it is using a shard of fresh obsidian, still razor-edged from the birth of the chamber, to pry at a barnacle-like cousin. A third hones its own shard against the basalt, patiently, primitively, *tap tap tap*. No nests. No hives. No buildings. Nothing is built here that lasts longer than a minute — a temporary cairn of balanced pebbles to reach a wetter seep, a dam of bodies to divert a rivulet, immediately disassembled and reused. Tool-use without architecture. Craft without settlement. The intelligence is in the hands, not in the houses, and there is no room for houses anyway.

They do not notice you at first, because you are too large and too slow. Then they do, all at once.

And the weirdness that had been background — the heat at impossible height, the light without source, the water that falls up — sharpens into the foreground. This pocket is not fully in the mountain. The geometry is wrong. The far wall is both ten meters away and pressed against your nose if you stare at it too long. Put your hand in the central pool and your knuckles appear to bend a few degrees behind your wrist. Sound arrives late. Your own exhale condenses and then, instead of dissipating, slides sideways along an invisible incline in the air and collects in a corner where gravity pools like water. Time is dense here, like the fauna. Moments overlap. You see a shrimp-thing move, and a half-second later you see its after-image move slightly differently, as if it had considered another choice.

There is only one rule here and it is enforced by pressure and steam and crowded flesh: *stay wet*.

The weeping never stops. The heat never breaks. The creatures never sleep, only slow their clicking into something even quieter, a collective hush like a held breath, before starting again. The room breathes with you — inhale, condensation; exhale, mist — active, cycling, alive in a way that has nothing to do with plants.

You cannot stay long. The air is too hot, too thick, too thin all at once. Your skin prunes in the dryness of the wetness. The eyes of hundreds of small, tool-holding things turn, wet and reflective, in the sourceless light.

The fissure behind you is still there — a sharp, new, black slit to a world of cold wind and empty rock and normal physics. The pocket does not want you to leave, not from malice, but from density; there is simply no space to move without displacing something that was already touching something else.

You squeeze back out anyway, and the mountain closes its hot, wet mouth to a crack again, humming quietly to itself under the snow.
