It is not a cave.

A cave is a hole in the world with a floor and a ceiling and darkness at the back. This is a world *under* the world, low and open, a buried valley the size of a county that never learned how to be a cave.

You go down through the throat — not a vertical shaft, but a long, sloping sink of warm, raw stone — and the air changes halfway. It gets wet without getting rainy. It gets bright without having a sky.

### The Hollow

The elevation is negative, but not deep; you are just below the skin. A hundred meters? Two hundred? Enough that the stone above you is a roof, not heaven. It vaults so high in places you can't see the seams where it was folded shut, so low in others you have to crawl on basalt knuckles still warm from being made.

Because this place is new.

The geology here is embryonic, pre-erosion. There are no smooth riverbeds, no worn canyons, no sand. Instead everything is *fresh-broken*. The walls are great stepped slabs of cooling basalt and uplifted limestone that haven't decided what shape they want to be. You see the extrusion lines. You see the bubbles frozen mid-pop in the lava glass. Pillars of stone don't rise to meet stalactites; they are stuttering attempts at pillars, half-pulled like taffy, dripping still-slow mineral from their tips. One day they will be columns. Right now they are stalagmites that gave up halfway.

Everything is angular, under-construction, glittering with unweathered faces. The ground crunches underfoot — not dirt, but shatter. Tephra, cinder, salt-crystals, gypsum flakes that have never been rained on enough to dissolve. The wind that sighs through has nowhere to go and so it circles.

And it is warm. A dry, kiln-warmth in the stone itself, 30, maybe 35 degrees Celsius, that makes sweat bead on your neck even though the air feels damp. The warmth comes from below and from within; the floor is thin here.

### The Light and the Water

Light should not be here, but it is — normal, daylight-bright, shadow-casting light, with no sun to source it.

It comes from the fauna itself, and from what the fauna does to the stone.

Not from lamps. From *them*. The light is a dense, layered sheet of it, a low white-yellow glow that clings to surfaces like pollen. In the openest part of the valley, where the roof lifts into a true dome a kilometer across, the whole air has a noon haze to it, though you cannot look up and find a disc. Close up, you see the source: seams of lichen-like biofilm that is not plant, not quite fungus, a mineral-eating crust that metabolizes sulfur and iron and sweats photons. And above it, around it, *on* it — movement.

Water is not abundant, but it is everywhere in the way sweat is everywhere on warm skin. There are no lakes. There are intermittent things: a braided river that only exists for an hour after the ceiling sweats, then vanishes into cracks; condensation forests of cold droplets beading on every overhang and falling *plink plink plink* in irregular rain; warm seeps that bubble up black and clear for a few meters before being drunk by the air. Pockets. Puddles cupped in basalt bowls. A long, shallow wash of rust-colored water that cuts twenty meters through cinder, then abruptly ends at a sucking sand.

Wherever there is a pocket, life clots around it. Wherever there is damp, the stone darkens and glistens.

The weirdness is gentle but constant: water doesn't always flow down here. In the warm updrafts off the floor, fine mist hangs and then *rises* in slow ribbons toward the roof, as if the hollow is breathing in. Droplets roll *uphill* along certain magnetic seams of basalt before falling. You get used to seeing a trickle climb a wall.

### The Overwhelming

This place has no plants. None. No green, no leaf, no root. The mineral world never got that far, or it died and left this.

Instead, the valley is *full*.

Fauna is the wrong word because it implies animals among other things. Here fauna *is* everything. Density is so high you cannot look at a surface without it moving. It is not teeming in the jungle sense; it is layered, near-touching, saturation. A living crust.

The base is the light-crust itself — bacterial mats in white, ochre, sulfur-yellow, humming faintly. On that, a second skin: flat, hand-sized things like wet shale that creep an inch an hour, scraping minerals, leaving glowing trails. On that, the runners: pale, many-legged, blind, long as your forearm, translucent so you see the dark grit in their guts, antennae flicking constantly. They flow in rivers of their own, a tide around your boots. On that, the fliers — not birds, not insects, but papery, hot-air things that inflate themselves with metabolic heat and drift like ash, two wingspans across, silent until they bump the roof and squeak.

And larger. In the river-washes, segmented coils as thick as oil drums roll and unroll, siphoning moisture through frilled mouths. On the half-pillars, colony-things build and unbuild chimneys of spit-saliva and stone dust, a foot taller every hour, then abandoned, then eaten by the next colony. Nothing lives long. Nothing needs to. There is so much warm energy coming up from the rock and so much moisture condensing out of the air that life barely bothers with efficiency; it just *proliferates*.

It is loud. Not roaring, but loud the way a bazaar or an anthill is loud — a composite of millions of small louds. The click and chitter of legs on cinder. The wet slap of mats pulsing. The hiss of seep-water hitting hot stone and flashing to steam. The high, glassy tinkle of those drifter-things colliding. The occasional deep *CRACK* as a new slab of ceiling cools and splits somewhere out of sight, and every creature freezes for a half-second, then resumes twice as fast.

And it is turbulent. Hyperactive. Solaris-like in its creation/destruction cycle. You can sit for ten minutes and watch a territory change hands five times. A seep opens, a black pool forms, a hundred drinkers converge, a coil-thing thrashes through and scatters them, the pool is drunk dry, the coil burrows, the bacterial mat seals over the damp like a scab, already glowing brighter. A few meters away, a chimney collapses under its own weight and is swarmed and carted off grain by grain.

The rules here are not eat-or-be-eaten so much as *occupy-or-be-covered*. Life is a mineral process that got impatient.

### The Scattered Marks

People have been here. Or something that uses hands.

Not a city. Not even a village. Scattered — like a few lost campers decided to stay a season and then thought better of it.

You find them by looking for straight lines in a place that hasn't learned to make them. A low wall of stacked basalt shards, waist-high, dry-laid with no mortar, meant to divert a seep into a basin. The basin now full of wriggling flat things. A cairn of heat-blackened stones around a fire pit that hasn't been a fire in months, filled with fine white ash and gnawed casings. A shelter leaned against a half-pillar: a frame of longer stone rods — not cut, but pried and abraded — lashed together with dried gut-like cord, draped with cured sheets of the larger fauna's hide, still faintly luminous.

The tools are primitive, hand-made, manual. A hammerstone with a finger groove worn into it. A flake of volcanic glass hafted to a bone with sinew, the edge still sharp. Scrapers. Pounders. A shallow bowl pecked out of basalt to collect condensation. No metal, or almost none — a single cold-forged copper hook, green with verdigris, left hanging on a wall to hold a water skin that is gone.

No one is here right now. But the shelters are not ruined. A footprint in cinder, half-erased by runners. A half-finished wall. It feels like the makers stepped away when the seep moved, and will be back when it moves again. They live *with* the turbulence, not against it — nomads of the warm hollow, following water the way surface herders follow grass.

You understand if you stay too long you will have to do the same. Keep moving. Don't build to last, because nothing here lasts. The floor will crack under your house. The wall will be eaten. The light will eat your shadow.

You stand in the open valley, the bright warm air shimmering with drifting ash-animals, the stone steaming gently around every damp pocket, the whole dense, rustling, clicking carpet of life flowing around your ankles like a tide that has forgotten to go out, and somewhere far off another slab cracks and the hollow changes shape again while you watch.
