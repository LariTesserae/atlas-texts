High enough that the air has forgotten how to hold you.

This is not a peak. Peaks are dramatic and pointed. This is the world after the peaks — an extreme, old table of stone lifted three miles into the sky, a mesa the size of a county that was pushed up so long ago it has learned to be level again. The elevation is not a climb anymore; it's a condition. The sky here is close and violent-blue, bright to the point of injury, light at 2.8 with no filter — no trees to break it, no haze to soften it. The sun is a white coin at noon that bleaches the basalt and quartzite to glare, and even the shadows are thin and cold.

The temperature never leaves cool. 1.0/3, not freezing, not kind. It's the cool that lives in stone that has never been warm. In direct sun your skin prickles with bright heat, but the second you step out of it — behind a wind-sculpted fin of rock — the air steals it back. Your breath always shows, just faintly. Sweat never comes. Condensation comes instead.

Water here doesn't flow. Not really. There are no rivers worth naming. This is **damp**, not wet — intermittent moisture in a place that should be desert-dry at this altitude. At dawn, and again at dusk, the whole plateau sweats. The cold stone exhales and the warmer air above it fails. Dew condenses in beads on every grain of rock, gathers in the thousands of shallow bowls and potholes that erosion has polished into the mesa top — established, old, settled erosion, not raw canyons. Think of a giant's soap dish left out for a million years: smooth, lipped, balanced. Each bowl holds a lens of water for a few hours. Some are the size of your palm, some the size of a house. They are connected only when it rains — which it does, suddenly, coldly, once every few days, a thin vertical rain that vanishes into the rock before it can run — and for an afternoon there are shining, braided rivulets threading between basins, riverine for an hour, then gone.

And because of that damp, there is life where there shouldn't be. Vegetation is sparse, 0.9/3, and it knows better than to be ambitious. No forests. No grasses waving. Instead, a crust — black-green lichen, rust-red lichen, grey lichen that looks like dried paint — covering the leeward sides of rocks in leathery sheets. In the permanent seep-cracks, where the basins leak slowly down into the stone, there are cushions of alpine moss, hard as upholstery, and tiny, waxy rosettes with leaves like polished jade, each one hugging the rock for warmth. One or two dwarf conifers per acre, if that, stunted and flagged by wind into bonsai twists, no taller than your knee. You can walk for ten minutes and see nothing but stone and light, then find a single perfect flower — purple, five-petaled — blooming out of a condensation pool, already dying by noon.

The scale is open but not oceanic. 1.8/3. You can see the edge of the world on all sides: the plateau falls away in weathered, staircase cliffs, tawny and striped, and on the far side, twenty or thirty miles off, it rises again into the white shoulders of still-higher ground. It's town-or-valley-size emptiness. You could walk across it in two long days if the air let you. Inside that openness, everything is vast and spaced apart — **density 0.5/3, near-void**. The rocks themselves are far apart. There is so much sky between things that distance becomes a material. You can see a single standing stone for miles.

Which is what makes the fauna obscene.

It should be empty. Instead it is **abundant**. 2.2/3. And it took you a while to understand why you hadn't seen them.

The stone moves.

Not all of it. The basins, the slicks — when you crouch, you see they are crowded with tiny, pale shrimp-like things, water-lice that hatch in the hours of rain and fold into cysts when the pools dry. The lichen crust shivers with mites. And above — God, above — the air is full.

They are not birds. Or not any bird you know. The permanent, deafening sound is them.

**Sound 2.9/3. Deafening, and never changing.**

The wind here never stops, and never varies. Dynamic 0.3/3. This place is in equilibrium, frozen in its own weather. The same cool, high-pressure cool coming off the icefields to the north pours over the plateau's lip every second of every day at twenty knots, steady as a river. It has done so for centuries. It has carved the rocks into flutes and organ pipes, Aeolian teeth, and it sings through them at a constant, complex chord — a low organ roar mixed with a glassy whistle so high it lives in your teeth.

Inside that roar lives the second deafening: the flock.

They are small, kite-shaped, translucent-gray things, with wings like stretched paper and long tail filaments. The locals — because there are locals, of a sort — call them shawl-wings, or wind-feeders. There are millions of them, an abundant biomass that replaces what vegetation can't do. They don't eat plants. They eat the wind and the condensation itself, straining airborne plankton, spores, ice crystals, mites with combed mouthparts. They ride the never-changing updraft at the plateau's rim. Every cubic meter of empty air holds three or four, flickering. From a distance the sky looks dusty. Up close you realize the dust is alive, and roaring.

The roar is not the wind. It's their wings. Each one makes a tiny *thrum*, and a million thrums make a cicada-scream so loud you have to open your mouth to equalize pressure. It never gets louder. It never gets softer. It is the baseline of the world. You stop hearing it until someone stops it — and nothing stops it. At night, under the brutal, bright stars, they glow faintly pink, like embers, and the sound continues.

The unusual — weirdness 0.8/3 — is quiet here, because the obvious is already so strange, but you notice it eventually in the **rules**.

Nothing erodes further. Nothing changes. The pools fill and dry and fill again to the same line. The lichen grows to precisely the edge of the damp and stops. Even the bodies of the dead — the shawl-wings that die and spangle the rocks in drifts — do not rot so much as sublime, flaking into dust that the wind eats. The cairns built by the people here are not structures — **built 0.3/3, none**. There are no houses, no walls to keep wind out because you cannot keep this wind out. Primitive tech, 0.8/3. The people are thin, high-chested, dark from light and wind, wearing cured shawl-wing leather and carrying flaked stone and bone. They do not build. They move. They crouch in the lee of the wind-flutes and drink from the basins with cupped hands. They carry long, hooked poles to skim the sky. When they catch the fliers, they do it with nets of sinew that sing. They leave no mark. If they placed a stone on another stone, the wind would have it down by morning — not out of violence, but out of balance. This place does not permit accumulation.

If you stay long enough, you learn to read the brightness and the chill as one thing: a bright, cool, damp desert in the sky, old and settled and perfectly maintained, empty of everything except the single, deafening, abundant note that it has chosen to hold forever.
