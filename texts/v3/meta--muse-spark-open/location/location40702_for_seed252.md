It is a pocket.

Not a cave system, not a cavern you hike to — a single held breath below the surface, embedded inside basalt like a bubble that didn't rise.

You find it only because the ground above gives way a little. A cooling crack in a young lava plain, still sharp at the shoulders where it tore open last year or ten years ago. The edges have not softened. Glassy black rock, conchoidal fractures shining under the sun, so fresh you can see the air-quench ripples. You have to turn sideways to slip down. Two meters down the heat hits you.

This is below surface. Not deep, but deep enough for the world to change rules. The outside sound — wind over the plain — cuts off cleanly at the lip, replaced by a contained hush.

The chamber is not large. Maybe twelve meters longest axis, maybe four at its tallest, an ovoid blister where gas once tried to escape the flow and failed. Its walls are all fresh, young. Nothing has had time to erode. Every surface is near-primary: sharp basalt teeth, ledges that snapped rather than wore, pillars of cooled melt that dripped and froze mid-fall into black stalactites that never learned to be smooth. You could cut your hand on the floor if you aren't careful, where the last skin of lava wrinkled into ropy pahoehoe and then cracked apart as it contracted.

It is bright in here. Unreasonably bright for a sealed pocket.

There is no opening to the sky except the slit you came through, but the light does not come from there. The light is in the rock itself.

When the basalt cooled, it did not cool empty. The melt here was rich — silica, trace metals, water under pressure — and as it hardened it laced itself with a lattice. Veins of opaline glass run through every wall, floor, and ceiling in a three-dimensional filigree, hair-thin at the edges and thick as your wrist where they knot. They are not built. No one laid them. They precipitated the way frost precipitates, chemistry finding its lowest energy. And they carry light.

Sunlight falls on the plain above, enters the black rock, and is caught. The lattice waveguides it down, concentrates it, re-emits it. Not as reflected glare but as a diffuse, even daylight that seems to come from everywhere at once, as if the air itself were luminous. The color is white with a slight amber shift, the color of noon seen through closed eyelids. It is bright enough to make you squint, bright enough to throw hard, short shadows beneath the sharp ledges, bright enough to see motes of steam sharply.

Because it is hot. 2.8 on any human scale — the air is 46, 48 degrees Celsius and saturated near the floor where water lives. The rock radiates it. Geothermal heat bleeds up from still-warm depths; this whole plain is thin-crusted, newly made, and the chamber sits like a kettle just above the source. Sweat does not evaporate here. It beads and runs.

And it is wet. Not flooded — wet. Water at 1.6, which here means present in every form except standing deep.

It weeps. Under pressure below, superheated water forces its way up through microfractures and meets the cooler air of the chamber and condenses. The walls are always slick. Beads form on the black glass, swell, and run in slow, determinate tracks down sharp planes, joining into trickles that collect in shallow pans on the floor. The floor itself is pitted with these pans — smooth, centimeter-deep basins etched by the same water when the rock was still soft enough to dissolve — each one holding clear, scalding water that mirrors the glowing veins above.

Between the pans, steam. The hottest trickles hit the hottest rock and flash. The air stratifies: a lower layer, knee-high, of warm mist that pools and drifts lazily; an upper layer that is clear and shimmering. The ceiling drips constantly, not from a leak but from condensation — droplets forming on the glassy daggers overhead and falling straight down with a soft, irregular tick, tick, tick onto stone or into pools. The whole chamber breathes this cycle. Evaporate. Rise. Condense. Fall. Over and over, active and unhurried.

There is no vegetation. Not a blade, not moss, not lichen in the way you expect. The temperature and the youth of the rock and the light that has no ultraviolet balance have kept plants out. This is a mineral world. What lives directly on the stone is simpler: thin, translucent biofilms, bacterial mats in the margins of the hottest pools where sulfur yellows the water, and sheets of pale silica-precipitating archaea that look like frost but are alive, laying down a millimeter of new opal a year over the black glass. They do not look like life until you get close and see the faint iridescence shift when you breathe on them.

It is sparse. Density is low. A few pans of water, a few mats, a few knots of lattice. Much of the floor is empty, dry black glass between the wet places, open to walk on. The emptiness makes each presence loud, visually.

There is nothing built. No tool mark, no cairn, no wire. No one has lived here; the crack only recently opened enough for anything larger than vapor to enter. untouched, undeveloped.

And yet —

The lattice is technology beyond modern, but it does not read as technology because it is not made. It behaves like engineering current physics strains to describe.

Watch the light for long enough and you see it is not passive. The veins do not just guide sun down. They redistribute heat. Where a pool is about to boil dry, the amber glow above it dims a fraction as energy is shunted elsewhere; where condensation is heaviest, the ceiling above brightens, accelerating re-evaporation. The lattice is a heat computer, a photonic thermostat grown by thermodynamics, keeping this pocket from cooking itself or drowning itself. If you hold your hand near a thick knot of vein, you can feel the faintest tug — not magnetic, but *sensible* — as infrared is pulled sideways through the glass to balance the chamber.

Touch the water in one pan and the level in another pan, meters away, shivers. Not through a pipe. Through phase-state coupling in the biofilm, or through resonance in the lattice, or both. The system computes with light and heat and chemistry what we would need pumps and sensors and logic gates to do.

It is not weird. That is the strangest part. In this place, it feels normal — as normal as a tidepool regulating its salinity, as normal as quartz growing in a hexagon because that is what quartz does. The physics is just what rock does here when conditions are exactly this young, this hot, this wet, this bright.

Sound is mostly quiet. 0.6. The dominant note is the dripping — irregular but ceaseless, each drop a clean click on glass. Under that, a low fizz where water seeps into hot crevices and sighs into steam; and occasionally a sharp *tik!* as cooling basalt somewhere in the wall contracts a fraction of a millimeter and crazes anew. Your own breathing sounds loud. Your clothes rasp.

Fauna is rare. Almost nothing lives here larger than a microbe. But if you crouch still for ten minutes in the mist layer, you will see them: pale, eyeless isopods the size of rice grains, grazing the bacterial frost. Translucent, so you see the dark line of sulfur in their gut. They move slowly, deliberately, pausing at the edge of a scalding pan to test temperature with antennae before detouring around. There might be four in the whole chamber. There might be six. Once, a small, dark wasp-like thing — that came down through the crack by accident, wings already wilting in the humidity — stumbles across the glass, drinks, and leaves. Nothing hunts here. Nothing needs to. Energy is not in eating each other; it is in the light-heat-water loop.

The rules of this pocket are simple and complete:

1. Heat comes up constantly from below.
2. Light comes down constantly from above, via glass.
3. Water cycles forever in between, never leaving, only changing form.
4. Life thins itself to a film that helps the cycle and does not interrupt it.

You do not stay long. The heat presses after twenty minutes, the brightness fatigues. You climb back up through the sharp slit and the plain is suddenly cold and dim by comparison, wind over rough black rock under an ordinary sky. Behind you the slit glows faintly, like a lamp left on underground. If the crack seals in the next eruption, the pocket will continue without witness — bright, hot, wet, quietly computing its equilibrium in a room no larger than a house, for as long as the plain stays young.
