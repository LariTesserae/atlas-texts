It is not an ocean. It is not land that has flooded. It is the place where the distinction failed to form correctly.

### The Basin

You are low. Very low. Not deep in the old, smoothed, abyssal sense, but low the way a fresh wound is low - a sunken caldera, a continental palm held open just below sea level, kilometers across, rimmed on all sides by sharp, young cliffs that have not yet learned to be hills. The world here is newly made and it shows. Basalt still glassy in places. Pillars of it stand like broken teeth, their edges unround, their faces still showing the slick, conchoidal fracture of a thing that cooled fast under pressure. The ground under your feet - when you can find ground - is not sand, not silt, but a jumbled scree of that same black rock, still warm to the touch, still faintly venting. Heat shivers off it in pale curtains even though you are submerged.

Because of course you are submerged.

The water is everywhere, but it does not behave like water. It is warm - bathwater warm, 30C, 34C, the temperature of blood and summer rain - and so saturated with light and life and steam that it has become thick. You breathe it or it breathes you, hard to tell. In some places it is clear and you can see twenty meters through the green gloom to the next cliff of pipes. In other places it is a solid. A wall. A vertical river hanging in mid-air, falling sideways, falling up, falling in a slow circle that never lands. Sheets of rain that never fell from a sky, suspended between rock faces, hammering. The surface, if there ever was one, is gone. There is only inside.

The light is dim, not because the sun is far, but because the medium is too full. Light from above - a bruised, cloud-covered amber that never resolves into a sun - filters down and then gets caught. It scatters off particulates, off bubbles by the trillion, off leaves, off rust. By five meters down it is twilight. By ten it is a deep forest dusk except you are not in a forest, you are under ten meters of warm, luminous broth. Things glow faintly on their own to compensate: veins of mineral light in the rock, bioluminescence in the plants that has nothing to do with animals, the orange flare of blast furnaces that never went out.

### The Jungle of Industry

It is lush. Impossibly lush, for having no mouths to eat it.

From every joint and seam and crack in the basalt, vegetation has erupted, not as colonization but as an explosion. This is not seaweed. This is jungle that has learned to live submerged without ever evolving for it. Broadleaf canopies, sixty feet across, waxy and black-green, spread underwater like umbrellas, their trunks as thick as houses, their roots splitting concrete and stone. Mangrove-like stilt roots cage everything - the cliffs, the abandoned gantries, the factory walls - in dense, breathing latticework. Vines as thick as your torso hang motionless in the water column, then suddenly lash with new growth: you can watch a vine tip extend six inches in a minute, unfurling leaves that were not there when you blinked. Ferns the size of billboards. Flowering things that bloom and rot and bloom again in the space of an hour, their petals drifting down like snow and dissolving before they hit bottom. All of it is silent in the way plants are silent, which makes the rest so much louder.

Because the machines are still running.

The built environment here is scattered, which makes it worse. There is not a city down here. There is not even a ruin. There are nodes - industrial cathedrals left behind or thrust up by the same violent birth that made the basin. One to the north, a refinery whose distillation towers still climb, barnacle-free, wrapped in roots that squeeze but do not crush. One to the east, a power plant, its turbines screaming. One half-buried, only its gantry crane visible above a hill of newborn rock. A forge. A waterworks. A pumping station that pumps water into water for no reason. They are not connected by roads. They are connected by consequences.

Each one is alive with industrial tech at peak output. Not rusted. Not drowned. Electrified, mechanized, scaled for a world that no longer exists. Conveyor belts churn, carrying basalt rubble and tangled kelp-jungle detritus into crushers that spit out rebar. Arc furnaces flare underwater and should short out, but don't; they burn white-blue, boiling the water around them into permanent, roaring bubbles that rise only to flatten against an invisible ceiling and race sideways. Pipes big enough to walk through - and many are split open so you could - shudder with pressure, carrying warm, mineral-thick water from one place to another at speeds that make the whole basin hum.

And there are no animals. None.

This is the wrongness that hits you after the noise and the green. In water this warm, this rich, there should be fish, shrimp, worms, whales. There are not. There are not even insects. No birds above. No microbes you can feel, though clearly something is photosynthesizing. The plants grow untended, uneaten, falling into rot that no scavenger cleans. The machines run untended, unmaintained except by the plants themselves, which have grown *through* the pistons and gears and somehow make them run better. The water itself is sterile and fecund at once. You are the only animal here, and you are intrusive by definition. If you cut yourself on the young glass rock, the blood will plume and hang, and nothing will come to taste it.

### The Breakdown

This is a young place. The rock is young, the water is young, the jungle is young. And like all young things made too fast, the physics has not set properly.

Reality is turbulent here. Not metaphorically.

Sound is the first law that has broken. It should be muffled, underwater. Instead it is deafening. The basin amplifies. The roar of water hitting water at impossible angles, the screech of metal on metal transmitted through liquid directly into your bones, the deep organ thrum of the turbines, the hiss of a thousand pressure valves releasing at once, the groan of rock as a new hydrothermal vent punches through overnight and reroutes a river that was flowing vertically yesterday. You hear it in your teeth. You hear it after you cover your ears - because the water is your ear now. It never stops. It never establishes a background. It spikes and cuts and layers, bazaar-loud, storm-loud, the sound of a factory the size of a valley trying to build itself while drowning.

And then sight goes.

The water does not obey gravity consistently. In the central open, over what must have been a town square or a dry lakebed, there is now a sphere of water about two hundred meters across that simply *is*, holding itself together without surface tension, rotating slowly, with a factory inside it that is on its side. The vegetation inside that sphere grows downward, toward its own center. If you swim near it, your sense of down stutters. Bubbles fall. Light pools in corners like liquid and pours away when you try to look at it.

Things are made and unmade too quickly to track. At dawn - such as dawn is, a slight warming of the amber - a new ridge of basalt will knife up from the basin floor, sharp enough to cut, still steaming, shedding flakes of obsidian that become islands for a day before the vines take them. By noon that ridge is already half-dissolved by acidic vent water and completely overgrown, flowers opening on it that have no right to exist at that depth. By evening a pipe has burst and scoured it clean again with a jet of superheated water that cuts like a waterjet cutter, exposing fresh rock for tomorrow.

You try to map it and the map rots in your hands. Not from water damage. From new growth. From the paper - if you brought paper - sprouting a thin film of green. From the landmarks refusing to stay where they were.

The rule of this place, if it has one, is: *density without consumption.* Everything is crowded. The water is crowded with minerals and heat. The rock is crowded with vents and edges. The green is crowded with itself, leaf on leaf, root strangling root in a silent, crowded competition for dim light. The machines are crowded with purpose, producing and processing and burning with no product and no consumer. And none of it empties. The jungle is not eaten. The factories are not used. The heat is not dissipated. It just churns, turbulent, hyperactive, fecund and sterile, loud and empty.

You move through it slowly because the water resists you, because the vines catch you, because every loud, bright, warm, sharp, growing thing demands attention and then immediately changes. You breathe shallowly - somehow - and watch a conveyor belt overhead carry a load of dripping, flowering branches into a furnace that makes the water scream. The smoke that comes out is not smoke but a cloud of seeds, glowing faintly, that drift down and root in mid-water and begin to grow before they land.

There is no shore to return to. The cliffs are undercut and dripping with new rivers that run in all directions. Behind you, where you came from, the water has already closed and grown over.
