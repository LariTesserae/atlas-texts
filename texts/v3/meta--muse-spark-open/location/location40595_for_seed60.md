It is not a forest that grew over a factory. It is a factory that learned how to be a forest, or a forest that learned how to run like a factory.

You come to it because the ground gives way.

The land here is low, not quite flat — a shallow bowl between older hills that have had a long time to settle into themselves. Nothing sharp remains in the shape of the land. The slopes are rounded, weathered down to a comfortable, maternal curve, and at the bottom of the bowl the earth is saturated and soft. This is not a lake, not a swamp. There is no open water to see. Instead the water is everywhere just under or just on the surface: sheeting down the weeping sandstone at the bowl's lip, beading on every leaf, condensing on every pipe, rising as warm breath from the soil itself. The air is warm — late-summer warm, greenhouse warm — and it holds so much moisture that light has weight. You breathe it in wet. Your shirt sticks to you within minutes. When you press a hand to the mossy ground, water wells up around your fingers without you having to dig.

And then there is the green.

It is not that plants grow here. It is that *place* here is made of plants.

From the rim you look down and you cannot see soil, cannot see stone, cannot see building except where the green has decided to allow it. The entire bowl — maybe three hundred meters across, hamlet-sized, a single great room — is filled. Not crowded. Filled. The canopy is low and continuous, ten or fifteen meters up, but it is not a canopy in the normal sense; it is a ceiling. Broad leaves the size of doors overlap shingle-tight. Vines as thick as a man's torso braid and re-braid themselves into columns, into arches, into buttresses. Epiphytes grow on epiphytes. Moss grows on the moss. A fig has rooted in the air itself, its roots hanging like curtains and never bothering to find the ground. The light comes through normal, bright, midday light, but filtered a thousand times until it falls in warm, mottled shafts that move only when the whole mass breathes.

Because it does breathe.

This is the strange thing. This is why people say the valley is wrong.

The industrial bones are still here, scattered, half-glimpsed. You have to learn to see them. A brick smokestack, its mortar long ago replaced by root-hold, now serves as a vertical garden — each brick cradling a fern, each fern dripping condensed steam. A gantry crane, rust-pitted and still, has become a trellis so complete that its steel is just the taste of iron inside the vine. Down at ground level — the true ground, somewhere under a meter of living mat — there are concrete vats, settling pools, boiler housings. You find them by the sound.

It is loud here. For a place with almost no animals, it is deafening.

There are no birds worth speaking of. No deer. Rarely even an insect swarm; a few pale beetles work silently in the deep mulch, and sometimes a frog calls once and then thinks better of it. The sound is all plant and machine, inseparable now.

Water ticks, drips, trickles, gurgles everywhere — off leaf-tips, along grooves in bark, through rusted gutters that still gutter. The leaves themselves rustle without wind, shifting against each other as they grow. And underneath that, with a patience that makes the sound feel less heard than felt in your teeth, the works turn.

Slowly. So slowly you could watch for an hour and swear nothing moved.

The great press — a flywheel five meters across, once powered by coal and later by oil, its purpose now forgotten — still turns. It is driven not by fire but by growth. A banyan has shouldered its way through the engine house, its roots coiled around the drive shaft, swelling year by year. Each infinitesimal expansion of living wood puts a new fraction of pressure on the gear. *Click.* Another tooth. *Click.* Another. The movement travels through a system of belts that are now belts of woven vine, pulleys that are now living wood, looms that weave nothing but more vine. The factory is producing itself.

Elsewhere a pump house sighs. A vast, root-choked bladder fills with rain and condensation over weeks, then, when it is full enough to be heavy, it sags and squeezes and forces water through glass pipes furred with algae. The water falls, turns a little turbine made of seed-pods and reclaimed tin, and the turbine tugs a wire, and the wire opens a vent, and the vent exhales that warm wet breath you felt at the rim. Then the cycle begins again. A month per breath.

No one tends it. No one needs to. The rules of this place are simple and complete:

1. Everything that can be grown, will be grown, and everything that is grown will be used.
2. Metal will rust until it can be eaten. Brick will soften until it can be rooted. Space will be filled until there is no space that is not alive.
3. Water will not leave quickly. It will hang in the air, sit in the leaf, sit in the stone, sit in the lung.

If you stay long enough — and the warmth and the damp and the slow hiss and creak make you want to stay, make you drowsy and rooted — you start to see how the scattered human artifacts were never the point. A tipped-over lunch pail sprouting mushrooms. A collapsed corrugated roof now just a ridge of ochre lichen. A single porcelain insulator still on its pole, crowded on all sides by orchids that use it as a bright white sun. They are not ruins. They are seeds of a different kingdom, and the green has germinated them.

There is a path, of sorts. Not a trail cut by feet — no feet come here often — but a negotiation. Where the interwoven trunks press closest, they leave a tunnel just wide enough for a person, and the floor of that tunnel is springy with living moss that weeps clear water when you step. The tunnel moves. Not while you watch. But if you mark a wall with chalk, come back next season and the wall will be a meter to the left, the ceiling a hand lower. The room is re-arranging itself around its slow, loud, patient breathing.

At the very center, where the bowl is lowest and wettest, the green opens its idea of a cathedral.

The columns become true columns — fluted, enormous, made of twenty different trees grown together until you cannot tell where one ends. The ceiling lifts just enough to let normal light pour down in a single, unbroken column. And in that light, water falls continuously — not raining, but sweating from the canopy, gathering, falling — onto a dark pool that is not quite water and not quite leaf. The surface is covered in a flawless mat of tiny floating ferns, so dense it looks solid, trembling with the impact of every drop.

Suspended over that pool, held aloft by vines as thick as cables, is the heart: the old factory bell, or boiler, or still — no one remembers which. It is green-black with verdigris and moss, and it is completely swallowed except for its underside, which weeps. From a dozen root-tips, water condenses, beads, and falls. *Plok. Plok. Plok.* Loud in the hush between the sighs of the pumps. Each drop hits the fern-mat and vanishes without a ripple.

You understand, standing there, that the place isn't strange because it is broken.

It is strange because it is perfect. It has achieved balance. The erosion that made the bowl, the water that fills it, the plants that fill it more, the machines that have learned to be plants — they have been doing this long enough to have worked out all their disagreements. Nothing is fighting. The industry is not polluting the marsh; the marsh is operating the industry, at marsh speed.

It will be here long after the hills around it have settled another meter lower. It does not need fauna. It does not need you. It allows you to be inside it for a little while, damp and warm and listening to it loudly, slowly make more of itself, before the tunnel behind you quietly, kindly, grows shut.
