This is not a valley. It is a room that decided it was a mountain.

You reach it by climbing — not up a slope, but through it. An iron stalk, a service tower the width of a silo, rising straight out of the cloud deck. Everything below is white and moving. The stalk itself is the terrain here; there is no other ground. Its sides are fluted with maintenance ladders and riveted plates that have never had time to weather. The edges are still sharp enough to catch your glove. A young place. Freshly made, or endlessly remade.

At the top, the stalk flowers into the Cistern.

From the outside it looks like a water cube held in the air by scaffolding that is too thin for it. About a cathedral's worth of volume — you can see all of it at once, and that smallness is disorienting after the climb. A perfect box of steel and glass and concrete, maybe forty meters to a side, perched so high that the sun hits it before it hits anything else. No bedrock, no soil, no hillside. Just platform, strut, wall, and the box. Everything is built. Even what looks like ground inside it is not.

From the inside, it is submerged.

Not flooded. Submerged, as if the box was sunk in warm ocean and then lifted, water and all, into the sky without spilling. The water does not pour or crash. It simply *is* there, occupying every inch of the interior volume from floor-tile to ceiling-tile, pressing gently against the glass. When you open the hatch and slip inside, you don't splash. You are taken. Warm — bath-warm, greenhouse-warm, summer-lake-warm — clear to the far wall, where sunlight cuts in as thick columns. Normal daylight, gold at noon, though it comes filtered through four meters of water and moving leaves.

And there is no room in it. That's the first law of this place: density.

The Cistern was industrial once. You can still read it under everything else: the floor is a factory floor, poured concrete with iron tie-downs; the walls are tiered catwalks and gantry rails; the ceiling is a low forest of pipes, conduits, sprinkler heads that will never sprinkle again, overhead cranes frozen on their tracks. Electrified, mechanized, built for scaled production — the whole box hummed once with pumps and sorters and vat-engines, the kind that make more of themselves.

Now everything is growing.

Lush beyond any plan. The built surfaces have become substrate. Mangrove-like prop roots burst from seams between wall panels and hang suspended in open water. Broad-leafed philodendrons the size of doors bolt straight out of rusting electrical cabinets. Coils of kelp-thick vine, dark green and waxy, use the old conveyor chains as trellises and then let go of them, continuing to grow out into the water column with no support at all. Water-ferns feather every pipe. Moss, or something that learned moss's job, carpets the undersides of catwalks in a velvet inches thick. There is no soil anywhere, no natural substrate left to find — only concrete, steel, glass, ceramic tile — and the plants do not seem to mind. They root in rust. They root in paint. They root in water itself, their white root-hairs unfurling like nerves into the open.

It is packed. Saturated. You cannot extend an arm without brushing five different textures of leaf. Sightlines close after three or four meters in a lattice of stems and fronds. Even the water feels occupied, perfumed thick with oxygen and green exudate, faintly viscous where light hits the particulates that drift with impossible slowness.

That is the second law: slowness.

Nothing here moves fast. The great circulation pumps in the floor still turn, you can see their shadows far below through the understory — three huge industrial screws, turning perhaps once a minute, pushing the entire warm ocean gently over. A leaf that comes loose takes an hour to fall upward or down — many hang neutrally buoyant in place for days. Bubbles form constantly on the underside of every leaf, perfect silver beads of oxygen, and they do not rise quickly. They detach reluctantly and wander, wobbling a few centimeters per hour, as if the water has decided to consider each bubble before letting it go. The plants themselves grow while you watch, but only if you are patient; a tendril will lengthen a finger's width over an afternoon, groping blindly around a beam with plant deliberation. The whole Cistern breathes on a diurnal sigh, water slightly clearer at dusk, slightly hazier at dawn, and otherwise holds.

It should be loud. An industrial engine-hall full of water ought to roar. Instead the sound has been taken out of it. The pumps make almost no noise through the water-mass and the leaf-pack — only a low, felt thrum in your chest if you press a hand to a column. Sometimes a distant tick of contracting metal, echoing through the box. Otherwise, the quiet is total and botanical: the faint fizz of photosynthesis if you hold still enough, the occasional soft *thup* as two leaves, swollen with growth, press against each other and slip. Your own breathing sounds wrong in it, too loud.

Which makes the absence louder.

There are no animals. No fish threading the vines, though the water is perfect for them. No snails on the glass, no water beetles, no minnows, no worms. No birds in the canopy of leaves near the ceiling, where light is brightest. Not even a gnat in the trapped air pockets under inverted machinery. The pollinators never arrived or were never invited. The jungle keeps itself going imperfectly, by other means — fragmentation, self-cloning, leaves that root where they fall, flowers that never open and simply thicken into heavy, green fruit that splits open into more leaves. It is a lushness that should be teeming, and it is empty of everything but plant. You feel watched, not by eyes but by the sheer attention of so much leaf tissue with nothing to eat it.

And the water itself is wrong in a way that grows on you slowly.

It has no surface. Inside the box there is no top to the sea; the water meets the ceiling tiles and stops, held without membrane. If you push through the highest leaves and touch the ceiling, your fingers go from warm water straight to cool concrete with no interval of air, no meniscus. In the middle of the room, where the sun is strongest, there are places where water thins into something you could almost call rain that never falls — vertical columns of slightly less-water, like warm glass ghosts, hanging motionless among the vines. The plants grow straight through them. Gravity seems optional. Orientation drifts; after half an hour inside you are no longer sure which way is down except for the faint, patient direction of settling debris. Some vines grow down from the ceiling and some grow up from the floor and both seem correct.

The light moves across all of this in a normal, clean arc, turning the whole small packed ocean into a lantern by late afternoon. Dust motes that are actually excised root hairs. Air that is actually water. A high mountain that is actually a small, recently built factory room full of silent, warm ocean and industrial jungle, hanging above the clouds and waiting very slowly to grow itself completely shut.
