This is the Low Works.

Not a city. Not ruins. That would imply there was once a country around it to build a city in. There is no around here. From horizon to horizon, there is only the floor.

You are standing on it.

### The Floor

At an elevation of almost nowhere — a few meters above an ancient sea that no longer exists — the world was paved. Totally. Some civilization, or succession of them, covered the curvature of this place in a single continuous edifice. Not buildings on ground. No ground is left. The substrate is made: layer upon layer of poured stone, iron slag-brick, basalt-concrete, steel plate and ceramic tile, fused and re-poured over deep time until geology itself was replaced with stratigraphy of industry. If you could cut down, you would cut through kilometers of dead factories before you hit anything that was ever a stone.

And then erosion got it.

This is not sharp, new industry. This is 2.7/3 ancient — the end of entropy. The Floor has been weathered until it has almost forgotten it was ever built. The great manufactories, the refineries, the vertical assembly stacks that must have scraped the stratosphere once — all have been worn down to low, rolling swell. Broad domes the size of hills, but if you look close you see they are rust-flaked foundry roofs sagging under their own weight. Vast shallow basins a kilometer across that were once cooling towers, now ground down to open amphitheaters of pitted concrete. The streets, the railbeds, the canals, the support struts — all planed flat by wind and chemical rain until only their ghosts remain as discolorations in the metal, as long straight lines of slightly different rust.

The horizon is not flat. It undulates, gently, endlessly. Low. Everything is low now. The only verticals left are where a harder alloy resisted the grinding — a gantry leg, a chimney core, a rebar skeleton left like a row of black teeth, 30 meters tall where everything else is ten.

You are not in a valley. You are on an open continental plain of worn machine. You can see for 15, 20 kilometers in clear air and there is nothing to block your view except the curvature of the weary metal haze. The scale is regional, town-or-valley size, but there is no town. Just more Floor.

### Water and Cool

It's cool here. Always. About 8 or 9 degrees Celsius, the temperature of a cellar in early spring. The air is wet without being underwater — 1.7/3 wet, marsh-edge wet, weeping-rock wet.

There is no rain, exactly. There is condensation. Because the entire Floor is still warm from below — some deep geothermal-industrial heart still turning, still exhaling — and the air above is cool, water sweats out of the atmosphere constantly. It beads on every surface. It runs in sheets down the low domes. It weeps from the undersides of overhangs where a million pipes still sweat. It gathers in the ancient, flattened channels that were once streets and now are slow, shallow rivers of black water a few centimeters deep and a hundred meters wide, moving sluggishly toward no ocean.

The water is near the surface everywhere. Step wrong and your boot breaks through a crust of iron-oxide and sinks to the ankle in brackish water that has been cycling through the rust for ten thousand years. Low pools fill the eroded factory floors — still, dark, perfectly flat mirrors that reflect nothing because there is no light. In the deeper depressions, post-rain landscapes that never dry: fields of standing water 20cm deep, full of rust-flakes and concrete silt, rippling under the constant vibration.

Everything drips. Everything. Listen —

Actually you can't, yet.

### Darkness and Sound

Light here is 0.5/3. Not pitch black, but deep dusk permanent. There is no sun visible. Maybe it burned out, maybe the cloud of industrial vapor is now a planet-enveloping stratum. The illumination is industrial twilight: the dull red glow of pilot lights that never went out, rivers of molten slag still visible through cracks a kilometer away, the occasional sputtering arc of a shorted transformer on a distant gantry-tooth, and most of all, bioluminescent rust — colonies of mineral-eating chemistry that give off a faint amber-green foxfire along waterlines.

Your eyes adjust until the dark feels like a thick, cool broth.

And the sound —

The Sound is the first thing. Before you see the Floor properly, you hear it.

2.6/3 deafening. Not loud in a sharp way. Profound, bodily, ancient-engine deafening. The Floor is still running. With no one to tend it, with no purpose, with its top planed off, the *underside* still works.

Somewhere below you, vast presses stamp. Pumps larger than houses throb every seven seconds and send a shudder through the sole of the Floor. Steam is vented through fissures with a scream that dopplers for miles. A conveyor belt kilometers long, flattened and exposed by erosion, still drags its rusted plates in a circle with a shriek of metal on metal that has been going on so long the groove it wears is a meter deep. Water hisses when it finds a hot fissure. Wind is pushed through factory lungs and comes out as a low organ note that never stops.

You feel it in your molars. You have to shout to hear yourself think, and then you stop shouting because the Floor does not answer.

### Ecology of Sparseness

Vegetation: 1.3/3 sparse. Fauna: 0.4/3 — effectively none. Density: 1.0/3 sparse — much space between presences.

Nothing lives here that moves under its own will. No birds, no rats, no insects. The sound alone would kill them. The water is too heavy with dissolved iron and oil.

What grows does so in the cracks that hold water longest. Not forests. Not fields. Scattered, lonely presences that make the emptiness feel larger.

Black lichen that eats concrete, forming coin-sized rosettes that glow faintly when wet. White thread-fungus that hangs like old insulation from the undersides of pipes, swaying in the hot exhalations. In the most sheltered shallow pools, mats of rust-red algae, so thin they are more stain than plant, spreading for meters just under the water surface. And occasionally, inexplicably, a stunted tree — a single birch or ironwood, pale and starved, growing straight out of a rust-pit where a little less-toxic silt collected, its leaves small and translucent, shaking constantly with the vibration. You can walk for an hour and see three of them. The fourth will be dead, a white spar.

They do not make this a garden. They emphasize that this is a machine plain that has been empty for epochs.

### The Strange Rule

Here is the weirdness — 1.8/3 strange, not surreal, but wrong in a settled way:

Gravity remembers the structures that are no longer there.

Water flows uphill. Gently, persistently. In the broad, shallow street-rivers, you watch the water ripple *toward* the rise of a dome, climbing a grade of two degrees to spill over a lip and vanish into a drain that should be higher than its source. Condensation beads run sideways along girders. If you pour water from a canteen, it hesitates at the lip, then curls upward along the underside of your hand for a few centimeters before gravity catches it again.

It’s as if the ancient mass of the towers, though eroded to nothing, still left a dent in the world’s field. The weight of what *was* there still pulls. The active dynamics of the place — the endless cycling of water, steam, vibration, slow algal bloom — have learned to work with this. The pumps below don’t need to push water up the domes. The ghost of the factory pulls it.

No one remembers this is strange. The lone birch trees grow slightly bent *toward* the empty air where a wall once stood, their roots reaching for phantom mass.

If you stay still too long, you feel a faint tide in your blood, a leaning.

### What It Is To Be There

You are wet. Your boots are wet, your shoulders are beaded with cold sweat from the air. The Floor hums up through your legs, a deep active throb. The dark is cool on your face. The air smells of wet iron, cold steam, and ozone.

You are utterly exposed — open scale, no shelter, no walls left high enough to hide behind — and utterly alone. The total-built world has been grinding itself down to a smooth stone for so long that it has become a kind of nature again. A primordial, final nature. Not wilderness. Work-erness.

And it is still working, deafening in the darkness, polishing itself flatter, pumping its weeping water in impossible loops, tending its sparse gardens of rust and lichen, waiting for no one.

You shout to test the sound, and the Floor takes your voice, folds it into its throb, and goes on stamping, dripping, exhaling, forever.
