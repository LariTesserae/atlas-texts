It is too large to be a city and too flat to be a world.

You are standing - or you have been placed - on a low plain that goes on until the haze makes its own weather out of it. The elevation never changes. There are no hills, no valleys, no horizon line that stays still. The ground is not ground. There is no bedrock under it, no soil on top of it. Everything here is made.

Everywhere, in every direction, the floor is laid.

Not poured, not grown, not extruded by a machine. Laid. By hand. Block after block of dark, still-warm material - something like unglazed brick, something like basalt clay that was cut when it was soft and never fully cooled. Each slab is about as wide as a sleeping person, pitted with thumbprints and rope-drag and the shallow score of a primitive adze. Between them, mortar that never set, a black tar that stays tacky. If you kneel, which you should not do for long because it is hot, you can see the maker-marks: palm lines baked into the edge where a block was shoved into place.

No tools are visible. No makers are visible. But the work continues.

This is an embryonic landscape. Raw geology, but none of it is geology. Erosion has not happened yet because there has not been time for erosion to learn how to happen. Corners are too sharp. Surfaces are too fresh. The whole vast lowland looks like it was laid down last week, or is still being laid down now, too fast to weather. You can find places where the floor ends - not at a wall or a drop, but in a raw feathered edge where the newest blocks thin out into wet, undone dark, like a knitting project abandoned mid-row. Next to it, a kilometer away if you walk, or right next to it if you turn your head wrong, there is another feathered edge going the other way. The plain is under construction from a thousand directions at once.

It is hot. Not sun-hot. There is no sun - the light is dim, sourceless, the color of an overcast dusk that never moves. It is kiln-hot. Floor-hot. The air is thick enough to wring. Water is everywhere and nowhere as water. It does not rain. It condenses. It sweats out of the bricks themselves. It beads on the underside of the air and falls up sometimes. Low channels run between the blocks, half-inch deep, half-mile wide, carrying warm water that has no source and no mouth. The weeping rocks - though they are not rocks - are always damp. You hear dripping before you see it. You hear a faint hiss where water touches a block that remembers being hotter. Puddles form in the shallow chisel-marks, perfectly still, reflecting a dim light that has no origin, and then without rippling they are on the ceiling, if there is a ceiling. The atmosphere is post-rain forever without ever having rained.

Because the scale is wrong, the weather is wrong. Clouds form three feet off the floor. They are small, personal clouds, the size of rooms, born of the heat and the wet. They drift at knee height and precipitate onto themselves. Far off, which might be ten miles or ten inches depending on how the space is folded at the moment, lightning happens silently inside a cloud the size of a house, illuminating no landscape because there is no landscape to illuminate, just more floor.

And the floor is not stable.

This is a turbulent place, though it is mostly quiet. That is the contradiction that makes your ears ring. You expect a construction site of this size to be deafening. It is not. Sound is sparse here - much space between presences, as if the world budgeted silence. What you hear is: a distant knock, as of a wooden mallet on stone, but elongated, taking three seconds too long to arrive. A creak from somewhere deep under the blocks, not as if weight settled but as if the idea of weight settled. Water slipping off an edge that just came into being. Your own breathing too loud.

But while it is quiet, it is never still. The dynamic is hyperactive, chaotic, a bazaar-speed creation and un-creation without any merchants. Blocks lay themselves when you are not looking. An entire district of floor, a continent of interlocking dark tile, will ripple like cloth and re-settle a foot to the left. A trench you walked around will not be there when you look back, and a new trench, full of steaming clear water, will have opened where your foot was about to fall. The built world is primitive - hand-tools, simple-coal, knapped edge and hammerstone logic - but it is building itself at a planetary rate, and it has no plan.

Here is where the weirdness lives, because this is a place where reality has thinned from the heat and damp.

Distance does not hold. You see a solitary structure far off - for the plain is not entirely empty, it is sparse: a low kiln-dome here, a leaning scaffold of lashed black timber there, a wall three blocks high that encloses nothing - and you walk towards it. After an hour you are not closer. You turn around and it is behind you. Or you are inside it without having entered. The dim light does not cast consistent shadows; an object's shadow may point toward your feet even when the object is behind you. If you set two identical bricks side by side and look away, one may be larger when you look back.

The rules, such as they are:

1. Everything is floor. If you dig - people do, with sharp stones - you find more floor: another course of blocks beneath, damp and warmer.

2. No one lives here. Not no one anymore; never. There was never a biosphere to collapse. No plants. No lichen, no moss, no scrub. No animal. The only things that almost live are the mineral films that bloom orange-black in the standing water for an hour before the water moves and they are gone. The fungus-like slick that briefly holds the shape of a handprint before dissolving. Pre-life, or post-life's echo, trying and failing to get purchase on a world that is already totally occupied by made things.

3. The makers are not here, but making continues. You will sometimes find a fresh handprint in mortar that was not there a second ago, still steaming. You will never see the hand made it.

4. The place is low. Everything is low. Even the scaffolds are low, as if building upward is forbidden or forgotten. The world has chosen horizontality. The sky, if it exists, is a low ceiling of moist air and self-made cloud, close enough that you could touch it if space would let you. It presses down with heat.

Stand still long enough and the wet heat will coat you. Beads run down your arms, down the blocks. A shallow sheet of water will creep across your boots, warm as blood, reflecting the dim above. Somewhere, a great distance that could be inches away, another block will thump into place with a soft, definitive sound, like a book closing. The air will shudder. The puddle at your feet will become the puddle on an invisible plane twenty feet up, without moving. The floor will have grown by one more row toward an elsewhere that never arrives.

It is vast, it is handmade, it is unfinished and unfinishable. A planet-sized mosaic laid by ghosts with stone hammers in a hot, wet dusk where physics has come loose at the seams.
