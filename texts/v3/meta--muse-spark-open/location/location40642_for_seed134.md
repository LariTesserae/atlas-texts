This is not an ocean as you know it, and not a mountain either, but it insists on being both at once.

You are high up and deep down. That is the first rule here.

Far below the open air, there is a high country that never broke the surface. A plateau of young basalt rose too fast and was drowned immediately — a post-flood highland, still submerged under hundreds of meters of clear, cold seawater. From a distance it would look like ordinary dark ocean. Closer, the light still reaches here — normal, white-gold daylight, filtered and scattered into columns — because the elevation is so high. The sun is not distant here; it is only water away. You can see it rippling on the surface far above you like a second, liquid sky. You are on a mountaintop in the deep sea.

It is small. That is its strange intimacy. This whole world is not a range, not a sea. It is a single room, a cathedral basin, maybe a hundred meters across, hamlet-sized. A caldera bowl or a collapsed vent amphitheater with sharp, young walls. Erosion has not had time to soften anything. Every edge is fresh and glassy. The basalt cooled yesterday in geological time — black, conchoidal, still holding the stress fractures of its birth. Pillars of columnar jointing stand like organ pipes. Sheets of volcanic glass flake off in translucent black knives and lie where they fell. There is no sand here yet, no rounding, no beach. Only angles, ledges, terraces, and a lip where the high plateau suddenly drops off into true abyssal blue. The water beyond that lip is a wall.

Because everything is submerged. Not wet — *submerged*. The air itself has been replaced. Water is not a feature of the landscape; it *is* the landscape. It hangs as atmosphere, heavy, cool — about 8 degrees Celsius, the kind of cold that is precise and mineral, not biting. It moves with slow, patient gradients. Currents turn over the bowl once every few hours, a slow breath. Particles fall like snow but never land. The light comes down in visible shafts full of motes, and sound travels too well, too loudly.

And nothing was ever built here.

There are no walls, no ruins, no artifacts, no straight lines made by hands. No wreck, no city. The stone is untouched in the sense that no tool has cut it. And yet —

It is industrial.

The young earth here is a factory without builders.

Along every sharp terrace, the rock itself is an engine. Hydrothermal seams exhale through natural flues and chimneys — not man-made stacks, but mineral chimneys six meters tall, thin as bone, layered with iron and anhydrite. They roar. Cold seawater is sucked down into cracks, superheated below, and then blasted back up as a continuous, deafening geyser of black, particle-laden water. It is loud. God, it is loud. Not the crash of waves but the constant hammering roar of a foundry floor: hissing, clanking, churning, the subsonic thud of water-hammers as pressure vessels of stone fill and empty. The cliffs tick and ring like cooling metal. Bubbles the size of houses detach and wobble upward, catching the light, imploding with a dull *whump* you feel in your chest.

The tech is not *in* the landscape. It *is* the landscape's behavior, and the animals that have learned to be its machines.

There are no plants. Not a single frond of algae, no kelp, no grass, no lichen. The water is too mineral, too newly made, too full of heavy metals for photosynthesis in the conventional sense. The world is pre-green, or post-green. Black rock, white water, and light.

But it is not empty. It is dense — obscenely dense — and full of fauna.

Every surface that is not actively venting is covered, layered, near-touching, saturated with life that has taken on industrial functions. This is not a reef; it is a living mill.

The dominant presences are colonial filterers the size of barrels and bathtubs, their bodies like soot-black boilers riveted with barnacle-like intake valves. They are called nothing, because no one has been here to name them, but you would call them Forgers. They suck in the mineral smoke by the ton, pump it through chambers in their guts where sulfur is precipitated, and exhale clarified water in hot jets. Their outer shells are iron sulfide, actually smelted by bacteria in their tissues — they grow their own housing as cast iron. Generations have welded themselves together along the basalt ledges into continuous banks, one animal bolted to the next, forming terraces of living furnaces. When they pulse, in slow unison, the whole basin thrums.

Between them crawl the Shuttles — pale, blind, crab-like things the size of dogs, with forelimbs that have become piston hammers. They do not eat in the normal way. They tend. They scrape fresh mineral crust from the chimney mouths before it can seal, hammering with a rhythmic, deafening *clack-clack-clack* that carries for kilometers through water. Others carry nodules of pure sulfur in their claws like ingots, stacking them in cairns that will become the foundation for new Forgers to settle on.

Above them, in the water column itself, clouds of smaller fauna — translucent, lantern-bearing shrimp and veil-things with cilia like turbine blades — spin constantly, filtering, churning, keeping the water itself dense with bodies. There are so many of them that swimming would be pushing through soup. The light catches their bodies and makes the water glitter with industrial soot.

The ecology has no producers as you understand them. There is no base of plants. The energy comes up from below, from the earth's heat, and is fixed by chemosynthetic veils inside every animal. They eat smoke and heat. They breathe metal. They excrete stone. The whole basin is slowly growing itself closed, building more sharp terraces out of its own waste.

The weirdness is quiet but absolute. Gravity here is indecisive. Because of the density of life and the supersaturation of minerals, the water sometimes stratifies into visible layers — cold, clear water on top, then a sharp halocline where black, hot, iron-rich water hangs underneath without mixing, like oil under water. Animals swim *on* that boundary as if it were a floor. Bubbles do not always rise; some sink. Sound arrives before light at certain depths. Time moves slowly — the dynamic is glacial. A Forger will take a century to complete one pump cycle, a slow inhalation of decades, though its valves chatter loudly every second. The young rock creaks as it settles, a sound like a cathedral cooling after a fire.

If you stayed here — suspended in cool, bright, thunderous water above that sharp black bowl, with the living factories roaring around you and no built shelter anywhere — you would realize the rules:

1. No one made this, but everything is being manufactured.
2. High ground does not mean dry ground.
3. Life does not need green to be abundant. It only needs a gradient to exploit.

The light shafts move a few degrees as the sun travels across the water-sky. The hammer-crabs keep hammering. A new chimney somewhere on the outer lip bursts, and the whole dense, crowded amphitheater fills for a moment with black snow that will, very slowly, become more wall.
