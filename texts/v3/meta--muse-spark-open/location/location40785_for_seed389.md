It is not on a mountain. It *is* a mountain, and the city is how the mountain is finishing itself.

You are very high. The air is wrong for this kind of heat.

### The Height and the Heat

At 2.9, elevation stops being scenery and becomes the condition of everything. The horizon is not distant, it is *down* - a long, hazy fall away on all sides to lowlands you can see as a different color of air, a pale, dusty blue-brown thirty kilometers out. The light is normal, a white-yellow sun almost directly overhead, but it has to pass through less to get to you. Shadows are razor-edged. Colors don't fade.

And it is hot. Not desert hot. Kiln hot. A low, pervasive, mineral heat that comes from below you more than above. The rock itself is warm to the touch, even in shade. If you place your palm flat on the basalt, you can feel it faintly thrumming. The high air should be freezing and thin and it *is* thin - your lungs work a little harder, sounds carry poorly, everything is slightly short of breath - but the stone exhales heat. This whole mass is new. Geologically embryonic. The mountain range is still in the act of being extruded.

This is what low erosion means here: nothing is softened. There are no rolling hills, no sanded valleys, no river deltas. The entire landscape is raw construction. Great stepped cliffs of columnar basalt and half-cooled slag, still showing the flow lines where they were poured. Planes of volcanic glass, black and imperfect, catching the sun and throwing it back in hard glints. Fields of clinker and scoria that have never been weathered, so every edge is still sharp enough to cut. Talus slopes that haven't learned how to be slopes yet - they are just piles where the mountain shrugged.

The ground crunches underfoot with fresh cinder.

### The City That Grew Like Crystal

And it is all built upon. Or built *into*. At this scale - open, a valley-and-a-half, a caldera big enough to hold a county - the distinction fails.

The city did not conquer the mountain. It colonized the cooling process.

Where the basalt was still malleable in the deep past, someone drove in foundations. Now the urban fabric is dense - 2.4 dense, shoulder-to-shoulder, layered like strata. Towers of riveted iron and black brick rise out of the living rock, but their lower floors are not built at all, they are *carved* - hollowed directly from the basalt columns. Streets run in terraces following old lava benches. Bridges of steel lattice leap across fresh fissures that are still, very slowly, widening. Avenues terminate abruptly where a new upwelling of stone has heaved up through an intersection overnight, and the municipal crews are already there with jackhammers and rebar, not to remove it but to incorporate it - to drill windows into it, to run pipe through it.

It is an industrial city, unmistakably. Everywhere: the silhouettes of headframes and cooling vanes, the vast, rust-streaked flanks of manufactories, the skeletal elevators that crawl up cliff-faces carrying ore hoppers. Chimneys stand like a forest, hundreds of them, brick and iron. Electrified cable runs in fat bundles along every wall, humming faintly. Gantry cranes perch on cliff rims like patient insects.

But nothing is frantic. That is the first strange thing.

For an industrial dense urban mass, it should roar. It doesn't. The sound is 0.9 - mostly quiet.

The machines here work on a different time. Dynamic 0.6: slow, patient, geological. The great piston-engines in the factory halls move with a full-minute stroke - *hush... chuff... hush...* - compressing air that will not be used for hours. The rolling mills turn once an hour, shaping a slab of basalt-glass that has been heating for a week. The power station turbines spin in superheated steam drawn from deep boreholes, but they are muffled by stone, by distance, by the thin air that swallows high frequencies. What you hear most is not industry but its byproducts: the tick of cooling metal, the ping of contracting cable, the low creak of the mountain settling under the weight of the city.

People - there are many, density demands it - speak quietly too. Sound doesn't carry up here. You lean in to be heard.

### Water Without Green

There are no plants. 0.1 vegetation is not scarcity, it is prohibition. The heat, the altitude, the raw chemistry of the new rock, the constant mineral dust - nothing photosynthetic has taken hold. There is no moss on the basalt, no lichen, no weed in a crack. The city is absolute mineral: iron, basalt, glass, brass, brick, salt.

And yet it is not dry. Water 1.2, damp in a dry world.

It cannot rain often - the air is too hot and too high to hold much - but when it does, it is violent and brief, a sudden hammering at midday that turns the cinder streets into instant rivers of hot steam. For twenty minutes the whole caldera sings with hissing, and then it is gone.

The real water is more strange, more intermittent.

At dawn, before the sun clears the eastern caldera wall, the temperature differential wrings condensation directly out of the thin air. Fog pours over the rim like a slow waterfall and pools in the lower terraces, beading on every iron surface, dripping down every cable. For an hour the city glitters. Gutters that have been dry for days run with cold, clear water that tastes of iron and sulfur. Basins and cisterns, carved into the rock on every rooftop, fill without rain.

Lower still, in the deepest part of the open bowl where the city is densest, there is the River. It is not a true river - there is no watershed to feed it. It is a wound. A fissure has opened in the caldera floor that weeps continuously - geothermally heated, mineral-heavy water that emerges at near-boiling, steaming furiously in the cooler morning air. It runs for three kilometers through the lowest district, channeled in stone and iron troughs, before it vanishes again into a sinkhole on the far side, back into the mountain. Its banks are crusted with precipitated salts, white and yellow and rust-red, building into terraces that grow an inch a year.

This is where life clings, without being plant life.

The water's edge is the only place anything moves on its own.

### Ecology and Weirdness

Fauna is rare - 1.4 - you can go a day without seeing anything not human or machine. And weirdness is 2.4, so when you do see it, you are not sure what you saw.

There are no birds at this height; the air is too thin for them to work. Instead, there are the Condensers: translucent, manta-like things the size of a sheet of paper that ride the morning fog currents over the caldera rim. They are not animals as you understand it. They seem to be membranes stretched over a frame of precipitated silica, pulsing faintly with electrostatic charge, drinking moisture directly from the air. They flock silently by the hundreds and vanish completely by 9 a.m., folding themselves into crevices, or perhaps sublimating.

In the hot river, there are the filament-worms - long, red-black threads that live in the boiling outflow, braiding and unbraiding in the current, feeding on dissolved iron. They leave faint, rusty trails on the stones. The workers give them a wide berth; the water that sustains them will scald a hand in seconds.

And there is the city itself, which may be fauna of a sort. At night, when normal light fades and the furnaces and sodium lamps are the only illumination, you can see it: the entire dense lattice of cable and pipe faintly glowing with residual heat, the slow crawl of ore-elevators like blood cells, the way steam from a hundred vents rises and hangs together in the still air above the caldera like a single, slow breath. The mountain is under construction, the city is under construction, and you cannot tell where one ends.

The rule of this place is simple, though no one wrote it down:

**Everything here is still cooling.** The rock, the buildings, the machines, the water, even the air. You have arrived during the long, slow solidification of a world. Nothing erodes because nothing has finished forming enough to be worn away. Nothing grows because the world is not yet ready for soft things. Your job, if you live here, is to tend the cooling - to bolt a plate over a new crack, to drain a cistern before it boils over, to listen to the great, quiet engines take their minute-long breaths, and wait.

It is hot, it is high, it is quiet, and it is crowded with patient, iron things.
