It is not under the sea. It is *in* the sea, and *under* the rock under the sea.

You find it by accident, because there is no reason to look for it.

### The Pocket

Far down where the continental shelf gives up and drops, the basalt is not solid. There is a blister in it — a single perfect void, a geode the size of a small town's downtown. Not a cave system, not a trench. One room. One hidden room.

From the outside, nothing. Just warm, dark slope, silted basalt pillows encrusted in white tube-worm lace. But there is a mouth, a collapsed lava-tube throat no wider than a subway tunnel, and it inhales. Cold deep water is pulled in with you whether you want it or not. After forty meters of scraping dark, the throat opens —

— and you fall into open water that never stops falling.

The chamber is full. Completely full. There is no air in here, never has been. Warm seawater fills it rim to rim, ceiling to floor, a held-in-place ocean the size of a cathedral district. The water itself is the atmosphere. It is 29 degrees Celsius, bath-warm, geothermally heated from below until it shimmers with heat-haze despite the pressure. If you open your mouth it tastes of iron and salt and hot stone.

The rock walls are old. That is the first thing you understand. This place has been settled in its final shape for a very long time.

The basalt is not jagged. It is smoothed, scalloped, polished by a million years of slow warm current into long flowing ribs and bowls, like wax melted and refrozen. Everything is rounded off. The floor is not floor but a slope of basalt terraces descending to a central rift — a glowing crack where the earth leaks heat. The ceiling is a single low dome, three hundred meters across at most, weeping mineral. From it hang curtains of dead chimney spires, now cold and thick with manganese crust, dripping hot-particle snow forever downward. Weathered. In-balance. A place that stopped eroding because it finished becoming what it was going to be.

And built into that — *on* that, *through* that — is the town.

### The City That Should Not Fit

It is urban. Unmistakably urban. Not a ruin, not an outpost. A working industrial town condensed and then unfolded to fit inside a pocket.

But the density is wrong. That's what makes your brain hurt. A town this small should be cramped. This one is hollow.

They built it not as blocks but as scaffolds — skeletal towers of riveted iron and pressure-steel, cross-braced and stilted, standing free in open water with nothing between them but blue-black volume. No streets. No ground. Each building is a separate island: a boiler-house here, a turbine hall there, a dormitory stack, a refinery drum, a lattice of pipes and gantries tying them all together like a half-finished erector set left in a fish tank.

The space *between* is the point. Fifty meters of empty warm water between one lit tower and the next. A hundred meters of void straight up to the next catwalk. The whole town is perhaps two dozen giant structures in a volume that could hold a hundred. Vast watery avenues of nothing, lit only by the faint spill from the buildings themselves. Empty, hollow, near-void — an anti-city, where the infrastructure is there but the crowd never arrived. Or left.

And it is all industrial. There is no prettiness here.

Every tower breathes and sweats.

The dominant structures are the pumps — four of them, one at each cardinal point of the rift, each as big as an apartment block laid on its side, bolted straight into the rock. Their intakes gape like cathedral doors, swallowing the warm bottom water and superheating it and firing it back out the top in screaming white plumes. That is the heat-engine of the place: it runs on the temperature differential between the rift and the cold ocean leaking in through the throat. It has run for decades. It will run for decades more.

Between the pumps run the factories. Not for making things to sell — there is nowhere to sell — but for making *more town*. Rolling mills that extrude fresh hull-plate underwater in glowing orange sheets that hiss and blacken instantly. Forges that never quench. Assembly frames where new scaffold sections are welded by squat, crab-like automata that crawl along the girders, showering sparks that die too quickly in the water, turning to brief clouds of steam that rip upward.

It is all electrified. That is the weird light. Not sunlight — no sunlight has ever reached here. Every building carries its own array of old sodium-arc lamps behind thick glass, and the water smears their light into long amber cones that reach maybe twenty meters and then dissolve into particulate dark. The generators hum through the seawater as a physical throb you feel in your teeth. Further down, the rift itself glows — a dull, deep cherry-red from the exposed rock, like a forge seen through deep water. The whole pocket is dim, but never dark. Amber and rust-red and the cold blue between.

There is no vegetation. Not a stalk, not a frond, not a sheet of kelp. The water is too warm, too mineral, too constantly scoured by current. The only green that ever tries is a slick of heat-loving bacterial felt that coats the lower pipes — brown-black, hairy, faintly luminous when disturbed — and is promptly burned off again when the pressure vents cycle. Everything else is mineral: iron rust blooming out from every weld in great blood-red clouds, white calcite beards, black manganese varnish. A mineral world. It smells, even through your suit, like a hot spring in a boiler room.

### The Sound and the Motion

You hear it before you see it. And then you cannot hear anything else.

The sound is deafening, and water makes it worse. Sound in water doesn't come from a direction; it comes from everywhere at once, inside your skull.

The four pumps are not synchronized. Each one hammers at a slightly different low frequency — *whump-whump-whump-whump* — so the beat constantly slips in and out of phase, creating a rolling, seasick throb that makes the entire chamber resonate like a drum. Over that, the high scream of the turbines, the clank and shudder of the rolling mills, the percussive *crack-crack-crack* of rivet guns operated by machines that never tire, the sudden, brutal *WHOOMPH* when a pressure valve releases and a train-sized bubble of superheated steam tears upward to smack the ceiling and spread.

You cannot shout over it. You cannot think over it. The town announces itself as pure industry, pure motion, and the rock dome throws it back until even the water seems to shake.

And the water *is* shaking. The dynamic here is turbulence made permanent.

Because the cold in-leak and the hot rift never equalize, the pocket is a storm that never leaves its room. Columns of warm water rise twisting from the center; cold tongues knife down from the throat; they collide mid-town and shear into vortices that spin off between buildings, carrying clouds of rust-flakes, welding slag, hot mineral snow, lost tools, all spiraling slowly and then suddenly fast. Scaffold towers sway on their flexible joints. Pipes whip. Loose sheets of metal clatter along gantries like leaves.

Nothing stays still. A walkway extended this morning will be half-dismantled by current by evening and rebuilt tomorrow somewhere else. The crab-automata cling with magnetic feet and keep working, welding in 3-knot cross-currents, paying no mind to whether the beam they are welding will be there next week. Creation and destruction are the same job here. The town is an anthill kicked over every hour, forever rebuilding the same anthill.

That is the rule of this place: **hold on.**

Currents will take you. You do not swim here; you clip. Every surface has hand-lines, grab-loops, pitons hammered into basalt. The old workers — and there *were* workers, you see oversized pressure suits hanging empty in open lockers, flood-helmets still dripping — moved hand-over-hand through the water, never letting go. If you let go you tumble through empty water between buildings, deafened, blinded by silt, until another current slams you into a hot pipe.

### Inhabitants

Fauna is rare. Almost insultingly rare for an ocean. A few things have found the mouth and come in to see what the thunder is.

Pale, eyeless zoarcids hover near the upper dome, mouths open, feeding on the bacterial snow, vibrating with the pump pulse. A single, huge blind isopod — the size of a suitcase — picks its way with infinite slowness across a girder, its shell crusted in the same manganese as the rocks, utterly indifferent to the steam blasts. Sometimes something bigger passes the throat — a shadow against the slightly colder inflow — and decides not to enter.

Nothing lives here if it can help it. This is not a reef. This is a boiler that happens to be full of sea.

But the automata are fauna of a sort. Squat, six-limbed, headless things of brass and sealed ceramic, they number in the hundreds. They swarm. When a vortex tears a pipe free, five are on it before it has settled, cutting, welding, hauling it back. When a new dormitory frame is needed, they erect it in the empty water with no plan you can discern, adding another skeletal tower to the sparse archipelago. They do not communicate. They do not rest. In the deafening roar, they work in complete silence, their electric joints whispering only to the water directly around them.

Are they maintaining the town for someone who will return? For someone who never left? Or is maintenance now the purpose itself?

You will not find out by staying in one place. The current takes you again, spinning you gently between two pump towers. Your lamp catches the long sweep of the dome, the whole impossible hidden city — too big to be a room, too small to be a world, too empty to be a city, too loud to be abandoned, too busy to be dead — hanging in warm dark water, churning, clanking, glowing, enduring.

It does not welcome you. It does not notice you. It just keeps working, turbulent and sleepless, in its pocket under the world.
