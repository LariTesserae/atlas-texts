It is a low place. Not a basin exactly, not a valley with edges you can see — the land just gives up on rising. It stays low and wide and flat for a long time, far past where you thought the horizon would be, and the air gets heavier the longer you look across it.

It is hot here without light.

The dark is not the dark of night. There is no moon to be missing, no stars behind cloud. It is a thick, hot dark, the color of wet basalt and deep water, illuminated only from within itself. Everything gives off a faint, damp luminescence — not bright enough to see by, just enough to know the edge of things. The stone sweats light. The water holds it a little longer than it should. If you stand still, your skin starts to prickle with condensation that never quite becomes a drop. The air is saturated. You breathe water as much as air.

This was, or is, a city. 

That word doesn't fit right at first because there are no straight lines left and there are no people, no tools, no wires, no glass, no hum of anything made. But the organization is still there — you can feel it in how you move. Blocks. Avenues. The ghost of a grid pressed into the soft earth. Buildings stand, but they are not built. They were grown or poured or accreted, layer after layer of something that was once perhaps limestone and brick and concrete but has long since been weathered into a single material: a dark, pitted, coral-like stone that is cool to the touch despite the heat. It is porous. It weeps.

The city is sparse. That is its strangest quality for a city. There is so much emptiness between structures. One massive, hollowed-out edifice — five stories, maybe ten, its floors melted together into terraces, its windows open like gills — and then a long, still plain of black water stretching for a quarter mile before the next one. The emptiness is not ruined; it is intentional. The builders left space the way a marsh leaves space for water.

And there is water everywhere, but not in an ocean. In sheets. In films. In shallow, hot seas that reflect the almost-light and are only inches deep, stretching between buildings like flooded streets that forgot they were streets. Canals cut perfectly square through the stone, still holding their shape after an age of erosion, their water slow and warm and completely clear, showing the siltless bottom. Every vertical surface drips. Every archway condenses and drips. The stone itself exhales. You hear it before you see it — a slow, patient *tick... tick... tick* from a hundred weeping cornices, none of them in rhythm.

Nothing grows much. A few things try.

On the terraces, in the windless dark, a sparse fur of vegetation clings — not trees, not grass. Low, flat rosettes of a black-red succulent that opens only to collect dew, its leaves as thick and waxy as rubber. Veins of dark moss run down the north sides of buildings that have no north. Reed-like stalks grow straight out of standing water with no roots you can see, a single stalk every twenty or thirty feet, absolutely still, absolutely alone. There are no animals to eat them. There are no insects on them, no birds above them. The city is silent of animal life in a way that feels deliberate, as if fauna was edited out.

You notice the quiet as a pressure. Your own footsteps — bare on wet stone or splashing in warm shallows — are too loud. When you stop, the only sounds left are the dripping and the very, very slow movement of water finding lower ground that isn't really lower. Occasionally a deeper sound: a sigh inside a hollow building as hot air shifts, or a pane of mineral skin cracking somewhere far off as it expands in the heat.

The active part is not fast. It is hydrological and geological. This place is in motion the way a swamp is in motion.

Water evaporates from the hot flat mirrors between buildings, rises only a few feet, and condenses again immediately on the cool-weeping stone, running back down in rivulets to rejoin itself. A short, endless cycle, three feet tall. Clouds try to form at head height and fail, becoming wandering patches of warm fog that drift down avenues and pool in courtyards before dissolving.

The stone erodes, but not into ruin. It has reached an equilibrium. Edges are rounded, corners softened, all ornament smoothed away by centuries of wet heat until every building looks like it was carved by water rather than hands. Yet they hold. They have settled into their own final shape and stopped collapsing. Gutters have become canyons an inch deep. Streets have become riverbeds. It is weathered and complete.

Then there is the breakdown.

You walk down a straight canal-street for a long time, counting the sparse reed-stalks — one, two, three — and the building on your left has the same weeping crack as the building you passed five minutes ago. Not similar. The *same*, drip for drip. If you turn around, the avenue you came down is longer than it should be. If you look up at the terraced hollows, the floors inside sometimes repeat. A room far above you contains another sky, darker and wetter, with another tiny city inside it, also dripping.

Distance slips. You can see for what feels like miles across the open, town-sized plain of water and sparse towers, and the towers in the far distance are both huge and oddly close, as if the air is a lens. Gravity is polite but forgetful — water runs downhill, then along a flat, then *up* a shallow step for a foot before remembering and continuing down. Droplets fall from an arch and hang for a half second too long before landing.

No one enforces this. There is no machine doing it, no technology humming underneath. That is the point. The city is not powered. It is just *like this*. Its physics grew tired and settled, like its stone, into a weathered, in-balance wrongness. It is a natural law here that space pools like water, that darkness can be hot, that a city can exist without anyone to build or inhabit it — grown by condensation and accretion and time.

You stand in ankle-deep warm water between two empty, breathing buildings. The dark is total and close and sweating. A drop falls from somewhere high above, hits the water at your feet with a clear, singular *plok*, and the ripples spread out across the flooded avenue, touching the distant, luminescent walls, and keep going far past where ripples should be able to go.
