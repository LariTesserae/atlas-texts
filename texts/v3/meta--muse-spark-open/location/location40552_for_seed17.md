This is the High Water.

Not an ocean *in* a basin. An ocean *on* a mountain — if you can call a thing that covers a hemisphere a mountain. A continent lifted until its back broke the weather. And then drowned.

### The Table

The rock underneath is young. That is the first impossible thing you notice once your eyes adjust to the scale.

You expect a drowned plateau to be smooth, worn down by pressure and time. It isn't. The seafloor here is not seafloor; it is summit. Fresh black glass, pillow basalt still showing its cooling cracks, sharp calderas that have never felt wind. Volcanic knives and cones, graben valleys shearing for hundreds of kilometers in straight, clean lines, as if the world was snapped and pulled apart last week and then immediately filled with cold water before dust could settle. There is no sand. No silt. No softening. Where the crust tore, the edges are still blade-sharp. Nothing has had time to erode them, and at 4 degrees above freezing, nothing ever will quickly. It is a mineral desert under an ocean sky.

And it goes on. God, it goes on. The Table is vast enough that it makes its own physics. You can swim for days over that basalt relief and see the curvature of the water above you change while the rock below never changes its character. Continental, planetary — you are not *in* a place, you are *on* a world whose only feature is this one lift. From any point on it, every horizon is the same: a dim blue-black line where black rock meets dark water, too far away to be real.

Normal light reaches here, which shouldn't happen. The water is kilometers deep. By all rights it should be abyssal black. But the sun here is high and hard and white, close at this elevation, and the water is preternaturally clear. Cold has stripped it. There is no plankton bloom, no algae, no green murk. No plant life — not even microbial mats to cloud it. Just water, cold-filtered to optical glass. Sunlight comes down in solid, cold columns, undistorted, illuminating every shard of volcanic glass down to the plain as if there were no medium at all. You can see for thirty, fifty kilometers horizontally, and see *nothing*. That's the emptiness.

Density 0.1. An anti-ocean. A real ocean is crowded — particulate, fish, drift. Here, a cubic kilometer might contain a single tower, a single animal, and a lot of illuminated absence. Space has been poured in. Your brain invents phantoms to fill it because it cannot accept distance.

### The Water That Doesn't Fall

It is submerged completely, but not enclosed. This is not a lake. The Table has edges; the Table ends.

If you travel far enough — weeks of steady propulsion in any direction — the rock simply stops and the ocean pours off.

Not as a river. As a wall. The water on the Table is held by nothing but its own strange behavior and the churn below. It spills over the continental lip in a continuous, unending cataract that falls ten, twelve kilometers straight down into cloud. Into nothing. The roar of that circumferential waterfall encircles the whole world. Even in the center of the Table, a thousand kilometers from the Edge, you hear it. Sound carries forever in cold clear water. It is a low, thrumming, bone-conducted thunder that never peaks and never fades, the deafening breath of a planet exhaling water forever.

Close to the Edge, it becomes physical law. The current begins to pull — slow, inexorable, cold hands drawing everything outward toward that drop. The sound there is beyond sound. It ceases to be heard and becomes *occupied*; it replaces silence inside your skull. Crew who work near the rim wear bone-dampeners not to protect hearing but to keep their organs from resonating apart.

No one knows where the water goes. Satellites show cyclones the size of Earth spiraling beneath the drop, endless storm systems fed by the fall. Somewhere down there, it must evaporate, rise, snow on the peaks and return. But from here, it looks like loss without replenishment. An equilibrium that shouldn't equilibrate.

Which brings us to -

### The Mills

They are the reason it hasn't frozen.

A cold, high, fresh, still ocean under normal light at elevation should be ice. A perfect, continent-thick lens of ice. It isn't. It's 3.8°C, uniformly, forever.

Scattered across the Table, at intervals so vast you can go days between them, stand the Mills.

Single structures. Each one alone. No roads connect them, no cables. No cities around them to justify their expense. They just rise — brutalist industrial — out of the sharp rock: a concrete-and-iron caisson the size of a cathedral pier, a tower that climbs two kilometers up through the water column without ever breaking the surface, capped with a five-bladed impeller that turns slowly, endlessly. Or a squat, barnacled factory on stilts, its furnaces burning a dull submarine orange, its smokestacks venting superheated plumes that dissolve instantly. Or a skeletal gantry, three hundred meters high, lowering and raising a hammer the size of an apartment block that strikes nothing, that just displaces.

They are electrified, mechanized, scaled for production — but they produce nothing. There are no nets, no harvests, no pipes taking anything away. They are engines running for the sake of running. Pumps pushing water in circles. Heaters bleeding waste heat. Stirrers preventing stratification. When you come upon one after a long empty crossing, you can hear it before you see it: a new note added to the deafening Edge-thunder, a whine of turbines, a clang of metal kept alive by automatic welders, a hiss of electrolysis.

They are clearly industrial. They are clearly maintained. By what? The weld-arms move with no operator. The factory doors open and close for submarines that never come. Occasionally a hatch will sigh open and spill a cloud of service drones — crab-like, stupid, industrious things — that scrape ice off nothing and retreat. No bodies. No voices.

Humans did not build this to live here. Humans built this — or something that builds like humans do — to *keep* this. To hold the world-state perfectly still. Dynamic 0.4: never changing.

And it works. Measurements taken fifty years apart show the same temperature to a tenth, the same current vector, the same light field. The Mills burn and turn and hammer and the water stays liquid, cold, clear, deafeningly pouring over its impossible lip, forever. Stable. Equilibrium. A machine for preserving a moment of youth — that fresh black rock, that sharp early-formation — under a sky-sea indefinitely.

### The Animals

Because there are animals, abundant, though they make no sense.

Without plants, without a photosynthetic base, an ocean should be empty. This one is full.

They move through the clarity like ghosts in air — too far away to judge scale, and then suddenly *too large* when they pass. Pale, elongate things built for cold and distance. Six-winged mantas the span of aircraft hangars, beating so slowly you think they're drifting, eating the bacteria that eat the chemical gradients that vent from the fresh rock itself. Chemosynthesis instead of photosynthesis; the entire food web rooted in stone and the Mills' heat.

Packs of knife-fish, long and eyeless, whistle constantly to navigate the void. Their clicks are the only sound that briefly cuts through the Edge-roar — a brief, bright chirp that locates a tower, a cliff, prey.

And the Benthos: lumbering, jointed, white-limbed things like conjoined oil rigs given instinct, that pick their way across the sharp glass with limbs that crackle and chip it, grazing on rust formed where engine heat kisses mineral water. When one dies — which is rare — it stands upright for decades, a white scaffold in clear water, before finally toppling and starting again as substrate.

None of them ever seem afraid of the Edge-current. They ride it like birds on wind, vanishing outward. New ones appear from the interior, grown in the warm lee of the Mills where the water shimmers. Abundant, quiet despite the noise, utterly indifferent to the fact they inhabit an artificial forever that should be ice.

### The Rule

Here is the rule that holds it all together:

Things fall outward, heat stays even, light goes through, rock stays sharp, sound never stops.

If you shut down a Mill, another one two hundred kilometers away spins a little faster. If you moor a habitat beside one, frost forms on the rock within a day. If you kill a manta, its body does not rot — bacteria here are too specialized to eat flesh, only stone — it drifts until something with jaws finds it, or it snags on a volcanic horn and becomes landmark.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing erodes. Nothing changes, though everything moves. It is a vast, high, cold, hollow engine the size of a hemisphere, filled with water that should have fallen long ago, tended by mindless factories that all beat in time to the deafening pour over the end of the world.

You float in normal daylight, suspended between black glass and falling sky, between industrial thrum and planetary thunder, and you understand: this is not an ocean that formed. This is an ocean that was *decided upon*, very recently, and then locked in place so it could remain forever young.

Listen long enough and even the deafening starts to sound like silence.
