It is not a canyon that was found. It is a canyon that is being made.

You come to the edge where the flatland ends and the ground simply stops, and below you is the City in the Cut. The sky is still the same wide, cool sky you have walked under for days — pale blue, high, with normal daylight falling straight and even across everything — but the land itself has been lowered. A vast, shallow bowl has been taken out of the earth, kilometers across at its widest point, town-or-valley sized, too large to see all at once but too enclosed to be a valley. From the rim you can see the other side as a stepped, hazy line of dwellings and smoke.

They call it emerging because it has not finished being born. The walls are raw.

Geology here is under construction. This is not old country, wind-scoured and smoothed. The cliffs that form the rim and the terraces are sharp and blocky and freshly broken, as if a god took a chisel to the plain yesterday. Stone comes away in clean planes and right angles. There are no riverbeds meandering down the sides, no talus slopes softened by time. Instead there are quarry-faces still showing tool marks, spoil heaps that have not yet settled, and vast shelves of exposed bedrock the color of wet ash and rust where the earth was peeled. The bottom is flat in the way a work floor is flat — leveled by hand, stamped hard by feet. You can see where the next cut will be: stakes driven in, lines scraped with chalk, a new terrace half-hollowed where men have left their stone hammers for the night.

It is cool down there, even when the sun is overhead. The air pools in the depression and stays there, a pocket climate a few degrees colder than the steppe above. You feel it as you descend the long stair that has been cut switchbacking down the north wall — switchbacks wide enough for two men to pass with baskets of rock on their backs. Your breath shows a little. Your hands come away cold from the stone rail.

It is also damp. Not wet. There is no river at the bottom, no lake. The Cut is mostly dry, dusty, full of stone dust that gets in your teeth. But it is never truly dry. Intermittent moisture lives here. Rain, when it comes, has nowhere to go; it sheets down the raw walls and collects in the hollows, in cisterns cut into the floors of courtyards, in barrels, in the scooped-out basins at the foot of every stair where children splash for an afternoon and then it is gone again, sunk into the porous stone. In the mornings, condensation beads on every surface — on the stone lintels, on the slate roofs, on the sleeping bodies packed close together — cold sweat that drips and darkens the rock until midday. In sheltered corners, behind walls that never see direct sun, the stone stays dark with it, slick with a thin skin of algae. The people build their drains shallow and everywhere, little runnels with flat stones laid over them, to guide water that is only sometimes there.

And packed — god, it is packed.

From the rim it looks orderly, almost beautiful: concentric rings of terraces following the shape of the excavation, connected by stairs and ladders and ramps. But as you get lower you understand it is not orderly so much as saturated. There is no room between presences. Every flat surface is occupied. Houses are one-room stone cells built wall-to-wall in rows along each ledge, their flat roofs serving as the yard for the row above. Laundry lines span alleys so narrow your shoulders brush both sides. Workshops jut into streets. Market stalls fill every intersection. Even the vertical space is used — lofts, balconies of rough timber, hanging looms, stacked firewood, drying racks. The outer stones are still the color of fresh quarry; the inner stones are polished dark by hands, by shoulders, by blankets.

This is urban, but not with towers or metal. It is urban done with primitive hands. Everything is early-tool-use: stone mauls and wooden wedges, copper picks that bend and have to be hammered straight again, baskets woven from reed, mud mortar, hand-shaped brick left to bake cool in the damp air. The people move rock the way ants move sand, endlessly, actively. The Cut is not a ruin you live in. It is a project you live inside of while you dig it. At any hour you can see them working the far wall, a line of figures tapping, prying, hauling. The spoil comes down in sleds. The new stones go up into new walls on the opposite side. The city extends itself outward and downward as it lives. The sound of it should be deafening. It is not.

It is quiet.

For a settlement this dense, with this many people, the quiet is the strangest thing after the raw walls, and it is not strange at all once you hear it. There are no engines. There is no metal ringing loud enough to carry. Tool on rock makes a dull *thuk, thuk, thuk* rather than a clang. People speak low, because stone nearby throws your voice back at you. Footsteps are bare or wrapped in soft leather and rag. The loudest thing at noon is the wind turning over the rim, and the softer sounds underneath: water dripping from a cistern lid, a mortar pestle grinding, a baby crying three terraces over and being quickly hushed, the scrape of a reed broom. Even the market is quiet — barter conducted by pointing and nodding, by laying out goods on mats: dried lichen cakes, little wrinkled root vegetables that grow well in stone beds, salt cakes.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of preciousness. No trees grow in the Cut; there wasn't soil for them and there is no soil now. What grows is placed there. On the sunward ledges people have made shallow beds of imported dirt held in by stone lips, where they grow thin barley and leeks and a tough grey-green succulent that stores water in its leaves. Lichen is cultivated like a crop on damp north-facing walls, scraped off with flat knives. A single fig tree, somehow alive in the shelter of a courtyard, is a landmark known to half the city; its few leaves are dusty but carefully tended. Everything else that is green is a weed finding purchase in a crack, gone in a week.

Animals are rare. You expect rats, pigeons, dogs in a place this packed, this alive with human motion. There are almost none. Perhaps the Cut is too new, too cool, too lacking in waste — everything is used. A few thin cats move like smoke across roofs. Small hardy sparrows flick through alleys in pairs. Deep in the grain stores you hear the beetle that lives there, a glossy black thing no bigger than a nail head, and its larvae that the poor eat without comment. Once a day you might see the long, grey rock-eaters — heavy lizards as long as your forearm — sun themselves on the quarry face, utterly still, before disappearing into a crevice.

And yet it is active. The stillness is not death. It is the intensity of a hive between pulses.

The dynamic here is not dramatic. It is multiple small cycles layered over each other, all visible because nothing is hidden: the cycle of the water appearing and vanishing; the cycle of stone being cut in the morning, carried at midday, and laid by evening; the cycle of the barley sprouts; the cycle of people ascending to trade on the plain above and descending again before dusk, a tide of bodies on the stairs; the cycle of the terraces breathing — expanding a few feet outward each season, then filling immediately with new homes. The place is under formation and it knows it. No one talks about finishing it. To finish would be to die.

At night, from above, the Cut glows faintly. Not with electric light — with hearths. Hundreds, thousands of small fires in stone pits, their light normal and warm, reflected off damp rock, off packed walls. From the rim you hear the quiet again, amplified by cold air: a cough, a low song, a hammer set down for the last time that day.

And the raw walls hold it all in, new and sharp against the sky, waiting for tomorrow's cut.
