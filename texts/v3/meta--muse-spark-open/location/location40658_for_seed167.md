There is a city at the bottom of a warm sea, and it was not built.

Not in the way building is usually meant. No hands. No tools. No cut stone.

You descend into it - though descending doesn't feel quite right, because there is no surface anymore to descend from. There is only water above you for a long, long way, a weight of warm, dark water that has been here so long it has forgotten how to be anything else. The pressure is not crushing; it is complete. It holds you. The water here is bath-warm, 30 degrees and still, not the cold abyss you expect from darkness. It is a tropical night that never ends and never had a day.

Light does not reach here. Not really. At 0.1, what we call darkness is not absence, but a texture. Far, far above, the sun is a rumor, diffused into nothing by a kilometer of water. Down here, the dark is thick, indigo-black and utterly uniform, until it isn't. Because the city itself glows - dimly, intermittently, biologically. Not like streetlamps. Like breath.

The terrain is low. This is not a reef on a mountain. This is a plain, a vast, gently bowed basin on the seafloor, low and settled and old. The bottom is not sand. It's a pavement of compressed time: ancient reef-death compacted into limestone, then worn smooth, then built upon again. Erosion here doesn't mean being worn away - it means being resolved. Every edge has been felt over by currents for ten thousand years until it found its final shape. Hollows where hollows want to be. Pillars where the flow needs pillars. It is weathered in the way a riverbed is weathered - nothing jagged left, everything in balance with water.

And on that resolved floor: the city.

It is dense. Impossibly dense for something with no architects. From the edge of the basin to as far as your eyes - which have learned to see in heat and motion more than in light - can follow the dark, it spreads. Not sprawling like a human town, but packed, layered, vertical without being tall. A low city, three, four stories at most, but every cubic meter is occupied.

It is made of living secretion.

The builders are still building it. They always have been. Colony animals, filter-beings, tube-worms the thickness of your wrist and as long as houses, black corals that grow into arches, sponges that calcify into walls. Each one excretes its home. Each home fuses to the next. A single structure might be five species in symbiosis - a foundation of brain-coral, buttressed by barnacle-concrete, roofed over by a sheet of leather-sponge pulled taut like a tent, then colonized over generations by fire-sponge, veil-anemone, lace-polyps. No streets were planned, but corridors emerge where currents flow strongest, kept clear by the constant beating of a million cilia. No houses were designed, but chambers appear - round, warm, slightly pulsing - where something lives, or lived, and left the room behind.

It looks, from above if you could see from above, like a brain the size of a town. Or a lung. Folded, convoluted, full of deep, winding sulci that are avenues. There are plazas that are just sudden widenings where three big filter-fans meet and the water slows. There are towers that are not towers but the calcified exhalation siphons of something the size of a whale, long dead, its mineral ghost still venting warm water upward in a slow, endless sigh.

Vegetation is sparse. That is the first strange thing. For a reef, you expect fields of algae, kelp forests. There is almost none. A few drifting hair-algae cling to the highest ridges like sparse, waving weeds, and pale, leafless stalks that are not plants at all but animal mimics grow in the quietest alleys. Photosynthesis is a myth here. Everything eats what the water brings, or eats each other. The color palette is not green. It is bone-white, rust-red, bruise-purple, sulfur-yellow - the colors of living mineral.

But fauna - fauna is everywhere. Density 2.0 doesn't begin to cover it. The city *is* fauna.

You cannot put your hand out without touching something alive, and it will touch you back - not aggressively, but curiously. Clouds of glass minnows, each transparent except for a single opal dot of light in its belly, move as one body through the avenues, parting around pillars, reforming instantly. Armored isopods the size of dogs cling to vertical walls, their backs themselves cities for smaller barnacles. In the chambers, things nest. You see eyelid-anemones the size of doors open and close slowly, filtering. You see long, ribbon-soft predators undulate through the middle stories, never in a hurry, and the minnows make way before they arrive as if they heard them coming minutes ago.

You hear almost nothing. 0.8 quiet. Water dampens everything. There is no crash of surf, no wind. What sound there is, is infrastructure: the low, pervasive hum of pumping. Every tube, every sponge, every breathing wall draws water in and pushes it out. It's the sound of a thousand thousand hearts at work, out of sync, creating a soft, arrhythmic hush that you feel in your teeth more than your ears. Occasionally, a deeper creak as a coral arch adjusts its growth. A click-chorus as shrimp colonies argue over a tunnel. And underneath it all, a faint fizz - the warm vents that make this whole place warm in the first place. Hairline cracks in the basin floor leak geothermally heated water, 35, 40 degrees, shimmering up through the city like inverted smoke, feeding the filterers, keeping the whole basin in a gentle, convective stew. The water turns over, slowly, endlessly.

The rule here is simple, and it makes the city work without tools:

**Nothing leaves.**

In a place with no light and no plants and no hunt for the surface, waste is death. So the city evolved to waste nothing. What one creature excretes, another extrudes into a wall. Feces become mortar. Mucus becomes net. Shed shells become roads. Even death is construction material - a fish dies and before it can rot, the wall-softeners have softened, the bone-borers have bored, the flesh is stripped and filtered and the calcium is re-laid as a new lip on a doorway. You will see it happening: a dark passage where a recent death occurred glows a little brighter for a day, feverish with recycling bioluminescence, then goes dark again, slightly thicker.

You can live here, for a time, breathing the warm water if you know how, moving slowly so you don't disrupt the flow. The residents will not mind you if you are slow. If you are fast, they will close. Door-anemones will seal. Passage-polyps will retract. The whole avenue can clench like a throat.

At night - though it is always night - there is a tide of light. Not from the moon, which doesn't exist down here. It moves through the fauna. One cluster of veil-polyps on a high ridge will flash amber, and the signal will spread, ridge to ridge, avenue to avenue, across the entire regional sprawl of the basin. A census. A greeting. *We are still here. Are you still here?* And the whole low city answers, in fading amber waves, until the darkness returns to being complete, warm, and dense with quiet, active life.

It is not unusual in a monstrous way. That would be too easy. It is unusual in that it shouldn't be a city at all. It is a town that decided to be an organism, or an organism that grew so large and so folded in on itself that it became a town, and then kept growing, patiently, in the warm, heavy dark where no one was watching, for longer than any built thing on the surface has ever stood.
