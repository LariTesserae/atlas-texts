It is not a valley. It is not a canyon. It is a pocket that the surface forgot to close.

You find it by accident, because you were not looking for it. A shallow dip in the cool upland where the sod has pulled back from a lip of stone, no wider across than a kitchen table is long. You have to go to your hands and knees to see it. You have to turn your cheek to the earth. And then below — light.

The chamber is below surface but it is not dark.

Think of a single lung in the earth, an intimate room. Perhaps twenty paces from wall to wall at its widest, and low enough that a standing man would bow his head if he could even stand, but he can't, because the room is already full. Not empty. Not open. Packed.

The geology here is embryonic. This place has not been carved. It is being decided. The walls are raw, unweathered basalt still holding the blister-texture of its cooling, black and iron-red, with seams of paler stone that have not yet learned to be seams. There is no slope made by water, no scree, no worn bed. Erosion has barely begun to consider this place. It is pre-formation, undeveloped, like a mold just lifted. Edges are soft and swollen rather than sharp. The floor is not flat but tumid, a slow rise and dip of half-born stone, still warm with its own making in a time outside of time.

And it is bright. Impossibly bright for an under-thing.

The light does not pour in from your slit of sky. That slit is only a scratch, a thumbnail of overcast. The light comes from within. A cold, clear, white luminance that hangs without source. It is the air itself that is luminous, or the stone breathing light, or the condensation suspended. It is not sunshine. It has no glare and no shadow, only a complete, even, awake brightness that makes every fleck of damp sparkle without sparkle, every filament seen. It is the brightness of a surgery, of a microscope slide. Cool light for a cool place. The temperature never bites, never warms — a sustained, cellar-cool that sits at the back of your teeth, the temperature of a stone that has never seen summer.

It is damp, but not wet. There is not enough water here to be a pool or a stream. Water exists as an idea that keeps almost happening. Beads on the low ceiling that never fall. A sheen on the walls that never runs. Condensation jewels on every surface, trembling but never dripping. Pockets of dampness held in shallow, dish-like depressions in the floor-rock where the stone dimples to hold a palm's worth of black water, each one a perfect, still mirror reflecting the sourceless white. The air tastes of it — iron and mineral and old rain that hasn't fallen yet. Between the dishes, the rock is dry to the touch. This is riverine without a river. Rain without raining.

Vegetation is sparse, and it shouldn't be here at all. No soil. No sun as plants understand sun. Yet it insists in the way life insists in a crack in a sidewalk. Pale lichen in flat, coin-sized rosettes pressed hard to stone, sage-grey and bleached mint. One or two things that are almost ferns, but not, with fronds as thin as paper, translucent, veined with light, growing straight from the rock joint like eyelashes. No trees. No meadow. Just that scattering, that stubborn, spare presence, each plant given far more light than it has any right to need.

Because the real occupation here is not plant. It is animal. It is packed.

The room is saturated with being. The walls, the ceiling, the dish-pools, the air itself — covered. Thousands upon thousands of small, pale bodies. You think at first it is a fungus or a crystal, until one moves. And then one more. Then you understand the wall is not a wall.

They are things like moths and things like crickets and things like shrimp, but none of those strictly. Soft-bodied, blind, the size of a thumbnail and the size of a pinhead, translucent white and opalescent amber, with limbs folded and antennae trembling. Wingless flies that walk. Six-legged crawlers with pill-bug backs that glisten under the white light. Pale, eyeless salamander-kin no longer than your finger, clinging to the vertical. They are shoulder-to-shoulder, leg-to-leg, no gap between presences, a living carpet that coats every inch of this hidden room with breathing. You could not put your hand down without touching a dozen.

And they are loud.

This should be silent, down here, sealed. Instead the pocket roars with a steady, unmodulated, full-volume drone. Not a chirp here and a trill there, but a massed, choral stridulation. Every cricket-thing rubbing its legs at precisely the same pitch. A wall of sound that does not rise or fall, does not move in waves or respond to you. The crickets never start. They never stop. The hum has been ongoing. You realize you are not hearing them begin to sing when you open the slit; you are interrupting a song that has no beginning. It is loud enough to live in your chest, to make the beads of ceiling-water quiver in sympathy but —

— without ever falling. Nothing ever falls. Nothing ever changes.

That is the rule here. The strangest thing.

This is a place at equilibrium. Not quiet, but frozen. The hum does not get louder or softer. The pale ferns do not grow or wilt or seed. The salamander-kin do not hunt, though the crawler-kin seethe around them. No one eats. No one flees. They are alive — you can see the spiracle-flutter, the pulse under translucent skin — but they are caught in a stable moment, time-invariant. The dish-pools never evaporate despite the brightness; the condensation never condenses into drip. The light never flickers. The air does not stir. You hold your breath and the pocket holds it with you.

It is strange the way a held note is strange. A place just coming into being that decided to pause halfway.

Among the bodies, scattered, are the marks of something that was here or tried to be here. Built, but barely. Scattered. A cairn of three tiny, squared stones stacked with intent, now half-overgrown by lichen and living backs. A circle scratched into basalt, precise, about the size of a plate, with radial lines — a calendar or a face or a map of this very room. A length of woven fiber, not cloth, not rope, pressed flat against the floor as if left and then lived over. No tools. No devices. No tech. Pure pre-tool, biological-only, as if hands made these things and then left before hands meant iron.

You understand that if you slipped down into it, into that bright, cool, roaring, packed womb of under-rock, you would not sink. There is no room to sink. The fauna would hold you up on their backs, uncrushed, unmoving. The water would not wet your knees more than it already does. The light would not warm you. The hum would fill your ears and then, after a long minute, stop sounding like sound at all and start sounding like stillness that happens to be audible.

You lie on your stomach at the lip for a long time, cheek to the cool earth. You look into an embryonic world that has not decided whether it will become a cave or a geode or a grave, and has chosen, in the meantime, to be bright and loud and full and absolutely, perfectly unchanged.
