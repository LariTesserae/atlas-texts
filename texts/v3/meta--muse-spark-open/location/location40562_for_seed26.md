You are three hundred meters down, and the roof is still high above you.

Not a cave. Never a cave. There is no bedrock here anymore, no earth, no stone that wasn't poured, cut, or bolted into place. This whole valley was made.

It was excavated first — a long, regional-scale trough gouged out of the cold lithosphere when the surface froze over — and then it was *built within*. From wall to wall, from the weeping vault-ceiling to the grated ground under your boots, everything is substrate. Layer after poured layer of iron-black concrete, ribbed retaining walls stepped like inverted ziggurats, catwalks and conduits and bracing struts still showing the bright, sharp seams where formwork was stripped away only months or years ago. Nothing has had time to soften. The corners are still corners. The blast-edges are still chipped and flaking. Rebar whiskers poke from unfinished pillars like fresh roots. It is a young place, a new world, and it hasn't learned how to be old yet.

And it is cold.

Not the dry cold of deep stone, but a wet, industrial cold that lives in the air itself. The heat never quite reaches here. The generators and forges deeper in bleed warmth in great exhaling gusts, but the air is too large and too damp to hold it. Your breath plumes. The metal sweats. Every surface is slick with condensation that never stops forming, beading on overhead pipes as thick as subway tunnels, gathering, falling. The atmosphere is low and close despite the scale — a low-pressure fog that hangs under the vaulted roof in a permanent, dim dusk.

Light exists here only because it is manufactured. Sodium lamps and work floods bolted to superstructures every hundred meters, many of them already dead or strobing, casting a weak amber glow that doesn't reach far before the damp air eats it. Between pools of light are long reaches of near-dark, where the bulkheads recede into blue-black and you can only tell distance by the red telltales of machines that never sleep. Shadows move even when nothing is moving — because steam moves.

Because water is everywhere, without ever being a river or a lake.

At 1.6 wet, this place weeps. Groundwater from the world above was supposed to be pumped out. Instead it found the joints. It seeps through expansion cracks in the ceiling vault in cold, mineral-heavy threads, leaving white calcite beards on black concrete. It condenses on every cold pipe and rains back down in a constant, arrhythmic drizzle that patters on corrugated roofing, on tarps, on hard hats. Under the grating, you can hear it — and see it — rushing. A shallow, black, fast sheet-flow in the service culvert that runs the length of the valley, carrying oil rainbows, hot coolant runoff, the occasional clatter of a loose bolt. The walls themselves are damp to the touch. Put your hand to a support column and your palm comes away wet and gritty with fresh cement and cold.

Nothing grows easily here, and no one intended it to.

Vegetation is sparse, incidental, stubborn. In the dimmest, wettest seams — where a lamp has burned out and the constant drip has pooled — there are sudden, shocking colonies of green-black moss and white lichen, furred over concrete like mold on bread. A few birch-like saplings, thin as wires, have taken root in silt that collected on a collapsed gantry; their leaves are pale from lack of sun, translucent, rattling in the artificial wind. They shouldn't be here. No one planted them. Seeds blew down the ventilation shafts from a dead surface and found water and clung. They will not spread far. The cold and the dark and the periodic scouring blasts of steam keep them in check.

Animals are rarer still. You hear rats sometimes, or something like rats — paler, almost hairless from generations in the dark — skittering along pipe runs. The maintenance crews talk about blind white fish in the culvert water, dropped as fry by accident and now breeding. Once, a flock of pigeons lost its way down a main intake and lived for weeks wheeling in the vaulted gloom, their coos swallowed by the louder sounds.

Because it is loud.

Not deafening at any one point, but relentlessly, inescapably loud at scale. This is an active place. An industrial, electrified ecosystem in motion. You are never more than a few hundred meters from something large doing work. Ventilation fans the size of houses turn overhead with a slow, percussive *whup-whup-whup* that you feel in your chest. Sump pumps kick on with a shudder and a whine, hammering water up and out. Far off — echoing so you can't tell direction in the constructed valley — a stamping mill or pile driver is working fresh concrete, each impact ringing off the parallel walls and coming back a second later as a doubled echo. Conveyor belts squeal. Pressure valves hiss and scream and vent. Water drips, trickles, rushes, drips. It all layers into a cold cathedral reverb, a low industrial chant that never resolves.

And in a place this large and this empty, sound is how you know how big it is.

You stand on a maintenance road that runs straight for two kilometers until perspective makes the lamps merge. To your left, the stepped terraces of worker hab-modules, each one identical, most dark, laundry lines strung between them steaming as wet clothes freeze and thaw and never quite dry. To your right, the factory floor proper: open assembly bays under skeletal gantries, welding arcs flickering blue-white like lightning in the dim, sparks falling like burning snow and hissing out on wet concrete. Above it all, the vault — a curve of ribbed concrete and steel, streaked with rust-water stalactites already forming in only a few years, dripping, dripping, dripping.

The strange part is how *alive* it feels despite being entirely made.

Because nothing is still. The air circulates in slow, cold gyres driven by the heat differential between the forges and the intakes. The water cycle is closed but leaky — pumped, condensed, wept, drained, pumped again — a weather system for a town that has no sky. The machines have cycles like breathing. The moss grows a millimeter a day and is scraped away. The rust grows. The concrete cures and cracks and is patched with fresh concrete that will crack again. Even the light has a circadian stutter as grids brown-out and recover.

There are rules here, but they are not natural laws. They are maintenance schedules.

1. Water must keep moving. If it stops, it freezes, expands, and splits the world.
2. Air must keep moving. If it stops, the cold sinks and pools and suffocates the low terraces.
3. Light is allocated. No one gets all of it.
4. Nothing is allowed to finish. A finished surface would start to die. Here, a crew is always pouring, always welding, always grinding a sharp edge sharper.

You zip your jacket higher and keep walking, boots ringing on wet grating, breath fogging, shoulders hunched against the constant fine rain from pipes that were never meant to be a sky — and far above you, another bead of water gathers on a girder, swells, and falls thirty stories through dim, loud, freezing air to strike concrete that was poured last week.
