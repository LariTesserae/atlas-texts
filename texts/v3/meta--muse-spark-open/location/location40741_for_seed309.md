It is not underground the way a cave is underground.

It is *below surface*, which is different. A surface still exists somewhere above you - you can feel its weight - but you have dropped beneath its grade.

The Sunk Hamlet.

Long ago someone cut down into the earth to make a place to work. Not deep, not a mine shaft disappearing into black. Just down. A rectangular bite taken out of the warm lowlands, three or four stories deep, with straight quarried walls on all sides. Big enough for a town, small enough that you can stand at one end on a rusted catwalk and see the other end through the haze. A cathedral of air with no roof. A room the size of a city block.

That was generations ago. The cut has settled.

### The Walls That Are Not Walls

The limestone quarry-walls are still there, but you have to know what you're looking for. They are weathered soft at the edges now, rounded, pitted, long-existed. Water has had its way with them for a long time - not flooded, never flooded - but damp. Always damp. The warm air above sinks into this pit at night and condenses on every cool surface. By morning, everything drips.

That moisture was an invitation.

Vegetation did not *grow in* the Sunk Hamlet. It *became* it. This is not a ruined city overgrown with vines. This is a plant mass that has learned to be a city.

The buildings were industrial - brick mill-houses, corrugated sheds, a boilerworks, workers' row houses stacked two-high along poured streets. Their frames are still inside there, but the living matter has used them as trellis, as bone.

What you see is:

- Fig and banyan and strangler roots as thick as factory pillars, descending from the lip above where the forest on the surface leans over, never quite falling in, just constantly dropping roots down like slow ropes. They hit the quarry floor and spread.
- Moss and liverwort and felted black-green mats that have swallowed entire walls. You touch what looks like a solid wall and your hand sinks a half-inch into damp sponge.
- Leaves as architecture. The roofs are gone, replaced by interwoven canopies of broad, waxy leaves the size of doors, overlapping layer after layer, making a second ceiling about ten feet above the old street level. They let the dim light through in mottled amber shafts. They collect condensation and funnel it.
- Flowers growing out of vertical space. Bracket fungi the size of tables. Vines that have thickened into walkways.

The streets are still streets - you can follow their grid - but they are root-channeled. The ground is a springy lattice of root, humus, fallen leaf-pulp, and old cracked concrete breathing underneath. Everywhere is warm and smells like green being cooked.

### The Water

There is no river here. There is not enough water for that. What there is is more insidious and constant.

At any given moment, a third of the surfaces are wet. Condensation beads on the underside of every leaf and falls with a soft, endless *pat*. The old gutters still work, but now they carry leaf-tea, brown and tannic. Pockets of water collect where they can: a boiler tank turned cistern, now a lily pond; a collapsed basement that holds a permanent shallow pool the color of tea; the great rusted flywheels that cup a few inches of rain for days before it evaporates back into the damp air.

After the midday heat peaks above, it rains for eight minutes inside the pit. Not real rain. The canopy releases. All at once. A brief, warm shower with no clouds. Then it stops, steaming.

### The Inhabitants

You would expect a place this green to be quiet. It is violently loud.

This is because of the fauna, which is wrong in its abundance. The Sunk Hamlet should not be able to support this much animal life. The plant mass is sparse in *presence* - there are no human crowds anymore, the factories stand empty, the streets are mostly empty air between trunks - but every cubic foot of that empty air is occupied.

Everything hums, clicks, croaks, saws.

- Clouds of moths as big as your hand, pale, silent except for the papery rush when they all lift at once.
- Beetles with mirrored backs thatclang against the corrugated-iron leaves.
- Frogs, thousands of them, living in the bromeliads that live on the branches that live on the buildings, each one shouting for territory.
- Swifts that nest in the old smokestacks, cutting screaming arcs in the dim shaft.
- Ants in industrial quantities - they have built highways as wide as pipes along the old electrical conduits, carrying leaf fragments in a living river.
- And deeper, things that are not quite any of those. Things that have adapted to an urban-green. Rats with mossy pelts. Vines that twitch when a fly lands because the vine *is* the flytrap. Slugs that have learned to graze on rust.

There are still people, a few dozen. You see them rarely because they are spaced out, scattered. A woman tending a line of bee-boxes set in a former storefront. A man sleeping in a hammock strung between two gear-wheels. They move slowly and don't speak loudly; the place doesn't require it. Their settlement is sparse, their houses are pockets.

The weirdness is in the rules. In the Sunk Hamlet:

1. **Metal grows.** Not metaphorically. Leave a wrench on a damp sill and in a week it has a fine green fur of oxidized moss that puts down hair-roots into the pitting. Leave it for a month and you have to tear it free. The industrial things are being digested very slowly, very patiently.
2. **Sound is a structure.** Certain cicadas here synchronize not by time but by location. On the east street, one tone. On the west, another. Their drone is so loud it vibrates the large leaves, and the young learn to navigate by the standing wave - where the sound cancels, there's a walkway. Where it doubles, there's a drop.
3. **Light belongs to something.** The dimness is not just shade. The air itself is a little particulate with spores after the canopy-rain. Light comes down the open top as warm columns that almost look solid. At dusk, the fungi on the quarry walls begin to glow faintly - not bright, just enough to see - in response to the vibration of the mills. Sound makes light here.

### The Machines Still Turn

This is an industrial place, electrified. That hasn't stopped.

Three of the factory wheels still turn. No one turned them on. No one can turn them off.

Root-tension and water-weight and vine-growth caught the old belt systems and kept them under slow, inexorable pull. One great wheel on the north wall groans around once every eleven minutes, dragging a chain of buckets that no longer carry coal but carry water and frogs and fallen blossoms up and over and down again. It squeals. The sound echoes. Sparrows ride it.

The generators hum. The wires are sheathed in vine now, but they still carry a low, leaky current. At night you can see a bare bulb flickering amber in an alley where no one lives, powered by nothing anyone will claim.

Everything is active. Nothing is still. Leaves unfurling with audible pops. Hunters hunting. Mold eating brick. Water moving. Gears moving. Frogs calling and answering.

If you stand at the bottom center of the Sunk Hamlet and look up, you see a rectangle of brighter, hotter sky, far away, framed by the overhanging trees of the surface that never quite fall in. That sky is silent.

Everything below it is not.
