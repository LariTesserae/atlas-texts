Beneath.

Not a cave in the way a cave is a hole in a hill - a dark pocket you have to crawl into. This is a beneath that has forgotten it is beneath.

You come down through a throat of young rock where the world above has cracked and settled, a long, cool slide of broken limestone and basalt still sharp at the edges. Nothing here is rounded yet. The stone was born recently, geologically speaking, shoved up and then dropped and then hollowed by water that did not have time to be patient. Everything is angular - fresh fault scarps that run in straight, clean lines for half a mile before they vanish into dimness, terraces of stone like giant stairs snapped rather than worn, shallow basins with rims you could cut your hand on. Erosion has only just started its work, so the place keeps its edges.

And then it opens.

The scale is wrong for being underground. You expect to be able to touch both walls; instead the floor drops away into a valley that should be on the surface. A rift-valley, a mile across at its narrowest and longer than you can see, its far end lost not in darkness but in a blue-grey haze of distance and cool air. The ceiling is so high you stop thinking of it as a ceiling. It is a low sky. Sixty, eighty meters overhead in the shallow places, two hundred in the deep, a vault of dark, damp stone that sweats constantly. There is no sun to burn it off. Condensation gathers there in beads, grows heavy, and falls as a slow, perpetual rain that never quite becomes a storm. On still days you can see it falling in curtains, catching what little light there is.

Because there is light.

It is dim, the light at the bottom of deep water. It comes from far behind you, from the three or four collapse-windows where the valley's roof fell through to the world above - distant ovals of overcast sky, too far to warm anything, enough to put a direction on shadows. And it comes from below, in a way: the wet rock itself holds a faint phosphorescence where colonies of bacteria and pale lichen eat the stone, a soft, greenish-grey glow along every seep and joint. Not enough to read by. Enough to see that the space in front of you keeps going.

It is cool. Not cold enough to frost, not warm enough to rot. The air sits at that exact equilibrium where breath shows faintly when you exhale hard, and holds there forever. The temperature does not shift between day and night, between summer and winter. The stone exhales the same cool it inhaled a thousand years ago. Condensation forms at the same rate it evaporates. The intermittent streams - there are a dozen of them, thin as wrists, cutting quick, sharp little canyons across the valley floor - run at the same level they always have, neither flooding nor drying. Pockets of standing water sit in the limestone bowls, clear and still and black, reflecting the vault above for months at a time without ripple until a drip disturbs them, and then they settle again. Everything here is damp, but never drowned. Your boots are always wet. Your hands are always cold. Nothing ever quite dries.

There is almost nothing built here, nothing made. No walls, no houses, no quarries, no roads, not even cairns. If people have ever lived here, they did not believe in leaving a mark. But they were here, or something like them was. In the gravel bars beside the streams you find them if you look: a chipped flint flake with the bulb of percussion still bright, a charred stick sharpened to a point and then abandoned, a hollowed gourd left to fill with seep-water. Primitive work, hand-work, dropped where it was used. Simple bone awls lost between rocks. There is no village to gather them into. They are scattered as thinly as everything else on the valley floor, one artifact to an acre of stone.

Vegetation is sparse, stubborn. There are no forests, no meadows. There is the dark green-black crust that lives on constant damp - mosses that never had to evolve for drought, liverworts slick as wet leather, ferns that grow single-file along the cracks where water runs. A few stunted trees manage to root where the collapse-windows have let a little soil fall through - pale birches with bark like paper, their leaves small and turned always toward that distant dim sun. They are widely spaced, each one isolated in its own pool of fallen light, with a hundred yards of bare, wet stone between it and the next. The empty space is the point.

The emptiness is also full.

Because for every square foot of bare rock, there is motion.

Fauna on a scale that makes the sparse stone and sparse green seem like a mistake. The valley is overwhelming, not with variety but with number. The air - cool, still, damp - is stitched by wings. Small, pale cave-moths in clouds so dense they make a second haze beneath the stone sky, rising and settling in breath-like pulses. Needle-bats, thousands upon thousands, hung in overlapping black shingles on every overhang, chittering without pause. On the ground, pale millipedes as long as your forearm move in slow rivers over the stone, antennae waving. Blind fish flash white in the black pools. In the streams, amphibians with translucent skin and external gills crowd flank to flank, never moving far because there is nowhere they need to go that is different from where they are.

It is loud.

Not with water, not with wind - there is almost no wind this deep, and the water is thin and constant, a background tick and trickle. It is loud with life. The bats never truly sleep; the moths never truly land; the air is filled with the papery thunder of wings beating in unison, the high, relentless chitter and echoing squeak that bounces from fault scarp to fault scarp until you cannot tell direction. On the floor, the chitinous rustle of countless legs, the plop of fish, the croak of amphibians - layered until the valley hums, until the stone itself seems to vibrate with it. You have to raise your voice to be heard, and when you stop speaking, the sound does not stop with you. It was there before you came down. It will be there long after.

Nothing changes here. That is the strangest and most normal thing about it. The sharp scarps do not soften. The dim light does not brighten or fail. The streams do not carve deeper in any time you can witness. The moths rise at the same hour, settle at the same hour, though there is no sun to tell them to. The bats shift but do not migrate. The condensation drips. The stone sweats. The cool holds. It is a place in perfect, humming equilibrium - a vast, damp, cool, dim valley under the earth that has found the exact balance between wet and dry, empty and full, silence and noise, and has decided, for now, for a very long time, to stay.
