This place has no sky.

Not in the sense that it is absent — but in the sense that it was closed off, a very long time ago, and has not been needed since.

You are standing — or if not standing, suspended, held — in a low continent. The elevation is almost nothing. If there were an ocean, you would be at its drowned margin, a delta the size of a subcontinent that never drained. The ground is young. It hasn't had time to be softened. Under the mat, when you can find it, there are ribs of fresh rock, black and glassy, still sharp where they broke. Unweathered basalt, still holding the heat of its birth. The land came up fast and flat, a lift of warm stone, and the plants took it before rain could carve it.

And they took it completely.

This is not a forest. A forest has spaces — clearings, trunks, canopy, ground. This has no spaces. Vegetation is not *on* the terrain here, it is the terrain. A substrate many hundreds of feet deep in places, a living mass that learned how to be ground and mountain and architecture without ever becoming wood in the way you understand it.

At the top — far above you, a mile or maybe three, it is hard to tell in the darkness — there is a roof. Leaves the size of continents overlapping continents. Not leaves, exactly. Plates. Black-green and slick, warm to the touch, each one exhaling a slow, wet breath. They are packed so densely they have made their own night. No light gets through. 0.1 lux, if that. The occasional bioluminescent thread, a dull amber fault-line where a leaf-edge drips, but nothing you could read by. The darkness here is not empty dark. It is full dark. Vegetal dark.

Warm. 1.5 out of 3 warm. Not hot. Womb-warm. Greenhouse-warm. The air never cools. It holds so much water it is almost not air. You breathe condensation. Every surface weeps. The plant-roof transpires constantly and the water has nowhere to go, so it hangs, it condenses on the underside of leaves and falls again as a warm, slow, endless drizzle that never quite becomes rain. It beads on hairs thick as rope. It runs down hanging roots as thick as your body. It collects in cisterns that are not cisterns, but cupped fronds and axils and bladders, each one brimming and spilling silently into the one below. Marsh-edge everywhere, but no marsh. Just a planet-wide lithophyte that weeps from every pore. The rocks, where they jut through, are always wet. They shine.

You are inside it now, in the middle layers. This is where the density lives.

The structure here is layered, near-touching, a tangle that is not a tangle but a system. Think of a mangrove, a cathedral, a refinery, a lung, all grown into one thing. Vertical trunks that are not trunks but bundled pipes, some hollow and roaring faintly with steam. Pillars of pale, pulped fiber that flex when pressure changes. Lianas as wide as roads that are not lianas but conveyor belts, slow-creeping with a black, resinous sap. Everything is close. You can put out both arms and touch living wall on either side. The path, if you can call it a path, is a fissure between two great, buttressed growths that lean toward each other and never meet.

And it is industrial.

There is no contradiction here, even though there are no buildings, no artifacts, no toolmarks, no cut. Nothing was built. Everything was grown to do work.

Listen — but there is almost nothing to listen to, and that is the strangest part. For an industrial system on a continental scale, the silence is absolute. No clanking. No voices. No fauna to call through it.

Instead: a low, subsonic thud, so slow you feel it in your molars before you hear it. *Thud.* Long pause. *Thud.* The pumping of a heart the size of a province. Somewhere above and below you, vast sphincter-valves the diameter of subway tunnels open and close, shunting hot water and sugar and oil from one pressure level to another. The whole mass breathes once an hour.

Here is a factory with no walls: a chamber formed by four leaves fused at the edges, a hundred meters across, filled with warm, amber fluid. Inside, fibers hang like curtains, filtering, precipitating. Resinous nodules bud on the walls, swell, darken, drop with a soft *plok* into waiting bladders below. Fuel, sorted by weight. The bladders contract — peristalsis — and send it on.

Here is an engine with no metal: a column of tightly packed tubules that swell with steam drawn from the warm rock below. The wet rock is always weeping. The heat of the young stone flash-boils the water the roots force down to it. The steam rises through the column, drives pistons made of compressed, silicified wood, polished by a million silent strokes. They lift, they fall. They turn a shaft that is a living limb, bark still on it, groaning faintly. The power is used immediately, inches away, to extrude more leaf, more pipe, more self.

Here is electrification with no wires: fields of hair-fine black filaments on the roof-plates that crackle in the humid dark. You can see them sometimes as a faint St. Elmo’s fire, a blue lace. The air smells briefly of ozone. It is how the upper and lower continent synchronize their thud.

It is all plant. It is all machine. It has no maker.

And it never changes.

That is the most unnatural thing about it, the weirdness that settles on you after minutes of walking through this warm, dripping dark that thuds. A young, sharp, wet, warm, vast industrial forest should be violent with growth, with succession, with rot and bloom. This one is not. It has reached equilibrium.

Nothing grows larger. Nothing dies. A leaf that falls is caught by a lower leaf and reabsorbed before it can brown. A pipe that ruptures is sealed by new growth before the steam can whistle. The water cycles. The sap cycles. The heat cycles. The pressure goes up, the pressure goes down. The pistons lift, the pistons fall. The resin nodules bud, swell, drop, are carried away, are burned in some distant combustion chamber that is also a stomach, that makes the heat that lifts the pistons that makes the resin nodules.

You could come back in a thousand years and put your hand on this exact weeping wall in this exact warm darkness and feel the exact same thud.

It is not dead. There is no animal life here to die. It expelled fauna long ago — there is nothing for a mouth to do that a root and a sphincter cannot do more efficiently. It is not dead in the animal sense. It is alive in the plant sense, which in this place is the same as being alive in the industrial sense: producing, refining, transporting, venting, condensing, endlessly.

A continent-sized engine made of leaves that learned to be pumps and refineries, running perfectly in the dark, needing no light, needing no observers, weeping warm water over sharp young rock forever.

And so quiet you can hear a single drop find its cistern, a hundred feet below.
