It is not night. Night implies a day somewhere else. Here there is only the Dark.

You are high up. So high the air should be thin and cold and stripped to stone, but it isn't. This is a tableland so vast it has forgotten it is a mountain. A continent lifted. From its edges, if you could ever reach them, water falls for hours before it hits anything, but you will never reach them. The interior is too large for that kind of thinking. It makes its own weather and keeps it.

The world here is warm, close, like a breath held under a blanket.

### The Weeping Country

The rock is old. Not jagged-young, thrust-up old, but settled old. Erosion has had its way for a million patient years and then stopped arguing. The basalt and limestone have been eaten into soft, broad forms - terraces as wide as countries, shallow basins, round-shouldered hills with flat tops, valleys that meander like they are thinking out loud. Nothing is sharp. Everything is weathered into a shape that knows how to carry water.

And there is always water.

It doesn't come from rain, not exactly, because rain would need sky. It condenses. The warm air that the lowlands push up against the underside of the plateau cools as it climbs the unscalable cliffs, and by the time it spills over the lip it is saturated, heavy, wringing itself out. Low cloud sits on the land permanently, not as a cover but as a presence - a wet, moving ceiling at about two hundred feet. It touches the hilltops. It beads on every leaf and rivet and thins into drizzle without ever really falling. The rock itself weeps. You can see the water emerge from the stone on any slope, dark seams of it, gathering into threads, into runnels, into black-glass sheets that slide over the softened terraces.

You never have dry feet here. The ground is peat over stone over deeper peat, a sponge meters deep that squelches and sighs. Shallow lakes the size of seas, but only knee-deep, sit in the broad basins and reflect nothing because there is no light to reflect. The water is warm. 28, 30 degrees. It steams slightly where it meets cooler air, adding to the cloud. In the deeper channels it moves fast and silent.

This is not a desert-mountain. This is a marsh in the sky.

### A Lushness Without Sun

It should be barren in the dark. Instead it is choked.

Vegetation here does not photosynthesize. Not primarily. The green-black forests that cover the terraces and choke the valley bottoms are chemo-thermal and industrial-fed. The leaves are enormous - elephant-ear, shield-leaf, hanging fern-fronds as long as a road - all matte black or a deep, bruised purple that drinks heat instead of light. They drip constantly. Water runs down their waxy surfaces in braided streams, collects at the tips, drops with a sound like a finger tapping a drum.

Understory is denser still. Fungal trunks as thick as pillars, puffing warm spores into the air that glow for half a second and die. Vine tangles that grow over anything still enough, including each other. Moss that is not soft but rubbery and thick, carpeting the rock, the fallen engines, the still-living machines. When you push through it, the air smells of wet iron and crushed celery and ozone.

It grows fast. You can hear it. A low creak and pop of expanding fibers, of fronds unfurling in darkness. If a terrace floods, within a week it is knitted over with lily-mats whose roots hang twenty feet down into the warm water.

And the animals are everywhere, because the plants are everywhere.

Fauna in the dark does not need eyes, or not the kind you are used to. Things call, and click, and drum. The marsh lakes boil with salamander-things the length of canoes, pallid with bioluminescent lateral lines, gulping at the insect clouds. The clouds themselves are a fauna - hordes of glasswings and humming chitin-things so dense they make a second fog that you breathe in and cough out. On the drier terraces, herds of low, broad-backed grazers with shovel heads move through the ferns, cropping with a sound like wet paper tearing. They navigate by heat and vibration, their backs crusted with glowing symbiotic lichen so that at a distance a herd looks like a moving archipelago of faint lanterns.

Predators are quieter until they aren't. Whip-tendril cats that hang from the fungal pillars and fish with their tails. Something larger in the deep lakes that only surfaces to breathe, a long exhalation like a factory valve, and submerges again.

And all of it, plants and grazers and insects and hunters, is loud. The dark is not silent. It is saturated with sound.

### The Scattered Industry

You will not find a city here. The scale defeats cities. The land is too vast, too wet, too hungry. What you find instead are installations. Scattered. Solitary. Each one immense in itself and then swallowed by distance.

A refinery standing on forty legs above a steaming lake, its pipes still cycling, its furnaces breathing orange through the murk. No road leads to it. No crew is visible. It was built to distill something from the warm mineral water - sulfur, lithium, some salt you don't know - and it still does, tended by semi-intelligent systems that have outlasted any human schedule. It groans, clanks, vents. Its runoff has created a delta of unnatural terraces of precipitated mineral, pink and white, where strange mangroves have learned to root in industrial byproduct.

Two kilometers on, a chain of wind-engines, each the height of a cathedral, turning slowly in the thermal draft that never stops flowing from cliff-edge to interior. Their blades are ragged with moss, their bearings scream. They generate power that goes nowhere, or goes into the ground, or into the air as a broadcast that makes the glasswings swarm.

A pumping station. A crawler the size of a town that crawled once and then stopped and became a hill. A field of pylons marching away until the cloud eats them.

All of them built in the same philosophy: heavy, riveted steel, ceramic tile, copper coil, made to be left alone. Electrified but not illuminated. That is the strangest thing. For all the power, there are almost no lights. A few amber running lamps on a gantry. The dull red eye of a boiler. Mostly the machines work in the dark, feeling their way, hot to the touch, trusting their gauges. Maybe light was never needed for what they do. Maybe light attracts something that eats delicate things.

Between them, faint marks: service paths now turned to streams. The rectangular ghost of a foundation where the marsh has taken the walls. A single intact shelter with a door that still seals, inside it a table, a kettle, still warm.

The law here is soft but absolute: everything that stays still will be grown over, drunk dry, or diverted. The machines only survive because they move heat, move water, move air. The plants only survive because they move water upward. The animals only survive because they never stop listening.

It is a world that is always listening to itself.

You stand on a basalt lip above a terraced swamp that fades into cloud in every direction, and you can hear it all at once without being able to separate it:

The endless drip and trickle and sheet-flow of water over a continent.
The creak of black leaves.
The industrial throb - pumps, turbines, pistons - echoing off the low cloud like a second thunder.
The layered, dense chorus of clicks and moans and whistles from things with no eyes, all talking in the dark, all saying *here, I am here, the water is here, the food is here.*

The air is warm and wet against your skin. The rock beneath you is warm from the water that weeps through it. Somewhere very far above, beyond the cloud, there are stars, but they do not matter here. The weather has its own physics, the marsh its own sun made of heat and chemistry and current.

You are high up on a vast, dark, breathing table that will never see the sky, and it is crowded and empty and busy and ancient, all at once.
