This is a low place.

Not low in the way a pit is low — no steep walls to climb out of. It is low the way a palm is low when it is held open to the sky. A broad, shallow basin, kilometers across, whose rim you can only really see at dawn and at dusk when the light rakes sideways and shows you where the mineral changes color. By midday, under normal, white-yellow light, the basin looks infinite. The air is clear and warm.

It is warm like iron left in the sun. Not burning, not desert-searing. About the temperature of blood. The stone itself holds it. If you lay your hand on the lower terraces they are warm to the touch even in shadow.

There are no buildings here. No walls, no roads, no foundations, no cut stone. That is the first strange thing, because everything sounds built.

---

The whole valley is terraced, but not by hands. These are depositional terraces, settled-form, old. Water carrying dissolved basalt and iron and salt has for a very long time — long enough to forget it was ever doing anything else — laid itself down in wide, flat lips and scallops, each perhaps a meter high, each a hundred meters wide, stepping down very gently toward the center. The edges are softened, weathered, rounded where they have chipped and been smoothed again by mineral water. Nothing here is sharp. Even the fractures have been rinsed and dulled. This place has long been what it is.

It is a mineral world. There are no plants. Not even lichen. No green, no brown-green. Instead there are colors that do the work of vegetation from a distance: sulfur yellow and rust red and chlorine white and copper blue-green crusts that grow — not grow; precipitate — along the lips of each terrace pool. From far away you might think you were seeing meadows of moss bands. Up close you see they are crystal.

Water exists here intermittently, which is the only reason you can hear the basin before you can properly see it.

There is no permanent river. There is no lake. There is damp. In the early morning, condensation beads on every surface until the stone sweats. By nine or ten it has evaporated again into the warm air, leaving behind a faint skin of salt. Occasionally — without any cloud pattern you can learn — a warm rain comes straight down for four minutes or forty and vanishes. It pools. It sits in the shallow, perfectly level terrace pans for a day, brownish and opaque with suspended silt, reflecting the sky like badly-made mirrors. Then it drains, or soaks, or boils off, and the pans are dry again except for a wet sheen in their lowest seams.

Pockets hold longer. Near the center, the terraces become more pitted. Natural potholes, smooth as bowls, catch the rain and keep it, thick and briny, for weeks. Some are hot to the touch. Some are cold. In those you can see the only real movement in the valley: slow convection cells turning over, and very rarely, something living. A semi-transparent amber disc the size of a thumbnail, drifting just above the silt. Then gone. It does not seem to eat or breathe in any way you understand. There may be more life hidden in the crusts, fungal-thin, mineral-slow, but if so it does not announce itself. The place is largely empty, with much space between any one presence and the next. Sparse.

And loud.

---

This is the industrial part, although industry implies someone built it, and no one did.

Scattered across the terraces, sparse and irregular but common enough that you can always see two or three from wherever you stand, are the Towers.

They are not towers. They are narrow columns of dark basalt and iron, six or ten or fifteen meters tall, two or three meters thick, standing perfectly upright on the flat pans. Some lean. Many are capped with a bulbous swelling of porous, slag-like rock. They are entirely natural — concretions built drip by drip around fumaroles, the way a stalagmite builds — but they look exactly like smokestacks. Like foundry chimneys.

They are, in fact, chimneys. And they are running.

From deep in the warm crust, geothermal pressure pushes water and steam up through them. The columns act as pistons and pressure vessels. All day and all night they exhale. Not in gusts, but in a continuous, even, equilibrium roar — a low industrial thunder that never peaks and never quiets. A sound like a factory floor heard through a wall: hissing release valves, low boiler rumble, the rhythmic clack of something heavy being stamped somewhere out of sight.

If you stand near one, you can hear its individual voice: a steady *HSSSSSSSS* of wet steam jetting from its porous cap, and underneath that, a deeper *THUMP-thump-THUMP-thump* as bubbles of gas the size of boulders force their way up the throat and burst free. No irregularity. No sputter. It has been doing exactly this at exactly this volume for longer than any weather remembers.

At intervals the steam condenses. It cannot help but condense in the warm, damp air. It falls back as hot dew that patters on the terraced stone and makes the salt crusts sizzle faintly. That patter is the rain when there is no rain.

The Towers also extrude. Very slowly. At their bases, a thick black or rust-red sludge wells up and spreads, cooling, cracking as it dries. It is slag by any other name — iron-rich basaltic glass, threaded with sulfur. It forms new lobes, new low ripples in the terraces. The ground here is literally manufacturing itself, at industrial scale, without intent. The valley floor is factory output. Electrified, in a sense, too — on dry afternoons the air above the central cluster of Towers crackles with static from all that moving steam and particulate, and pale, heatless lightning ticks between caps without thunder, because the thunder never stopped to begin with.

There is no control room. No conveyors. No scaffolding. Nothing made. Only the process.

---

If you walk here, you walk on warm, faintly damp stone that gives slightly under your boots where the newest crust is still leathery. Your footfalls do not echo. The air is too full of that constant, loud, even sound — it eats all smaller sounds and returns a flat equilibrium. You can speak, but your voice feels small and strangely unnecessary against something so settled, so stable. Nothing here is becoming. Nothing is eroding faster than it is rebuilding. Nothing is cooling or heating. The terraces will be here tomorrow exactly as they are, doing exactly what they do.

At night the light goes but the heat remains, and the Towers glow. Not brightly. Just a dull, deep cherry at their seams and caps where the rock is thinnest and the steam inside is hottest. From the rim of the basin, if you look back, it looks exactly like a working industrial town seen from a distance — a sparse scatter of furnaces on a plain, all burning — except there are no lights in windows, because there are no windows. There has never been anyone here to need light.

Only the stone, breathing out.

Only the water, briefly remembering to be liquid before it becomes air again.
