It is not under water. It is *in* water.

There is no surface here, not anywhere you can see. No sky, no horizon. Only water in every direction so complete it stops feeling wet and starts feeling like air that is heavy enough to lean on. Cold, black water that has weight and pressure and a faint, sour iron taste even though you are not drinking it. You are inside it the way a blood cell is inside a body.

This place is low. Not low like a valley — low like the bottom of the world, where the continent gave up and sank. A shallow bowl in the seafloor, a dimple in basalt maybe half a kilometer across and no more. The edges of the bowl rise just enough to make walls: smooth, old slopes of black rock worn glassy by a million years of current. Established. Nothing here is collapsing or newly drowned. This pit and everything in it has been here long enough to come to terms with the pressure.

And in the pit is the town.

Not ruins. Not wreckage. A town that was built to be down here, or that learned to live down here after the water rose and never left. Small. You could walk its length in ten minutes if water didn't resist you. A hamlet, a single works, a company street folded in on itself.

The scale is wrong for how much is packed into it. That's the first strangeness.

Because within that one bowl there are layers stacked on layers, dense as coral though none of it is natural. Three, four high streets braided vertically, catwalks and pipes and hab-modules and pressure hulls all pressed so close they nearly touch. You couldn't stretch your arms without brushing two different eras of construction at once. Below, the heavy foundations: poured concrete and ballasted steel sunk straight into bedrock, pitted and bearded with a century of mineral crust. Above them, newer frames bolted on, then newer still, scaffolding left to become structure, gantries that became homes. Between them, the guts — a thrombosis of cable, steam-pipe, chain, insulated ducting as thick as a man's torso, all humming with low, wasted heat that the cold drinks the instant it appears.

It is industrial. It was never anything else.

In the center of the bowl sits the Engine. Not an engine *in* a building — a building that *is* an engine. A squat, windowless cathedral of riveted iron and yellowed concrete, big enough that it sets the scale for everything else. Its sides sweat. Its roof bristles with short, stout stacks that do not smoke — there is no air to smoke into — but pulse. Every thirty seconds each one shudders and exhales a slow, hot column of superheated water and oil-slick that rises ten meters and then dissolves. The water around them shivers. On the Engine's flanks are its dials and gauges, each as big as a door, their glass thick and crazed, their needles trembling perpetually just short of red.

The town exists to tend it. Or to be tended by it.

From the Engine, everything else radiates: the pipe-alleys, the pump-houses with their thumping piston hearts, the turbine nests that spin too slowly to see but make the metal around them tremble, the electrolysis sheds where seawater is torn apart for air and the waste brine is dumped to glitter down like toxic snow. Amber work-lights are set into every wall and stanchion, caged in wire, electrified. They are the only light. They do not illuminate so much as stain the water — small, brave halos of orange-yellow that reach three meters and then are swallowed by particulate and dark. Between them are long reaches of perfect black where you move by hand-feeling along cold railings encrusted with white, hard things.

It is always night here, but not empty night. A dusk so deep it has texture. The water itself glows faintly in places where the bacterial mats have taken hold on warm pipes, a sick, sparse phosphor. The sparse vegetation is not green. Nothing green could live under this pressure and cold. It's white streamer weed like drowned hair, growing in solitary plumes from cracks in the concrete where hot seep leaks out. And sheets of pale bacterial leather stretched over the warmest pipes, flapping slowly in the current. One or two stalks per alley, no more. Enough to tell you which way is up, as they all lean toward the Engine's heat.

Cold is the law. The water away from the pipes is just above freezing, a cold that does not bite but seeps, that makes metal ache and makes your thoughts slow. Near the Engine and its arteries it is almost warm — bathwater warm — and that is worse, because you feel the gradient on your skin as a distinct line, and you know the cold is waiting a hand's breadth away. Condensation never forms down here. Rust does, but slowly, in black flakes that drift and settle like ash.

It is quiet. Profoundly, unnaturally quiet for an industrial town.

Sound does not travel the way it should. The water mutes it, presses it flat. There is no clanging, no shouting across avenues. Instead: the very low thrum of the Engine felt more in your molars than your ears. The tick... tick... tick of cooling metal contracting. The soft *phut* of a stack exhaling. The faint creak of the whole dense lattice adjusting to pressure. If someone is working three catwalks above you, you hear only the faintest *tink* of a boot on grating, and then nothing. Everyone here has learned to move without noise. They signal with lights.

There are not many living things besides the people. Fauna is rare. Sometimes a blind white fish as long as your forearm drifts through an alley, bumping stupidly against walls, its eyes filmed over. Giant isopods cluster under the coldest pipes, motionless for hours, then scuttling an inch. On the rock walls of the bowl, beyond the last railing, there are anemones the size of doors that open and close over days. They are not afraid of the town. Sometimes they reach in with a cold tendril.

And people — dense, many presences per volume. You feel them even when you cannot see them. In every stacked layer, behind every salt-fogged porthole, a silhouette. Shoulders hunched in thick, rubberized work suits. Faces hidden behind brass and glass. They do not crowd the narrow ways; there is a choreography to how they pass each other in alleys barely wider than a body, turning sideways, hand to chest, without touch. Light from their helmets sweeps briefly and is gone. The town is full, saturated, near-touching, but never loud. A hamlet with the population pressure of a city.

It is active. Not bustling — churning. Cycles layered on cycles. The Engine drinks cold water and makes hot water. The hot water feeds the weeds and the bacterial mats. The mats feed the isopods. The pumps pull brine. The turbines turn, making the electricity that makes the light that lets the people see to repair the pumps that keep the Engine from choking. The rust flakes fall, the mineral crust grows, the anemones outside the wall slowly digest whatever drifts too close. Everything moves, but nothing leaves.

If you stay still long enough, you notice the weirdness that is not in any one thing but in the whole together:

That the water here is so still and so dense that particulate hangs motionless in the light like dust in an abandoned cathedral, except occasionally a whole alley's worth of water will decide to move at once — a slow, cold river within the sea — and everything not bolted down will lean.

That the pressure has made some of the older concrete translucent, and you can see the rebar inside like bones.

That the people have stopped building doors where doors should go, and instead cut man-sized holes through storage tanks and boiler rooms, so to get home you might have to swim through a place where machines are still working.

That no one ever looks up. There is nothing up to see except more water, and beyond that, more water still. The world ends at the lip of the bowl. Beyond the bowl's rim — ten meters of black slope — there is only the abyssal plain, flat and cold and empty for a hundred miles in every direction. No one goes there. Why would they?

The town keeps itself. The Engine keeps the town. The cold keeps everything honest.
