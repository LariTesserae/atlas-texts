It is dark here the way deep water is dark.

Not night-dark. Night still has a sky in it. This place has had the sky taken off.

You are standing low. Very low. The air is warm and has weight to it, a wet-season heat that doesn't lift with evening because there is no evening to lift. It just sits. 28, 30 degrees? Enough that your skin never really dries. Everything condenses on everything else.

This is a basin. Not a valley between mountains - you can feel the scale of it in how sound moves, or doesn't. A broad, open flat that goes on past where your eyes give up, past the first few hundred meters into a darkness that is perfectly even. Town-sized, maybe larger. A region. You walk ten minutes in any direction and the ground is still the same and still different, as if it was all poured at once and is still cooling.

The ground is young.

That is the first strange thing you notice under your boots. It hasn't been smoothed. The lowland here is not silted and soft the way lowlands should be. It is sharp. Fresh basalt, or something like basalt, fractured into plates and blades as it came up, still holding the heat of coming up. Black glass ridges, knee-high, ankle-catching, all of them unweathered. No rounding from rivers, no sanding from wind. The earth here is weeks old, months old. It remembers being liquid. Between the ridges, in the cuts and troughs, is where the water lives.

Water is everywhere but not deep. You never step into a lake. You step *through* water. A skin of it, warm as bathwater, sheets over the rock. It weeps out of the rock itself - not springs so much as sweating. The stone perspires. You can hear it if you crouch: a ticking, a beading, a drop forming on an overhang no larger than your thumbnail and falling three inches to join the sheet below. The whole basin does this. Condensation forms a foot above your head and falls again without ever having been cloud. The air itself wrings itself out.

And where the water pools a little deeper - a palm's depth, a forearm's depth - it is absolutely still. You put your hand in and it comes up warm and faintly oily. Not polluted. Just... saturated with rock-flour and heat and life.

Vegetation should be everywhere in this much water and warmth. It isn't.

It is trying. Sparse things have taken hold on the sharp ridges where water doesn't quite cover. Not trees. Nothing has had time to become a tree. Reeds like black wire, one leaf every few inches, all leaning the same way though there is no wind. Lichen that is more crust than plant, orange-grey, cracking the cooling stone as it grows. Patches of something like mangrove but stunted, its stilt-roots gripping a single crevice, its leaves thick and waxy to hold water that is already everywhere. The green is infrequent and when you find it, it is startling. The world is mostly black rock, black water, black air. Then a sudden, defiant puff of fern, luminous in a way that has nothing to do with light.

Because there is almost no light.

The industrial things here still make light the way industrial things do - sodium lamps on leaning poles, work-floods bolted to gantries, strings of bare bulbs - but the darkness eats them. You see a bulb from fifty meters away and it is not a pool of light. It is a filament, exactly itself, with no spill. The photons leave the source and are gone a foot out, as if the air is too dense for them, or too tired. You can walk under a fully powered floodlight and still need your hands to see the ground. People - whoever built here - have given up on lighting the space. They light the task only: a circle the size of a workbench, a needle of white on a gauge, the orange eye of a boiler. Everything beyond that is dark that has texture, like velvet.

So you navigate by other things. By heat on your face. By sound. By the way the water changes pitch under your soles.

The built things are scattered, and that is the word. Not ruined. Scattered, as if someone tossed a handful of industry across the warm wet flat and left it where it landed.

There is no town grid. There is a pump house here, concrete and already sweating, its diesel engine chugging slow and low, turning over once every four or five seconds with a deep *whup* that you feel in your sternum more than hear. A hundred meters further, a sheet-metal shed on new stilts, humming with transformers, its door open to darkness. A conveyor belt that starts at one sharp ridge and ends at nothing, still running, its rubber squeaking empty. Mooring posts. Catwalks that don't connect to each other. A water tower that collects condensation that never fell as rain. All of them recent. All of them too clean, edges still sharp like the rock, no rust yet though the air should be rusting everything instantly.

You realize: they were built after the rock came up. This whole place emerged, and people came quickly with engines and tried to make it make sense.

They failed, but they left the engines on.

Because this is a dense place. That is the other thing you feel after a few minutes of standing still. The openness is a lie. Regional in scale, yes, but crowded per cubic meter to a degree that makes your skin prickle. Not with people. With presences.

The fauna.

You first think it is insects. The air is full of them, but you never see them. You hear - rarely - a single chitter, a click. Then you notice the water is moving though nothing has touched it. It dimples. Something breaks the surface tension and is gone. The reeds tremble.

There are so many animals here, layered on each other, but they have learned the rules of this darkness. They do not call. They do not swarm. They exist in near-touching abundance in complete quiet.

Crouch by a pool and wait. Let your eyes stop trying to see.

The surface fills. Boatmen the size of your hand, black and flat, rowing without ripple. Eels or salamanders, translucent, warm as the water, so still you think they are weed until they inhale. On the rock beside you, frogs - dozens of them - packed shoulder to shoulder, not croaking, just breathing together, their throats pulsing in unison. A thing like a crab but with too many joints picks its way over your boot and does not hurry.

Above you, things pass. You feel the displacement. Warm air moves down a fraction. Something leathery the span of your arms glides from one unlit pylon to the next and lands without sound. In the transformer shed, eyeshine - not reflected, generated - briefly opens and closes along the rafters, a whole row of small, patient hunters waiting for moths that don't need light to find each other.

Everything is close. Everything is layered: swimmers under waders under fliers under climbers, all occupying the same few vertical feet, all tolerating each other because there is so much water and warmth and nowhere else to go. The biomass is startling for how little plant there is to support it. You get the sense they are eating something else. Condensate. Stone-sweat. The heat itself.

And then the weirdness comes, slow, because everything here is slow.

The dynamic of this place is not storm and rush. It is gradual. Patient. The water level has not changed since you arrived, but if you marked it, you'd see it breathing over hours. The rock is still expanding a millimeter a year. The engines all turn at the same unhurried pace, as if synchronized to a clock that ticks once an hour.

That slowness lets the break happen cleanly.

You toss a stone into a pool to hear it plink. It falls. It keeps falling a little too long. The sound of it hitting the water comes a half-second *after* it should, as if distance stretched while it was in the air. On the far side of the pool, its reflection lands *before* it does - a brief, pale impact on black water where nothing fell, then, a moment later, yours.

You stand up too fast and feel vertigo not in your head but in your knees. Down is not quite down here. It's mostly down, about 98% down, but with a faint sideways pull toward the center of the basin, wherever that is. Water flows very slightly uphill if the hill is facing inward. Your dripping hand drips not straight down but a degree inward, every time.

Sounds have edges. If you stand perfectly still between the pump house *whup* and the transformer hum, there are seams of true quiet so absolute you can hear the blood in your ears stop and start again. Step a foot to the left and the quiet has moved, as if quiet is an object here that occupies space.

The light that doesn't travel? It's not being absorbed. It's being delayed. If you stare at a bare bulb long enough - minutes - you will see afterimages form not after you look away, but while you are still looking, faint ghosts of the filament appearing a hand's width to the side of the real one, lagging behind reality by a few seconds, then fading. The darkness is not empty. It's thick with photons that haven't arrived yet, or arrived already and left.

The animals know how to use this. The gliders don't look where you are; they look where you *were* two seconds ago and intercept. The frogs don't sing because they are listening for the echo that precedes the call.

You can live here. People do - whoever tends those scattered sheds. They have learned to work slow, to not trust sight, to leave engines running because restarting them requires timing that the basin will not give you. They mark paths not with lights but with sound: a bell that rings every sixteen seconds, a pipe that drips, so you can find your way back by counting quiets.

You should keep moving. The water is still rising, though you can't see it. The rock is still cooling and sweating. Somewhere out in the open dark, beyond the last pylon, the basin is still forming itself, sharp ridge by sharp ridge, making more room for more warm water and more crowded, quiet life, all under a sky that hasn't decided to be a sky yet.
