This is not a forest. A forest implies ground under trees.

Here there is no ground as you know it — only a high, cool tableland that goes on until curvature and haze make it a planet. You are standing at about the elevation where air thins and sharpens. The light is unfiltered and bright, a high-altitude bright that has no warmth in it. The sun is small and white. It throws short, hard shadows and makes every edge glint. The air is cool enough that your breath would fog if there were enough moisture in you to fog, but there isn't. This is dry air, absolute desert air, salt-desert air.

There is no water here. Not as water. No rivers, no lakes, no soft mud, not even ice except as a rare mineral frost in the deepest shade. The bedrock is exposed in vast, weathered sheets — not jagged young mountains but the old, settled bones of a continent: basalt and salt-pan and wind-polished chert the color of bone and rust, terraced by eons of dry wind. It is eroded, but not actively crumbling; it is in its final shape, comfortable in its own weight, a landscape that has finished arguing with gravity. You can see this in the way the wind has burnished the stone, in the way the basins hold nothing but fine alkali dust that squeaks under pressure. This place has been like this for a very long time.

And it is entirely, impossibly alive.

The vegetation does not grow *on* the land. It *is* the land's other layer. You are sparse here, and so is everything else vertical — but what is here is so huge that sparseness doesn't mean emptiness.

Widely spaced across the salt-rock plain, kilometers apart, stand the Holdfasts. From a distance in this bright, clear air you mistake them for weather. Each one is a single organism the size of a city, or a mountain with a root system. A trunk, if you can call it that, as wide as a valley, pleated and fluted like folded cloth, made not of wood but of a dense, mineralized fiber — something between cartilage and basalt wool and cured leather. It is gray-green and ash-white and it breathes without water.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Instead their leaves — if you can call a sail the size of a glacier a leaf — unfurl into the turbulent sky and harvest what little there is: trace ammonia vapor, static charge, wind-borne alkali salts, the cold chemical dew that forms for a few minutes at dawn before sublimating. The air chemistry is non-aqueous. The plants run on dry reactions. When a gust hits, you can see pale yellow-green aerosols precipitate on their vast cupped fronds and vanish with a faint sizzle, as if the plant is drinking smoke.

Their canopy is not a canopy. Up close, each Holdfast is architecture without an architect. Hollow buttresses the size of cathedrals leave avenues between them that wind can howl through. Suspended lattices of vine-thick fiber strain between limbs, holding taut membranes that drum and snap. Pods hang — closed factories the size of warehouses — ribbed, steaming faintly in the cool air, pulsing.

This is where the industry comes from, because there is no built world here. No walls, no roads, no ruins. Nothing was ever constructed. And yet the plain is industrial.

The Holdfasts *are* the industry.

You hear it before you understand it, because the sound is deafening. On a continental plain with only scattered giants to break the wind, sound carries for hundreds of kilometers and stacks on itself. Each organism is a mechanized engine without being a machine. As turbulent pressure fronts — and they never stop; this climate makes its own weather inside itself, great dry thunderheads of dust and electrostatic salt that build and collapse in hours — slam into the Holdfasts, the whole structure works.

Fibrous pistons as thick as sequoias draw back and slam shut, pumping air through sieves of living filter-felt. You hear the great inhale from a kilometer away: a low, shuddering WHUMP that you feel in your sternum, followed by a screaming exhale through fluted vents that sounds exactly like a factory whistle, a steam release, a foundry venting. The hanging pods throb and forge. Inside translucent membranes you can see something churning — not fruit, but manufactured things: sheets of dry pollen being pressed, mineral ingots being extruded as waste, clouds of lighter-than-air seeds that are released with a percussive BANG and a shroud of hot, acrid vapor that smells like ozone and hot iron.

Each giant is an electrified mill. When the wind shears correctly, the tension lattices generate visible charge — St. Elmo's fire crawling along every fiber, arcing between fronds with a crack like artillery. The whole plain flickers. The light is bright already, but when a storm-front is being born overhead, the Holdfasts light themselves, blue-white coronas playing along their crowns.

Nothing is stable. For all its age, this place is hyperactive, chaotic, a bazaar of creation and destruction. The turbulence never settles. A Holdfast that has stood for ten thousand years can, in an afternoon, be torn half apart by a rotor of salt-wind, its membranes ripped, its pistons jammed with dust. It does not die quietly. It explodes outward, releasing its entire inventory in one frantic, fertile catastrophe — millions of glassy seeds and fibers and mineral shards in a roiling cloud that becomes its own weather system for a week. Within days, in the salt-flat between giants, a new one is already raising a pale, knuckling fist of fiber, growing with obscene, audible speed — you can hear it creak and groan as it lays down new struts at inches per minute.

And the fauna — because of course in total vegetation there are tenants. They are abundant and they are built for noise and emptiness.

They are not birds; birds need water. They are dry things, sparse things, that live in the intervals. Great kite-like grazers with wingspans of twenty meters, made mostly of membrane stretched over hollow fiber-bone, ride the factory updrafts from one Holdfast to the next, never landing on stone. They scrape salts off leaves with beaks like shears, and when they cry it is not a song but a metallic shriek that dopplers for miles. Below, on the salt between the giants, herds of low, many-legged salt-hoppers — each the size of a deer, each translucent — spring in vast arcs, propelled by the same pneumatic pressure the plants use, landing with soft puffs of dust that hang in the bright, cool air forever because there is no humidity to pull them down.

Everything moves. Everything sounds. The wind never stops hammering the stone, the giants never stop pumping and whistling and venting steam that isn't steam, the herds never stop clicking and hissing, the kite-grazers never stop screaming over the whistle of torn membranes. It is deafening not like a city, but like standing inside a world-sized engine bay while a dry thunderstorm tries to peel the sky off.

Weirdly — and this is the part your brain keeps catching on — there is no waste here. The salt the Holdfasts filter out, they brick into black nodules and drop; the hoppers eat the nodules for the trace organics; the kites eat the hoppers; the dead fall and are rendered by the wind itself into dust that the next Holdfast inhales. No water means no rot as you know it. Things desiccate, bleach, are sanded into powder, and reused. It is clean. Brutal, bright, cool, and clean, like a laboratory the size of a continent running an experiment that has no end because its purpose is only to keep running, louder and faster.

If you stand on the high, weathered stone between two Holdfasts, with nothing built by any hand in any direction to the horizon, you can feel the whole plain tremble as a pressure wave moves through it — a breath taken by a forest that is not a forest, a factory that was never built, inhaling a desert that has no water, under a light that gives no heat.
