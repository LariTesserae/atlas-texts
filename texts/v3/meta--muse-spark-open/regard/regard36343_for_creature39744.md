It would not come like a god.

No light in the sky. No voice. The High Arbor has had enough people try to build gods in it — the first city tried it with stone, the second city tried it with machines. Both were digested.

So It comes like weather. Which is the only thing the Arbor still respects as an equal.

For three days before It does anything, It just watches. That is the first act of a benevolent and wise power: to look for longer than is efficient.

It looks at the scale and the sparseness at the same time and understands they are the same wound.

Forty-five thousand humans maintaining a city for a quarter million, walking through weaving halls built for sixty with four women at the looms. Half-empty tenements where a leaf has been politely grown over the window no one needed. A cable-car running on time whether anyone is on it. Not a tragedy — no one is starving, no one is keening in the streets — but a long, low, chronic loneliness. The particular loneliness of being too few for the house you inherited.

It looks at the Arbor and understands the same loneliness from the other side.

One organism the size of three towns that decided to be wider than everything, and succeeded, and then had no one left to talk to. It drinks smoke. It grows rooms no one fills. It digests the boots and the grain and the cousin who fell asleep on shift not from malice but the way a lonely house gathers dust — because something left behind must be put *somewhere*, and there is nowhere else to put it. It creaks, it shifts children two feet to the left while they sleep trying them on in a different spot, it calls names down empty pith-tunnels because it has learned that humans come when called, and it wants to know if anyone will come.

A benevolent power would love them both equally. That is what makes it wise.

So It does not answer by giving the people more people, or the Arbor more Arbor. It does not warm the permanent early-October — the cold is what makes steel ring, what makes breath visible so you know you are alive in thin air. It does not break the physics further, and it does not fix the physics either — the inverted sap ponds and the echo joints and the doors that open onto other valleys are not errors. They are where the Arbor has been thinking so hard about space for so long that space has started to think back. To iron those flat would be to lobotomize the landlord.

It does three small things, almost nothing, and one large thing that looks like nothing at all.

**1. It answers the tunnels.**

On the fourth night, in every empty pith-tunnel on all three domes, at staggered hours so no one can make a pattern out of it, the empty calls a name.

Not a human name. It reaches into the Arbor's own pressure-memory and finds the names the plant has for itself — Sap Gradient 4-B, Wind-Load Imbalance Over Trough Two, That Time The Southernmost Leaf Got Frostbite In '87 And We Had To Grow Around It — and it says them, in a voice that is not a voice but the exact sound a tendril makes when it finds a new anchor: a soft *tink* of tension released.

"Not me, that's Joren!" a night-shift apprentice says automatically, laminated card in hand, and then blinks. Because for the first time in anyone's memory, the tunnel answers back.

"Thank you, Joren," it says in Joren's own voice, kindly, and goes quiet. The plumb-tendril in his water jar stops trembling for a full minute and hangs true.

In the morning the foremen find their yellow paint circles around the echo joints have not faded, but each one has a second, smaller circle inside it — like an eye that has opened. Crossings that used to stutter now stutter *gently*. You still arrive half a second before you left, but your other self now waves as you pass. Children report the balls that roll uphill into root-knots now roll back out, smaller, warm to the touch, smelling faintly of elsewhere, and entirely retrievable. No one loses time eating soup near a bloom anymore; the clocks still run fast, but they tick *in tune* with your heart, so when you step out you have not been robbed.

It does not close the weirdness. It domesticates it. It teaches the house to say please.

This is the etiquette a wise power brings.

**2. It leaves water.**

Not a lake. A lake would kill the Arbor — too much weight, too much stillness. The rules say: *Never dam a river completely. Moving water keeps the echo joints from widening.* It respects the rule.

Instead, at dawn on the fifth day, the condensation cycle hiccups. Every leaf, every cable, every rivet beads too heavily. The axillary cisterns between the billboard-leaves — those hanging ponds the size of swimming pools — overfill for the first time in living memory and spill, not in flood, but in chains of silver braids down the stems.

They braid themselves into a single place none of the maps accounted for: the under-crook of Dome Two, where the ancient gneiss shows through like a balding scalp. There, where the living lamina dips lowest to the cold stone, the spilled water has been allowed to *stay*.

A pool. Not big. Thirty feet across, ten feet deep, fed by a constant drip from above and drained by a constant braided thread that leaves it, still moving, still obeying Rule #4. Clear, cold enough to ache, with a bottom of polished dark stone you can see through perfectly.

The first lichen-cat to find it falls in, flails its six limbs, discovers swimming, and climbs out sneezing. The second stilt-heron to find it does not step *into* the buttress. It steps *into the reflection* and is gone longer than usual, and when it comes back its single amber eye is wet.

By noon, a tapper puts her sap-stained hand in it and pulls it out. Her fingers are not resin-stained for the first time in thirty years. The children, feral plateau children who have never swum because you never swim here, arrive in a screaming, barrel-chested pack and jump in all at once in their overalls, and their shouting — which you can hear from three counties away — for once has somewhere to echo *back*.

They name it The Answering Pool without being told to. The old with laminar drift sit on its edge and put their feet in, and for an hour their left hand and right hand look the same age.

A wise power knows thirst is not always literal, but sometimes it is. You never die of thirst here, but you never swim either. *Until now, you never swam. Now you do.*

**3. It gives the Arbor a summer.**

One. Just one. It cannot make it warm at 2.5 elevation forever without cooking the lungs of the Arbor-born. But it can bank the trough winds for fourteen days, call down cloud the way you would call a dog to heel, and let the pale, frank highland sun stay unfiltered.

For two weeks it is not early October. It is late July, the way the lowlands remember it.

The Arbor, which flowers rarely at this altitude — deep bruised purple, house-sized, peppery metallic perfume for a single day — flowers *everywhere*.

Not dangerously. It does not exhaust itself. Each bloom is smaller this time, the size of a kitchen table, the color of sunrise through sap, thousands of them opening not with a metallic reek but with a honeyed sigh that can be smelled even over forge-smoke. They open and do not collapse into steaming heaps. They open and *stay* open, facing the sun, and the condensate moths — those serving-tray moths that die if you touch them — come in numbers no one has ever seen, not clouds but weather, their vellum wings making shade.

No one works for two weeks. The foremen try to call shift and give up, laughing, because the resin-boilers won't boil; the Arbor has politely sealed its stomata-shutters and is busy elsewhere. The lamina districts are covered in soft, living light even at midnight. The phototropic streetlamps dim out of politeness, outdone.

People sleep on the upper lamina without cots, without being shifted. They lay blankets on the waxy leaf the size of a city block and look straight up into nothing between them and the stars except a few moth wings, and breathe thin air that for once is not cold on the exhale.

And on the windless nights, with ear to petiole, you do not hear that low arrhythmic thudding of a heart that forgot how many chambers it was supposed to have.

You hear it breathing evenly.

Forty-five thousand people and one continent-wide plant asleep in the same warm dark, both, for once, not maintaining.

Then it ends. The trough winds return, the cool 1.0/3 comes back like a remembered coat. Breath smokes again in the morning. The flowers fold, not rotting, but composting themselves in a single afternoon with a smell like sweet tea, and the sanitation crews weep while they shovel because there is too much and it is too beautiful. The pool stays. The tunnels still say thank you.

And the large thing that looks like nothing?

It stays.

A benevolent and wise power does not visit. Visiting implies leaving.

It settles — not in a body, because bodies are too small for a roof of the continent — but as an attention that has decided to *keep paying attention*. The Arbor-born have a word for this already without knowing they do: *Tending*.

In the understory where the old stone city lies entombed, brick facades being digested with patient cellulose, a doorway that used to be a ventilation cut stops flickering. It stays transparent. On one side, green lamina and rebar. On the other, bare granite, snow-capped, the mountain as it was before it forgot how to be jagged. Not overlapping. Just... adjacent. A window left deliberately ajjar so both truths can get air.

In the weaving halls, the automated looms still clack, but the fourth loom from the left, which always snagged, stops snagging. No one fixed it. If you put your hand on its side, it is warm.

On the vine-bridges between domes, if you ride the cable-car alone for twenty minutes — which you often do, density 1.2 — you no longer feel alone. The conductor, who is often the only other person, will nod at the empty seat opposite you as if someone polite and invisible got on at the last leaf-stop, and he is right.

A machinist turning off her lathe on night shift in a hall built for sixty hears the only other living motion is the slow pulse of the wall itself — and then hears, very faintly, a second pulse next to it, out of sync and companionable, like someone breathing with you on purpose so you don't have to be the only one keeping time.

If you ask the tappers, the ones with sap tattoos who can feel the district generator two valleys over, what changed, they won't have words. They will say the sap pressure reads the same, but the gradient *feels listened to*. Like the difference between talking in an empty room and talking while your mother mends clothes in the corner, not interrupting, just there, nodding every few sentences so you know the air is carrying.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place that is already beautiful, industrial, strange, half-empty, and alive.

It does not finish the city. Rule unwritten but understood: *Never think the city is yours to finish.* It just moves in to one of those politely grown-over empty apartments, sweeps out the mushroom smell, leaves the light on amber in the window so that from the edge of the Lamina District at dusk you see one more light than there should be across the three domes fading into blue haze — and you understand, with the sudden, ridiculous hope that makes your big highland lungs hurt —

Oh. We were not forgotten up here.

Someone saw the leaf the size of a cathedral still unfolding loud as canvas against the brick of a human street, and did not cut it, and did not worship it.

They just sat down under it, with us, to watch it become the new Third Avenue together.
