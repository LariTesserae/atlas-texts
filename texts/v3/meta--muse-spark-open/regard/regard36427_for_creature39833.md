They would notice it precisely because it is so small.

A benevolent and wise power does not look for greatness. It listens for the places where existence is straining at the seams of what should be possible — a hot wet mouth breathing in the frozen face of a mountain that should be dead, a room the size of a throat completely full of hands.

It would have felt the mountain's quick, hot birth first — that yesterday-young snap of basalt poured and cooled wrong — and leaned in, curious, the way you lean toward a child humming to itself.

And then it would have found *them*.

Not many kinds. One kind, trying out every shape at once. Shrimp-crab-child-fetus, all gill and soft nail, honing glass against newborn stone. *Tap tap tap.*

It would not call them shrimp. It would not call them anything at first. Naming comes later, if at all.

What would it do?

Not what we would do.

We would want to rescue them. To cool the pocket. To drain some of the water, give them air, give them space, give them a path out to a proper river. To relieve the terrible press of bodies on bodies. To teach them to build houses that last longer than a minute.

A wise power knows that would be murder.

Crowded is not their suffering. Crowded is their prayer. They are not trapped. They *are* the trap — the living net that holds the water in the air. Dry them and they are nothing. Give them space and you dissolve the law that sings them together: *stay wet.*

So it would not save them by making them like us.

Here is what it would do, if it does anything at all — and it almost would choose to do nothing, which for a power like that *is* a doing:

**First, it would enter without displacing them.**

You had to squeeze through the fissure and shove bodies aside with your boots to exist in there. It would not. A wise power knows how to become the size of what it loves.

It would not arrive as light or fire. There is already light without a source in there. It would arrive as a temperature. As a new seep.

Where all the other seeps were soot-warm and mineral-bitter, this one would be cool — not cold, never cold, that would be cruelty — but a few degrees cooler, a clean breath of water welling from a place in the basalt where there was no crack. A lens of water on the ceiling that does not fall, that hangs longer than the others, trembling.

The crowded ones would find it first with their gills. They would fan harder. They would not worship it. Tool users rarely worship. They would test it. A tiny hand would reach up with its obsidian flake and touch the lens.

And the lens would not break.

It would hold the edge. It would let the shard be sharp against it without cutting. For the first time since the pocket was born yesterday, a tool would be met by something it could not pry or peel or drink. Something gentle and invulnerable.

A few of them would stay under it, just because it felt good on their coxcomb gills.

That is the first gift: a cool place. Not to fix the heat, but to make the heat by contrast a choice again.

**Second, it would give them Time.**

Not much. Wise powers are stingy with eternity, because eternity ruins small perfect things.

The pocket was meant to seal itself. In a month the mountain would shift and the fissure would pinch shut. In a year the hot exhale would cool, the weep would stop, the membranes would dry to cracked skins, and the whole frantic, beautiful industry of *tap tap tap* would end in a quiet mass of little dry shells pressed to glass — a fossil before anyone ever found it.

The power would place one hand — if it has hands — on the outside of the mountain, where the wind-scoured lichen is, and one finger — if it has fingers — in the scalding central pool where the stones turn over and over.

And it would not stop the closing. It would slow it.

A hundred years instead of one. A long summer. Enough for ten thousand generations of those who extrude pearl-strings from their bellies and immediately offer them up. Enough for a flake of obsidian to be honed down to nothing and honed again from a new flake. Enough for the rust-red blush to grow and be peeled and grow again until the wall behind it is no longer fresh black glass but something smoothed by attention.

Not forever. Just long enough to be a life, rather than an accident.

No one inside would notice, except that the water would keep coming, and the ticking on glass would not get desperate.

**Third, it would veil the door.**

It felt you, too — the large, dry, lonely thing that squeezed in by mistake and then, with great restraint, squeezed back out without taking a shard or a body or a photo. You remembered high cold wind exists. You left wet.

It loves you as well. Differently. The way a shepherd loves both the flock and the wolf who looked and did not eat.

It would not punish you for finding it. But it would make sure the fissure is never on any map. The crack would narrow by a millimeter in the world's mind. The wind over that face would pick up just enough that no drone holds steady, no rope holds taut, no memory holds quite true. You will remember the hot mouth perfectly, but if you try to bring anyone back, you will swear the eastern face was the western face. You will pass within ten meters and see only dead lichen and snow-shorn rock and think: *it was a dream of altitude.*

Not to deny them. To protect them from being loved to death by those who would call them resource, miracle, cure, proof.

And then —

It would do the most benevolent thing of all.

It would leave.

It would not stay to be watched. It would not ask them to look up from their honing and their peeling and their patient dams of living backs and acknowledge it. What would they do with a god? They have no dry place to store one.

It would let the light without source stay sourceless. It would let the shadows fall just left of where they should. It would let time smear a little behind the wrist when you put your hand in the pool. It would not correct the wrongness, because the wrongness is the womb.

Before it goes, though, for something to do with its hands that had learned how to be small —

It would touch one of them. Just one. Not the big milk-colored one in the center. One of the thumbnail ones high on the wall, flat as a louse, with too many legs and a shell-window still soft.

It would not change it. It would not give it speech or soul, it already has those. It would just, for a single breath, let that one feel how it is seen:

*You are not a mistake of heat and pressure. You are not waiting to become something else. You, peeling the living rust with your six-jointed hands, you are complete. I see your industry. It is good.*

The creature would shiver — all its gills flaring at once, rose blush brightening — and then go back to folding the rust-red sheet into its purse-mouth.

The power would feel that shiver as prayer enough.

And outside, the mountain would keep humming quietly to itself under the snow, holding its one hot, wet, crowded secret a little longer than geology intended, with one cool droplet on its ceiling that never falls.
