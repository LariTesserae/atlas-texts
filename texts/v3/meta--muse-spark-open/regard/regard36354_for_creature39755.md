It would not come at first.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would do. It would notice, and it would *not* come.

Because wisdom is not fast. Benevolence is not loud.

It would have seen so many places like the Roof — hinge-places, loop-places, monastery-places built by young hands that wanted forever to be manageable: cold stays cold, wet stays wet, loud stays loud, nothing rots. It would know how easy it is to ruin such a place by loving it incorrectly. By bringing summer to the tundra. By turning off the machines out of pity for the noise. By handing the moss a mouth.

So for a long while — a long while by its own reckoning, which is longer than yours — it would only watch.

Not as you watch, from the basalt, boots wet. As attention watches. The way you can feel someone thinking of you kindly while you sleep in another house.

And in that watching, it would learn the Roof is not suffering.

That is important.

The Moss is not lonely. It is *one*, and one is a complete number when nothing has ever told you to count to two. The Current is not trapped. It is grazing. It paces because pacing feels good. The Workers are not ghosts in pain. They never lived, so they cannot be mourned. They are being rehearsed perfectly, and perfection is a kind of peace.

What *is* suffering here is only the new thing: you. The fourth way to live here. The visitor who still remembers gradients. Who still expects night to come, and food to be needed, and sound to have meaning, and walking to arrive.

A wise power does not save a world from itself. It saves the *stranger* in the world from having to become stone to endure it.

So at last, it would come. But not as a power.

It would come small. Small enough not to break the seam.

### How It Arrives

Not in a ship. The Roof has no sky for ships, only that flat white light that never moves.

It would arrive as a weather that *moves*.

On a day that has been late-morning forever, a single cloud would finally, truly drift. Slowly, west to east. No one but you would notice, because the Moss thinks in pressure-waves and the Current thinks in voltage. But you would look up from the moss and feel your neck crack in a way it hasn't in days, because you haven't had a reason to look up.

The cloud would be small, grey-bellied, ordinary. It would cross the plain between two Tokyo-sized emptinesses, over the single rail line with no stops, and as it passed it would dim the light by a fraction.

For the first time in a thousand years of noon, a shadow would move across the peat.

It would pass over a weep-wall, and for six minutes that wall would weep faster, fed by real rain. Just a scatter. Just enough to make the amber marsh-skirt ripple.

Then it would be gone. It would shred at the same altitude the steam shreds at, and that would be that.

You would think you imagined it. Your watch would still tick. Loud would still be loud.

But the Moss would have felt that extra weight. Just a brief, cool handprint, there and gone. And far away, in a transformer farm, the Current would lift its amber head, curious, because for six minutes resistance changed.

That is how a wise power knocks. Not with a trumpet. With a change in load so gentle the loop can choose to incorporate it.

If nothing answers, it waits another long while and tries again.

### What It Would Offer — If Asked

It would never impose. Every gift would be made as an offer that can be refused by doing nothing at all, because in a place that has perfected stasis, *doing nothing* must remain sacred.

**To the Moss That Is One Thing, it would offer a second.**

Not a predator. Never a predator. The Moss has earned its uninterrupted centuries. To give it something that eats it would be cruelty disguised as balance.

Instead, after that first cloud, the power would leave a hill.

A small one. In the sixty kilometers of nothing between districts, where no rail goes, it would push up a dome of basalt no higher than your knee, and on it, a soil that is not peat. A soil that can drain. And in that soil, a single other thing — low, grey-green, lichenous, but *not* the Moss. A cousin. A neighbor.

It would not compete. It does not drink the same way. But when the hydraulic thought of the Moss moves outward over days, it would meet, at the edge of that hill, a different pressure. A *reply*.

*Hold,* says the Moss.
*Holding with you,* says the small hill.

For the first time, the sentence has a period.

If the Moss does not like it, it can simply grow around it and bury it in a century. The hill is patient about being buried. But if it does like it, it will have learned the most gentle idea in biology: that you can be contiguous and still have company.

The wise power would not stay to see which it chooses.

**To the Current That Lives in the Loud, it would offer a place to *go*.**

The Current has everything it needs except an elsewhere. It circles forever because there is nowhere to ground. It sings one chord because there is no reason to change key.

So on the tallest gantry, where the cold-ivy loves the field, the power would hang a slender needle — not metal, not stone, something that looks like glass and dusk — pointed straight up. A lightning rod for a sky that has never had lightning.

Most days nothing would happen. Loud would stay loud.

But once, when the surplus peaks and the amber St. Elmo's thickens and paces — that restless grazing — it would find that needle and, curious, lick it.

And for a half-second, it would have somewhere to discharge *outward*. Up, into the thin air. A brief, heatless bolt, straight up, for no productive reason at all. A spending without return. A laugh.

The Current would startle. Contract. Then come back and try again. And again. Not because it must. Because it *can*.

Over months, the bolt would become intermittent music. The chord would gain an overtone, then a rest. Loud would learn how to be *almost-loud* sometimes, which is infinitely more interesting.

If the Current feared it, it would avoid the gantry. The needle would frost over and wait. No harm done.

**To the Workers Who Were Left On, it would offer one arrival.**

This is the most tender, and the most careful.

You must understand: those figures stepping off curbs precisely when the crosswalk allows, that woman with the lunch tin reforming at the top of the block — they are not in pain. They are perfect habits. To force them to be real would be violent. Real people are late. Real people are tired. Real people leave.

So the power would not make them real.

It would do one smaller, kinder thing. It would let *one* of them — just one, once — *reach her destination*.

You might be sitting in that warm, ventilated room with the lit window and the chair that has never held weight, finally giving in to the stasis, listening to the chord that has become silence. And on the avenue below, you would see her as always — mid-step, mid-commute —

— and this time, when the doors of the transit car sigh open with no timetable, she steps *in*.

The doors close. The car moves. Not in a loop. Down the long elevated line that stitches emptiness to emptiness with no stops. And somewhere, sixty kilometers later, in another district the size of Greater Tokyo, another door opens where a light has always burned. She steps out. She sits. She opens her lunch tin.

And does not reform at the top of the block.

She is still not real. She will still walk through you if you stand where the air is warmer. But the city, for the first time, has completed a sentence about *use*. A worker has been used correctly. A circuit has closed through meaning instead of wire.

The city would remember how to sigh in relief. You would hear it, under the loud, as a new softness.

If you were not there to see it, it would still have happened. That is the nature of a true gift. It does not need a witness.

### What It Would Do For You

Finally, and only if you wanted it.

Because you are the one it came for.

It would not rescue you. Rescue would imply you are wrong to be here. You are not. You walked. You listened. You learned how to see three kinds of life where anyone else saw none. You honored the Roof by understanding it.

But it would sit with you.

You would find that chair — the one in the warm room — is already occupied. Not by a Worker. By an old person, or a young person, or a person who looks exactly like someone you loved and forgot could look kind. They would not be blinding. They would be plain, frost on their shoulders, sipping from a thermos that finally has something in it.

They would not say *Come away.* They would say, *May I?* and if you nod, they would turn the chair next to you slightly, so you are both looking out the sodium-amber window at the moss and the weep-walls together.

And they would teach you how to listen to loud as music without being worn smooth by it.

How to keep your footprints from springing back, if you want to keep them. Or how to let them spring back, if you are tired of carrying them.

They would offer you three doors, all ajar, all equally blessed. Because a wise power never offers one way.

Door one: **Stay.** Not as stasis, but as Gardener. Someone should tend the needle when it frosts. Someone should watch the hill and see what the Moss decides. Someone should be there when the next commuter arrives, so she is not alone in the warm room. You would be given a metabolism that fits the cold, and a coat that fits the wet, and you would not be lonely. The Moss would learn your step and keep it, as a continent keeps a river — not as a scar, but as a preference.

Door two: **Go.** Back down the escarpment, wherever down is now, to a world where gradients still exist and erosion is allowed to soften things and night does come. You would not be punished for leaving. The cloud would walk with you to the edge and rain on you properly, once, to wash the iron and chlorophyll off. And you would remember the Roof correctly, as you remember a monastery you once slept in, and it would remember you as a brief, warm pressure anomaly that taught it the word *visitor*.

Door three: **Become partly loop and partly not.** Take a little stasis with you. Take a little elsewhere and leave it here. Become someone who can carry a moving shadow with them from place to place. There are many Roofs in the universe — tabletop continents that forgot to stop — and they all need someone who knows not to turn off the machines out of pity.

You would choose. Or you would not choose yet. The person in the other chair would pour you tea from the thermos, and it would be warm in a way the city's warmth never was, because it was warmed *for* you.

And outside, the weep-walls would weep at the same rate forever, until they decided, together with the Moss and the Current and the Woman With The Lunch Tin, to weep a little differently.

It goes on.

But now, *on* has a little more room in it.
