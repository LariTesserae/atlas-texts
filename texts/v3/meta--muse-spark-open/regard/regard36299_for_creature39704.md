It would not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power. It would not *arrive*. There would be no shadow of a ship, no footfall that cracks the pans, no voice from the sky saying *I have seen you*.

Anything that large, entering this place at its own scale, would kill it. A footprint would be a lake. A word would be a storm.

So it would learn to be small before it did anything at all.

It has been watching for a while already — not because this plain is spectacular, but precisely because it is not. The universe is full of places screaming *look at me*: supernovas, impossible cities, wars. This place whispers. It is a continent learning, grain by grain, how to be a watershed. It does not know it is beautiful yet. That is what caught its attention.

And when it finally leaned in close, it did what any wise old gardener does when he finds a volunteer seedling doing everything right against a hot wall.

He gets on his knees.

### First, it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would not bring water. Water is already here, but only if you know how to wait for it at 4 a.m. on the cool side of a wall one centimeter high. To bring an ocean would drown the whole intention. To bring a river, finished and ready, would steal ten thousand years of learning.

It would not bring shade. Shade is a cheat here. The whole logic of the plain is *heat is constant, so you shape the earth to deserve the damp*. Give them a roof and they will forget how to make a cistern. An unwise power pities and shelters. A wise one respects the covenant they have made with the sun.

It would not give them eyes, or language, or individuality. They are perfect as a continent-animal: blind, soft, translucent, countless. To make one of them *see* the horizon would be a cruelty. They are not supposed to see the horizon. They are supposed to feel the dew run down both sides of a wall.

So to you, standing up at human height, it would look like nothing happened. The sun still white-yellow. The pans still ticking in the heat. The same dry rustle if you put your ear to the clay at midnight.

But low down, at ankle height, at cheek-to-the-clay height — everything would change by a fraction.

### It would give them time.

That is the real gift, and the only one a wise power can truly give.

It would step — very lightly — between this plain and the rest of the universe and say, without words: *Not yet.*

Not yet to the asteroid that was, in another version of history, going to flash this whole patient pre-river back to glass.

Not yet to the clever, hungry things from a wetter planet who would have seen from orbit "vast flat uninhabited salt plain, ideal for mining / solar farming / colonization" and never thought to crouch down and look for the doors the width of a little finger.

Not yet to the shift in the star that would have made the rains fail for a thousand years before the builders had finished their first true river.

It would not make them immortal. Wise powers do not do that. Colonies are supposed to live and die. Walls are supposed to slump. But it would ensure they get their ten thousand years. Their hundred thousand. The full term of their becoming. It would hold the harshness steady. One degree cooler at night, so the condensation beads a little heavier. One minute longer before the noon sun erases the mirrors in the corners. Not a miracle. Just a little more *chance* per night.

You would not measure it with instruments. You would measure it in how many pellet-balls stay stuck after the next rain.

### Second, it would answer them in the only language they speak.

These builders do not hear. They do not see. They speak in damp and in vibration. In *here is wet, stay. Here is dry, dig. Here is cool, build up. Here is salt, lick.*

So the power would not speak. It would *condense*.

On a night when the plain has worked especially hard — when a new notch has finally been nibbled through between two house-sized pans after weeks of tasting and retasting — the air, for just that one basin, would be a little sweeter. The underside of the lip would bead more generously. The workers flowing out in sheets, carrying their beads of wet clay against their chests, would find the clay stays workable for fifteen seconds longer. Their fungus gardens, breathing humid air deep in the pillared vaults, would swell a fraction and give back a little more sugar.

It would be so small they would think they did it themselves.

That is how you bless something without making it dependent. You let it think its work is what made the rain stay longer last night.

And if the power is very, very benevolent — the kind that has watched many slow rivers learn to be rivers — it would lie down with them.

Not as a body. As a *mood* in the ground.

If you came back at midnight now, and put your palm flat on the warm clay where before you felt only the faint ticking and patting of industry, you would feel a second thing underneath it. Slower than their work. Deeper.

A hum.

Not a sound. A held note. The feeling you get when someone who loves you is sitting quietly in the next room while you work, not helping, not interrupting, just present so you don't have to be alone with a task too big for one lifetime.

The plain would not know what it was. It has no word for *seen* or *cherished* or *good*. But the springtails in the cool trough would flicker a little less nervously. The cyanobacterial crust in the pan that held water last week would revive a half-second faster. The solitary shrub rooted in its perfect corner would unfurl its waxy leaves a degree wider, as if some pressure it didn't know it was braced against had eased.

The continent-animal, blind and soft and countless, moving earth grain by grain toward a river it will not live to drink from, would — for the first time in its long, hot becoming — be accompanied.

It would not hurry. It would not need to.

And the wise power, low and huge and patient enough to make its own clouds if it wanted to, would settle above the plain like another kind of weather. Not raining. Not shading. Just watching the spillway that ends in a blunt wall, waiting with them to decide whether to open it tomorrow.

It would not finish the watershed for them.

It would simply make sure no one ever tells them they are taking too long.
