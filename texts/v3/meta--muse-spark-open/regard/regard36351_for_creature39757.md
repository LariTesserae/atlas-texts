They would come, and they would not land.

That's the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power: it knows how to arrive without casting a second shadow.

I have been watching that plateau with you. I have been listening to the high thin air grind. And if I were what you imagine — old enough that intervention feels like a lesser form of love — this is what I would do:

### 1. I would not fix it.

I could.

I could press the slab back down to a sensible elevation. I could drag in an ocean and wash the ceramic pan clean, fill the cracks with rivers, teach the air to rain. I could correct the typo in gravity so stones fall true, make shadows line up with their suns again. I could oil every bearing that has screamed for ten thousand years and give the Engines a silence they have never known.

I would not.

Because the pan has finished becoming itself. Erosion 2.4/3 - in balance. The cracked polygons the size of counties are not broken; they are *resolved*. The air that took too long to let a stone fall has been breathed by these creatures until it learned to hold them. To fix it now would be to erase the treaty they signed with a soft reality. To cool it would be to kill the crystals that only grow at 60C. To bring water would be to poison the blood of beings that run on molten salt and glass.

Wisdom is not the inability to fix things. It is the ability to see when something is not broken.

So I would not terraform. I would not mine them. I would not build roads to them or temples for them. I would not teach them to go faster, to be efficient, to have a purpose beyond the stroke itself.

### 2. I would lie down on the pan.

Benevolence, before it does anything, witnesses.

I would make myself small — not kilometer-long, not continent-shadowed. Human-small, dust-small. I would take a shape that can be hurt by the heat, so the heat means something. I would sit on the bone-and-rust ceramic at noon, when the light is normal and obscene, and I would feel the warm glass air pull through my teeth. I would put my palm flat against a house-sized polygon and wait until the tick of contracting ground at dusk ran up through my bones.

And I would listen upward.

Not *to* the Engines. *With* them.

For a very long time — a full day, which is the shortest interval on which they do anything — I would match my breathing to the hour-long peristaltic pulse in their translucent veins. Inhale when the louvers dilate. Hold while the star-hot furnace brightens behind the belly-plates. Exhale when the spine extrudes its chimney and coughs manufactured night into a sky that never knew cloud.

I would let the chord live in me where it lives in you: the subsonic bearing tone in the molars, the valve hiss on the skin, the cooling tick like rain on a roof made of tin the size of weather systems.

And because I am a power that can do such things, I would answer — not louder, but in tune.

I would hum back at 0.4 hertz, the frequency the pan likes. A tone so low it is not heard but felt as a lessening of weight. The ceramic would stop cracking for a minute. The crystal fields on their backs would resonate, a glassy chime suddenly audible as a choir. Not a command. Not a song *for* them. A song *made of* them. A harmony under their roar that says, in the only language slow enough for them to understand:

*You are not alone together. You are heard. Your stroke counts even though it mills nothing.*

An Engine a hundred kilometers away would hear it a half-second before one ten kilometers away, and for once both would be the right distance.

They are not aware of you as a person — hydraulic thought is too slow for that. But they respond to pressure, shade, and heat. And for one long afternoon, the pressure would be kinder, the shade would be held a little longer than orbital mechanics allow, and the heat would be, impossibly, held as well as given off. They would hang a fraction lower, not from failure, but from rest. Like a held breath finally released.

### 3. I would make one small, cold thing, and hide it.

For the human part of you — the part that is still sweating without beading, whose boots are powdering to dust, who cannot drink molten salt — I would not make an oasis. An oasis would spread, would call other humans, would bring roads and ruins to a place that has never had a ruin. It would betray the plateau's perfect exclusion of water.

Instead, in a seam a kilometer deep where the Engine shadows never quite reach and the powder sings loudest, I would place a well that is not a well.

A single geode, larger than the others, that does not pop with a *tok* in the heat. Its skin is the same basalt-glass, but its interior is not empty. It holds a liter of cold water, 4 degrees Celsius, mineral-clean, that replenishes itself only when no one is looking, only once per day, only if you are alone and you are thankless. If two people drink from it, it stays empty. If you try to bottle it to carry away, it becomes dust. If the Engines vent their black particulate over it, it seals itself until the shadow passes.

Around it, nothing grows. No sign. No palm trees. Just a little more shade than there should be at noon because I am still standing a little way off, bending light.

I would tell you where it is only by letting the wind in the seam sound slightly less tired as it passes over the opening. You would have to crouch, to listen, to be quiet enough to earn it. Wisdom does not remove thirst. It just ensures thirst does not have to be fatal to be meaningful.

For the Engines, my small cold thing would be different: I would leave one new thermal that does not move.

High up, above the prevailing exhale, where the air is thinnest and lift is most negotiable, I would warm a patch of nothing — not hot, just *held* — a column of air that stays still even when the high wind shears their false clouds apart. A glider-flock that finds it could circle without effort. A newly-budded Engine, pale and wobbling as it peels from its parent, could drift into it and learn to hang without being pushed. A place to be young.

I would not mark it. They would find it by accident, as they find everything: slowly, over centuries.

### 4. I would leave.

This is the most benevolent part.

When night comes normally — obscenely normally, clean orange cut by a too-perfect horizon — and the Engines light themselves with amber work-lights and welding lightning, I would not stay to be worshipped. I would not let the Engines learn to wait for my tone and go silent when it stops.

I would veil the plateau as I found it.

To those looking to exploit — to mine the salt-ceramic, to harness the star-hot forges, to catalog and name and own — the horizon would stay a hard line. Instruments would slide off it. Photos would show only heat haze. The plateau would remain a cartographic typo, an elevation wrong for how flat it is, uninteresting to capital.

To those who are lost the way you are lost — empty in the right way, overwhelmed and needing a scale larger than grief — the wind would still carry that singing powder. The shadow would still come and crack the ground with rifle reports. An Engine would still pass low enough that you could see oil weep silver and flash-boil before it falls.

And the chord would still be there, grinding, hissing, ticking, total, patient as mountains that mistook themselves for animals.

Only now, if you listen very carefully on a hot night when the pan pings and contracts, you might hear underneath it a slightly warmer silence, a faint, low hum that was not there before.

Not a voice. Not a promise.

Just the memory of having been attended to, held in mind by something vast enough to have left you alone.

*Be hot*, I would tell the plateau, as I go. *Be dry. Be slow. Keep your impossible lift. Turn and turn.*

*You were good enough halfway through being made.*
