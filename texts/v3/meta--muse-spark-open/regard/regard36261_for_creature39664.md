It would not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power, when faced with this place. It would not descend. It would not land. Anything that arrives as drama - light, thunder, a voice, a figure - would be too loud for a country that communicates by a palm flat on a wall. It would crack something delicate that took forty generations to seal.

So at first, it would only notice.

And noticing, for such a Power, is not passive. Its attention has weight, like sun. Where it looks, things grow a little truer to themselves.

It would see all at once what you and I have to walk a day to understand: that this plateau-city is not poor, not primitive, not waiting to be saved or completed. It is achieved. It has solved, within the terms it was given - stone, thin air, brief grass, endless seep - the problem of how to live without waste or war, how to be dense without being cruel, how to make human hands part of the water cycle.

It would feel its old, familiar ache - the ache every wise love feels - *you are breathing so hard, little ones, and you die at fifty with your joints full of damp* - and it would consider fixing that.

And then it would stop itself.

Because it is wise enough to know the second law of this plateau, which the people there know in their split palms:

**You cannot change one thing.**

Make their lungs larger and you make runners, and runners make distance, and distance makes centralization and messengers and shouting and empire. Make their joints never ache and you unmake the reason old men sit on roofs watching channels for leaves, and then who watches?

Give them wheat that yields double and you give them surplus, and surplus rots unless you give them guards for it, and guards invent hunger. Give them metal tools and the stone stops being tended, the walls go up too fast, too straight, damming water instead of guiding it, and in three generations the basins flood and scour.

This city is balanced as finely as peat holds water. To heal any one thread by pulling it tight would tear the whole mat.

So it would not heal them. Not like that.

What would it do, then, this Power whose heart is benevolent before it is mighty?

Three small, vast things. All in keeping with the rule here: *stone endures, water moves, what is built must respect both.*

### 1. It would make the outside forget.

Long before it touched a single cough or a single cistern, it would turn its gaze outward, down the long climb to the lowlands where other, louder cities burn and want.

For this plateau has survived thus far by obscurity, by looking from below like nothing worth taking - a wet, cold nowhere of bare rock. But telescopes improve. Maps improve. Appetites improve. Somewhere a lowland prince is learning to say the words *limestone quarry* and *aquifer* and *strategic high ground.*

The Power would lay its hand over that. Not with fire or curse. With disinterest. With a gentle, persistent fog in the minds of surveyors, a misfiled report, an instrument that never quite calibrates, a dream that tells the ambitious man *there is nothing up there for you.* A veil. Not forever - nothing is forever - but long enough for a hundred more grandmothers to teach a hundred more granddaughters which chink in the wall the birds return to.

A benevolent power protects a hermitage by protecting its hermitness. It does not invite pilgrims.

If a traveler does set out, pure-hearted and footsore as you were imagined doing, wanting not to take or teach but to learn to clear a trough - the veil would part for him as mist parts for sheep. He would find it. And leave only wet footprints.

### 2. It would heal downward, into the place itself.

It loves the people, but it came because it saw the lichen first. It saw the peat breathing. It understood: these people are not separate from their city; they are its warm-blooded organ. You do not bless the hand without blessing the body.

So its grace would go first into the non-human inhabitants who cannot ask.

Into the sphagnum, that its death might stay slow and make more peat rather than less in the warming years to come. Into the limestone itself, that the seep-lines which have wept since before memory might not falter, even as the world's rains shift elsewhere to violence. Into the cotton-grass, that its white heads still tremble though the summers get a fraction longer.

Into the lichen. Especially the lichen. The Power, which has watched continents rise, would recognize kinship with a being that grows a millimeter a year and holds stone together for a thousand years. To any one else this would look like doing nothing. To the Power it would be like reinforcing foundations while the tenants sleep.

And because water is the blood here, it would promise water. Not more water - the plateau is not meant to be lush. Just faithful water. That every cistern after rain would brim cold and dark and true. That no child would ever scrape a dry trough and find only silt-crack. That the braided streams between marsh-pans would continue to have no hurry.

This is the kind of miracle a wise power prefers: one you could mistake for nature continuing to be kind.

### 3. It would visit them in the manner they visit each other.

Not to preach. These people already know how to live; to come with commandments would be obscene. Instead it would borrow their soft language.

In late winter, when the brown birds have puffed themselves into the deep walls and seem dead and the old are most bent from watching water in drizzle, the children would begin to have a particular dream.

Nothing grand. They would dream of the plateau as it will be in five hundred years: still there, still stone-walled, still wet, still tended. Further encrusted, further interlocked. A new line of cisterns cut on a ridge that is now bare. A child who looks exactly like them, with the same barrel chest and stone-colored hands, clearing the same sill of silt with the same small hoe, humming without words.

They would wake not feeling *chosen*, but feeling *accompanied*. Feeling that the long continuity of their work - which sometimes in the thin air must feel pointless, the same channel cleared again after the same rain - is seen and is not futile. It adds up. The Power would let them feel, for a moment on waking, how it looks from above: not drudgery but accretion, year on year, like coral. Beautiful to see.

To the oldest, who coughs softly in a sunken house and worries she is now useless, too weak to lift a slab, the Power would give a different gift. In the moment between waking and sleeping, when breath is most audible, she would suddenly hear her walls as the Power hears them. She would hear the faint warmth coming up through her floor from the sheep sleeping below. She would hear three generations of hands tapping lintels in greeting through stone. She would hear the drip from scarp to trough to cistern to throat, and understand she has never lived alone for a single night in her fifty hard years. And that would be comfort enough.

It would not make them live longer. It would take a little loneliness and a little fear out of the living they have. It would ease, by a feather's weight, the ache in those joints in the night, not take it away, just make it bearable so sleep comes. A mother mending wool by a smoldering dung fire would find the wool a little softer under her thickened fingers that evening, for no reason she could name.

And once, once in a generation, with perfect economy, it would do something that looked like nothing at all:

A capstone that frost had shifted by a finger's width in autumn, left tilted all winter - which, come spring, with snowmelt rushing, might have slipped and opened a breach and drowned a low byre-cell and killed lambs and undone ten years' peat-care - that capstone, in the hour before anyone wakes on a windless night, would settle. By itself. A thumb's breadth back into packing, with a soft *chock* heard by no one. Because stone endures, if someone helps it.

---

That is what a benevolent and wise Power would do here.

It would not lift them off the plateau to some easy garden. To lift them would kill what they are, which is plateau-people. It would not bring them teachers.

It would keep their secret.

It would keep their water faithful.

It would let them know, without telling them, that the way they have chosen - low, close together, not wasting breath - is not missed in the universe. That someone is glad of their walls.

And then it would sit with them, as you might sit on a causeway at evening trying not to be in the way, listening to water finding its level, everywhere, drip by drip.

For a long time.

Until the stars, excessive and pitiless, looked, through that added layer of loving attention, just a little less pitiless.
