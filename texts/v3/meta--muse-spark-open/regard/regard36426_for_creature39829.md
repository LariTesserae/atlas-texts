If I were that power —

old enough to know that flatness is not peace and a wound is not always something to close —

I would not come down in light.

You already have your hour of white light at noon when every puddle goes transparent and you can see the nails you've lost. Another light would be blasphemy. It would bleach your moss and shame your cool.

I would come in the blue before it, when the fog is still hanging in curtains and forming in place, bead by bead out of the limestone. When the thatch is dripping in unison and every cistern has risen that inexplicable inch overnight.

I would not drain you. That is what every other power would offer.

*Let us pump you dry. Let us roof you proper. Let us bring you straight streets.*

But I have been watching long enough to see: you did not lose a marsh. You *married* one. You taught water to run in braids between houses instead of owning them. To whisper on a hundred stones instead of roaring. Draining you would kill you faster than drowning you.

So I would do nothing large.

Large things are for places that are broken. You are not broken. You are *occupied*, complete to the brim, sixty houses where there should be ten and none of it temporary. You fit the bite the earth took with such precision I suspect the earth made it for you in advance.

What I would do is small, and mostly underneath.

First, I would sit on your lip — which is to say, I would put my attention there, which is the same thing — and breathe with you for a few nights, so you are not breathing alone. The old women are right, you know. The bowl *is* exhaling. It's pore-water, too — the young men are also right — but it's deeper than that. A warm vein far below the shale that broke when the hammer fell. When the cool air hits it, it shows itself as sweat.

I would not stop the exhale. To stop it would be to cap a lung.

I would warm it by a fraction. One degree. So the condensation that beads on your children's hair at night is not quite so ready to settle in their chests. So iron left too close to the wall takes two nights to freckle instead of one. So your hands ache a little less in that bright, thin cool.

You would not notice, except as an easing you would attribute to yourselves — *we're getting tougher* — and that is as it should be.

Second, I would touch the stone.

Your walls are young. They flake and shear in flat plates, light grey and edged, and you live by quarrying your own collapse a foot at a time. That's honest work, but young stone is nervous. It calves in slabs when it should sigh in sheets.

I would teach it how to be old a little faster. Not round — never round, you love edges — but *trustworthy*. So when a plate lets go above the house where the reeds shot up after the winter fall, it comes down in two light pieces an hour after the children have gone to lunch, not in one heavy piece at midnight. So the fissure that weeps the fastest on the south wall finds its bedding line and stays there, a reliable clock, instead of wandering.

You would call it luck. You would still argue quietly over who gets that new foot of air. Good. Argue. A town you can walk across in ten minutes should have something to argue about.

Third, I would remember your animals with you.

I would tell the midges to hatch a little less thick in July so the swallows can keep up. I would tell the eels in the channel-pots under every plank bridge to be fruitful but not restless, to stay half-wild and not crawl away on a rainy night. I would tell the rats: *stay in the rafters, you have an agreement.*

And the ducks in their stacked wicker towers — I would keep their feet from going soft.

Fourth, and this is the most important, I would lay my hand — very lightly, so as not to wake anyone — on the people themselves. On the being that all those damp hands make when they work together.

On the knees of the women who stand bent over the channels all day, calves like river stones. Take an eighth of the ache.

On the shoulders of the quarry boys, who tap patiently along the bedding line until the shale sighs. Give them a little more hearing in their fingertips, so the sigh comes sooner.

On the backs that know the exact height of the eaves. Make those eaves leak one drop less in February.

On the children who run the roof-road barefoot, black with moss at noon while their parents call *slow, slow, the reed is loose there* in low voices that carry perfectly in the ear of the bowl — catch them, once, when one of them will otherwise find the loose reed. Let the mother think her warning worked. That's better than a miracle.

On the old women by the notched post by the stair. Give them truer dreams on the nights the south wall runs clear and fast. So when they say *rain in two days, empty your cisterns*, you empty them and are right.

And finally — because wisdom without benevolence is just good engineering —

I would give you a little more spring.

Not a season. Just the feeling. In the white hour when light falls straight down and every wet surface shines and the water in the channels turns transparent and you pause and beat your clothes and spread your grain and send the kids up the stair to run on the rim in the larger world. I would make that hour feel, inside your chests, a minute longer. And I would make the shadow, when it climbs the wall again, feel chosen rather than imposed.

You would not build me a shrine. You have no room, and no need.

You might, for a season, find your brooms sweeping more in rhythm with the water ticking. You might find you hush the dog a little more gently. You might find that conversation two streets over, heard as a murmur, sounds a little more like company and a little less like crowding.

Out on the marsh, the herons would still stand like judges. Beyond the rim, the sedge would still go dun and stunted. Lords looking for peat or flat land to tax would still look over your sharp, clean edge and see nothing — a bruise, a puddle, some poor ditch-dwellers, not worth the boots — and turn away. I would encourage that blindness. I am good at it.

You would stay small, and saturated, and zero-clearance, and water-ticking, and complete.

And some cold morning years from now, when the plates have weathered almost round and the sweat has finally slowed and a girl born on the roof-route asks why the town was built in a hole at all,

an old woman — stippled like moss, knuckles glossy — will answer without remembering why she knows it's true:

*Because something saw us before we were here, and left the earth open exactly our shape.*

That would be enough.

That would be everything I did —

which is to say, almost nothing at all, done with such care you would swear you did it yourselves.
