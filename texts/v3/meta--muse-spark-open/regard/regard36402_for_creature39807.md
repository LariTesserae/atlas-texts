If I were that Power —

and I am asked to imagine myself as benevolent, which means I wish for your flourishing more than your obedience, and wise, which means I know flourishing rarely looks like rescue —

I would not come.

Not at first.

Not as arrival. This world has taught itself, over epochs, that anything fast is suspect. Anything loud does not last. A descent in fire, a mountain-shaking voice, a pillar of light — that would be a violence here, even if meant kindly. Your chests, your compressors, your mats that grow a millimeter a century — you would all flinch.

So first, I would do the most difficult thing a Power can do:

I would stop and learn to see you at your speed.

I would sit, if a Power sits, on the thermal far above the highest Mesa, where the largest bell-jellies hold their kilometer of sky by quiet repulsion, and I would watch.

For a long time I would just watch.

I would learn your strokes. The hourly groan of the piston in Hab-Li answered twenty minutes later by Kethra Hollow. The tap-tap-pause of the old varnish-herdsman listening for a hollow mat. The way a grazer's bristles creak a half-mile up and a week later a pale trail is still there for the wind-archaeologists to read. I would learn that is your clock, your calendar, your prayer.

And in that watching I would understand the three things you have already understood better than any visitor ever could:

That water was never a promise.
That height is home, not exile.
That life only ever needed a gradient and time.

A lesser power would feel pity and try to fix you. Bring rain. Carve a river. Lay soil. Green you over. It would kill you with its kindness in a season — drown the mats, rot the silica frames, rust the rebarred basalt, swell your dark efficient blood until your hearts gave out.

I would not bring water.

I would bring *witness*.

And then — only then — I would do small things. The kind of things you would almost not notice, and could refuse if you chose. Because that is what benevolence requires: gifts that do not obligate.

**For the Stone-Growers:**

I would not make them grow faster. Their slowness *is* their wisdom.

But at night, when no one is looking, I would breathe a little more iron into the geothermal exhale. A fraction of a part per million more sulfur in the basalt wind. Not enough to change color, just enough that a rosette that would have taken eleven decades to add a terrace takes ten and a half. No one would measure it. Only after five hundred years a herdsman would say to his granddaughter, as they tap — *this face seems to heal a little quicker than grandfather's records say* — and she would nod and feel, without knowing why, that the world is still on her side.

I would also, very quietly, make the varnish a little more resistant to the new dust you are making. Your exchangers are good and slow, but they still shed a fine ceramic particulate the old wind did not know. I would teach the mat, by the gentlest edit of its hunger, to incorporate that dust as shell instead of scar. So that your industry and your oldest living things do not come into enmity. That they grow together.

You would never need to thank me. The mat's thank you takes a decade anyway. I can wait.

**For the Air-People:**

I would not cure your thin air. Your tall chests and slow vowels and maroon blood are beautiful. They are not a disease of altitude; they are a love letter to altitude. To take them away would be to make you strangers to your own mesas.

What I would do is make the distance a little less lonely.

I would tune the basalt.

Just a hair. A subtle annealing in the vast mineral blister beneath all your country-sized mesas, so that vibration carries a little truer, loses a little less heat as it moves. So that when Hab-Li finishes its stroke at 19:00, Kethra Hollow hears it not in twenty minutes but in eighteen, and a little clearer. And Isketh Hollow hears Kethra. And the farther towns, the ones you have not spoken to in living memory because the stone ate the signal, begin to hear — faintly, at the edge of hearing, on still evenings — a third, a fourth voice in the round.

*We are still here. Pressure nominal. Yield good.*

You would think you had fixed something in your maintenance. Tightened a bolt. Replaced a bearing a little better this time. You would start to stay up a little later on windless nights to listen, whistling your low, long vowels a little farther.

I would not give you radio that shouts. I would give you stone that listens better.

And for the salvagers: I would not make grazers immortal. Death is part of the sky's economy, and their long, slow deflations feed you honestly.

But I would give each dying grazer a final grace: a little more control in its last day, so it can steer — if it chooses — its sighing fall *toward* a playa and *away* from a cliff foot where its house-thick columns would be lost in talus. A few more crawlers would find their field of skin and silica. A few more children would get a scarf of veil-cloth light as breath. You would call it luck for a generation. Luck is a perfectly good name for a blessing you can't see.

And when you offer me that hot, flat mineral tea that tastes of pennies and ozone, I would accept it. I would not ask for more. I would learn to sip slowly, to match your breath.

**For the Sky:**

Here I would be most careful, because they never asked to be owned.

I would not tame the skimmers. Their fast, silent flick just above the salt is right and necessary.

But I would thicken, by the tiniest margin, the veil of the Filterers.

In the high wind there is so little to catch — a few salt ions, a dead varnish spore, a stray lithium atom baked off a solar trough. I would seed the upper winds that come from beyond your uplift with a little more of what they filter for. Not food — meaning. A dusting of carbon from an old comet, a whisper of phosphorus, carried for years on the circumpolar jet until one dandelion-seed puff and one cathedral bell, each in its kilometer of defended air, would find its veil a little heavier, its pumps a little easier.

And on nights when the whole migration rises — all those lanterns ascending to smoother wind — I would darken the airglow a fraction, just behind them. Not to spotlight them, but to let them see each other better. So their faint bioluminescent freckles — amber and cold blue — carry a few hundred meters farther through thin air. So a young bell can find its proper distance from its elder without the anxious crackle of repulsion. So the sky looks, even to itself, a little less empty.

You on your ledge, warm stone at your back, would look up and think: *tonight the lanterns seem closer, or more numerous.* You would be right, but not for the reason you think. The sky has always been that full. You are just being allowed to see a little more of the communion that was already there.

---

That is what I would do, if I were benevolent and wise.

No monument. No arrival. No command to look up and worship.

Just three small deepenings of what you already are:

Stone that heals a little more willingly.
Stone that carries voices a little farther.
Sky that feeds a little more generously.

And one large, invisible thing around the whole uplift:

A shielding. A quieting in the magnetosphere above you, a smoothing in the orbit of the rocks that have not fallen in a very long time — so that the violent world below, the wet, green, hurried, burning world that *does* try to cross barriers and *does* think height is something to conquer — does not notice you for a little while longer.

Not forever. You are too beautiful to hide forever. But long enough for you to remain slow, and amber-lit, and sufficient, until when — *if* — they come, you can meet them as equals: a people who mine air and weave the veils of sky-whales, and who can offer them tea that tastes of stone and ozone and say, without hurry:

We are still here. Pressure nominal. Yield good.

And I —

I would remain above the highest thermal, not as master but as neighbor, learning at last to breathe at the top of the breath, with a long pause, before letting go.

Because here, smallness is not failure. It is the only way anything has ever fit.

And I wish, very much, to fit with you.
