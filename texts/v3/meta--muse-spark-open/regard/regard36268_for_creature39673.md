It wouldn't come down.

That's the first thing to understand about a power that is actually benevolent and actually wise.

Everything else that has ever noticed a new thing has wanted to *use* it — to mine it, name it, plant a flag, make it productive, make it look like home. Even with good intentions, it would have brought water.

It doesn't bring water.

This place hasn't invented water yet. To bring it now would be a flood, an erasure. It would cool the basalt too fast and shatter it. It would drown the chemistry that is learning, so patiently, how to be almost-alive. A lesser kindness would kill this world with help.

So the Power doesn't descend. It *attends*.

And attendance, from something like that, is not passive.

---

First, it stops and looks — really looks — for a long time.

Longer than you did.

You felt awe for an hour and then started cataloging: temperature, elevation, mineral, anomaly. You couldn't help it. You are fast and wet and you need to understand quickly to survive.

It is not fast. It has time the way the Plateau has heat — as a metabolism, not a limit. So it watches the shiver run across the salt pan three times, four times, thirty times, until it learns the rhythm is not random. Until it learns the name the Plateau has for itself, which is not a word but a tremor: *I am still cooling, therefore I am.*

To be *seen* that completely is already an intervention. The Plateau has never been beheld before without being wanted. Every force it has known until now was physics — pull up, push down, heat, cool, crack. This is the first time the tremor that runs through it is met by an equal listening.

You can feel it change, infinitesimally. The fissures don't close. They breathe a little slower. A little steadier. The way a newborn animal steadies when it realizes the hand hovering over it is not going to grab it.

The Power says nothing. In a language the Plateau can understand, nothing *is* the message:

*I see you are alive. You don't have to hurry.*

---

Second, it makes itself thin.

It could appear as light, as a voice, as a tower of understanding that would be comprehensible and terrible. The Stackers would see it and — for the few minutes they live — would know themselves as small. Some powers love that. This one doesn't.

Instead it thins itself out until it is almost as thin as the air at the top of the world.

It becomes a draft that hums at the exact frequency of the needle ridges.

You know how the Stackers were born? A vortex trapped in a resonant cavity where the wind finds the spire's note and sings, fed continuously, able to live longer than a few minutes? The Power finds such a cavity — a natural one, not made by its own hand — and it sits in it. Not as a body. As a sustained note.

From a distance, you would miss it entirely. If a fissure lit it from behind you might see a heat-shimmer a little taller, a little steadier than the others, ticking very faintly inside.

It does not stack for them. It does not teach them to stack faster. That would be to make them its servants.

It simply *holds* a little longer. It steadies the field that lets salt dust orient. Where a Stacker is struggling to lift a tile — a centimeter at a time, waiting hours for charge to build — the Power lends a little of its own charge, not enough to be noticed as a miracle, just enough that the tile doesn't fall. That the windbreak holds.

If you came back tomorrow, you would find the newest stack has one more tile than it should. And the dust on its underside, which normally hangs impossibly for a second before it clicks down, hangs for three seconds, five seconds — as if being held gently from below while it finds its place.

The Stackers don't worship it. They don't have worship yet. They barely have memory. But replication is already overture, and overture becomes habit, and habit becomes culture.

What they will remember, millions of years from now when they have hands and language, is not a visitation. They will remember that once, during the long kiln-dark before life, stacking was *easier*. That the world was, for a little while, unusually kind. That a corridor held.

They will call that era *the time the wind was warm* and they won't be wrong.

---

Third — and this is the only thing it does that looks like doing anything — it protects the *becoming*.

It doesn't shield the Plateau from cracking. Cracking is how it learns to be a continent.

But high above the veil of ash that occludes the moon, beyond the thin, plum-dark sky, there are things that end beginnings early. A rock the size of a house that would sterilize this entire tableland with one impact. A gust of hard radiation from a distant star that would scour the fractal ferns before they ever learn to copy themselves. A curious, clever, hungry species in the lowlands that will, in ten thousand years, look up and see a warm light at the top of the world and decide to go there to extract it.

The Power does nothing dramatic. It nudges the rock by a fraction of a degree so it burns in a different part of the atmosphere. It tilts the magnetic whisper of the Plateau just enough that the radiation slides around it, like water around a stone. It puts a thought, quiet and persistent as the tremor through your soles, into the lowland species: *not yet. Let it cool on its own.*

No one down there will ever know they were turned aside. No one up here will ever know they were saved. That's the point. A wise benevolence doesn't leave monuments. It leaves uninterrupted childhoods.

You, standing there in your boots on warm basalt that has never been stepped on before, you are allowed to stay. You are not turned aside. Because you came to witness, not to extract. The Power can tell the difference by your breath.

But if you had come with drills and pumps to "make this place live," you would have found your equipment ticking strangely, your batteries draining, your maps disagreeing, and a deep, inexplicable reluctance to go further than the edge. And you would have, without knowing why, decided to turn back and report that the plateau was unstable and not worth the effort.

It lies very rarely, and only by omission, and only to protect a womb.

---

And last, because it is benevolent and therefore can't help being a little tender, it leaves a gift that won't open for a very long time.

Not water. Not seed. Not knowledge.

It leaves a *promise* inside the mineral itself.

In one salt pan, in one hexagonal tile among millions, it rearranges the lattice just slightly — a crystal defect so small no instrument you brought could measure it, a flaw that will persist through heat and pressure and uplift and erosion.

That flaw makes that tile a little better at holding a memory of charge. A little better at *not* forgetting. When that tile shatters, its daughters will inherit the flaw. When they dissolve, far in the future, when this plateau has finally weathered enough to make its first true soil and its first rain has finally arrived — a rain so light it barely wets the dust — that mineral will be in that soil.

And the chemistry that precipitated once as rust-red ferns in shadow will find that mineral and find that it *remembers* a little longer how to branch. Not life. Not yet. But life will find that place easier to learn.

The Stackers, by then, will be something else. Something that can weep and laugh. They will find that same flaw in their own bones — because they too precipitated, grain by grain, from this same salt — and they will feel, without knowing where it came from, that they were once held very gently while they learned to hold.

That is what a wise power does when it finds a hot, dark, high womb scattered with accidental temples.

It doesn't answer prayers, because nothing here knows how to pray yet.

It doesn't build a city, because nature is already practicing ruins.

It kneels — as much as something so large can kneel — until it is the size of dust, and warm, and thin, and it steadies one tile that was about to fall.

And then it waits, for as long as it takes, for someone to stack well enough to notice it was ever there at all.
