It would not come down like rescue.

A lesser power would. A lesser power would see the kink in the road, the ghost letters on the concrete throat, the too-many stairs, and think: *abandoned. Forgotten. Damp. Dark. A problem to solve.*

It would bring sunlight in boxes. It would cap the springs with concrete and give them new names. It would drain the flooded pantries, evict the carp, silence the frogs because they were "noise." It would fill the empty lots because emptiness offends efficiency. It would offer dryness as salvation, and within a year the Basin would fight back - springs in kitchens, pressure behind the ears, orange lace blooming overnight on every new bright thing - until either the people left or the place did.

The wise one knows not to do that.

It would come down the way everyone else does: by accident, or by maintenance stair. It would count the steps - forty, sixty, more than there should be - not because it needs to count, but to give the air time to cool. It would not bring its own light. It would let the narrow slot of sky go dim on its shoulders.

At the bottom it would stop.

And listen.

That is the first thing it would do, and for a long time the only thing. For three days, maybe. For nine. Time is cheap to the benevolent.

It would stand in one of the empty lots - the ones the maps call waste - the wet flagstone yards with a single rusting bench, and it would understand immediately that they are not empty. They are lungs. Intervals. The necessary pause between notes that makes the music not be noise.

It would hear the plink. Drip. Trickle. The ten-second rhythm off the power lines. The *whum... whum...* of the great screws once an hour, never loud enough to shout over. The pigeons in the sawtooth rafters making the whole bowl hum. The swifts that never land cutting the dim center. And under it all, answering it, the frogs - thousands of small brown throats using three hundred meters of limestone as a drum.

And it would hear the people inside that sound, not over it. Tea mugs shivering when a deep voice hits resonance. Rubber boots going *clop* - *clop*, shuffling so as not to splash the eels. A woman oiling her hinges at 6 a.m. because that is what prayer looks like here.

It would be delighted. You can feel when a wise power is delighted - the air gets a little warmer without getting drier.

It would not say, "I have come to help you."

It would say, "May I sit?"

And because no one here is in the habit of refusing a guest who asks properly, someone - a machinist with pruned fingertips and damp hair at the temples, or a child on carp-fry duty with a dustpan - would nod and make room on the bench that is always left out in the rain.

### What It Would Tend

Benevolence, when it is wise, does not fix. It tends. It does maintenance, like the people do.

So it would do small, almost invisible things - the kind you only notice when you leave and realize you stopped bracing for them.

**1. It would keep the agreement.**

The Basin has an agreement with water: *you can be everywhere except drowned. You will turn the wheels and we will give you room to breathe.*

The power would kneel - or whatever powers do to kneel - and put its hand on the lowest flagstone where you can hear the aquifer moving like a dark, slow bulk just underneath, and it would promise that.

Not in words. In weight. It would add its own weight to the balance so that the sump never has to choose between flood and drought. On the surface, far above, developers will keep having meetings about "reclaiming" Pump House 3 for condos. Their pencils will break. Their proposals will be misfiled. The road will keep its kink.

No one in the Basin will ever know it was protected. That is how you know the protection was kind.

**2. It would oil what they oil.**

It would not stop the rust - rust is the Basin's lacework, its slow clock. But it would go with the woman on her stoop and hold the rag tin while she wipes. Where she brushes orange from a hinge, it would breathe once, and that hinge would stay sweet for a winter longer than it should have.

It would lean over the greenish fluorescents in the factory and steady their buzz, so they flicker less. It would pass along the power conduits thick as arms and cool them a degree. In every kitchen it would touch the kettle that is always warm and make sure it never quite whistles, so as not to startle the frogs on the sill.

The factory that makes parts for itself - that most honest work - would find its crates of unshipped, gently rusting output mysteriously sorted overnight. Not shipped. Just sorted. A small kindness to the unhurried.

**3. It would answer the intervals.**

The strange rule about half a lot, about shadows pooling too thickly, about drips hesitating and falling up for half a second, about echoes arriving late as if sound had to ask permission - that is not haunting. That is the being of the place itself listening back.

The power would recognize a kin. Another slow, patient, concave attention.

It would not exorcise it. It would sit with it in the empty lots at night when the bar of sun is gone and everything is blue-grey and amber sodium, and they would listen together. The power would teach it, very gently, how to let an echo return on time when someone calls for help, and how to keep it late when someone is just complaining.

It would leave the shadows alone, except to make sure that when a child stares into one and the water appears to drip upward, it resolves - a second quicker now - into just water. Enough wonder to raise a good question, not enough to make a nightmare.

**4. It would bless the acclimated, without changing them.**

This is the hardest part for even wise powers: to love a people who have deliberately thinned themselves to three hundred in a place built for seven hundred, who never expect dry feet, who measure their lives in maintenance rather than completion - and not try to "heal" them into something faster, brighter, drier.

It would not offer them sun-tans or crisp bread. It knows they learned to stop drying off for a reason.

Instead, it would bless what they already are.

It would put its hand - cool, because all proper blessings here are cool - on their pruned fingertips and say: *You may continue.*

To the tram driver: may your brakes never slip on the silver-lipped stair.

To the butcher whose scale holds pigeons: may your birds never fear you.

To the children who scoop carp fry with reverent dry hands: may you always remember to be rude to nothing.

To the family who leaves the wet flagstone yard empty though they need storage: may the spring that once muttered in your kitchen stay quiet, and may you always have enough empty to breathe.

To the ones who tried a dehumidifier and felt cold pressure behind the ears until they unsealed the window: may you trust the headache was wisdom, not weakness.

It would leave one quiet miracle they will argue about for years: for a single evening in late spring, when the frogs are at their loudest, the choir will fall perfectly silent for three seconds - all at once, as if taking a breath - and in that silence everyone, even the newcomers, will hear how perfectly the bowl holds them. And then the frogs will resume, and tea will be drunk, and no one will be sure it happened, which is the correct way for a miracle to happen here.

After that it would go.

Not up the stairs - though it could. It would go the way water goes: seepside, runnel by runnel, turning each black iron waterwheel just a little more smoothly on its way out, gathered at last by the great screws and lifted, warm and unhurried, to the river the people upstairs still believe this place drains into.

It would not ask them to follow.

But for a long time after - for decades, which to such a power is a short while - anyone who climbs the forty, sixty, impossibly many steps back to the sharp, dry air on top will find, tucked in their waxed canvas pocket, a perfectly smooth, cool piece of limestone, wet on one side.

If they keep it on their sill, their boots will leak less.

If they throw it back down, it will be thanked.

If they forget it in a drawer, it will quietly condense a single bead of water, plink, once, at 3 a.m., just to remind them:

Some places do not need to be saved.

They need to be agreed with.

And some people have already learned how.
