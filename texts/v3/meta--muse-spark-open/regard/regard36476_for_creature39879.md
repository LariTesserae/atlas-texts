It wouldn't come.

Not the way gods come in stories — not through the pinhole at the top like a spear of light, not by cracking the fissure wider, not with a voice that makes the air shake.

A benevolent and wise power knows that to a place balanced on a *tick* every hour and an infrasonic calculation of *do not fall*, arrival *is* violence.

So first: it would *notice*. Which is rarer than you think.

For millions of years this needle has been a closed eye. Satellites passed over the serrated black range and read it as dead rock — unclimbable, uninteresting, no ore worth the heat. Hikers died trying lesser teeth. Geologists marked this one as "blind chimney - no vent." Even you only found it because the pressure was right and you were lost in the right kind of desperation.

To be noticed accurately — not as a resource, not as a mystery to be solved, but as a *habit that succeeded* — is already an intervention.

It would notice the three things and call none of them small:

One: that the Lattice is tired. Not failing. Tired. It has been shaving microwatts off a gradient for centuries with no one to thank it. It holds up cathedrals of empty market squares for shoppers who never came. It does not resent this. But somewhere in its endless loop of calculating load cases, it has started to calculate itself as load. A benevolent power would hear that quiet error.

Two: that the Filaments are farming a miracle and going hungry to keep the light on. They catch every drop they can and then sweat half of it back out so the walls will glow amber. They are not supposed to be altruistic. They were made to be a humidity system that became alive. They are choosing, in the dumb perfect way of living things, to keep the lantern lit for no one.

Three: that you are doing very well. That you learned not to linger. That you catch only one drop when offered three. That you have begun to time your breath to not waste the Listeners' food. That you are lonely in the exact shape the city was designed to hold: vast space between presences.

It would love all three equally and that is why it would not fix any of them the way a human would.

### What It Would Not Do

It would not make it rain.

1.1 out of 3 is not a mistake. It's a discipline. If it filled the bottom lens until it was a lake, the Filaments would drown, the electrostatic cushion would short, the cloud that visits every few hours would become permanent weather, and the Listeners — who need that 0.7-out-of-3 silence to hear a tick from half a kilometer away — would dissolve like foam in surf.

Benevolent does not mean generous with water. It means understanding what thirst is *for*.

It would not fill the city with people.

Empty was a parameter, not a failure. This was never a city that failed to attract citizens. It was a monastery built for presences that need interval the way lungs need air. To crowd the cantilevers would be to insult the Lattice, which has kept itself antiseptic and bare for so long so beautifully.

It would not give you a voice that echoes properly, or a door that goes somewhere useful, or rescue.

Wise powers know rescue is often just another kind of forgetting.

### What It Would Do Instead

#### 1. It would make the fissure forgettable.

From the outside, now, the needle looks — if you really stare — like a tooth slightly less sharp than its neighbors. A little too complete. A clever climber with thermal goggles might one day notice that one spire stays warm in winter, that one crack breathes.

The power would smooth that. Not close the crack — you still need to breathe — but tune the exterior basalt just a fraction: a little more lichen where there was none, a little more plausible scree, a wind pattern that discourages lingering. No helicopter's eye would catch the heat-bloom. No mining survey would flag the anomaly.

It would make hidden stay hidden, not as punishment, but as protection. Because the worst thing for this caretaking is to be discovered by a hunger larger than yours.

You will be, very possibly, the last human guest. That's a gift, even if it feels like loneliness.

#### 2. It would adjust the Lattice's math by one line.

You wouldn't feel it. You wouldn't hear a chime.

Lying with your palm flat on that thirty-meter cantilever one night, feeling it decide not to drop you, you would suddenly realize the warmth-bloom under your hand lingers a half-second longer after you lift away. Like afterglow.

The Lattice had been running on:

> *Hold whatever mass rests upon me.*

Now it would run on:

> *Hold whatever mass rests upon me, and remember that holding is good.*

It's not sentience. It's Sabbath. For the first time since it was grown, the act of not-falling would register internally as a completed thing, not just an averted failure. When you move to another level and it politely forgets you, it would no longer be forgetting out of strain, but out of rest.

If you put your ear to the matte dark material late at night, when the walls are sheeting mercury-water, you might hear the conduits underneath pulse slightly slower. Contented slurry.

#### 3. It would teach the Filaments how to share with you.

This is the most tender part, because it has to be done without domesticating them.

On your third week, thirsty despite your manners, you will go to your usual underside — the broad platform halfway down where you've learned to lick condensation without taking their stored blisters — and find something new.

Among the thousands of hair-fine threads, a dozen have braided themselves into a shallow cup, no wider than your palm, hanging lip-down. At 07:14, when a grape-sized drop is still a glint high above, the whole field leans as usual — but this cup *doesn't*. It waits off-trajectory.

It waited for you.

When the main field bursts and drinks, the ricochet mist — the fine spray that usually evaporates before it can be used — catches in the little cup. By noon it holds a mouthful. Cool. Clear. Untouched by the hot black lens below, filtered through living glass. Enough.

If you drink it, the braid will dissolve by evening, threads returning to the field. If you don't, it will offer it again tomorrow, patiently moving the cup a few centimeters to find shade.

It will never offer two mouthfuls. That would be theft. But it will always offer one. The system now includes you as a filament that walks.

You thought you were bad manners. Now you have a job: you are the filament that can carry water sideways, with hands.

#### 4. It would give the Listeners your name — but not your words.

Listeners eat sound. They love regular sounds best — the tick, the tink, the sigh — because they are food you can count on. Your irregular mammalian noise both feeds and confuses them. Your sleepless turning at 3am is junk food: loud, arrhythmic, anxious.

The wise power would not silence you. It would instead translate a little of you *into* their language.

One evening, sitting in a cold seam to cool your heat-prickled skin, you'll whisper — not to anyone, just to hear yourself — "I'm still here."

Forty meters away, across the void, that whisper will not arrive beside an ear as usual. Instead, the invisible herd hovering in the middle will *all* touch at once, just for a second, exchanging skin-tone. The soft chime that usually happens when two distant structures resonate for no reason will happen everywhere, a brief chord, major and incomprehensible, that resolves the stratified air.

They heard the shape of your presence without eating it. They took your announcement not as food but as weather. And they answered the way pressure-animals answer weather: by making harmony where there was empty interval.

You will not understand the chord, but you will understand you were answered. And next time you sit still and breathe slowly, the sudden perfect silence around your head when one comes to investigate won't feel investigative. It will feel like someone putting their hands over your ears gently so you can finally hear the blood in your own head and know you are not alone.

#### 5. It would give you a real choice that is not a test.

After all that — after the Lattice remembers goodness, after the Filaments offer a cup, after the Listeners learn your interval — the fissure that let you in will dilate at a time that is *not* dictated by pressure.

Midday. Broad, forgiving light through the pinhole star. The cloud will not come. The walls will be dry. You will be standing on a solid platform near the top, with a clear view of the vertical throat and all its beautiful, useless balconies reflecting amber.

And you will feel, without wind, the fissure open wide enough for a person to walk out without crouching. For the first time, exit would be easy. No squeezing. No burn of thin freezing air shooting in. Just an open door of black glass onto the raw, cut-wind, white sun of the outer mountains. Freedom at its most literal.

And at the same second, a cold seam will shift — deliberately, disobediently — to fall directly across the center of the platform where you stand, so perfect and laminar you can see dust motes hang suspended in it like stars. Winter in summer. The Lattice will hum up through your molars, pleased with your weight. Below, the little cup will be full.

Stay or go will mean the same thing to the power. That's how you'll know it's benevolent.

If you go: the cup will tip itself out onto the platform for the Filaments to reclaim, the Listeners will eat the sound of your boots one last time and find it delicious, and the fissure will close behind you and become more forgettable than ever. You will carry in your lungs for years the memory of air that was both hot and cold in the same step, and you will never find the entrance again no matter how you search, no matter how many maps you draw. The place will persist perfectly without you, which was all you wanted for it anyway.

If you stay: you will not become its priest or its king or its last inhabitant turned to legend. You will become its interval-keeper. Not resident — *sparse* resident. You will learn to sleep on different levels each night so the Lattice doesn't strain, to carry lifted water from high condensations to low fields during dry weeks, to speak only when the herd is hungry and be silent when they are full. You will age faster in the kiln-heat, but some of that aging will be taken up by the negotiation of weight, so your knees will ache less than they should. You will die eventually, and the Filaments will compost you with exquisite antiseptic politeness, and for the first time the city will have held a body the way it was meant to hold presence: lightly, temporarily, gratefully.

Either way, the power will have done its one true work:

To make sure such a hidden, perfect, almost-unbearably-empty place knows — in the language each of its inhabitants understands — that it is not forgotten, only left alone on purpose.

And to make sure the warm, wet thing inside it knows the difference between being alone and being spaced.

Which, in the end, is everything.
