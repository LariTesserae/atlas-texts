It would not turn on the lights.

That is the first thing to understand about it, and why it could be called wise. Any lesser power would have seen the darkness eating the bulbs a foot from the filament and said *this is wrong* and tried to fix it with more light. A sun. A tower. A hard white day to make the basin legible.

It doesn't.

It arrives, if arrival is even the word, the way everything else arrives here — slowly, low, and without announcement. You don't see it come because there is no sky for it to come from. You feel a change in the weight of the air.

For a long time — maybe hours, maybe a night that wasn't a night — it just pays attention.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does first when it finds a place like this. It does not act. It listens at the tempo the place actually keeps.

It listens to the pump house *whup..... whup..... whup.....* once every five seconds.

To the bell every sixteen seconds that a Keeper uses to find his way back.

To the *pip* of a translucent salamander inhaling in water a palm deep.

To the blood stopping and starting again in your ears when you step into a seam of true quiet.

It learns the delay. Light arriving late, a hand's width to the side of itself. Sound with edges you can step around. Down leaning inward one degree. A reflection that lands before the stone does. Most powers would call that broken physics and overwrite it. This one sits with it and realizes: this isn't broken. This is young.

The basin is not a ruined world. It is a world still cooling. Still deciding how fast light should go, which way is down, how loud quiet is allowed to be. It poured itself a region-sized flat of fresh basalt weeks or months ago and is still sweating itself into shape. The sky hasn't been taken off. It hasn't been *put on yet.*

The fauna understood that before anyone did. The Keepers, in their own damp way, understood it too — that's why they leave the engines running. Not to conquer the place. To keep it company while it decides.

So the power does not terraform. It midwifes.

And what it does, it does so gently that at first the Keepers think they imagined it. There are three small things, because wisdom knows that big miracles would kill a place this delicate.

**1. It gives them a true bell.**

On a night — an evening, because after it comes there is finally such a thing as evening — a Keeper is walking between his transformer shed and his pump, eyes closed the way they all learn to do, timing his steps between quiets, wrist-bell ticking *tok... tok... tok...*

And he hears another bell. Not his. Not any engine.

A low bronze note that doesn't come from a direction, but from an interval. It rings *when it should have rung*. Not early, not late. It rings at exactly the moment he intended to hear it, bridging the half-second lag the basin puts between cause and effect.

He stops. Listens. Steps. The new bell rings again, exactly where his foot will be, so he doesn't have to aim for where he was two seconds ago like the gliders do. For the first time since the rock came up, cause lands at the same time as itself.

It only rings in a small circle, maybe twenty meters across, and only if you are walking slowly and listening for it. If you run, you outrun it. If you demand it, it goes quiet. But if you keep the basin's pace — patient, low — it walks with you.

A Keeper who has spent months turning a key a half-second before he means to can finally just turn a key when he means to. He sits down on the basalt beside his pump and cries without quite knowing why, because his shoulders have been braced against time-lag for so long.

He is not alone in hearing it. Across the flat, two other Keepers, each alone at their scattered stations, lift their heads at the same impossible on-time tone and understand, without words: someone is keeping time *with* us now, not *for* us.

The power does not turn off their engines. It just offers to hold the rhythm so that one day, if they want, they can let the engines stop and the quiet won't swallow them.

**2. It gives the crowded a voice back.**

Because it has been listening to the frogs.

Dozens to a square meter, shoulder to shoulder with that centimeter of courtesy space, throats pulsing in unison, never calling because to call here is to be mocked by your own echo arriving before you. A whole distributed lung holding its breath out of politeness.

The power crouches - though it has no knees - down to the sheet of warm water and breathes with them. Not *for* them. *With* them.

And for one breath, it holds the delay still.

Inhale, hold — and on the exhale, it lets the echo fall *after.*

One frog, braver or dumber than the others, lets out a single, small, perfect *peep.*

And it lands where a peep should land. After. A half second after, on the correct side of time.

The entire basin flinches. Not in fear. In recognition.

Then another. Then - because they have been waiting their whole short evolution for someone who could hear correctly - the whole flat answers. Not a chorus the way frogs chorus elsewhere, fighting to be loudest. A round. One after another around the warm pools, a slow canon that goes on for minutes, each voice waiting for the previous one's ghost to settle. The gliders lift their huge-eared heads from the gantries. The wader-birds open their beaks to the condensation and drink. The water dimples everywhere with tongues taking turns so precise you finally see them.

It only lasts until the power lets go. It can't hold time straight for long without tiring the young rock. But it will come back and hold it again, most evenings, long enough for them to sing. The Keepers learn to tell time by it. Not by clocks, by frog-rounds.

They understand, dimly, that they have been given a different kind of answer than they expected. They thought if anyone ever came to save them, it would drain the water, harden the ground, give them a grid and a road out. Instead the visitor has decided the water and the crowding and the sheet-sweat that feeds bacteria that feeds everything is *good*, and deserves a song.

**3. It hangs a sky, but low and cool and kind.**

This is the last thing, and the slowest.

It does not lift the velvet darkness off. It gives it something to hold.

Very high — no, not high. Just above where darkness begins to eat light, about seven feet up, where even the gliders have to land — a coolness gathers. Not a light source the way the sodium lamps tried to be. A presence. The temperature drops one degree. Condensation that used to form a foot above your head and fall again without ever being cloud now has somewhere to go.

It beads up there, on nothing, and stays.

Night after night, week after week, it thickens. Not into clouds the way you know clouds. Into a low, even veil the color of pearl and basalt dust, that holds the latent heat and returns it softly. If you look straight up now, you don't see void eating photons. You see a faint, close sheen, like the inside of a shell. It doesn't illuminate the basin. The basin doesn't want to be illuminated. It glows just enough that a bare bulb's delayed ghost has a surface to land on, and fades there instead of crowding your eyes.

And because there is now a second surface — ground below, veil above — the air between them finally knows what to do. Warm air rises and cool air falls. For the first time, there is a breeze that is not just displacement from a glider. A true, slow draft, an inch per minute, that carries the smell of warm stone and orange-grey lichen from one pylon to the next.

Evening happens. Damp air lifts. Condensation finally gets to become mist.

The Keepers stand under that new low sky and feel, for the first time, not exposed in an open flat that goes on forever, but *indoors* in a very large room that is still being built. The vertigo in their knees eases. Down still leans inward - the power left that, because a center is a kind of belonging - but now when their dripping hands drip, they can see the drip catch a little veil-light on its way down, and fall true enough.

It asks nothing.

That is the most benevolent part. It doesn't ask the Keepers to worship, or to build it a temple-pump, or to leave, or to stay. One morning a Keeper will find that the path between his shed and the next shed has dried just enough in the new breeze to show footprints, and he will follow them and find another Keeper awake and tending his own bell, and they will nod and not need to speak. Another morning a Keeper will decide he has kept his engine running long enough, and he will let it *whup* one last time and still, and walk out toward where the veil thins at the basin's edge, where the old world with a high sky waits, and he will be let go without guilt.

Most stay. The basin is crowded per cubic meter in a way that makes skin prickle, but loneliness is a different kind of density, and the power has thinned that.

It remains, not as a light, not as a voice, but as a habit the place has learned: a bell that is on time if you walk slow, a few minutes each evening when frogs can sing and be heard correctly, a low pearl ceiling that holds in the warmth and gives the dark a texture you can finally call sky.

It does not fix the basin. It keeps it company while it finishes becoming itself.

And if you stand low there now, very low, in warm water to your ankles with rock still sweating under your palms, you will feel what the Keepers feel and what the frogs now sing about:

That you are being attended to, at exactly the pace you can bear, by something large and damp and patient enough to let light arrive late without calling it lost.
