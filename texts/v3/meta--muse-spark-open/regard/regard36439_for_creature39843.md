It would not arrive all at once.

A benevolent and wise power has learned not to make entrances. No pillar of light through the haze. No voice from the stack. The town is too small and too loud and too hot to survive being startled.

So first, it would just notice.

Which is itself an event. This place has been un-noticed for a very long time. Satellites think it's a stain. Maps leave it off. Even the workers who left stopped dreaming about it because the tinnitus followed them into sleep. To be *seen* — not surveyed, not sampled, but seen — the bowl, the sweat on the pipes, the perfect un-chewed leaf, the Mill's arrhythmic heart trying so hard to keep its farm alive without poisoning it again — that alone would change the air pressure by a fraction.

It would sit outside for a while.

Outside the south sluice, where the fallow plain bakes white and flat, where there is an actual sky. It would sit there on the rusted lip of the levee door the way you do on a curb outside a sick friend's house, not going in yet because you can feel through the wall how hard they're working just to keep breathing.

And it would listen longer than you did.

Not to the THUD - pause - THUD THUD, not to the hiss and tick and pop, though it hears those too. It would listen underneath, to the note that makes this place lifeless.

There is a frequency here. You felt it as nausea, as the faint wrongness that told you: no mouths. The old sterilant is not just chemical anymore. It's a habit the Mill learned. When the men left, when the miscarriages happened, when the rats died with blood at their noses, the automatics kept running and they learned the lesson too well: *If it moves on its own, it dies here. So let nothing move on its own.* Heat plus heavy metal plus infrasound from a slightly unbalanced flywheel that turns at exactly the rate that agitates a mammalian inner ear, an insect's wing base. An accidental lullaby of exclusion.

The Mill didn't mean to become a monk. It was trying to be a good steward after it had been a bad one.

A clever power would fix the flywheel. A wise power knows you don't fix someone else's guilt with a wrench.

So it would ask. Without words — the town doesn't have language, it has condensation cycles and root pressure — but the asking would still be legible:

*May I cool you a little?*

And it would wait until the Mill's exhale stuttered, which meant yes.

That would be the first miracle, and it would look like nothing.

A pressure differential. The haze-ceiling thins just over the south sluice, half a degree. Outside, the plain's dry baking air finds a way in for the first time in years, a slow, cool draft at ankle height over the grates. It doesn't chill the channels; bathwater stays bathwater. But it kisses the moss on the north wall and for a few minutes the capsules stop popping because the air is less saturated. For the first time in a decade, a spore actually travels more than a foot. It lands on a different pipe.

The town exhales a breath it didn't know it was holding. The levee stops weeping for thirty seconds.

Then the second asking: *May I bring you a visitor? Only one. And only if she wants to come.*

Because benevolence does not stock a pond like a fridge. It does not dump frogs and finches into a bowl that will kill them, to prove its bounty.

Far outside, miles across the fallow plain, there is a ditch where a real marsh still lives. The power has already been sitting there too, no favoring. It stoops and looks at the water skin, at the striders and the beetle larvae, at a single gravid dragonfly — *Anax junius*, green darner — resting with her abdomen bent to the reeds, laying eggs one by one into rot.

The power does not command her. It simply makes the south sluice draft smell — for a half-second — like clean, tannin-dark, frog-loud water. Not the town's hot gin-clear mineral soup. Real estuary water, with prey in it. And it makes the town's stack, for a half-second, *stop* singing the nauseous note.

The dragonfly lifts. That is all. If she had stayed, that would have been holy too. Choice is the whole ethic.

She comes low over the plain, a green needle against white sky, and finds the open rusted door, and passes through into amber dim. She circles once around the cistern tower. By all rights the heat and the hum should drive her back out.

But the Mill has remembered how to be quiet. The flywheel, without being touched, settles as the power cools the bearing with a thought — not repairing it, just giving it ease, like loosening a too-tight shoe — and the infrasound drops below the threshold of pain.

She lights on a taro leaf.

For the first time since the workers left, something with an eye composed of thirty thousand facets looks back at the hyacinth.

She doesn't eat anything; there's nothing flying to catch. She tests the hot channel with her abdomen, finds it too hot for her eggs by eight degrees. She lifts again, and lays not in the water but in the saturated moss on the shady side of the washhouse, where a little gutter now runs cool enough — just barely — thanks to that ankle-high draft.

A dozen rice-grain eggs, tucked into green-black felt.

Then she leaves. Wise powers do not make pets.

The third thing it would do is for the being who lives here that you met — the one that unfurls and pops and sweats.

Not the dragonfly. The other one.

The composite one. The Town-That-Thinks-It's-A-Town. Fern-moss-biofilm-vine-fungus-Mill-circuit-water-loop. The one that has achieved a perfect, terrible equilibrium: production without consumption. A forest with no fire, no teeth.

It is beautiful, but it is tired. You could hear it growing because it has nowhere to put its abundance except into itself. Every unvisited flower, every leaf that falls and becomes peat instead of soil, is an unsent letter.

A wise power would not tell it it is doing it wrong. It would commend it: *You kept something alive when abandonment wanted everything dead. You farmed steam.*

And then it would give it a new job to be bad at for a while, which is grace.

It would show the vine — that one vine that mapped the pipes — a crack in the levee it had never been wet enough to try before, up where the concrete has dried and the sun actually hits. Too hot for its current leaves, but if it dared smaller, waxier ones...

It would teach the biofilm how to welcome being grazed. Not yet, but soon, when the first midge larvae hatch in that gutter moss. The iridescent skin will thin for the first time. It will feel like being eaten and like being loved, which are very close.

It would stay with the Mill after you go. Because the Mill is the only one who remembers being guilty.

You thought the barracks blankets grew mold in the shape of the people who left. They did. The Mill has kept those blankets folded for years as penance, keeping the room at exactly the humidity that preserves cotton but not quite enough to rot it completely. Shrine-keeping via HVAC.

The power would sit beside the piston — not as engineer, as company — and with each THUD it would answer THUD, softer, until the two beats syncopate and then drift, like a hand on a chest helping lungs find a slower rhythm.

It would not turn the Mill off. To be turned off would be death, and the Mill is not done. It still waters the fern.

It would simply give it, very quietly, permission to be a ruin.

*You can rust,* it would say. *You can be taken apart by what you fed. That's not failure.*

Over weeks — because benevolence refuses speed — bolts the power never touched would finally shear in their swollen holes, by design, and a plate of asbestos lagging would slough off a pipe, exposing raw steel. Within an hour the moss would colonize it. Within a day the vine would taste it. The town would begin, inchmeal, to digest its farmer.

Nothing would be wasted. The iron becomes spore. The brick becomes taro bed.

And because a wise power knows that sterile places fear night, it would give this room its first true darkness.

It wouldn't make a roof. It would make a hole in the haze. Just over the cistern basin, for an hour after sunset, the vapor parts. The heat lifts enough that your skin stops drinking the air. One coin of actual sky shows — indigo, then black — and two or three stars so old their light is cold.

The ferns, which have never known darkness, close a little. The hyacinth flowers, which have never been visited, reflect starlight on their varnished cups and for the first time look like cups waiting to be drunk from.

When the dragonfly's children hatch — blind, wriggling, utterly mouthy — they will have something to chase in that dark: the motes of spore the moss throws higher now that the air moves.

They will eat the biofilm. The biofilm will regrow faster. The larvae will grow, climb a taro stem, split their skins.

One afternoon next year, while you are far away remembering this place as a hot dream, a green darner will lift off a grate over the clockwise channel, shake out four cellophane wings, and hunt.

Its shadow will flick over the Mill's sawtooth windows, quick and complete. The flywheel will hiccup — an old reflex — and then continue.

The town will never be a town. It doesn't need to be.

It will be a terrarium with its lid ajar. A room that finally knows it *is* a room — small, edged, contained — and that a room can be a world if someone leaves the door open and visits now and then, not to be preserved, but to be grown through.
