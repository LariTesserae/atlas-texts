It wouldn't come down.

That's the first thing to understand about a wise and benevolent attention. It doesn't arrive.

It was already passing over - the way weather passes, the way light passes - and it paused. Not because this place was broken or pleading or brighter than other places. But because it was *complete*, and completeness is rare and it draws a certain kind of eye the way a perfect note draws the ear.

It would have stood - if standing is what it does - at the rim, where the wind circles and doesn't enter. It would not have stepped inside. To step inside a room that small is to change its air pressure.

So it listened.

For a long time, a power like that would just listen. Longer than you did. Long enough to hear the whole piece, not just the hush.

It would hear the hundred-fingered weeping in the back wall and understand that this is not erosion. This is circulation. The mountain is not losing itself here; it is holding water in the air the way a person can hold a thought without saying it.

It would hear the *tok... tok... tok* of the moss cushions drinking, and the papery creak of the grove-keepers shifting their fronds a millimeter an hour to share light with the orchids on their own shoulders. It would hear the glug of the patriarch taro tipping its door-sized leaves one at a time, making its own private rain, and the single *plink* of the frog every half hour that says *taken, taken, taken.*

It would see what you saw: that life here has made a covenant - **be an island or be stone.** No carpet. No crowds. No chorus. Each pan a solitary sea with one newt. Each fern-trunk a solitary forest. Each moss a solitary lake. And between them, the bright, scoured emptiness that is not failure but is part of the design, so the dripping has somewhere to go.

And it would love it immediately. I think that would be involuntary, even for a wise power. To love a place that has solved itself without needing to argue.

What would it *do*?

Nothing that looks like doing.

A lesser power - clever but not wise, kind but not wise - would have tried to help. It would have seeded the bare stone with fast green, to "fill in the gaps." It would have joined the pans with little runnels so the newts could visit each other. It would have given the flightless beetle wings so it wouldn't have to take a day to walk six feet. It would have made the taro fertile, made a second and a third, so it wouldn't be lonely.

And all of that would have been killing with kindness. It would have broken the rule that makes the place work. The emptiness *is* the function. The solitude *is* the stability.

A wise, benevolent power would know not to improve. It would know the most ethical form of power is restraint.

So this is what I think it does:

**1. It veils.**

Not hides. It doesn't put up a dome or a glamour. It just deepens what is already true: that this cirque is small and unremarkable from above, its lip closed by tumbledown boulders, its creek too shy to map. It makes it a little more boring to the wrong kind of eyes. The satellite that passes over sees only wet rock. The prospector's mule shies from the drop and chooses another pass. The hiker with a taste for conquest feels, without knowing why, that there's nothing up that shoulder worth the climb. The place becomes - even more - a room people walk past without noticing the door.

Not forbidden. Just uninteresting. That's the kindest wall there is.

**2. It ratifies the water.**

It leans in - not physically, but the way attention leans - and it *agrees* with the water about how much is allowed to be held.

You noticed the weirdness: a cup left out never quite fills, poured water creeps uphill toward the weeping wall, moss leans toward the open drop instead of the sun. The cirque is already a slow lung with its own decision about holding.

A benevolent power would put its hand there, invisibly, and say *yes, like that. Keep holding like that.*

So the weeping wall doesn't falter even when the rains shift. The peat under the moss cushions doesn't dry into dust in a hard year. The pan lips don't erode too fast when one freak storm tries to tear them. It doesn't add water. It doesn't need to. It just helps the place *remember* how to hold. A little more resilience in the memory of stone.

If you were there on that day, you wouldn't see anything change. You'd just notice that a moss cushion you parted with your fingers springs back a fraction faster. That the taro's leaf-pool, tipped, refills a breath sooner.

**3. It gives the lonely ones a future without crowding them.**

This is the tender part, because a wise power would feel that ache you felt: the taro with no seedlings, the beetle that dies in the cushion it was born in, the newt that will never meet another newt beyond its pan. It would feel that loneliness and want to answer it without betraying the solitude.

So it wouldn't spawn. It would whisper *continuance.*

To the patriarch taro under the overhang: one viable seed that finally takes, wedged in a crack two steps away that weeps just enough, finally. Not a grove. Just one child, so the lineage doesn't end on this one magnificent holdout.

To the beetle: a scent trail that lasts an hour longer on wet stone, so the next time two beetles wander, they actually find each other instead of turning away.

To the pan-newt: that its three eggs a year become four, just this once, when a heron never happens by.

To the moths: that the warm slow current that circles the rim - the cirque's breath - holds steady for their whole night of drifting, so dust-like eggs actually land.

Not abundance. Just enough. The difference between extinction and endurance is often one more. A benevolent arithmetic.

**4. It witnesses, and leaves the witness inside the place.**

This is the most important thing it would do and the least visible.

Because nothing here is built to remember being seen. For thousands of years this cirque has breathed in and out without a mind to mark it. That doesn't make it sad, but being *known* changes a living system the way being loved changes a person - not by adding weight, but by adding coherence.

So the power - before it moves on, because it must, there are other rooms - would leave a small part of its attention behind. Not a watcher, not an angel with a sword. Just an indwelling regard. What the old monks would have called a blessing.

You would feel it only if you sat long enough on that dry lip of boulder between two moss chapels, feet just above the seep, as you did. The loud hush would resolve for a second into breathing, in and out, very slow. And you would feel, without words: *go on. You are good like this. Continue.*

The moss would lean a degree surer toward the blue haze. The water that creeps uphill would do so with a little less hesitation. The taro would tip and spill and right itself and the sound would be - definitively - enough.

Then the power would go. It wouldn't carve its initials. It wouldn't ask for thanks. The most benevolent things never want you to know their name, because naming turns gratitude into debt.

If you came back a year later - if you found your way back past the boring boulder lip - you would swear nothing had happened. Same hundred-fingered weeping. Same shy creek disappearing to mist. Same sparse, lush chapels in a mostly empty cathedral.

Only everything would be holding its water just a little better.

Only there would be two taros where there was one.
