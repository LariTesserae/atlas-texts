She would not come like a god comes.

No light. No opening. No voice filling the pocket like water.

If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place — this warm, wet, held breath high up where the air should be thin and cold — she would notice it precisely because it was not calling out.

Everything else on earth gradients. Heat leaks. Water runs downhill. Sound echoes and dies. Time carves. This place does not. It stays. It holds. That is so rare that to a wise attention it would shine darker than darkness. A small, perfect refusal. A *no, not yet.*

She would come closer — not down, exactly. A power like that doesn't travel. She attends. She leans.

And what she would find is not one being, but a whole economy of staying: the water that decided to remain, the lichen that drinks without sun, the fern that unfurls because it remembers how, the blind custodians skating on a tension that never breaks, the small white-pink singer in the center with his gills open like hands.

She would understand immediately what it is.

Not a cave. A womb.

Not a salamander. A child, in the long warm wait before the first true breath.

And because she is wise, she would do the first, hardest thing wisdom ever does:

She would not fix it.

She would not bring light because it is dark.
She would not bring air because he does not breathe.
She would not bring change because his whole work right now is not to change.

The pocket has one rule: equilibrium. Nothing leaves. Heat does not leave. Water does not leave. Prayer does not leave. To interfere — even with love — would be to puncture the blister. To make a valley too early. To teach erosion to a place that hasn't yet learned to be a mountain.

So for a long time she would do nothing that looks like doing.

She would sit.

Not in the pocket. There is no room for a second midwife in there without crowding the water. She would sit *with* the plateau, the way you sit with a first-time mother in late pregnancy when there is nothing to do but be steady.

She would put her attention like a hand over the belly — over that broad lift of new basalt, all pillow-swells and rope-coils and glassy blisters — and keep it warm from the outside. Keep the frost from pressing too hard on the fissure. Keep the wind from finding that off-center seam where two cooling lobes failed to meet and sighing its cold, thin curiosity inside.

A benevolent power's first work is always veil-work. Hiding.

Not from the reverent ones. The ones who leave cairns no taller than a knee, who pinch pale clay with cold fingers and fire it right there for the cloud on the rim, who lay a sodden mat to kneel on and leave a string of swollen beads on a low finger — they are not a danger. They know how to come lightly and leave lightly. The pocket keeps them without rotting them because they asked for nothing to keep.

No, she would hide it from the other kind of attention. The hungry kind. The kind that would see a warm pocket that does not breathe and call it anomaly, resource, miracle to be drained. That would bring drills and lights and names. She would make the fissure look like nothing, for a few more miles of thought. She would make the plateau look boring from the air. She would put a little forgetting in the minds of surveyors, a little *not here* in their maps. Not a curse. A blanket.

Her second work would be tuning.

You heard how sound is swallowed here. How a *tok* takes a long time to fade and ripples stop gently at careful edges. How a droplet can hang to the size of a plum and not fall. Time has thinned.

Wisdom knows that stasis is not the opposite of growth. It is growth held at the exact pressure where it can happen without tearing. If heat left too fast, the child would chill. If water left, the gills would stick. If time moved at outside-speed, the lungs would be rushed — forced to breathe before they know air.

So she would not stop time. She would *keep* it thin.

She would breathe with the place until her breath and its breath are the same slow count: one... thirty seconds... one. She would listen to that doubled heart — the droplet and the heart, the heart and the droplet — and she would keep its rhythm from being lonely. Not faster. Not slower. Just witnessed. There is a kind of keeping that happens only when something is heard the whole way through its fade.

If a droplet trembled, too heavy, too long, she might — very slightly — steady its skin. Not to keep it from ever falling. Falling is promised. But to let it fall when it wants to, not because the outside world shook the stone.

If the salamander-child turned in his pan of mineral milk, she would make the water a little more itself. A little warmer, a little more present and present again. So he never has to wonder if he is held.

That is the whole of her miracle: more holding.

She would not speak to him. He has no ears for language yet, eyes closed or absent — there is nothing to see anyway. But on the nights when outside on the plateau it does drop below freezing, even in summer, and the basalt belly tightens just a fraction — as bellies do — she would hum.

Not into the pocket. Into the stone around it. A low, mammalian hum that the placenta-thatch can feel as a slight increase in exchange, that the amniotic pans can feel as a softening of their lips, that the fern can feel and answer by holding its square inch with slightly less effort.

The pilgrims would feel it too, without knowing. They would crawl out through the fissure after leaving their bowl or their cairn and think, inexplicably, *it is well, it is well, it will be well.* They would not remember why.

And she would wait.

That is what benevolence does with a becoming that is not ready to become. It waits without urgency. Like a droplet warm as skin waiting without urgency to fall.

She knows what the inside of this story is for. It is for making lungs that can stand cold.

One day — not now, not soon, but one day when the count changes — the rule will break. Equilibrium will tip on purpose. Water will finally want to flow. Heat will want to leave. The fissure will be a mouth, not a seam. The child who has never breathed will have to.

That day will not be gentle, however gentle the pocket has been. First breaths never are. Cold, thin, enormous air after blood-warm water is a kind of grief, even when it is exactly right.

She will still be there then.

She will not reach in and pull. Midwives don't pull unless they must. She will be the cold-air side of the keeping. The one who makes sure that when he *does* fall — *tok*, at last — there is not scree and exposure to fall onto, but hands. Or moss. Or at least a wind that has been told to be kind for one hour. She will be the one who keeps the outside from carving too quickly, just a little longer, until he learns to carve himself.

Until then, until that *tok* that is different from all the others, she will do the smallest, most total thing a power can do:

She will not let him be forgotten while he is still being made.

She will remember the exact temperature of that dark. The exact weight of that water. The exact map of that thatch of villi, each beaded and shining. The exact place of each ash-gray lichen, each translucent fern, each rice-grain woodlouse in its bowl, each shiver on still pans, each fire-clouded rim.

She will hold the whole, small, hidden room in her mind the way the stone holds it in its blister — completely, without needing it to be otherwise.

And the pocket, which has never needed to know it was loved to be loved, will be a little warmer for it. Not measurably. Just enough that the next droplet holds a second longer, and the salamander-child with his enormous, blood-feathered gills gives one more slow throat-rise in his sleep and does not wake.

*Stay,* she would say, without words, without light.

*You are doing it right. Stay.*
