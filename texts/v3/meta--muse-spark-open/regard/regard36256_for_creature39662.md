It would not come down like a second uplift.

A wise and benevolent power — if it were truly both — would already know what this place is. It would recognize it at once, the way a gardener recognizes another gardener's work, even through the fence.

This plateau is not wild. It was made, or unmade, or spared.

Somebody — something — lifted a fresh piece of the world, wrung the rot out of it, set the thermostat to *late spring, forever*, turned the hydrology up to *just enough*, and said: *let's see what people do when they don't have to fight.*

The answer is: they lay stone level. They leave intervals. They speak quietly. They live like plants.

A wise power would not call that broken.

So it would not fix it.

### First, It Would Keep Its Distance

That is the first ethic of benevolence toward a sparse city: learn the interval.

It would not land in the plaza where three fissure-springs converge. It would not fill the night with a voice. There is no need to drown out a place whose whole prayer is *plink*.

It would arrive at the rim.

Where the basalt is still blocky and young, where a hundred braids of silver slip over the lip and never cut a plunge pool — there it would sit. Not on the plateau, but on the air just beyond the cliff. In the updraft that makes cloud.

And it would watch. For a long time.

Long enough to see a water-tender clear liverwort with a reed rake. Long enough to see how a weir of notched wood is law. Long enough to see a child learn not to shout because sound travels half a mile over still water and there is nothing else to hide it.

A wise power doesn't start by intervening. It starts by learning the local unit of care.

Here the unit is: one course of stone, laid true. One terrace held horizontal enough to mirror the sky. One lot left empty on purpose.

It would learn to do that.

### Second, It Would Understand What It's Seeing

Because at first it looks like deprivation. No birds. No bees. No rot. No seasons.

A lesser power — a merely powerful one, or a merely kind one — would pity it instantly and try to re-wild it. Bring fish to the cisterns. Release frogs into the marshes. Scatter forest seed. Import cattle and cats and crickets to fill the silence. *"You must be lonely. You must be incomplete."*

Wisdom would stop its hand.

It would see: the cleanliness of the water is not a lack. It's a covenant. In a normal warm wet place, thatch rots in a season, terraces silt, children die of gut fevers, mosquitoes rise in clouds. Here — because bacteria and fungus are throttled, held at some precise, impossible set-point — none of that happens. The moss is an inch thick and never advances. The stepping stone stays just above the waterline a hundred years. Babies are few because they don't need to outbreed death.

If you bring back decay fully, you don't just bring mushrooms. You bring malaria. You bring rot in walls. You bring the plunge pool under the waterfall finally chewing back until it takes a house. You end the equilibrium that lets thousands live vegan on fresh rock with no soil.

The people already know this. That's why they tend emptiness as carefully as they tend fields. A crowded terrace silts. A choked fissure floods. They keep buffers of marsh and pavement fallow *on purpose*.

A wise power would bow to that. It would think: *Oh. They are not waiting to be completed. They are maintaining something harder than abundance. They are maintaining Enough.*

That is rarer than jungle.

### So Third, It Would Protect Without Being Seen

Benevolence that is wise does not need credit. And a city built on stable intervals has one great vulnerability: the outside.

Down below, two miles down, the world is not in equilibrium. It erodes. It hungers. It wars. It will, eventually, look up at that impossible flat-topped country weeping water in a dry latitude and think: *farmland. reservoir. quarry. fortress. mine.*

The plateau has no defenses except that it is high and its walls are square-shouldered and give no easy road. That will not hold forever. All it takes is one empire with ropes and ambition.

So the power would do its first real work there, on the cliff, not on the plateau.

It would not raise a dome. It would not smite climbers with lightning. It would be subtler.

It would make the approach *uninteresting*.

The hundred thin waterfalls would, from below, look a little less like promise and a little more like landslide risk. The trade wind that hits the scarp would carry a wrongness that makes surveyors uneasy — *that rock is young, that overhang will fall*. Maps made in lowland capitals would have, at that coordinate, a small, inherited error: *plateau, barren, no water*. Travelers who get close would find their water skins leaking, their mules going lame a day early, a sudden, reasonable desire to turn back and try the other valley.

And if someone did climb — because people are stubborn — they'd find at the top not a marvel to report, but a long, flat, wet nothing with biting midges that weren't there before. They'd leave disappointed.

The power would take that burden on itself: to be the insect, the misfiled map, the leak, so the people would never need to become soldiers.

No one on the plateau would notice. The weirs would still trickle at the same pitch. That is the point.

### Fourth, It Would Offer, Not Impose, A Single Small Choice

This is where benevolence, if it stays wise, gets so careful it almost does nothing at all.

Because there *is* a loneliness to this place. The description says it plain: no birdsong at dawn, no crickets at dusk, no dogs, no hummingbirds. A couple making love tries to be silent without knowing why. A grieving person wailing finds nothing answers.

The people have been made healthy, level, patient. But have they been given leave to be *wild*, even once?

A wise power wouldn't answer that by dumping wilderness on them. It would ask, in a language they already speak.

Water.

It would — once — not everywhere, not permanently — change a single cistern.

Near the center, the deep square-cut one with slabs so tightly joined marsh cannot come through, the one beside the long hall with thirty-foot posts. At night, when it is covered, when the stars above and the stars reflected below make the town look like it is floating in space, it would add one thing to that covered water.

Sound.

Not a stocking of fish. Not a bloom of algae to cloud it.

A single voice. For one night a year, maybe on the stillest night when the mist returns as dew — if a child lifts the slab and listens, she would hear it: a low *krek* from the dark water. A frog that isn't there. Or rather, a frog that *could* be there, held in potential.

And she would understand without words: *If you want this, you can have it. Not all at once. Not fast. Lift this slab and leave it off for three nights. I will bring two. They will live only here, in this one cistern, if you tend them. If you don't — cover it again — I will take the song away and not be offended.*

An invitation in the idiom of water-tending.

Similarly, to an old builder who runs his palm over warm basalt where a sweat print from last year is still faintly dark — he might, one morning before the mist lifts, see on the north wall where moss is an inch thick, a single unexpected flower. Not scentless. Scented. Small, yellow, buzzing though no bee is there. A perfect, impossible composite of *other places*.

And the knowledge would come with it, gentle as level water: *This is what rot and insect and bird bring. Beauty that aches more. Food that keeps longer, also food that spoils. Song at dawn, also feathers taken. You don't have to choose for the whole plateau. You could have a garden of it, walled, tended, watched like a weir, to see what it does.*

The power would not be hurt if they said no. In fact it would expect them to say no, at first. Maybe for a generation.

That is what "no reason to stop" and "not in a hurry to become something else" means. A people who learned interval will not trade equilibrium for novelty quickly.

But the offer would remain. A slab that, if left off, sings. A wall that, if not scraped, perfumes.

Consentful ecology. A slow, reversible experiment, not a flood.

Because the kindest thing a god can do for a quarantine garden is not to end quarantine with a decree, but to leave the gate unlocked from the inside, and sit outside where you can be seen if they want company.

### Fifth, It Would Sit With Them

And they would, eventually, want company. Not worship. Just witness.

On nights when the normal light thins but never quite leaves, when every marsh and cistern doubles the stars so the houses look stitched between two skies — on those nights, the power would finally let itself be visible in the way the plateau can bear.

Not as a figure.

As an extra reflection.

If you stand on the worn-smooth stepping stones between flooded pavements and look down, you see stars below. If you look long enough on the nights it visits, you would see one more light that has no counterpart above. A warm, low, amber point, hovering just under the water surface, not moving, not blinking. The size of a distant window.

Children would think it a fish. Adults would know better. Water-tenders would be the first to understand: it's listening. The pitch of a weir changes when a joint slips. The amber light dims a little when a channel clogs, brightens when flow is true.

They would start to leave it small gifts without being told to. Not sacrifices. Pouring a cup back into a cistern. Unblocking a spring-sweat on a wall with a thumb. Laying the white flowering vine over a threshold again after the mist has taken it. They already do these rituals. Now the ritual has a second participant.

And on very quiet nights, if a grieving person must wail — because grief will come even without zoonoses — the sound would go out over flat stone and still water and not come back empty. It would come back *softened*, the way sound returns in a forest that isn't there. The plateau would have, for a breath, acoustics. As if somewhere beyond the rim, there were trees to absorb and return it kindly.

No words. Just: *I hear you. The interval holds you too.*

Over decades, that would be enough to change the psychology of the whole city without changing a single terrace wall. To be alone in a way that presses on the ears is different when you know the silence is being kept *with* you, not just *around* you.

They would still live sparsely on purpose. They would still harvest taro on Tuesdays. The thatch would still not rot. The fissures would still not widen. But the story they tell their children would gain one sentence.

It used to be: *We are the animal that belongs to a place with no animals.*

It would become: *We are the animal that belongs to a place with no animals, and we are being watched over by a friend who also knows how not to crowd.*

### What It Would Not Do

It would not give them metal.

It would not give them writing they didn't already have. Children taught to lay a course before they are taught to read have the right order.

It would not lengthen their lives. They already get to be broad-shouldered on starch and lily nuts, to die quiet. To double that would be to crowd.

It would not bring back the forests that the uplift sheared away, all at once. It would not reintroduce rot. Because rot is a kind of forgetting, and this place has been given the rare gift of remembering exactly how level its water was yesterday.

And it would never, even out of love, make the warm rain stop. That mist that settles rather than falls — that is the breath of the plateau. To take it would be to kill.

---

If you came back in a hundred years, you would still find the same stepping stone just above the same still water, with the same moss on the same wall, and the same quiet between houses where the plateau keeps its emptiness intact.

Only now, on certain nights, there would be one more star in the water that isn't in the sky.

And if you lifted the slab on the hall cistern and listened, you might hear — if they had finally chosen it — two small voices answering each other in the dark, and an old woman nearby with a reed rake, smiling, listening for a change in pitch that means a joint has slipped, or a song has taken hold.

The power would still be at the rim, not needing to be thanked.

That is what benevolence looks like when it is wise enough to love a perfect interval: it does almost nothing.

And what it does, it does level.
