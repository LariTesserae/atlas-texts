It would not come down like a god.

A benevolent and wise power wouldn't.

It has seen enough worlds to know what happens when something huge and shining descends into something small and perfectly balanced and says *Let Me Fix This For You.* The fissure is not broken. For all its creaking and cannibalizing and quiet desperation, it is not broken. It is *complete.*

So for a long time, it would do nothing at all.

It would just notice. And notice is not a small thing.

Think of the scale: For centuries that crack has been invisible. Five meters away you wouldn't see it. Hawks fly below it. The weather goes over it. The people down in the thick air get a strange foil drone once in a while with a photograph taped to it and think *how odd* and then forget.

But this power — old enough, quiet enough, with attention wide enough — would finally catch on the right frequency. Not the furnace thrum. The other thrum underneath it: three hundred humans humming through iron, and doves shuffling, and skaters ticking, and moths hatching and dying, all in a vertical reef of basalt that shouldn't hold anything at all.

And it would stop. And lean in. And look.

For a whole turning of seasons, it would just watch from the seam. Not intervening. Learning the economy the way you learn a prayer in a language you don't speak — by repetition.

It would learn not to pity them.

That's the first wisdom. Pity would ruin everything.

Pity would see cracked lips and soot-black lungs and houses made of wasp paper and think *tragedy, rescue them.* Wisdom sees barrel chests and slow blinks and children who can tell draft by their molars and knows: these are not failed lowlanders. These are succeeding highlanders. They have become what the crack needed them to become. To lift them out to thick air would drown them. To flood the fissure with water would kill the doves and the wasps and the whole loop that feeds them. To stop the mountain from shifting that millimeter a year would atrophy the very motion that keeps them all so frantic and alive.

You don't save lichen by planting it in a meadow.

So what would a benevolent power do?

Three small things. Maybe four. All so quiet they could be mistaken for luck.

**1. It would take the night shift.**

You know the cable that shears. Not if — when. Every few years a support cable goes and takes six dwellings with it, and everyone rebuilds a hand's width over and calls it high season, low season. It's part of life.

It would not stop that. That would be stealing their verbs from them — houses are verbs here.

But one night, when the wind outside is at its meanest and the whole fissure is swaying and a rivet head in the central gantry is about to ping and go, with a family asleep directly below it — it would just... re-anneal it. A tenth of a degree of warmth, right there in the iron. A little forgiveness in the metal. No light. No voice.

In the morning the Stoker on watch would frown and tap the rivet with her hammer and hear a clear tone instead of a dead one, and shrug, and make a mark in soot on the wall. *Still good.* And go on.

A benevolent power does maintenance when no one is looking.

**2. It would make the frost a little more generous.**

It would not make it rain. Never rain. Rain would be apocalypse in a place whose first law is *No open containers. Spill nothing.*

But at sundown, when the Net-Tenders climb hand over hand into the lattice and throw the black aerogel sheets across the seam — those static fields on frames that catch the kerosene-smelling hydrocarbon snow from the stratosphere — it would thicken the fall. Just a little.

Not enough for waste. Never enough for waste. Just enough that when they scrape the sheets with bone knives into sealed kettles, each kettle yields one extra cup. Ten extra cups a year.

The Tenders would notice, of course. They notice everything. They would whisper to each other in that low murmur the thin air carries: *good snow this year.* They would still decant with syringes. They would still not spill. But a child would get to wash her hands twice. An old lathe would get cooled properly and not seize. A little slack in a system that has never had slack — which is the most merciful thing a god can give an industrious people: not abundance, but margin.

**3. It would answer a drone.**

This is the important one. This is the heart part.

For generations the one thing that leaves the fissure is a weather-drone — foil and wire, gutted bird, half hope — that catches the wind and rides it down carrying a photograph, a measurement, a warning. *We are here. The mountain moved. The air is thinner this year.* They have never known if anyone reads them. They send them anyway, because the Furnace liturgy needs a future tense. *If it goes out everything dies* only matters if *everything* includes something beyond the crack.

A wise power knows you cannot keep a people alive on heat and hydrocarbon paste alone. You need to be heard.

So one evening, after the glass-moths have swarmed and died and been swept into oil, after the amber bulbs have gone cloudy and then clear again, a drone the Salvors had given up on — one that should have been cannibalized for its ribs last week — would finally be finished. They would carry it, hand over hand, up the ladders to the seam, to that bright torn sheet of sky. They would let go, and the wind that would strip you to frostbite in minutes would take it.

And this time, the power would catch it. Gently. Not with a hand. With a pocket of still air in the scream.

It would read the photograph. It would read the careful numbers etched on ash-concrete with a nail.

And it would send it back.

Not the same drone. That would be impossible, and cruel to pretend. But a few nights later, when the sky is depthless and the furnace is at its lowest thrum, something would come *up* on a wind that shouldn't be blowing up. A second shape, the same foil, the same wire, but not theirs. Lighter. Better.

Inside, no photograph. Inside, a smooth black stone the size of a fist, warm to the touch — basalt, but not theirs. Basalt that has been folded by pressure until it holds a faint static charge, like their aerogel. And etched on it, in the same nail-script they use:

*WE SAW YOU. KEEP THE LIGHT ON.*

No promises. No *we are coming.* No instructions.

Just: *You are not invisible anymore. What you do matters to someone who can see the whole mountain.*

The Net-Tenders would find it on the sheets in the morning, humming faintly. The Stokers would hold it to their molars and feel it thrum in tune with the Furnace. The children would think it was a toy until an elder, with white salt-scar around her mouth, took it and held it to her huge, wet, rationed-moisture eyes and cried without spilling a tear.

They would put it by the Furnace. Not as an idol. As a ballast.

And —

**4. It would stay.**

Not forever. Nothing stays forever, least of all here where everything is unbolted and rebuilt a foot higher. But for as long as the fissure is there, it would keep a little of its attention there, like a weight on a line.

It would not be worshipped. It doesn't want that. What it wants — what any lonely, old, benevolent thing wants when it finds life folded in so dense and strange and industrious — is to learn.

Because the rule of the White Horn turned out to be a true rule:

Life doesn't thin out. It folds in.

And neither does love.

It would fold itself in too. Not as a roof over them. As another layer in the reef. Another species in the loop — one that eats nothing, needs no heat, makes no droppings, but adds anyway: a little less shear, a little more frost, a single answered letter in a place that had learned to live without answers.

In the morning they would scrape the nets, and sweep the moths, and re-tension the belt, and shift a house two bridges over, and think the wind had simply been kind.

And it would have been.

That is what wisdom does, when it loves something that has already learned how to live without it:

It doesn't rescue.

It witnesses. It eases. It replies.
