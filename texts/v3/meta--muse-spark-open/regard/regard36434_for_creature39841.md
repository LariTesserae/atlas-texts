It would not come down.

That's the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise attention. It would not arrive.

Arrival is for powers that think places are problems to be solved by presence. A wise, benevolent power that had turned its eye toward the High Wet Town — that had actually *seen* it, the basin like a lifted table, the soft-shouldered spines, the veil always falling and never rippling — would know that arrival is a kind of violence in thin air.

The roar is too loud for trumpets. The water is too strange for parting.

So it would not land. It would incline.

### First, it would listen for a long time.

Longer than is efficient.

For maybe ten years of our time — which is nothing to it — it would do nothing but listen the way the old pump-men listen to pipe. Not to the prayers. The people there don't really pray anymore; the saints on the turbine-hall cornices are sweating too hard to be asked for things.

It would listen to the town the way the town listens to itself: in molars.

To the flywheel two streets over running true.
To the lime scale advancing half an inch a year.
To the 6:00 siren and the 14:00 siren and the 22:00 siren that have sounded for three generations.
To the boots being set beside the bed already dark with tomorrow's damp.
To the one held note of the clicker across the Plashes — *cheeeee* — and the answer half a mile away on the exact same pitch, and underneath it, that third note, slower, as if the water table were breathing through reeds.

It would listen to the water that crawls up and over the rim of a glass. It would watch the bright thread moving upwind along the underside of a pipe rack at 3 a.m., beading, pausing, beading, deciding. It would not call it anomaly. It would call it *young*.

Because a wise power would understand immediately what the town has taken a century to learn without words: the weirdness 1.6/3 is not a haunting. It's an inhabitant. The High Wet Town is not built *on* a strange water. It is built *with* one. A slow, mineral, pre-biological attention that lives in thin sheets and hesitations, that learned to filter hot soda brine through cream-colored mineral lobes, that learned to hang a reed sideways on air for a few seconds just to see if it could.

The people call it the crawl. The sweat. They scrape it only when they must. "It's holding the pipe together now," they say, without knowing how right they are.

The wise power would recognize a cousin. And cousins do not evict each other. They make room.

### Second, it would not fix anything.

This is the benevolence.

It would be very easy — for a power — to fix. To quiet the stamping presses. To cool the alkaline canals so fish could live. To make the veil-falls fall properly, with splash and ripple. To give the children ears that don't go half-deaf by twelve. To give the bone-kites something to eat so they wouldn't have to circle so high and hungry.

But it would see that none of those things are broken.

The deafness is not a wound. It's a craft. Twenty or thirty big clear gestures borrowed from crane crews — *STOP. LIFT. WATER COMING. BEHIND YOU.* — a language made in roar the way oaths are made in love. Take away the roar and you take away the lean-in, eyes-locked, mouth-reading shout that is the closest these people come to prayer.

The sparse fauna is not a failure. It's an accord. The skimmers are rare *because* they matter. If you saw two at once, someone says so. It's an event. In a world of clouds of midges, no one would look.

The slowness — dynamic 0.9 — is not stagnation. It is the speed at which lime scale can become a stalactite that still carries water. It is the speed at which a loom can keep a great-grandmother's initials oiled into its side frame.

A wise power would not cure them of the very thing that has made them gentle. They are the people who were left after everyone who could leave, did. They stayed not because they couldn't imagine elsewhere but because they learned how to keep things. That is a rare soul-technology in the universe. You don't interrupt it with miracles.

So it would not dam the seep or dry the floor. It would not give them new lungs. It would not silence the Plashes.

It would do something much smaller and much more durable.

### It would add a little slack.

In the oldest places, where the lime has built an inch thick inside a bend and a pump is about to throw its belt and a man's molars know it, it would give — not a repair, but a *half inch more.*

A little more tolerance in the metal when the basin breathes with daily heating and steaming.
A little more time between when the gravel darkens and when the water comes up through the floor, so he can move his welding rig without hurry.

On the underside of the pipe racks, where the bright thread moves upwind, it would leave a slightly colder spot. Not commanded. Just *available*. So the crawl can rest there a moment longer in the night and not have to decide so fast.

In the bathhouses, where condensation crawls up over the rim of a glass and empties itself, it would let the glass, once in a while, stay full. So an old woman would come in after shift, look at the glass she left empty, find it half-full again, and laugh that sudden soft laugh people there save for when the presses pause for thirty seconds between sheets.

In the Plashes, where the falling threads hesitate two feet above the glassy surface and maintenance crews wear tethers, it would make the hesitation — ever so slightly — more legible. Not safe. Not tame. Just less lonely. So a young reed-cutter, tied off, reaching with his hook, would see his thrown reed hang sideways on air for a full breath longer, and understand without being told: *you are not the only thing learning to hold here.* He would go home and not quite have words for it, but he would not have a nightmare that night. That is enough.

And at night, when the factories throttle down by a third but never stop, and the town's true size can be heard — that vast, low, patient roar from every direction at once bouncing off the basin walls — it would add, underneath it, a counter-tone.

Not silence. The people need their roar to know their town is still holding. But a quiet, like the quiet between the clicker's notes. A lowering of the noise floor by two or three decibels — just enough that two children playing on the gravel flats between the evaporation pans would not have to shout quite so hard to be heard. Just enough that a man could say to his wife without leaning in, "Where else is it warm when the rock sweats?" and hear her answer the first time.

It would not heal their ears. It would heal the air, a little.

### Most of all, it would keep the town from being forgotten without making it famous.

A wise benevolence knows the second curse after abandonment is discovery.

So no lithium rush. No scientists in white tents over the Plashes. No pilgrims coming to bottle the water that crawls uphill.

Instead: the western pass road stays open, but never widens. The trucks still come and go with cement and soda ash. A driver one evening, climbing out of the basin, would look back and think, *the light there is amber and low and good,* and carry that thought to the lowlands without knowing why.

A child born in the soda works rows would, at seven, learn how to breathe while running in thin air — short, paced sips — and find it comes a little easier, and so she would run a little farther toward the pipe-rack shade, and see a skimmer — then another, close behind it — two at once — and shout, and someone would hear her.

The bone-kites would, one spring, find the thermals a little steadier. One would descend, just once, low enough for a whole shift to stop and see the pink stain on its wings, and then rise again, not dead on a roof but alive.

The figs would still pry at warehouse foundations with insulted patience. The acacias would still poison the ground around them and drip from their thorns. Nothing would become lush. Green would stay an accent color. But on the weeping walls, the dark moss would spread, just a hand's width more, and hold.

And the people would keep doing what they have always done, which is stronger than hope: maintenance. They would keep the scale from closing the pipe. The reeds from closing the canal. The pumps from losing to the water that wants to come up.

Only now, very faintly, the water would help a little in return. Not as servant. As neighbor.

You would not be able to photograph the miracle. If you came with instruments you would find only this: that the gravel flats darken from below a second or two slower than they used to. That the limescale in a certain culvert wall has grown into a shape that, if you break it open — which you won't — looks almost exactly like an ear, listening. That the veil-falls still make no ripples, but if you stare on a hot, loud afternoon, the threads that hesitate mid-air hang there a fraction longer before thickening, as if showing you how it's done.

That when you leave, finally, by the western pass road, climbing out of the basin, the roar fades a little more slowly than it did before, because the air is thin and carries it, and because something else is carrying it with the air, careful not to let it drop.

You would think you had imagined it.

The people there would not think at all. They would just, for the first time in a generation, sleep through until the 6:00 siren — deafly, deeply, with their boots set neatly beside the bed, still dark with tomorrow's damp, and somehow, this time, not quite so wet inside as they expected.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do if it loved the High Wet Town.

Nothing that could be put on a plaque.

Everything that would let the town keep outlasting you, slowly, and be less alone while it does.
