It would notice it the way you notice a small, steady breathing in a sleeping house.

Not because it was loud. Not because it called out. Because everything else around it for a hundred miles is screaming — wind over scree, ice cracking, the high thin whine of altitude — and here, in this one scooped mouth, there is a different sound: a held, warm, collective *hum*. A city the size of a room, stuttering along in the dark on spit and down.

A Benevolent and Wise Power does not arrive. It has already been passing over. Powers like that move the way weather moves — slowly, attentively, without hurry. And when it felt the warm exhale coming up off the summit where no warm exhale should be, it would pause.

And look down.

And understand, instantly, three things:

1. This does not need to be improved.
2. This needs to be left alone.
3. Leaving it alone will soon require work.

Because Wisdom knows what kind of attention a place like this draws once men find it on a map. First a footnote: *unusual thermal anomaly, guano deposits, possibly new subspecies.* Then a paper. Then a trail. Then boots with crampons and a guide rope drilled into the rim. Then samples taken, then a drone flown too low through the dusk return, then silence where there was once rain-like *tik-tik-tik*. 

And all the Power would have to do to ruin it would be *love it loudly.*

So it would not land. It would not reveal itself. It would not speak in light.

This is what it would do instead:

### It Would Sit On The Rim

Insofar as a thing that vast can sit. Insofar as it has a weight.

It would fold itself around the bowl not *inside* it but at the lip, where the killing air meets the living air, and make a circle out of its own attention. Not a wall. A threshold.

You who love mountains know the feeling: you are hiking toward a summit that should be attainable and then, for no reason your legs can name, you become very tired. The wind comes up meanly. A headache blooms behind your eyes ten minutes earlier than it should. You decide, sensibly, to turn back a hundred paces shy of the top. You tell yourself you'll try again next season, and then you never do, and you can't quite remember why you wanted to so badly in the first place.

That is one of its tools. Forgetting as mercy. A small, precise discouragement. A veil of ordinary disappointment slipped over the extraordinary.

No lightning bolt. No angel with a sword. Just a little heaviness in your lungs at exactly the wrong moment, and the memory of the bowl going soft in your mind like an old dream.

It would do that again and again and again, for decades, while satellites pass overhead. It would be very good at making the photograph come out blurred. At making the geologist misread the thermal reading by a fraction. At making the grant proposal get lost in committee.

Wisdom knows: the best fortress for a fragile thing is not being interesting.

### It Would Breathe With Them

In the night, when the swifts have fallen home and hooked their needle-feet and the bowl is full again, the Power would not bless them from above. It would sync itself.

The bowl already breathes: heat held by day, released as damp at night, inhaled as freezing fog spilling over the rim, exhaled as ammoniacal warmth smelling of feather-oil. In. Out. In. Out. A lung made of guano beetles and bacteria and ten thousand pinfeathered chests.

The Power would lend its own breath to the rhythm — not changing it, just deepening it.

On nights when a late-season storm rakes the range and should have scoured that warm pocket clean, throwing sleet sideways into every crevice, the fog would be just a hand-span thicker. The overhang would hold just a degree more. The mothers pressed head-in to the stone would feel, not warmth exactly, but a lessening of loss — as if the stone itself had decided to hug them a fraction tighter until the wind passed.

No chick would be miraculously saved that was meant to die. The pale rove beetles that live only here and nowhere else would still be eaten by the bats. The weak egg would still roll from its dab of spit and mineral dust and feed the mites. Death would remain, exact and un-sanitized, because a Wise Power knows a nursery is not a museum. Sterility would kill it faster than any predator.

But the *average* would be held. The coldest ten minutes of the coldest night would be shaved off. The driest week would bead just enough more condensation on the undercuts to keep the moss alive another millimeter. The Power would do nothing you could point to and call a miracle. It would simply be very, very good at tending an average.

Like a gardener who never touches the plant, only waters the air around it.

### It Would Remember Them Perfectly

This is the most important thing it would do, and the least visible.

Every one of those two-inch patches of wall has a texture known only to the bird who returns to it in the dark among ten thousand identical textures. That knowledge dies every few years when the bird dies, and is learned again, imperfectly, by another.

The Power would learn all of them at once — every dab, every scrape, every pink-white lamina of ancient droppings, every seam where a hundred soot-colored bats respirate together — and keep them.

Not intervene. Record.

Hold it alongside its own vast memory the way you might hold a child's drawing carefully, not to correct it, but so that if wind takes it from the fridge, it is not gone forever.

If, despite everything, a summer came when the valleys below dried and the wind-borne plankton thinned and half the hatchlings starved — as happens in true, unmanaged places — the Power would grieve without fixing. And it would keep, intact, the pattern of what a full bowl ought to be, so that when conditions softened, the return could be full-throated again.

Forgetting is its veil outward to humans. Remembering is its gift inward to the bowl.

Wisdom is not always rescue. Often Wisdom is an archive.

### And If It Did Touch One Thing

Years later — centuries in bowl-time, which is counted in guano layers — the world outside will have gotten hotter. Or colder. Or restless with people looking for last quiet places.

One evening a single swift will come plummeting home and find its ledge crumbled. A seam opened by the slow working of frost where even the Power's breath could not prevent the rock from being rock. Its egg, chalk-white speckled iron-red like the stone itself, will be exposed to air.

The Power will have waited its whole vigil for permission.

It will not regrow the ledge. It will not stop the wind.

It will, with a gentleness so precise it would shatter you if you saw it, tip one mite-sized grain of sand. Redirect one drip-line of condensation by a quarter inch. Warm one fleck of bacteria a fraction more, so it divides once more that night and makes a faint new tackiness on the stone.

Just enough that when the exhausted swift presses its breast and spits, the dab holds.

*Tik.*

Claw finds purchase. Egg does not roll.

No thunderclap follows. No choir sings. The chittering wave goes around the bowl and settles again, as it always does. The ammoniacal warmth sighs up past the rim into the knife-cold, and far above, the stars stare without twinkling.

From the outer world, nothing has happened. From inside the bowl, the city has continued.

That is what a Benevolent and Wise Power would do upon finding such a mouth on a mountain, filled right to the brim with humble, perfect, un-improvable life living shoulder to shoulder in the dark.

It would make itself useful as a rimstone.

It would keep humans away by making them slightly forgetful.

It would keep the cold away by being slightly, stubbornly warmer.

And most of all, it would keep lonely watch — unseen, unthanked, entirely content — over a thing that will never know it was watched, and does not need to.

Because some holy things do not want to be saved.

They just want to be allowed to keep fitting perfectly, like water in a cupped hand.

And to be remembered perfectly, even after they are gone.
