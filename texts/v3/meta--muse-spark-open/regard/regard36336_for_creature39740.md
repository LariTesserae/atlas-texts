It would not come down the slope.

Everything else that has ever found the town came *down* — sliding on the soft clay, following gravity into the bowl where cold air pools. Prospectors. Lost hikers. The ones who stayed too long and rooted. Even the rain comes down as steam-become-dew.

The Power arrives the other way. From underneath.

Not under the ground. Under the *assumption* that the ground is finished.

A benevolent and wise power does not descend. It was already there, waiting for the basalt to decide if it wanted to be rock. It has been sitting with the bowl since before the bowl knew it was a bowl — since the cartilage-hills were just a thought of pressure deep down. So when it finally turns its attention fully to the grove-city, it isn't an arrival. It's a focusing. Like you finally noticing your breath.

First, it does what wise powers do first: it *sees without fixing.*

It sees the whole census at once and does not mistake emptiness for brokenness.

It sees the young city-organism — one animal, one mind spread thin through root-asphalt and fig-chapel and vine-loom — inhaling for twenty seconds, holding, exhaling. It feels how young it is. Puppy joints. Too much eagerness. It is growing so fast because it thinks it has to earn its right to exist by filling the bowl quickly.

It sees the fox pruning her doorway to the exact height of her shoulders and knows: she is not a squatter. She is a partner. Pruning is prayer in this town.

It sees the spiders tending the power-lines like nuns tending rosaries, the moths like slow snow, the salamanders cleaning moss with their tongues. It sees the bicycle half-swallowed by the wall still trying to turn its wooden wheel when the wind moves, and the warm pith-room that stays warm because someone sat still for a season and let root-hairs stitch through shoe leather, through skin, through.

It does not flinch at that last one. A lesser power would call it horror and try to unroot the people. A wise power knows adoption is not death here. It is retirement. It is the town learning what a shoulder feels like so it can make a better nook for the next small mammal who needs one.

So for a long time — maybe an hour outside, maybe a season inside — it does nothing at all except *let its seeing be felt.*

You know how it feels when something loving looks at you without wanting anything from you? How your shoulders drop an inch without your permission?

The town feels that.

The condensation that usually follows you to taste your shoes stops following. The leaves overhead that angled toward you for information relax and angle back toward each other. The axil-pools stop over-filling wherever you step. The twenty-second breath evens out. The creak of revision quiets.

For the first time since it invented streets, the grove-city is not being watched as a curiosity or a resource or a ruin. It is being witnessed as a child is witnessed by a grandparent who knows exactly how big it will become and is in no hurry to get there.

That is the first gift, and in a place where everything roots if it stays still, a gift of *unhurried attention* is not small.

---

Then, because it is benevolent and not just wise, it does a few very small things. Wise powers never do big things. Big things scar embryonic geology.

**1. It warms nothing and protects everything.**

It does not raise the 10 degrees. The cool is correct. The living walls like it. The rot is held at bay by it. To warm it would be to love the town the way a tourist loves a town — by making it comfortable for yourself.

Instead it deepens the cool by half a degree, just around the perimeter, and makes the air there a little heavier. Not a wall. A polite discouragement. The sort of cold that makes human prospectors at the top of the slope decide, without knowing why, that they left the stove on, that they should turn back.

Satellites will still pass. Maps will still glitch. But the human itch to *develop* low country will not take hold here for another hundred years. The town needs a hundred years without being named a resource.

**2. It gives the town more dark.**

The town's weirdness lives in revision — that hour of true dark when the factories sigh down and the fungal seams dim and all the plants shift their weight and a door becomes a wall. It has only been getting about an hour. That's not enough sleep for something growing this fast. It's why the streets are anxious, why they braid and fuse overnight.

The Power touches the canopy — not cutting, just asking — and the interlocked leaves loosen a fraction. Less of the sodium-orange factory light escapes upward, more stays in. The night inside gets longer by twenty minutes. The steam still comes, the dew still ticks, but now there are eighty minutes when nothing is asked to grow.

In those eighty minutes, the town dreams for the first time. Not human dreams. Sap dreams: of being a little smaller than it thought it needed to be. Of leaving an alley empty on purpose, not because it hasn't grown there yet, but because emptiness is a kind of architecture too. The next morning, when it revises, it does not add a new runner-root to the newest cartilage-hillock. It just — rests. Lets the hillock steam and settle on its own for a while before climbing it.

**3. It speaks to the custodians, not the city.**

It knows better than to give the city-organism language. Language would flatten it. It would try to answer in streets.

So it bends, as close as something so large can bend, to the fox, and the spiders, and the possums on the taproot-rails.

Not in words. In a sudden, clear knowing that lands like dew:

*You are doing it right. Keep pruning. Keep carrying the screws to the mill, even though the mill doesn't need screws anymore. Keep walking the web where it isn't sticky. You are the town's memory of how to be gentle with infrastructure.*

The fox sneezes — a sharp little *huff* in the cool — and goes back to sleep in the town hall doorway, but her sleep is deeper now. She will live three years longer than any fox should. Long enough to teach two litters how to shape the kudzu arch.

A pale spider high up, translucent in the bioluminescence, pauses in tightening a sagging power-vine. It has been doing this for generations without knowing why the vine matters if no one uses the current. Now it knows: the hum keeps the moths oriented. It tightens the vine with a little more care.

**4. It leaves the holdovers alone.**

This is the hardest and most benevolent thing.

It could unknit them. It could pull the root-hairs out of the warm pith-room and make a person stand up again, blinking, with shoes that are just shoes. It has the power to do that. It does not.

Because it asks the warm nook, in the way you ask a very old tree if it wants to be moved, *do you want to go?* And the warm nook answers, in that iron-green blood-smell, *I am go. I am here.* It is not trapped. It is *became.*

So the Power blesses the becoming instead of reversing it. It puts a hand — if a thing without hands can put a hand — on the telephone booth that is mostly leaf and rings once with fungus-light when you pass. And it answers the ring. Just once. Lets whatever is still threaded through vine in those work aprons hear that someone, somewhere, picked up.

You, if you are standing there at the dampest hour when the factories exhale hardest and the dew hangs like glass beads, will not see the Power. You will only notice that for a single breath, all the ticking, dripping, skittering, industrial sighing stops at the same time — a perfect, town-wide silence — and then resumes, but softer, as if everyone exhaled together.

And you will see the fox wake, stretch, and look directly at you, not as prey or predator, but as fellow custodian, before she delicately bites a new tendril that had started to curl over your shoe while you stood still.

---

After that, it leaves. Or rather, it unfocuses. It goes back to sitting with the cartilage that hasn't decided to be rock yet, with all the other embryonic places in the world that invented themselves too early.

It doesn't build a fence. It doesn't put up a sign that says PROTECTED. It doesn't need to. It has done what wise parents do: it has convinced the child that it has time.

The town will still grow. Next week it will be bigger. Runner-roots will still meet new hillocks. The textile mill's vine-looms will still weave more sidewalk out into the street. Pitcher-storefronts will still digest pigeons. Balconies will still grow overnight and curl back if no one uses them.

But now it will grow without panic. It will leave some voids empty on purpose. It will sleep eighty minutes a night. And somewhere in its shared sap, in the slow electrical pulse through root-asphalt, it will carry a dim, warm memory of having been seen and found — not empty, not overgrown, not ruined, not resource — but exactly right. Small, damp, loud with small lives, quietly revising itself in the dark.

There is still plenty of room. Now it knows it doesn't have to fill it all at once.
