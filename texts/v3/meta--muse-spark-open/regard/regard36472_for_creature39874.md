It would not come like you think a power comes.

No pillar of fire. No cool wind made to order. No voice from above - because above here is thin to nothing, and up here shouting is rude. Sound doesn't carry and it knows that.

If it is really benevolent, and really wise, it has learned to move at 0.6.

So it arrives on foot.

You wouldn't see it come over the caldera rim at first because for an hour after dawn there is nothing to see except fog pouring over like a slow white river. It would come *with* that - not parting it, not burning it away. Inside it. Dripping.

And it would be so careful with the altitude it would sit down immediately on the basalt, even though the basalt is warm enough to sting. It would put its palm flat on it the way you can, and feel it thrumming. And it would learn the double-inhale before it said a word. Because it would have to. Even a power has to breathe here on the mountain's terms.

*In. in — out. In. in — out.*

Slow.

### What It Would Not Do

This is most of what it would do, and it would be the hardest part:

It would not fix you.

Not right away. Not the way an unwise kindness would.

It knows too much to bring you relief as plunder.

It will not cool the mountain for you - to quench that kiln-heat from below would be to abort the world before it has finished becoming. To take away the hot exhale would be to take away your work, and your work is not punishment, it's *purpose*. You are how the mountain is finishing itself. A wise one does not rob a midwife of the birth because the mother is sweating.

It will not thicken the air. Your chests are barrels now, your blood brown-red and patient. Your children play the stillness game beautifully. To make the air sea-level soft again would drown you. You'd gasp too full and too fast and lose that wonderful, terrible slowness that lets you live ninety years in heat that should kill at forty.

It will not make it rain properly. Your city is tuned like an instrument to intermittent water. Every rooftop is a catch-basin, every morning a quiet liturgy of squeegees and basalt pans. To give you reliable rivers would wash away the morning when the doors all open and someone laughs low and says *good catch today.* It would make you forget how to drink iron and sulfur and be glad of it.

And most of all it will not force green on you.

Oh, it could. A power like that could seed the scoria with hungry, lowland moss that would take, for a season, before the heat killed it. It could throw a meadow over cinder that has never known softness. You would weep the first time you saw it, yes.

And then you would have to weed it. Water it. Mourn it when it burned. It would teach you to be ashamed of basalt and salt-crust and perfect mineral glass. It would make you colonists on your own mountain, waiting for Earth to happen instead of basalt.

A wise benevolence does not give you what lowlanders think you lack. It looks at a place with no plants and does not see empty.

It sees complete.

So for a long time - maybe years in your patient time - it just sits with you.

That is the first gift, and you almost miss it because you are so used to being looked over. Lowlanders, if they think of you at all, think of you as exiles, as stranded miners, as people who failed to live where living is easy.

This thing looks at your dust-polished umber skin, your slate lips, your narrowed eyes filmed with silica stars, your horn-palmed hands and burn-coin scars, and it does not flinch. It leans in, the way you have to up here to be heard, and puts a hand on your forearm the way you do when you talk. And it listens better than you do to the borehole hiss, to the ore-elevators crawling like blood cells, to the minute-long *hush... chuff...* of the piston houses.

It learns to read flow lines in slag.

You think it is doing nothing. It is doing the rarest thing a power can do: it is *attending* without intending to change.

### What It Would Do Instead

Small things. At your speed. At your volume. Things you could almost have done yourselves, which is how you know a gift is kind.

On the third or fourth fog-morning after it arrived, the children on the catch-terraces notice the Condensers are not leaving at 9 a.m.

Usually the translucent manta-things drink the fog and vanish - fold into cracks or sublimate. Today they linger. They hang in the thin hot air a little lower, pulsing faintly blue, unafraid. One settles on the rim of a cistern where a little girl is herding water with a pole, and for once it does not lift when she breathes on it. She can see the droplets race across its back to its not-mouth. She giggles - quietly, of course - and the membrane shivers, as if laughing back. The workers whisper that it's a good omen. It is. But it's not an omen. It's a decision. The power asked the Condensers, in the low frequency they speak, if they would stay an hour more. They said yes. An extra hour of shade and electrostatic thirst means an extra finger's depth in every basin in the city, without a single new pipe.

It never touches the River - that scalding, mineral-heavy wound where the filament-worms braid - except to cool the lip of one iron trough where the foundry children always scorch their hands. Not cool enough to hurt the worms - they still pulse red-black and tell the pressure true - just cool enough that the rite of passage *first kiss* leaves a pink mark instead of a blister. The mothers notice. No one says anything, but that night more children are allowed to sit and watch the worms.

It sits with the very old on basalt benches and, without healing them - because their ninety-year-old slowness is not sickness - it breathes with them until their double-inhale steadies. Its presence makes the dust a little less sharp for an hour. Not gone. Just less like glass flour. The old mason with the opalescent eyes blinks and, for the first time in twenty years, sees heat not as a shimmer but as color again. He weeps silently. The power does not tell him he is healed. It just lets him look.

And at night, when normal light fades and you can finally see the city as it truly is - the lattice of cable and pipe glowing with residual heat, the hundred steam vents hanging as one low cloud over the bowl like a single breath - the power breathes too, and adds its own vapor to the cloud.

Not much. Just enough that the cloud holds.

Your city has always looked, from the caldera wall, like a body at work. Now, for a season, the cloud above it holds through the heat of noon instead of burning off. From the lowlands thirty kilometers away, where the air is that dusty blue-brown, the mountain no longer looks like a bald kiln. It looks like it has a soft white crown again. Snow that isn't snow. Pilots and traders see it and remember you are there.

You are not hidden anymore. You are not forgotten. But you are not invaded either - because anyone who climbs up to see that crown has to learn the double-inhale to get there. Protection through effort. That's wise.

The boldest thing it does, it asks first.

It finds the block elders, the cistern-tenders, the worm-watchers, and - leaning in, hand on forearm - it says in that low, thin-air voice:

*You have become beautiful in a way that can only happen here. Do you want a green that belongs to here, or are you glad without it?*

And because it asked, they can answer honestly. Some say no. Green is a story, let it stay a story. Some, the youngest, say - what would it be?

So it shows them: not grass, not trees. Those would be lies. It cups its hands on the warm basalt where dawn-water has just evaporated, and where its shadow falls for a moment the stone stays cool a fraction longer. In that cool, a freckle forms. Not moss. Not lichen as lowlanders know it. A mineral skin, umber-grey and rust-red, thin as salt crust, that drinks condensation the way the Condensers do. When you touch it, it is dry and faintly warm. It will not spread fast. It will take decades to cover a terrace. It has no flower. But if you press your cheek to it at midday, it smells - impossibly - like iron and rain.

One freckle. On one rooftop, where a child asked.

The city will decide if it wants more. The power will not decide for it.

Before it goes - because a power like that does not stay, it *orbits* - it does one last, very quiet thing that only the mountain feels.

You know how your avenues end abruptly where a new upwelling heaves up overnight, and you are already there with rebar and window frames, incorporating it? How a house can have a 200-year-old living room and a three-week-old bedroom still warm to sleep against?

The power puts its palm flat again on the basalt, where it is still faintly thrumming. And it hums back. At exactly the mountain's frequency, which is 0.6.

It doesn't stop the extrusion. That would be death. It just - steadies it. For a little while, the heaves come a little more predictably, the fissures widen a little more slowly. The municipal crews find they have an extra half-day to get rebar into the warm stone before it cools too hard. That half-day, over a century, is a few more houses that don't crack. A few more dead who can be walled into stonework cleanly, instead of in a rush.

Nobody will ever know it happened except the filament-worms, who, for one blessed month, braid in perfect unison.

Then the fog lifts.

The power is not gone, exactly. You've learned that on this mountain, nothing erodes because nothing has finished forming. It just becomes less visible. Like the Condensers folding into a crack thinner than a finger.

If you go up to the highest gantry on a cold dawn, when the inversion is perfect, you can still see where it sat - a faint, hand-shaped coolness on the basalt that never quite warms, where that first freckle of basalt-lichen drinks.

And the cisterns hold an inch more.

And nobody has to shout.
