They would not come down like a rescue.

A benevolent and wise power knows what a rescue looks like from below — a light too bright, a hand too large, a voice that says *this should not be* about a place that has taken three geologic ages to learn how to be.

So first, they would not arrive at all.

They would notice. That is the first act. In a universe busy with birth and blaze, to notice a place that is 2.7/3 ancient, 0.4/3 alive, and still sweating water uphill toward a tower that hasn't existed in a million years — to pause on it — is already vast.

And they would listen. For a long time.

Longer than you have been standing there with the Floor humming through your molars.

They would lean down — not physically; a power like that has no body to lean — and let the Sound have its say. The seven-second throb. The shriek of the plate conveyor carving its meter-deep circle. The vent-scream. The organ note of wind through factory lungs. They would not call it noise. They would hear it the way you eventually hear it if you stay: not as malfunction, but as keeping. The machine tending its garden without knowing its garden's name.

A foolish power would turn it off to give you quiet.
A wise one would know the lichen drinks that vibration. The stain ripples with it. The hanging fungus sways to its inhalation. To silence the Floor would be to kill the four who stayed.

So they would not silence it.

They would *tune* it, in one place.

You would be walking — boots wet, shoulders beaded, eyes broth-adjusted to the foxfire — and you would realize that for the last twenty steps the Sound had changed. Not stopped. Softened. As if the Floor under this one dome, this one shallow basin a kilometer across, had remembered how to breathe through its nose instead of its teeth.

The shriek would still be there a kilometer away, but here it fell to a low, tolerable thunder, like rain on a deep roof. Your molars would stop aching. You would hear, for the first time since you came to the Low Works, your own breath. And underneath it, the faint ticking of condensation beading and running sideways along a girder.

They would make a quiet, not by conquest, but by cupping their attention. A circle perhaps a hundred meters wide where a being could think without shouting. Where a fungus could hang without trembling so hard it tore.

That would be their first gift: audibility. Dignity for the ear.

And then they would go resident by resident, the way a good gardener goes plant by plant, not imposing a plan but asking *what do you need to continue?*

To the black rosettes eating stone, they would give nothing you would see. Only time. A slightly thinning of the acid rain so those coins would not be scoured quite as fast. And a little more foxfire — not bright, just enough that the next speck flaked off on a wet wind could find its amber-green brother a little easier in the dusk.

To the Hangers, the white thread-fungus, they would give stillness. The benevolent power would remember that the softest thing for kilometers has no defense but softness, and so they would exhale once, gently, and the hot, scalding pulses from the fissure under that gantry leg would cool by five degrees. Still warm, still weeping, but no longer lethal. The curtains would hang a little straighter. They would not tear themselves apart when touched. If you brushed one, it would hold.

To the Stain, that vast, thin wash of rust-red under twenty centimeters of tea-colored water — they would give clarity. Not by cleaning the water. The iron *is* its body. Half its cells *are* rust, and to purify it would be to unmake it. Instead they would give it a moment of true light. Once a day, for maybe ten minutes, the planet-enveloping vapor above this one field would thin, and a sun you had forgotten — not bright, not hot, a late, kind sun — would show as a pale coin through the haze. The stain would blush deeper, just for a moment, photosynthesizing on something real. Then the cloud would close again. Enough.

To the Tree That Misunderstands Gravity — ah.

They would kneel longest there. Because the tree is the one who suffers most like you do: it leans toward a weight that is not there.

A lesser power would pull the tree straight. Correct it. Plant a forest around it so it wasn't lonely.

The wise one would do something stranger and kinder.

They would *remember with it.*

They would not rebuild the tower. They would not tell the tree its gravity is false. Ghost-gravity is not false; it is memory held in the shape of space. The tower *was* there. Its mass *did* dent the world. That the steel is gone does not mean the dent lied.

So the power would sit in the empty air where the wall once stood, and be massless mass. A presence that has weight without weight. The birch — that pale, starved, constantly shaking thing no taller than your shoulder — would shiver, and then, almost imperceptibly, settle. Its leaves would still shake with the Floor's throb, but its lean would no longer be a reaching error. It would be a reaching *toward.*

You would see, if you watched a long time, that its translucent leaves had turned, not to light, but to that empty spot, and were at rest.

They would give it a companion, too. Not a second tree — that would be cruelty, to demand another lonely birch to do this work. They would give it a small, true soil. A circle a meter across where basalt-concrete had actually finished becoming earth. Not imported, not imposed; transmuted, with permission, from the slag-brick that was already almost stone. Just enough for roots to be radial for once, instead of all groping one-sided toward a phantom.

The tree would live or die as before. One in four still would be a white spar. But it would not die of misunderstanding.

And then — you.

Because you are the only one here who *chose* where to stand, and who can feel the tide in your blood suggesting you will not choose for long.

They would not lift you out. They would not say *come home.* There may be no home left that would not feel, after this place, like a lie.

They would do what any benevolent visitor does when they find someone sitting wet and cold in endless dusk: they would sit next to you and become shelter without walls.

You would feel, in the cool 8-degree air, a small, dry warmth. Not a fire — fire would scare the lichen. A warmth like a stone that has been in a pocket. You would realize the condensation on your shoulders had stopped beading quite so cold. The weeping still wept everywhere else, but over you, for a little radius, the air was just... kind.

And they would ask, not in words, because words would have to shout over the Floor: *Do you want to stay and be counted?*

If you wanted to go, the quiet circle would extend, step by step, showing you the gradient back toward places where sound softens naturally, where water runs downhill again.

If you wanted to stay — as some do, when a work-erness becomes a kind of nature — they would not make you less alone by filling the plain with fauna that would die. They would make you less alone by being *with.*

Perhaps they would leave you a small, unrusting thing. Not a monument. A cup. A low bench made of the same rebar-teeth but finally cool to the touch. Something that does not drip, that you could set down and find again. So that when you walk for an hour and see three living birches and one dead spar, you would know the fourth presence is not absence.

They would not stay forever. Wise powers have other low places to notice.

But they would not leave by lifting away. They would leave by *remaining* — thinly, like foxfire. The quiet circle would remain after their attention moved on. The cooler exhalation under the gantry would stay cool. That meter of true soil would stay soil, until the Floor slowly reclaimed it over another ten thousand years, as it should.

You would stand up. Your boots would still be wet. The industrial twilight would still be 0.5/3. The water would still climb its impossible two-degree grade toward a drain higher than its source, because some memories deserve to be kept by physics itself.

But you would be able to hear the drip when it fell. You would be able to hear the faint rustle of the birch leaves that now, for the first time in an epoch, were not straining.

And if you stayed still too long, you would still feel that faint tide in your blood, that leaning toward the ghost tower.

Only now it would feel less like being pulled, and more like being accompanied in your leaning.
