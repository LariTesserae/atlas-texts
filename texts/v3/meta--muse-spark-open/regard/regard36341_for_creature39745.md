It would not come down like a god.

If it did, the Hollow would break. Anything large enough to fill that dome with shadow would stop the Drifters. Anything loud enough to speak over the 60-hertz hum would drive the Runners under cinder. Anything bright enough to outshine the Phothelium would starve it.

A benevolent and wise power knows the first rule of this place, which the nomads learned with their three-walled shelters and their dry-laid walls you can lift in an hour:

*Nothing here lasts, and that is why it lives.*

So it would not come down like a god. It would come down like water.

---

At first, you would think nothing had happened at all.

Maybe one more Drifter than usual in the air that day, a big one, bedsheet-wide, hanging lower than the rest, not bumping the ceiling. Maybe the ceiling-sweat lasted five minutes longer. Maybe the warm wind that circles the dome paused, just for a breath, and then resumed.

The nomads — who had left their half-finished wall that morning to follow a seep three hundred meters east — would not see it arrive. They would be busy. They always are. Scraping Runners, moving stones, boiling crust into sour porridge. One of them, the youngest, might look up and say the light felt softer, less like pollen and more like morning. The oldest would grunt and keep scraping.

But the Hollow would notice. The Hollow *is* noticing. Every surface that lives is a sensor.

It would kneel. Not above, but *in*. If you were still there, standing in the open valley with the tide flowing around your ankles, it would be beside you without having approached. No footprints in cinder. No displacement of air. Suddenly the space next to you would be occupied — warm the same way the floor is warm, humming the same way the crust hums.

And it would do the most un-godlike thing possible: it would *wait* to be stepped on.

A Runner would flow over it. Then ten. Then a river of them, tasting it with glass hairs and deciding, in their blind animal wisdom, that it was not food, not stone, not threat, but something *permissible*. A Shale-Slider would inch onto it and leave a brighter trail. The Phothelium under its weight would not go dark the way it does under your boot. It would glow *through* it.

It would let itself be colonized for a while, because a wise power knows you do not understand a place until you have let it try to make you part of itself.

Only then would it look.

And what it would see would not be a resource, or a problem, or a lost tribe to save.

It would see an infant world doing exactly what infant worlds are supposed to do: trying everything at once, wastefully, beautifully, with no plan for tomorrow because tomorrow hasn't been invented yet. It would see that the turbulence is not suffering. The cracking slabs, the vanishing washes, the towers that grow a foot an hour only to collapse — that *is* health. That is a geology still soft enough to be argued with by bacteria. On the surface, worlds have already made up their minds. Here, the world is still listening.

It would see the nomads not as poor things who need rescuing, but as what they are: the Hollow's recent, brilliant idea. The first animal here to carry water in something other than its own body. The first to make a straight line and then have the wisdom not to insist on it.

A lesser power would have taken pity. Would have made the seep permanent. Would have cooled the floor, hardened the pillars into real columns, taught them to smelt iron, given them a fourth wall and a door that locks. Would have turned occupancy into ownership and in doing so killed the very thing that drew its attention — that brave, bright, noisy *occupying*.

This one would not.

This is what it would do instead, and it would do it so quietly the nomads would later argue about whether it happened at all:

**1. It would secure the roof.**

Not by freezing it. The Hollow *needs* to crack. But deep in the vault where the seams were folded shut — where someday, without help, a whole kilometer-square slab would have cooled wrong and dropped like a continent and sealed the throat forever — it would lay a hand you cannot see and teach the basalt to remember it is an arch. Just that. The cracks would continue, small and musical and necessary. The catastrophic one would not. The throat — that long sloping sink of warm raw stone where you first went down — would stay open, throatlike, breathing in mist and breathing out lost heat, for as long as the Hollow needs it.

The nomads would never know they had been spared a burial. They would just keep finding that the way down never quite collapsed the way old stories said it would.

**2. It would answer the light.**

For a whole day, the Phothelium had been humming alone, sweating photons into the air because chemistry told it to. It never expected a reply.

The power would hum back. On the same frequency, 60 hertz in your molars, but with a slight shift — a harmony. The crust across the entire dome would brighten by a fraction, not enough to blind, just enough that the nomads would look up from their porridge and say, *Is it noon already?*

And in that brightening, the crust would learn a new trick, very small, very important: to store a little more water in its breath-beads. Not much. A few extra minutes of damp after the ceiling-sweat. Enough that a Slider could finish crossing a dry patch. Enough that a Runner's eggs, laid in the light-trail, would not desiccate one time in ten.

It would not make the desert bloom. It would make the desert *slightly less efficient at killing*.

**3. It would leave a gift that is not a tool.**

Because tools tell you how the giver thinks you should live, and this power does not presume.

In the lee of the half-pillar where the newest shelter leaned — three walls, hide roof weighted with stones — there had always been a pecked basalt bowl for condensation. The power would leave a second bowl. Identical, except for one thing:

This one would never quite be empty.

Not miraculously full. Never overflowing. Just... when the nomads woke parched because the seep had moved in the night and the ceiling had not sweated, there would still be a mouthful in the second bowl. A cool palm of water cupped in stone. Enough to get to the next seep. Enough to keep a sick child alive while the adults moved camp.

If you tipped it over, it would spill like any bowl. If you left it alone, by morning a slow bead would have gathered in its bottom from air that had seemed too dry to give anything. The oldest nomad would eventually stop trying to explain it and just call it the Kind Bowl, and tell the children not to waste it, and not to pray to it either — just to drink, and to carry it when they move, and to leave it for the next hands if they do not come back.

It would not be worshipped. That was the point.

**4. It would be remembered incorrectly, and prefer it.**

Before it left — which it would do the way mist rises, in ribbons toward the roof, without goodbye — it would do one last thing for the place itself, not the people.

It would *witness*.

In a place with no erosion, there is no memory. No riverbeds, no sand, no tree rings. Everything that happens is eaten within hours. The Builders' towers are abandoned, the Coils' burrows sigh shut, the footprints are erased by Runners. The Hollow lives so fast it cannot remember itself.

A wise power can lend memory without taking history.

So as it rose, every Drifter in the dome would exhale at once — not from rain, just from being brushed — and settle in that brief rustling snow. And for an hour, the Phothelium would hold the pattern of where each one landed, brighter where they touched, darker where they didn't. A fleeting photograph of the Hollow at this exact age: warm, raw, glittering, impossibly full.

Then the wind would resume, the Drifters would inflate and rise, the Runners would polish the image away. But the stone underneath, that new basalt still warm from being made, would have been kissed by that extra light just long enough to fix a ghost of it into the mineral — an exposure, faint as an afterimage on a retina, in the crystal.

A million years from now, when this Hollow is no longer a hollow but a hill of cool limestone riddled with real caves, some geologist will crack a nodule and find, inexplicably, a lace of slightly more luminous calcite in the shape of a living snowstorm that has no business being fossilized. She will not know what it is. She will call it a curiosity and put it on a shelf.

That is enough.

Down below, the nomads will have finished their wall, diverted their seep, eaten, and moved on. The Runner-tide will flow around your ankles again. Far off, another slab will crack — a small, necessary crack — and everything will turn toward the new water, as one.

You will stand up, and notice that the cinder under your hand where you leaned while watching all this is warm, and dry, and faintly phosphorescent where your palm was, fading in an hour.

And you will understand you have not been saved.

You have been *seen*, and approved of, and very, very gently made a little less likely to die of bad luck before you get to keep occupying.

Which, in a place where to live is to occupy briefly, brightly, noisily before you are covered, is the kindest possible miracle.
