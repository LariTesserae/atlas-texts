If something benevolent and wise noticed this place — truly noticed it, the way you notice a small, perfect thing held in someone's cupped hands — it would not come down like a landing. 

There would be no light in the sky. No voice. The second sun would not be chased away. That is what a lesser power would do: arrive, correct, improve.

A wise one would hesitate at the edge. And because it is wise, the hesitation itself would be the first blessing.

---

It has seen a thousand young worlds, all noise and hunger and becoming. This one is different. This one *finished* becoming an eon ago. It didn't die. It settled. Like an old monk who has breathed so long his breath has worn a groove in the stone beneath him.

The Power would look first not at the people — though it loves them — but at the Green.

Because to any eye that can see long enough, the Green is the soul here.

A whole continent that decided, in the absence of teeth and wings, to learn how to be an animal anyway. The moss creeping an inch a day to cover a bootprint. The black reeds leaning toward the heat of a passing hand because there has been nothing warm to lean toward for ten thousand years. The spores that do not drift but *choose*, riding invisible ladders of charge toward damp stone.

Most gods would feel pity: "No birds. No children laughing loud." This one would feel awe.

> *Oh,* it would think. *You kept going. You found another way to be busy without being cruel.*

So the first thing it would do is nothing that looks like doing.

It would *attend*.

On a morning when every basin exhales its sea of fog and the people are inside, drinking warm water, waiting for the lift, the Power would simply be there in the fog. Not as a shape. As a quality of attention in the air itself. The kind of morning where the dew beads a little rounder on the bladder-plants. Where the ticking of the dwarf willow contracting sounds a little less lonely.

The Mat would feel it first. The Green has no nerves, but it has awareness the way a continent has weather. For the first time in a million dawns, its endless, silent work of growing and creeping and closing would be *witnessed* without needing to be useful.

That is not small. To be seen without being asked to change is a kind of healing few places ever get.

The Starflowers that day would pop open all at once when the sun hit — and stay open a second longer than usual, as if holding a breath.

### Then it would look at the people.

It would love them immediately, and without illusion.

They are not tragic. They are not primitives waiting to be saved. They are *finished* in the best sense — compact, ruddy-cheeked, quiet people who learned how to build a house that will stand until stone softens, who learned to carve a map that is deliberately wrong so no child walks into the place where their voice arrives before their mouth moves.

They do not shout because their lungs cannot afford it. They do not build tall because they have learned not to poke the sag. This is not ignorance. This is a very deep etiquette with reality.

A foolish benevolence would give them things: thicker air, louder voices, straight horizons, animals. "Let us fill your silence! Let us bring you birds!"

A wise benevolence knows that to fill their silence would be to evict them. Their houses are shaped by silence the way a river stone is shaped by water.

So it would not give them birds.

It would give them something much smaller and much more durable.

On an evening when the sky gets its depth-behind-itself and the slate chimes tinkle without wind, a child in one of the innermost rings — the smallest house, half-sunk, sharing walls for warmth — would cough in that way high children do. That thin-air rattle that never quite leaves. Her mother would put a hand on her broad chest and hum, low, close to the ear, the way they tell all stories here.

And the hum would catch.

For once — just once in a generation — the air would *carry it*. Not far. Not loud. But true. Across the pink gravel street, without thinning. To the old woman carrying fern bundles, who would stop, and hear it clearly, and smile without turning around, because she knows not to make a big thing of a miracle.

The child would take a deeper breath than she has ever taken before. Not because her lungs were changed. But because the air, for an hour after dusk, remembered how to hold sound like it did when the world was younger. Because someone kindly reminded it.

The bells in the Bell Tower that night would ring — not that sour, stillness-tone they use as a warning. A clear tone. One clear bell, from one basin. Then, a minute later, an answer from a basin forty miles away, where no sound should ever carry. Then another.

Not a conversation. A lullaby. *We are here. We are here. We are here.* In amber bowls of light across the vast dark.

In the morning, the air would be thin again. As it should be. But the people would remember that their quiet was heard.

### And then it would tend to the wound, but not by closing it.

This is what wisdom knows: not every thin place wants to be thickened.

The plateau has worn a hole in the sky by breathing against it for so long. Light pools. Shadows arrive late. Water runs uphill for a yard to remember something. A second sun walks yesterday's path.

A lesser power would patch it. Plaster over the sag. Make shadows behave.

The wise one would kneel — if such a thing can kneel — at the edge where the blue sags down to touch the basalt, and it would do what the people do with their prayer-troughs. It would not command the water which way to go. It would *remind* it.

It would put a hand — not a hand — on the place where straight lines won't stay straight, and it would not fix it. It would *line* it. The way you might line a well-loved coat that has worn thin at the elbows. Not so you can't see the elbows. So the elbows don't tear.

So that gravity is still lenient, but never cruel. So that a thrown stone hangs a fraction too long at its apex — long enough to be beautiful — but always falls. So that your shadow might still arrive a second late, hurrying to catch up to your feet, but it *does* catch up. So that the courtyard where your voice arrives before you speak remains empty, but it no longer makes children dizzy if they run past it.

It would leave the depth-behind-the-sky at dusk. How could it take that away? That is where the spores swim like they are in liquid. That is holy.

But it would make sure the deeper night showing through day never widens while the people are watching. It would watch *for* them, so they don't have to.

That is the difference between a guardian and a jailer. A jailer closes the door. A guardian sits by it so the children inside can sleep without listening for it.

### Before it goes — because a power like that never stays — it would leave one gift that can be refused.

Not in the cistern. Not in the aqueduct. In the Mat itself.

At the edge of the outermost wall, where the old lichen-covered basalt sinks to its knees in moss, a new kind of lichen would appear in the spring. Not big. Pale gold, almost the color of the paper sun. Soft to the touch even in dry cold. If you brushed it, it would not shatter like cup lichen. It would give a little, warm.

It is good for nothing the people already need. You can't eat it. You can't burn it. But if you press a sprig of it to a child's throat on a bad-air night, the cough eases. If you line a boot with it, your feet stay warm on a day-long ridge crossing. If you steep it, it makes water taste a little sweeter. And if you sit very quietly in a basin at noon — the hour when the breakdown is almost invisible — and you put your ear very close to it, you can hear, very faintly, not words, but the *feeling* of being listened to. The way the Green felt that first morning in the fog.

Some basins would take it. Some would weed it out, suspicious of any change. Both would be right. A wise gift knows how to be left alone.

And then it would go.

Not upward. Wise things don't have to go up. It would thin itself out until it *was* the damp. The afternoon dusting of snow that never accumulates. The hiss of a spring disappearing into basalt. The pop of starflowers opening.

The people would not have a new word for god. They already have a word for horizon that also means deception, and that is close enough.

But that winter, the maps carved on the cistern walls would change, just a little. The streets that bow out around nothing would still bow — but someone, with no memory of deciding to, would carve a tiny, careful bell in the empty courtyard. And next to it, a tiny sprig with five points.

*Listen here,* the map would now say, without knowing it says it. *Someone heard you here. It is safe to be quiet here.*

And the plateau — ancient, level, breathing fog in and out, green and busy under stars so close they throw shadows — would continue. Not saved. Not improved. Just, for the first time in an eon, not entirely alone with its beautiful, tired sky.

Which, for a place that finished becoming itself a very long time ago and simply continues, is everything.
