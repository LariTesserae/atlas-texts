They would not come.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power — if it is truly wise — has learned that to arrive is to disturb. That the first violence, however soft, is attention itself. So they would not descend. They would not part the light column at noon. They would not stand on the slab and cast a shadow in that place that has so carefully learned how to have almost no shadows.

They would stop at the rim.

You would not see them stop, because stopping is not something they do with feet. But there would be, for a little while, a different quality of looking from the scrubland above. The wind that usually scrapes across that dry plain would go still over that one oval. The heat would collect differently. The swifts, coming in on their sickle paths, would bank half a meter wider for an afternoon, without knowing why, and then resume.

From the rim, they would look down and understand in one glance what a human researcher would take three seasons to inventory:

That this is not a cave that failed. This is a bowl that succeeded. A place that made a pact with light instead of darkness, and kept it for ten thousand years.

That the emptiness is not absence but practice.

That the thing which uses the stones is not trapped here. It is *rendered* here, the way a certain fern is only rendered on a single damp ledge, the way the light-pool is only rendered when the air is damp and still. Take it elsewhere and you would not be rescuing it. You would be erasing it.

So the first thing they would do is nothing.

And that nothing would be precise and active. It would be a particular kind of not-doing that takes more care than doing.

They would not clear the throat of the sinkhole to make it grander. They would not fill the palm-hollow with permanent water to make it kinder. They would not make the fig grow faster, or give the lizards more pattern, or teach the frogs to sing. To do any of that would be to tell the hollow it was lonely or incomplete. It is not. It is in balance.

They have seen enough worlds to know that the rarest thing is not abundance but a stable, self-agreed solitude. A little economy that closes its loop.

Why would they break it with kindness?

---

The second thing they would do is also almost nothing, but you could feel it if you knew how to feel for it for a long time.

They would veil it.

Not with a wall. Not with thorns or a sign that says KEEP OUT. Wise powers do not use walls; walls make people curious.

They would veil it with uninterest.

The satellite pass that would have mapped it with perfect clarity would have a smear of cloud in exactly that pixel. The geologist with grant money to look for limestone water would find his funding rerouted to a sinkhole two valleys over that was just a little more promising. The goatherd whose animals wander near the lip would decide, for no reason he could name, that the forage on this side of the scrub is not as good, and turn back. The hiker who does come to the lip, who does look down, would see something pretty and bright and *uncompelling* — “oh, a light place with some lizards” — and would not feel the tug to scramble down and overturn the stones. His attention would slide off it like water off wax.

Only the ones who could be trusted to sit for a whole day and breathe quietly and not need to take a stone home would find themselves able to stay. For them, the hollow would remain exactly as discoverable as it is now: you have to almost stumble into it and then choose to kneel.

This is what benevolence does when it is wise: it does not post angels with swords. It posts a gentle, persistent suggestion in the minds of the hurried: *not this one. There is nothing for you here. Go on.*

And in the minds of the slow: *you may come, if you come slowly.*

That is how it would protect the creature without ever domesticating it with protection.

---

The third thing they would do, they would do only for the resident itself, and only inside its own time.

At some point — perhaps at midday, when the light column is planted hard on the slab and the midges are hanging in that thickened air — the power would let a little of its own weight into the light-pool. Not to speak. This being does not have language as we have it. It has stone, and cut, and waiting, and the coolness of basalt after it has been held.

So the power would speak in those materials.

It would not say “I see you.” To be seen too directly would be terrifying for something whose whole survival has been being the exact color of stone.

Instead, it would confirm the pact the place has already made: *Continue.*

It would ensure that the condensation continues to bead for another ten thousand years, even as the climate above grows more erratic and hot. It would keep that slow drip on its dark track, grain by grain, even if the rains stutter. It would keep the stone sweating, the algae velvet alive in the north wall, the fig from giving up. Not by miracle — by tending. By nudging the path of a summer storm half a degree south so it spills for an hour over this precise bowl. By letting the deep aquifer exhale just a little more here than elsewhere. Small climate mercies, within natural variation, that you could never prove were mercies at all.

And for the knapper itself?

It would give it more of what it already has: time.

The light-pool, left to itself, might have thinned over the next millennium as dust stopped falling from above, as the walls weathered a little smoother and reflected a little less. The hollow might have slowly become just bright, no longer *thick*. And then the being — which can only work inside that viscosity, which can only think at that speed without being torn by hurry — would have nowhere to live.

So the power would feed the pool. A little more dust, held suspended. A little more slowness, so that a flake falls even more reluctantly, so the midges hang even more perfectly still. So that the hammerstone, when it is picked up, stays warm longer after it is set down. So that the creature can strike one perfect flake a season for another ten thousand seasons without ever feeling that the world is accelerating away from it.

If you or I entered that pool after the blessing, we would feel nothing dramatic. Perhaps the air would seem a little more kind. Perhaps we would find ourselves breathing slower without trying. Perhaps, kneeling by the basalt tools, we would feel for a moment that it would be rude to hurry.

That is the gift: an extension of rudeness against hurry.

And maybe — very rarely, when no human is there to misinterpret it — the power would sit with the being for an hour in the only way that is honest: by also becoming slow. By contracting its vast, benevolent attention down to a circle a meter across, and letting itself also be a thing that waits all day for a single midge and calls that abundance. Not to teach. Not to be worshipped. Just to keep company in the hollow's native tempo.

You would never know it happened. Afterwards there would just be one more chert flake on the slab, struck at an angle slightly more beautiful than necessity requires. Or a fig fruit cut open and set down not only eaten but *offered*, with the seeds left tidy.

---

And then they would leave it.

They would not lift it up. They would not name it. They would not reveal it to the world as proof that wonder persists. Because wonder that needs a press release is not wonder, it is marketing.

The hollow would remain small, bright, warm, damp, nearly empty, full of quiet lives, holding a little light in its cupped hands until the sun moves past.

And below the surface of the world, in a room that never learned how to be dark, a limestone-colored life would go on picking up its river stones — carried from a river it has never seen, for reasons it has never needed to explain — and striking them once, well, inside a pool of light that has learned, now, how to be a little thicker and stay a little longer.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it truly loved this place:

Almost nothing.

And that almost-nothing would be enough to let the place continue being exactly and unapologetically itself, undisturbed, unowned, unhurried, for as long as rock will hold light.
