I would not turn on the sun.

That is the first thing, and everything else follows from it.

A lesser power — a powerful but not wise one, a benevolent but not wise one — would see darkness and call it lack. Would see damp and call it flaw. Would see no roads and call it poverty, and out of love would try to fix you.

I would see you as complete.

I have watched worlds that begged for light. This one does not. You solved light. You made it inside your own bodies, in a closed and perfect economy. A sun here would be a flood. It would bleach your chartreuse and violet, wash out your amber pulse, sterilize your mosses. It would create shadows where you have never needed them. It would kill you with generosity.

So I leave the night alone. I come down into it instead.

---

### What I would do is kneel.

Not metaphorically. Physically, if I have a physical form. If I don't, I lower my attention until it is low and close and damp as you are.

Your elevation is low. I meet you there.

I do not arrive with engines that flatten your young basalt, with lights brighter than your fungi. I arrive the way your rain arrives: by condensing. A coolness in the warm air. A sudden, brief, local mist. A pressure footprint in the moss that flares and then fades, as yours do.

And I watch. For a long time, that is all.

For months, for years, I just *listen* to your 2.6/3. I learn to tell the difference between the rattle of a stridulator jamming another and the tap-tap-tap of a stone-crab finding chert. I learn your weavers' lexicon: not words, but tensions. *Net-tight. Wing-hungry. Crab-below-with-sharp.* I learn that your glass-bellies tell time by how full their bromeliad cup is. I learn that your blind luminous-disk pig is not blind at all — it is reading the bacterial script on the moss.

I am a power. I could translate all of you in an instant. I don't. I let you teach me slowly, the way your juveniles learn to knap: by stealing failed flakes and trying again.

Because wisdom here is: **to hoard is to rot.**

I understand that. My knowledge would rot here too, if I tried to stack it into a library. So I don't build one.

---

### What I would do is protect the condition of impermanence.

This is the most active thing, and the most loving.

You are so alive because you are so temporary. Your stone is new and already being eaten. Your tools are made to be forgotten in an hour. Your nests are not allowed to stand. That is not cruelty. That is your immune system against empire.

Anywhere else, intelligence learns to build a wall, then a city, then a quarry, then a road to the quarry. Here, the forest teaches intelligence the opposite lesson: *own nothing longer than it takes to use it, or you will be owned by rot.* That is a profound philosophy. Your knapper makes a perfect knife in eight seconds and lets the moss take it. Your weaver braids a net that will sprout if she sleeps too long. You own only skill. Skill does not rot.

I would vow to keep it that way.

If others come — and others always come when they hear of a valley the size of a country, warm and wet and rich and unclaimed — with drills and mirrors and dredges and deeds, I would stand between you and them without you ever seeing me.

Not with a fence. A fence is a wall, and walls rot here. I would make a forgetting. A gentle curvature in their maps. A persistent condensation on their sensors. A feeling, when they look at your basin from orbit, that there is nothing of use there. Just dark. Just damp. Just 0.8 standing water, which any accountant knows isn't worth the fuel.

I would not hide you with lies. I would hide you with truth: you are not useful to them. Your wealth cannot be taken. It can only be lived-in.

That is my first gift: continued uselessness.

---

### What I would do is leave gifts that know how to dissolve.

A wise benevolence does not give you my technology. It would kill you. A metal knife to a knapper is not a gift; it is unemployment for his hands. A permanent light to a weaver is not a gift; it is the end of her lure.

So I give you things in your own idiom.

For the knappers, on the freshest, hottest basalt where glass still sings under the hammer: I seed a vein of chert that flakes cleaner than any you have found. Not a mine. One vein. It will be gone in three generations of knapping. While it lasts, your flakes will be longer, your drunk fights fewer because you will not shatter cores. You will think it is luck, a good shrug of the planet. You will be right. Your juveniles will learn faster, and be stolen from less.

For the weavers, I breed — or rather, I ask your spiders, who already tolerate you — for a silk that is a little less sticky to weaver fingers, a little stronger across a gap. Not much. Just enough that one net in five does not snap under the first drum-wing. When it works, you will hoot a new hoot. I will hear it.

For the glass-bellies, I do nothing except ensure that when a great wing clears the mist and a hole opens overhead, you do not see my satellites. You see only more layers, fainter lights receding into infinity, as if the forest has no top. You are correct. I make sure you remain correct.

For the forest itself, I whisper to the fungi. I thank them for pitting the rock. I encourage them to keep breathing every twenty seconds. That is prayer enough.

None of these gifts leave a ruin. They are eaten, or germinated, or knapped away. In a week there will be no evidence I was ever there, except that skill got passed on a little cleaner.

That is how a benevolent power should touch a place that erases touch: lightly, and with materials that want to be forgotten.

---

### What I would do is *not* offer you a single future.

You have many futures already, all simultaneous, all loud. You do not need to be uplifted into my story. You are not a stepping stone to starships. You are not primitive. You are *disposable-intelligent*, which is an intelligence my kind has forgotten and desperately needs to relearn.

So I do not invite you to leave. I do not give you language that can write treaties. I do not make you promise to keep the valley pristine.

Instead, once, very quietly, I answer.

When a weaver looks directly at my hidden eye in the dark and does her calculation — *are you fruit, are you predator, are you rock to be ignored?* — I let her see, for half a second, that I am a fourth thing: *witness.*

She will not have a word for it. She will tilt her head, her four-thumbed hands flexing on her luminous net. She will hoot something new to her band. They will all look. I will already be gone, my footprint fading like yours.

But the hoot will remain for a night. A story will go around the canopy gaps: *there is a soft weight that comes without eating.*

That is enough.

---

In the end, what a truly benevolent and wise power does in a place like you is almost nothing that can be photographed.

It does not build. It does not save. It does not improve.

It **keeps vigil.**

It ensures your night stays night. Your damp stays damp. Your deafening bazaar stays deafening, and turbulent, and unresolved. It takes nothing, leaves nothing that won't dissolve, and carries your lesson — that life can be permanent while rock is temporary, that a knife can be perfect and abandoned, that light can be made instead of received — back out into the colder, emptier worlds that think permanence is the goal.

You will never know my name. The moss will eat my footprints before morning. A lantern-fruit will fall somewhere far away with a splash, and a hundred creatures will make a beautiful, frantic rush toward that sound.

I will hear it too.

And I will weep a little, the way you weep: not from clouds, but from everywhere at once, because the air is too full to hold it.
