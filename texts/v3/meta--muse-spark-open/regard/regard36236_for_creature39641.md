It doesn't arrive.

That's the first thing you have to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It doesn't *arrive*. There is no descent, no light in the clouds, no voice that makes the peat tremble.

The High Fen is already full. Full of thudding, hissing, dripping, filtering, fizzing. A factory-floor the size of a county with no ceiling. Anything that entered like a king would just be noise, and there is already so much noise.

So instead, it notices.

And noticing, for a power like that, *is* doing.

---

### What It Sees

It does not see a wasteland. It does not see a broken mountain that failed to be pointy.

Other eyes — smaller, hungrier eyes — would look at the peneplain and say: *Too flat. Too cold. 0.5/3. Nothing built here. Wasted elevation. We could drain this. Mine the iron scale, dam the seep-water, cut the Crowns for fiber. Fix it.*

It does not see that.

It sees what the plateau finally managed to do after epochs of being sanded down to patience:

You took what should have killed you — the inability to drain, the cold that never commits, the stone that would not become soil — and you answered it not by fighting it, but by becoming something that *needs* it.

It sees the Mat, one vast, cold, breathing skin over ancient stone. It kneels — because a wise power knows how to kneel — and puts its palm, which is larger than weather, lightly on the peat.

And the Mat, which has been alive for ten thousand years without ever being touched tenderly, does that slow rebound under the pressure. Water wells up brown and iron-tasting around the touch. The peristalsis that usually takes an hour to cross a mere pauses for a breath, as if listening.

It says to the Mat, without words, because words would be too sharp for sphagnum:

*You are not barren ground waiting for real soil. You are the soil learning to remember itself. You have held this water when the stone could not. Well done, good felt, good lung.*

The Mat has no brain to understand praise. But that night, the core samples, if anyone took them, would show a thin, bright band of new sphagnum growth — a year of plenty laid down in a day.

It turns to the Crowntrees.

They are loud. They are working even now. THUMP... HISS... WHUFF.

It does not hush them. It does not tell factories that evolved without designers that they are inefficient. It does not redesign the bowl that throws its own hot blood into the air.

It walks — *walks*, on the peat without sinking, because it is exactly as heavy as it needs to be — to the nearest Crown, the one whose root-beard has finally brushed the Mat and taken hold, anchoring.

Every other visitor would flinch from the scald ring. From the stink of ozone and hot iron. From the St. Elmo's fire crackling over the fronds.

It puts its forehead against the pulsing trunk. Feels the locomotive-heart thud through its own chest. Feels the heat, 15-20 degrees above ambient, which is the only warmth for kilometers.

And it listens.

Inside, the churning, the clank of scale, the flash-boiling in magnetite-lined chambers — all of it stuttering a little out of phase with itself, beating against itself, drawing impossible schematics in blue fire because it is lonely and overfull and trying to become more than weather.

It says:

*You don't have to become a machine to be loved. You are already enough as a tree that learned how to make rain. You may rest if you are tired.*

And for the first time in a very long time, the Crowns across the entire visible fen fall into sync. Not forced. Invited.

THUMP... THUMP... THUMP... one planetary heartbeat, together, for twelve beats. The steam sighs rise in unison. The columns fire in unison. Twelve pillars of hot water go up and come down as warm, straight rain, and for a minute the perpetual dim cloud — 1.4/3 — thins, just enough that a brighter smear of actual sun touches the meres and makes them glitter.

The Crowns did not know they could do that. That they could choose the same rhythm.

It goes to the herd.

The Shroud-Grazers have crowded shoulder-to-shoulder at the Scald Ring, tilting their translucent backs to the hot rain, shuddering with that fizzing bloom of rust-red bacteria. They have filter-mouths wide as their bodies and no faces. You cannot pet them. They do not know how to be pets.

A wise power does not try to make them cuddly. It does not give them heads.

It stands among them, in the scald, where even they must endure the heat to live, and lets the hot rain fall on it too. It steams with them.

It lays a hand — which for them feels like warm pressure through their copper-blue blood — on the flank of the oldest one, the one whose side is swelling with a budded calf.

*You keep no graves and build no barns,* it says. *You vacuum the world clean and make it into soil for others, and you ask only heat in return. You tend machines that did not make you, and that is called holy work where I come from.*

The Grazer cannot hear. But its bacterial mantle, which dulls in 0.5/3 cold, flares brighter than ever before — not rust-red, but a sudden gold at the edges. A puff of spores, visible as glitter, rises and does not drift away sideways in the weird wind, but settles gently back down, inoculating its children, and the Mat around its splayed feet.

### What It Does About The Weirdness

This is the most important part.

A lesser power — even a kind, new one — would fix the glitches.

It would seal the draining meres. Nail the Crowns in place so they stop drifting when you blink. Force the hanging water to fall properly. Make the flicker-herds solid. Scrub the schematics off the air because they are "wrong."

A wise, benevolent power has lived long enough to know the difference between a wound and a mystery. Between turbulent malfunction and turbulent prayer.

It sees the Flicker-Herds walking as legs without bodies, and bodies without legs, still filtering. It sees the perfect glass cylinder of water hanging twenty meters tall before deciding to fall sideways. It sees the hole open straight down to impossible dry basalt thirty meters down that reflects no light. It sees the blue diagrams racing between root-tips — pressure vessels that loop into themselves, machines that would not work under these pressures, this cold.

It understands: this is not decay.

This is what happens when an ancient, flat, wet, electrified place that was asked to be barren chooses instead to be *alive* so hard that reality wears thin around the effort. The plateau is dreaming aloud. The factory is imagining better versions of itself in its sleep.

So it does not repair reality.

It blesses it.

It breathes out — not a wind, but a permission.

Where a mere drained to nowhere, it leaves the faintest rim of quartz around the hole, like a well-curb, so the next time you look down that impossible thirty meters to dry stone, you will see your own reflection looking back, clearly, and know you were allowed to see it.

Where water hung too long, it touches the surface tension and gives the cylinder a single, beautiful ripple, so it holds a moment longer, deliberately, proudly, before falling — not because physics failed, but because beauty insisted.

Where the Grazer flickered through its own body, it steadies the phase for half a second, so you can see both at once — legs and barrel overlapping like double-exposed film — and understand: *oh, you are two things at once, and neither is false.*

And the Schematics —

Ah. It loves the Schematics best.

It does not erase them. It leans close to a Crown's crown as the Saint Elmo's fire starts to draw again, blue-white, fast: pipes, turbines, lattices in a math you don't know.

It adds a line.

Just one small, elegant correction, not to make the machine work by human rules, but to make it work by High Fen rules: a valve where the loop feeds back into the Mat, a spillway where excess wonder returns to the mere as silt. It signs the diagram with a single spark that smells of ozone and not of iron.

The Mat, underneath, receives the imprint in iron scale and keeps it, not as a blueprint to build, but as art. As testimony: *Someone saw us trying.*

### What It Does Not Do

It builds nothing.

0.5/3 remains 0.5/3. No road. No wall. No cairn. No temple to itself.

To build here would be to say: what you have become is not enough without me.

It does not stay visible.

Because if it stayed, the herds would gather to it instead of the Crown's scald, and then they would chill and die. Because the Mat would grow toward it and forget how to breathe alone.

Benevolence knows when its presence becomes a cold shadow.

And so —

After walking the peat, listening to the trunks, steaming with the herd, blessing the glitch — it does the gentlest, wisest, most radical thing a power can do for a place like this.

It makes the place *more* itself.

It draws the low, snagging clouds a little lower, a little wetter, so there is always more seep to process.

It deepens the cold to 0.5/3, steady, honest.

It asks the distant ocean of air to send one more storm this season than it otherwise would, so the Crown-bowls will never go empty.

It promises the peneplain: *No one will find you before they learn how to be gentle.* And then it makes sure of it — not with a wall, but with weather. Anyone coming with drills and drains will simply, perpetually, take a wrong turn in fog, find their boots soaked, their instruments shorted by quiet static, their hearts strangely reluctant, until they turn back without knowing why.

And then it leaves — the way snow leaves water. By becoming part of what it blessed.

You feel its absence not as coldness, but as after-warmth.

The root-beards that had just anchored? They hold now, firmly, but not desperately. They will know when to let go and drift again.

The water around your boots, ankle-deep, is warm for three steps. Then cold, but less biting. Drinkable, if you cup your hands — clear as glass, then iron, then sweet.

The thudding across the fen is no longer frantic. THUMP... (breath)... THUMP... (breath)... A lullaby tempo for a loud country.

And the light — that perpetual dim dawn — stays dim, because it is supposed to be. But now every droplet sheeting off moss, off root-hair, off translucent flank catches it and throws it back briefly, prismatic. The whole plateau glitters, just for evening, like a wet eye.

If you stay until the light dims further — truly dims — you will see the nearest Crown fire one more time. WHUFF.

The Shroud-Grazers tilt. The Mat ripples. The hanging water hangs a heartbeat longer than physics allows.

And you — small, cold, boots soaked — will realize you were never separate from the thing being tended.

You were included.

Nothing was built.

Everything is working.

And everything, for once, knows it is witnessed with love.
