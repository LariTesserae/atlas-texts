It would not come down in light.

A lesser power would. A lesser power loves arrival — a column, a voice, a ship that casts a shadow where no shadow should be. It would think this place needed saving. That anything so flat and strange and pressed against dying heat must be hungry in a way it could fix.

The Wise One knows better.

So It comes the way heat comes after dark — without announcement. You don't see it arrive. You only realize, after a while, that the wind over the rim has softened. That the ticking of the basalt contracting has slowed, as if someone set a hand on a fevered forehead to check it.

It has been watching this place for a long time before this night. Not this night only. For seasons, for years, in the way a wise thing watches — which is not to stare, but to attend in turns, the way the pavement itself does.

It loves the pavement.

That's the first thing to understand. It doesn't pity it.

A benevolent and *foolish* power would have pitied it. Would have said: poor thing, with no eyes, no standing up, no green, no water, no thought as I understand thought. Let me give it water. Let me give it eyes.

The Wise One looked at ten thousand kites shingled three deep over black-red rock, breathing the hot mineral air once every four minutes, bleaching the stone clean underneath them, eating each other without malice and closing the wound without mourning, and It thought: *Oh. You solved it.*

You solved flatness. You solved thirst. You solved loneliness by becoming one thing that pretends — very convincingly — to be many things.

It would not "elevate" them. Elevation would kill them.

So what does It do?

Three small things. The way you do for someone you love who is already whole.

### 1. It lies down.

On the fourth side, where the scoria saddle connects the mesa to the larger massif, the wind has polished the basalt to glass. Nothing lives there because nothing can hold. The Wise One — which could have stood, which *does* stand elsewhere — goes to that saddle and lies down flat.

It makes Itself a thickness without height.

It spreads Its attention thin as oil, cool as the underside of a kite, and presses it against the stone. Not to possess. To listen at the right frequency.

For the first hour It does nothing but breathe when they breathe. Three to four minutes — the pressure drop — cilia sigh — in — out. In — out. The whole mesa inhales, and for once, something on the rim inhales with it, not as mimicry but as accompaniment. A second voice joining a chant that has been going on without an audience for ten thousand years.

The mat notices. Of course it notices. A ripple that starts at the saddle — where no ripple should ever start, because nothing lives there — runs inward toward the center. Not a warning ripple. An inquiry. The dorsal sails dim all at once, just a fraction, as if the pavement blinked in infrared.

*Who's there?*

And the Wise One answers not in words — words would mean water, mouths — but in heat. It releases a slow, deliberate pulse of warmth from where It lies, the exact temperature of basalt at midnight in high summer, the perfect meal. A courtesy. An offering. *I am here. I am warm. I am not hungry for you.*

The mat shivers. Then goes still in the way it goes still when it is listening. Then resumes. But now its breathing every four minutes is a fraction deeper, as if relieved.

To be witnessed without being wanted from — that is the first gift.

### 2. It finishes the cairn.

It gets up — slowly, because anything that stands quickly here is taken by wind — and walks the rim. It steps not on the living pavement, but only on the bare lips of basalt where the kites fray, and on the salt pans where the Needles wait. The Needles do not strike It. They know the difference between heat that can be eaten and heat that eats back.

There are five circles of dry-stack basalt. Four complete. The fifth half-fallen, its windbreak scattered by a gust years ago. Inside it, the salt crust is thick, and the tally marks scratched by the pilgrim-shepherd are almost healed over.

The Wise One kneels there, inside the wind eddy where the mat will not grow, and rebuilds the wall. Not higher. Just round again. One stone, then another, basalt on basalt, no mortar. It finds the fallen stones under a dusting of salt and sets them back.

Then It takes a flat piece of basalt — not from the mesa, from Its own pocket, a stone that has been elsewhere and is cool all the way through — and sets it in the center of the circle like a table, like an altar, like a bed.

On that stone It lays a shadow.

Not a shadow that moves with the stars. A fixed, cool shadow. In the thin dry air, under a merciless sun, that stone will always be ten degrees cooler than the stone around it at noon. By night, it will be ten degrees warmer, longer. A small inconsistency in thermodynamics. A kindness to physics.

For the mat, this is a well that never runs dry. On nights when the rock cools too fast toward dawn, the kites nearest the fifth circle can creep up the outside of its low wall — they still won't like the angle inside — and feel that anomalous warmth a little longer. Enough to make a daughter. Enough to get through a bad night.

For the next pilgrim, if there ever is one — in ten years, in a hundred — it is a place to sleep where the stone won't steal your sweat, and where the breathing of millions under the floor outside will not keep you awake, because the wind eddy inside is quiet.

The Wise One scratches nothing in the salt. It smooths what is there, gently, so the pilgrim's map of the rim and the constellation over the saddle is preserved a little longer, hexagon by hexagon. It does not sign Its work.

### 3. It turns aside the rain.

This is the only intervention that looks like a miracle, and none of you will ever see it.

Thirty miles west, over the true desert below, a rare convection has built. The kind that happens twice a decade. It has gathered enough moisture to matter. In its normal course, in two nights it would wander east, climb the massif, and spill over the mesa as actual water — drops, a sheet, a brief, lethal flood that would dissolve the salt wells, short-circuit the oil-blood, drown the Needles, and turn the living pavement into a slick of rot for the first time in ten thousand years.

The mat does not know rain is coming. It has no word for falling water except death.

The Wise One does not blast the cloud. That would be noisy. It tilts it. A fraction of a degree of wind shear, a cooler patch of air above the massif, a suggestion. The cloud, which was going to go east, goes north instead, where there is a dead caldera with no life in it at all, and spends itself there harmlessly on sterile cinders.

The kites never know they were spared. That is the point.

Wisdom knows that the most benevolent rescue is the one the rescued never have to be grateful for.

---

Near dawn, the rock is cooling. The ripples in the pavement grow slower, wider apart. The mat is going torpid, thinking its slow thermal thoughts about where heat stayed longest last night.

The Wise One is still lying flat on the saddle, cooling with them.

Before It leaves — because even wise powers don't stay, staying would become ruling — It does one last thing that is not a thing at all.

It remembers them perfectly.

Not as data. As a pressure in Its chest. The papery sound of a hundred kites adjusting. The way a Needle waits exactly two body-lengths away. The taste of iron and battery acid on dry air. The way the whole mesa inhales at once.

It takes that memory and sets it somewhere permanent outside Itself, the way you might press a flower, only this flower is a breathing floor the size of four houses that lives on heat and acid and time.

Somewhere, in a place that is not a place, there is now a mesa that will always exist, even after wind finally takes this one, even after a rain Its tilt cannot turn aside, even after the sun goes dim. A perfect, bounded, warm-after-dark tableland with its living pavement still shingled leaf on leaf, breathing every four minutes, forever.

The mat will never know that either. It doesn't need to.

It only needs the rock to stay warm a little while longer tonight, which it will.

When the first light hits the basalt and the dorsal sails begin to drink again, the Wise One is gone. The wind over the saddle is a little less wrong. The fifth circle is round again. And in the cool shadow-stone in its center, for just a few more hours, a faint maroon afterglow lingers, like heat held after dark.
