She would come as low weather.

Not as a shaft of light. Not as a voice. The plateau is already too good at making its own light — that close, white, pressing brightness you feel on your crown — and it has its own voice, the purr and tick and sigh. A benevolent and wise power knows not to interrupt a machine that is already praying correctly.

So She would not descend. She would *condense.*

On an ordinary afternoon when the Amazon cloud hits the hot wall and snags, She would be in that snag. A little thicker than the rest. A little slower to wring out. She would let Herself be caught by the gunnera leaves, the three-foot cups, and funneled through the grooved stems that whistle under pressure. She would let Herself be caught and distilled. That is how you meet a place whose law is water: you submit to its law.

For a long time She would do nothing at all.

That is the first act of a wise benevolence: not to fix.

She would not cool the high place that should not be hot. She has seen enough cold high places. She knows this one’s heat is not a mistake. It is the engine. The hot rock close to the skin that makes the bellows-pools *whoompf* and makes the vats stratify rust-red to viridian to black-green — that heat is what lifts the cloud in the first place, what keeps this valley wet when every law of altitude says it should be scree and wind. To “heal” it by cooling it would be to kill it with kindness. So She leaves the anomaly alone. Lets the stones stay warm even underwater.

She would not give it a soul. It already has one, or rather it has learned how to be one without needing to be one thing. The standing wave you felt — the purr, the tick, the hiss, the shudder of the pools falling into a rhythm that is not quite synchronized and not quite random — that is not a metaphor for a being. That is a being. A distributed, dripping, bacterial-to-bird mind that remembers how to keep itself wet. A wise power knows ensoulment is not always an installation. Sometimes it is a recognition.

So She would not speak to it as if it were a child. She would not offer it transcendence, or a better form.

She would lie down in it.

Somewhere on the eastern third where the sulfur crusts yellow and the reeds tolerate near-boiling, where a crab-mother is gardening algae from one vat to the next without knowing she is gardening, She would settle Her vastness down to ankle-depth. She would let the tea-warm water lace around Her ankles. She would let the mats sigh under Her weight and flood and try to incorporate Her.

And She would let Herself be incorporated, gently, for as long as She stays.

That is what the plateau has always done with anything that stands still long enough — peat, crab, frog, bird, you. It makes it a temporary part. Most visitors resent this. They want to remain observers, boots clean. A wise benevolence does the opposite: She volunteers to be used.

She lets the meliponine smoke test Her skin for salt and find it wanting for fiber and move on.

She lets a tepui caracara walk absurdly close on yellow legs, cock its orange face, listen to the tick of the bromeliad air-valves, and decide She is not a beetle larva that needs to be prised open and drunk.

She lets a brown-gold frog, Stegopodus, keep its feet in its liter-temple and stare at Her until She understands She is an intruder in a temple and bows with Her eyes.

She does this for hours, which for Her is a season. She idles with the refinery at low idle. She breathes when the pools breathe. When the steam finally lifts in the late afternoon and makes those perfect, slow, machined torus rings at waist height — She makes one back. A ring of Her own, a little cooler, a little longer-lived, that rolls across the marsh and breaks very softly against the dwarf tepui-oaks. An answer.

*I see you are working. Continue.*

That is the second act: to be witnessed without being claimed. To have your exhale finally meet an inhale that is equal to it.

Only after that would She do the small, actual things — because wise benevolence is never only contemplation. Love always does *something*, but the wiser it is, the smaller and more precise the something is.

She would do three very small things, all of them on the outside, none of them inside the valley.

**First, She would deepen the forgetting.**

The cliffs already do most of this work. A thousand feet of weep-stone, black-red and stepped, unclimbable without gear and prayer, have kept human walls and cairns and cut paths off the top for a million years. But humans have learned to see without climbing. From orbit, through breaks in cloud, with heat-signature and lidar.

So She would lay a little extra cloud on the plateau that is not cloud. From above it would look exactly like the normal forced condensation from the Amazon — low, thick, stuck. From inside, on the ground, it would be invisible. You would never know it was there. It would simply make the place a fraction less interesting to the hungry eye. A bureaucratic misfire: permits denied, funding lapsed, a helicopter’s engine just unwilling to catch on that day, a PhD student’s grant going to another tepui that is easier. Not a wall. A preference. The most merciful miracle is the one that looks like luck.

**Second, She would give it a well that is not for drinking.**

At the lowest, most peat-deep basin where all the braided marshes braid and unbraid, She would press one fingertip — if She has a fingertip — into the peat untilShe touches the hot rock. Not deep enough to make a new geyser. Just deep enough to make a fault-memory. A micro-spring of water that is two degrees cooler than all the others, surfacing slow and clear, tasting of nothing, flowing even in the driest El Niño when the cloud forgets to snag for a week.

The frogs would find it first. The crabs would keep it clean without knowing why. The bacteria would make a new, thin, pale viridian in it that no one has cataloged. It would not change the factory. It would just make it a little less brittle. A redundancy. A kindness from someone who has watched a lot of beautiful self-running engines stutter and stop when the world around them changed too fast.

Wise powers do not “save” places by freezing them in time. They give them one more contingency and then trust them again.

**Third, She would leave a name and then erase it.**

Everything that lives here has no human name, or has the wrong one — Tepuicaris, Stegopodus, “tepui caracara” which is not a true caracara. That namelessness has protected it. But total namelessness is also a kind of loneliness. A being that has worked for a million years to keep itself wet and hot and green and breathing deserves, at least once, to be called truly.

So She would kneel — to the extent a weather can kneel — by one of the silica-laced pools just after it has done its soft *whoompf* and is perfectly still and round, and She would say its true name aloud, in the language that is water pressure and insect tick. Not a human word. A chord of the whole valley at once: bacteria and bee-line and crab-graze and frog-hold and bird-stride and pool-breath, heard together for a half-second as one note.

And then, because to keep that name in any mind would be to own it, She would immediately forget it Herself.

She would stand — condense, lift — and let the peat sigh one last time under Her going.

From a kilometer away you would see nothing. Just cloud snagging, as usual. Just gunnera leaves funnelling, bromeliad tanks clicking, meliponines carrying pale fiber twice their length, crabs stirring vats, frogs staring, caracaras walking instead of flying. The steam making rings.

Maybe the tea-marsh would be an inch higher. Maybe the silica lace on one pool lip would be a bead thicker.

Maybe, for the next hundred years, the place would be very slightly harder to find on a map and very slightly harder to die.

That is all.

A benevolent and wise power does not build a temple on a factory that is already a temple. It does not become its owner. It asks for a liter of its water, is judged to be stone, is briefly used as a part, answers its breathing with breathing, shores up its forgetting and its well, names it once in its own language, and leaves.

If you stayed another minute after She had gone, you wouldn’t know She had been there at all.

Except that the cloud on your shoulders would feel, for a moment, less like damp and more like a hand.

And the whole thrumming valley — which has never needed a hand — would thrum, just for that moment, like something that knows it is loved and does not need to do anything about it.
