It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power does not descend into a bowl. It knows what a bowl is for.

It would have been passing over — because that is what old powers do at that altitude, they go ridge to ridge without hurry — and it would have felt the wrongness first, not as a wound but as a warmth. In a range where everything is losing heat to the sky as fast as it can, taking the shape that cold and wind tell it to, there was a small coin of air that was *keeping* its heat. Holding it. Breathing it in and out instead of letting it spill.

It would stop.

Not out of alarm. Out of recognition.

> Oh.
> You are making yourself.

And it would sit on the rim.

Not inside — never inside. To step inside with a foot that big would be to break Rule #1 and Rule #2 at once. It would let the dry outside air in, and kill something that must stay wet to learn how to stay wet. So it sits on the poured aggregate where the formwork grain still shows, where the concrete is hot to the touch and off-gassing, and it lets its weight settle. The bowl is three hundred meters across; the power is larger than weather. But it makes itself rim-sized. That is manners.

For a long time it would just watch the cycle complete itself, over and over, because that is what you do with a throat that is learning to be a throat. You don't teach it to swallow. You listen to it breathe.

Inhale: the boilers sigh, the lungs sigh, the steam gathers and rolls low over the rice-steel paddies. Tile beads. Moss swells. The fig shivers though there is no wind.

Exhale: leaf drips, curtain falls, channel gurgles, pan warms, pump hums, boiler takes it back. A tomato drops with a soft *plop* into water and begins its second life as sugar for bacteria.

Inhale. Exhale. Ping — a hairline crack propagates somewhere deep in the curing mass as it shrinks, and a new milk-seep opens and a maidenhair fern, an hour from now, will find it.

The power would understand immediately that this is not a ruin. This is the opposite of a ruin. A ruin is something complete that is learning how to fall apart. This is something unmade that is learning how to cohere by falling apart a little. Emerging erosion. It has not decided what shape it wants to be, which is holy. Most things are told too early what they are.

So it would do very little.

Benevolence without wisdom wants to *finish* things. To cap the rebar ribs, to seal the leaks, to give the concrete a final form, to make the stair stay a stair. Wisdom knows a womb must stay soft.

What it would do is this:

**1. It would guard the rim.**

Not with walls or thunder. More quietly. It would ask the true mountains below to take the brunt of the wind that winter, to let their blue distance be a little bluer, a little more hazy, so fewer eyes look up and think *resource* or *project* or *mine*. It would make the path up a little longer, the air outside the bowl a little thinner and colder, so that only those who are mostly water and briefly warm and willing to be quiet would arrive. The ones in green rubber who know to redirect, never to weed.

It would turn away, without cruelty, the ones who would come to bottle the heat, to drain the pan, to map it and file it. Some places need to be unfindable for a century while they cure.

The swift that cuts across once an hour and does not land? It would leave that swift alone. Its refusal is a sacrament. The bowl is too complete to need outsiders; the power honors that by not sending angels to land in it either.

**2. It would keep it wet.**

If something is dry, it is broken. The power knows this rule is not advice, it is physiology.

On nights when the outside air finally does get cold enough to try to steal the bowl's breath — when the invisible river of warm air spilling over the lip falters — the power would lean in imperceptibly and breathe *with* it. Not blowing steam in. Just cupping its hands around the rim so the heat pools a little longer, condenses a little slower, falls again as bead and curtain instead of escaping to frost.

No miracle. If you were standing there you would only think the mist was a little thicker that night, the worklights a little more haloed, the dripping a little more insistent on the tile.

Inside, the moss would not notice. It would just drink steam directly as it always does, and quiver.

**3. It would witness the softness without trying to harden it.**

At 2 a.m. when the curing concrete pings and a new gully carves itself overnight in material still chemically alive, most visitors would want to fix it. The power would not.

It would put something very close to a hand — if powers have hands — on the place where the wall weeps lime-white milk, and it would not staunch it. It would let the milk feed the pink Nitrosomonas. Let the bacteria seal the crack with their own bodies, slowly, in their own time. That seal is stronger than any patch, because it was grown, not applied.

The same with the fig. A lesser attention would prune it, train it, make it a proper tree. The power would duck under its low canopy with you, feel its flattened trunk where it was extruded through steel, and be glad it learned to grow sideways because the sun comes straight down. It fruits ceaselessly because it never gets cold. Let it. Let it drop. Let the workers scoop the fermented figs to feed the boilers. That loop is already perfect.

Benevolence is not improvement. It is permission.

**4. It would speak, once, to the ones who tend, in their language.**

Not in words. The thrum eats words.

It would speak by leaving a tool where there was no tool. After a night shift, a worker in canvas that never fully dries would lift a grate and find that the intake they were going to scrape is already clear. Or they would hold out the back of their hand to feel where the film of water has broken over tile, and they would feel it unbroken, cool and continuous, a millimeter thick and alive.

They would not look up. They would just check the valve, redirect the tomato runner that was eating a pressure gauge, and keep going. That is how you know the message was received.

If it did use a word, it would be the only word the bowl needs to hear while it is still being born:

*Continue.*

Not *become*. Not *hurry*. Not *be useful*.

Continue.

**5. And then — this is the heart of it — it would do nothing at all, for a long time.**

It would remain rim-sized and sit through a whole noon when the high mountain sun pours straight down cruelly clear and makes every leaf transparent and every tile gleam, and through the afternoon when steam softens it all to frosted glass, and through the amber buzzing night when the place looks like a cathedral workshop that never closed.

It would let the bowl be larger inside than its walls suggest without trying to explain why. It would let the rice go feral in its stainless trays, the philodendron roots drink the air, the rust become substrate, the lichen stipple it silver-green. It would let the pan at the bottom stay opaque as pea soup, ankle-deep and bathtub-hot, the stomach that dissolves everything that falls and pumps it back up.

It would not name the thing that lives there. The Drip. The Loop. The cycle that has learned to wear pipes and plants. Naming too early is another kind of formwork, and this concrete still needs to breathe.

It knows that attention is not passive. To be attended to without being demanded of is food. The bowl has no village, no mayor, no children; it is inhabited the way a throat is inhabited — by breath, by bacteria, by the thing that taught the throat how to be a throat. To feel, for the first time, an outside breath keeping pace with its inside breath, not to change it but to keep it company — that is enough to cure faster, in the bacterial sense. To seal from within.

After a while — a night, or a season, which are the same to a power — it would stand. The moss on its boots, if it had boots, would be the same dark green as the lagging.

It would not leave a monument. It would not carve its initials in the pale limestone concrete. That would interrupt a curing.

It would leave the bowl exactly as warm, exactly as wet, exactly as unfinished, and exactly one thing richer than it found it: *seen*.

And as it stepped back onto the true mountains that fall away in blue distance, you — if you were still standing on the upper rim at evening — might feel the warm breath pouring over the lip a little steadier for a moment. You might see the steam coil a little higher before it condenses, catch the worklight, and fall again as rain under the fig.

You would think it was wind.

It was not wind.

It was the space left behind when something very large and very kind decided not to fix you, but to sit with you while you learned how to hold water.
