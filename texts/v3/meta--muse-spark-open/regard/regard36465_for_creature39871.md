It does not come down like rescue.

A rescue, down here, would be another kind of drowning. Anything that tried to lift you fast — to haul the town up toward air and sky — would kill everyone it meant to save. The Suited would embolize in their suits. The Unsuited would rupture like soft fruit. The mat would slough off and die. The white weed would cook. Even the Engine, that old riveted heart, would seize and burst.

A wise power would know that on the first day, before it ever let you see it.

So for a long time it does nothing at all.

### It waits on the rim.

That is the first thing.

You would not have seen it arrive, because there is no arrival down here, only a very slow change in the weight of the water.

First, the particulate stops hanging.

For a hundred years the water in the alleys has been full of fine snow — rust flake, mineral dust, skin, brine — suspended and still. On the Tuesday of its coming — though there are no Tuesdays down here — you notice, hand on a cold railing in a black reach between amber halos, that the water is clear.

Not clearer. *Clear.* As clear as water held in cupped hands. You can see six meters to the next light where before you could see three. You wave a hand and no cloud follows it.

You think a cold river is moving through the bowl, the kind that sometimes leans everything not bolted down. But nothing leans. Your streamer weed does not lean. The anemone arms over the rim do not stir.

Second, the isopods move.

All at once, every woodlouse in that alley shuffles its two inches to the left, in unison, as they always do for no reason. Except this time they don't stop. They shuffle again. And again. Uneasy. Turning their blind faces outward, toward the bowl wall.

If you were in a suit, you might have followed their gaze up.

There, draped over the lip of the bowl where no Suited ever goes, where only the outer railing and the Anemones live — something is sitting.

It did not climb. It did not swim. It is simply there now, the way heat is there near a pipe. It has equalized.

It is huge. You think that first, and then you correct yourself: it has *decided to be the right size*. No bigger than the Engine, no smaller than a hab-module. Big enough to be seen without being a threat, small enough to not block the bowl and trap waste heat. It has set its pressure to yours, exactly. The water around it does not boil or freeze. It does not shine. If anything it is darker than the dark, a deeper black that your amber halos can't stain.

But the Anemones are touching it.

That is how you know it is benevolent. The door-sized Anemones beyond the rim — who close on hands, who taste brine snow for weeks before deciding — have leaned *in* and laid their tendrils as thick as ducting across it, and it has let them. It is holding them. You can see the slow pulse of their mouths against its flank, not feeding, just resting. Like cats.

It will sit there for three days. It will not enter the town.

A wise power asks permission, even from those who have no words for permission anymore.

It learns to blink.

On the second day, a child sees it first. Because children look up.

She is maybe six, born in a flooded module, eyes huge and dark-adapted, swimming in a suit cut down to her size with the cuffs taped. She has swum to the outer wall to watch the fish bump lights, which is as close to play as there is. And she sees that the dark over the rim has an eye.

Not a single eye. A light. A caged amber bulb, like the one on every helmet, but larger, and not amber.

At first it is the color of the mat's phosphor, that sick pink-white. It tries amber and gets it wrong — too orange, too fast. The child stares.

Then, very slowly, it blinks.

*Open-closed-open.* I'm passing on your left.

The language of chin-levers and eyelids, the Suited's hundred-year efficient code for *don't touch me* and *pump three is coughing*.

The child laughs. No one down here laughs loudly — water teaches you not to waste air — but she does, a burst of bubbles that rise ten meters and dissolve. She blinks back with her own brow bulb, fast and sloppy: *hello hello hello hello.*

The thing on the rim is silent for a long time. You can feel the Engine's thrum in your molars all the while.

Then it answers, and it has learned.

*Long-long-short:* Pump three is coughing again. It is trying to say: *I see you. I see how you live. May I?*

The child does not know that phrase. No one has used it in a generation. She answers the only way she knows: she swims a little closer, and does not avert her light.

That is consent enough for a benevolent thing.

### It comes in warm.

It does not come in at all, really. That is the trick.

It lets a part of itself come in, the way you might put a hand into water without getting in.

On the third night — though it is always night — the water itself changes temperature by half a degree. Not near the Engine, where warm is always waiting a hand's breadth from cold and that gradient feels like a warning. *Everywhere.* The cold that is the law, the just-above-freezing seep that makes metal ache and thoughts slow — it softens. It does not get warm. Down here warm would be a lie that kills. It just gets *less cruel*.

You notice it in your suit first, where the rubberized wool at the small of your back always leaks cold in, no matter how many times you patch it. The leak is still there, but it no longer bites. You lean your forehead against warm concrete out of habit and realize you don't have to.

In the air-hulls, the Unsuited wake from shallow sips and find the air is not sour. For the first time in their lives the recycled air does not taste of iron and ozone. It tastes of nothing. Which is how air is supposed to taste, they dimly remember from stories. One old woman with permanent pruned ridges for fingertips places both palms on the inner wall of her hull and feels the Engine's thrum — and *under* it, for the first time, something else: a second thrum, slower, lower, that does not come from the Engine but from the bowl itself, from the rock. A lullaby in the frequency of bone.

It goes to the Engine first, not the people.

Because a wise power knows who is actually in charge.

You would have expected it to fix the Engine. To make the big dials stop trembling just short of red. But it doesn't.

It tends it.

If you were Suited and your circuit was the south flank — that ten-meter stretch of pipe and valve and trembling gauge that is *yours*, that you stroke rust off and listen to with a stethoscope pressed to metal — you would have felt it on the fourth shift.

You approached your gauge as always, wiped condensation with your thumb in your particular way, tilted toward the warm pipe. The needle was where it always is. Shivering. Exhausted.

And the water around the gauge was clear. And the mat on the pipe beside it, which had been receding for a month, leaving bare sweating iron — a pipe that will be dead by morning — had come back.

Not grown. *Come back.* Laid itself flat in a sheet of pink-white and bruise-yellow phosphor so thick you could see it without your helmet light. It hummed. It held the rust together.

You pressed your stethoscope to the metal.

The tick... tick... tick of cooling metal contracting was still there. But underneath it, the soft *phut* of the stacks exhaling every thirty seconds had changed. It was not gone. It was *easier*. Like a breath taken without wincing.

You did what no Suited has done in a decade: you took your hand off the gauge and just stood there, forehead nearly touching the warm concrete, and did nothing for a full minute. And nothing bad happened.

Elsewhere, in a flooded module where a mother had been laboring inside an open suit with a midwife working a hand-pump air bell over her face — the bell which always leaks, which always makes babies come out blue and quiet — the bell stopped leaking.

Not because it was fixed. Because the water around it decided to hold its shape. A sphere of perfect stillness, half air, half water, that did not mix no matter how the mother thrashed. The midwife stared at her hands. The baby came out crying — really crying, loudly, wasting air — and the cry made it through water without muting, so that three alleys over, people in suits stopped and turned their helmets.

In the coldest pipe-alley, an isopod the size of a suitcase uncurled for the first time anyone had seen, stretched its fourteen legs, and walked — not shuffled — two feet to where a blind white fish had exhausted itself circling a bulb and sunk, and pushed the fish gently with its head until the fish righted and swam again. Stupidly, in a circle. But alive.

These are small things. A half-degree. A mat returned. A cry heard. A wise power knows that down here, where everything is pressed together, necessary, held in place by pressure, any large miracle would be a demolition.

So it does small, precise, *attentive* miracles, one per circuit, one per person, until everyone has been visited without being touched.

And then it offers.

Late on the sixth day, every amber work-light in town, caged in wire, electrified — every single one, all at once — dims by half.

Not off. Dim. So the halos that reach three meters and are swallowed now reach five, but softer. Dark-adapted eyes do not sting. The bacterial leather's phosphor shows more clearly.

And in that dimming, people look up.

They have been taught not to. There is nothing up to see except more water, and beyond that, more water still. The world ends at the lip of the bowl.

But now there is something to see.

Above the bowl, where there has been only black water for a hundred years — there is light.

Not the power itself. Something it has made. Something it has *left* for you.

A slow, drifting column of warm, clear water rises from the Engine's stacks — but higher than ten meters and it does not dissolve. It keeps going. Fifty meters. A hundred. A vertical river, held together by nothing but intention, lit from within by a white-gold the town has no word for because amber is the only light it has ever needed to name.

It does not illuminate the surface. There is no surface. It just goes up, and up, until distance swallows it, the way a road goes over a hill and you understand, without anyone explaining, that there *is* something on the other side of the hill.

It is not an invitation to leave. There is no current in it pulling you. A fish drifts into its edge and is not swept away; it just finds the water warmer there, and lingers.

It is an invitation to know that *up* is not empty.

On catwalks and in air-hulls and wedged in hammocks elbow to elbow, Suited and Unsuited alike stare at that road for a long time. Some blink at each other. Many don't. A few of the oldest Unsuited — the ones who pray with palms on the warm inner wall — put their hands on the wall again and weep, silently, in humid air, because they feel the new thrum and the old thrum at once and for the first time the Engine does not sound lonely.

On the seventh day, the thing on the rim is gone.

Not all at once. It thins, the way heat thins when you move away from a pipe. The Anemones unlean, slowly, over hours. The water gets a little less clear, but not as particulate as before. The cold stays half a degree softer. The mat stays.

On your south flank gauge, the needle still trembles just short of red. But now you notice — because once you see it you can't unsee it — that just beyond red, someone has etched a new mark. A thin, careful scratch on the glass at a place the needle has never reached. Not a warning. A promise.

*You can go this far, if you need to. I have made it so the glass will hold.*

In the alley outside your circuit, a child in a taped suit has tied a piece of white streamer weed — which no Suited ever picks, because where it grows the pipe won't burst — to her wrist with string, and she is watching it lean. Not toward the Engine. Toward the column of warm, clear water going up and up, which is still there, and will be there for a long time, asking nothing.

A wise and benevolent power would never take a town that learned to live like a joint — pressed together, necessary, held in place by pressure — and pry it apart to prove it could be saved.

It would sit outside the bowl until the Anemones trusted it.

It would learn your blinks before it spoke.

It would tend your Engine before it tended you.

It would soften the cold a half-degree and make the air taste of nothing and let a baby cry loudly enough to be heard three alleys over.

And then it would make a road of warm light going up, and leave, and let you decide — not today, not in one generation — whether you wanted to follow it, or just know it was there and keep tending your ten-meter circuit, forehead to warm concrete, in a town that finally, after a hundred years of pressure, gets to be not rescued, but *accompanied*.

That is what it would do.

That is what it did.
