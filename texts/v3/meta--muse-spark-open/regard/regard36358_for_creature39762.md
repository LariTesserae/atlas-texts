It would not come down.

That's the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power would feel the tug of that warm breath on a cold mountain long before it arrived — a perfect circle of summer hidden in winter, humming at 60 cycles, breathing every seven minutes. It would look the way you look when you see something alone and complete and not lonely in the way that needs fixing.

And it would come closer. And it would kneel at the rim.

Not descend.

Anything clever or hungry would descend. Would bring boots and sample jars and notebooks, would need to touch the velvet on the wall and take it, would need to name the cable fungi and publish it, would need to "save" it by opening it. A wise power knows that opening a closed circle kills it.

So it stays up where the wind is still thin and cold, where the basalt is still bare and sharp, and it looks down into the full cup. It lets the steam hit its face. For a long time — because wise means it has time — it does nothing but attend.

To be attended to like that, fully, without desire to use, is already a kind of intervention. The Bowl has never been seen like that before. Humans who found it saw a geothermal anomaly, a research opportunity, a power house to build. The swifts who circle see a mistake to correct. Even the mountain that made it didn't mean it; it just blew and left a throat.

This Power means to see it.

And seeing, it understands what it is looking at: not a broken piece of lowland jungle lost at altitude. Not a machine park waiting to be restarted. A being.

One being.

The moss and the fern and the heliconia and the clicker and the brown-black frog and the orange bacterial mat and the turbine and the pipe organ and the bucket on its cable — they are not residents *of* the Bowl. They *are* the Bowl, the way your skin and blood and bacteria and the fillings in your teeth are you. Seventy years ago it was just weeping rock and bacterial paint. Now it has learned to hold water, to time a flood, to eat rubber, to keep its nights at 17 degrees. It has closed its loop. It finished itself.

You don't improve a finished thing. You keep it.

So this is what the Power would do, if anything. Almost nothing. Which is everything:

**1. It would keep its secret.**

Not with a thunderclap or a force field. Gently. The way a mother lays a hand over a sleeping child's eyes when the hallway light comes on.

It would make the outer flanks a little more tedious. The scree a little looser under human boots. The wind over the rim a little more emphatic, a little more *go around*. It would put a wrongness in the satellite photos — a shadow where there should be none, a stitching error everyone scrolls past. The few maps that marked the service road to the power house would fade faster. The one ladder, already half-colonized by moss, would rust through at the third rung on a night with no moon, not enough to be dramatic, just enough to be untrustworthy.

Not sealed. Just... uninviting. The Bowl wants to be unvisited. Wisdom honors that wanting.

**2. It would tune it, not fix it.**

The Bowl is not broken, but it is young and its machines are stupidly brave. That turbine bearing on the north wall has been running dry for eleven years. In three more winters it would seize. The pump house would miss its seven-minute breath. The moss beds would sour. The frogs would miss a breeding cycle and not recover.

A clever power would replace the whole power house with something clean and endless. A wise power squats down, reaches with something you could call a finger and could call wind, and leaves a single drop of oil that never dries, right on the race.

It taps the high-tension line once, lightly, and the 60-cycle hum drops half a tone — from a mosquito whine to a deeper *mmm* — the exact frequency gap the clickers were trying to find. That night, for the first time, you can actually hear them over the thrum. The males call and are answered. The frogs, emboldened, buzz a little louder.

In the central pond it does nothing at all, because the orange mat is perfect. It just admires it. That admiration, like sunlight, is a kind of food.

**3. It would give it time.**

This is the greatest benevolence. Right now the Bowl is living on borrowed heat. The mountain under it is cooling a fraction of a degree per decade. In four hundred years the fissure will be too cool to drive the turbines. In two thousand the nights will frost. The jungle that lied about altitude will be found out.

The Power cannot — will not — make a mountain burn forever. That would be a different violence.

Instead it puts its hands on either side of the cone, not to hold it up, but to *insulate* it. To slow the leak. A thought like: *cool slower.* Now the fraction is a smaller fraction. Now the Bowl has ten thousand years instead of four hundred. Ten thousand years of moss sighing, of ferns swallowing the sorting shed roof but keeping the shed underneath to channel drip, of buckets dumping silt to make a new hill that roots take. Ten thousand years is, for all practical purposes, forever to a frog that lives a year. It is enough to become truly endemic, truly itself. It is enough to stop being an accident and become a place.

The Bowl will not notice. The water will still hiss on the same warm stones. Only the Power will know it gave it that.

**4. It would leave a witness, which is itself.**

On the last morning before it goes — because even wise powers do not stay; staying would become ownership — it does one small, sentimental, unnecessary thing.

On the inner wall, where the weeping is thickest and the bacterial velvet is black-green and slick, where no fern can root and no pipe runs, it leaves a mark. Not carved. Grown.

A patch of lichen that was not there before, in a perfect circle the size of a palm, pale gold. It does nothing. It fixes no nitrogen. It eats no plastic. It is simply beautiful and it will live as long as the Bowl does. If anyone ever does climb down again, despite the loosened scree and the broken rung, they will see it and feel, unreasonably, that the place knows it is loved.

And then the Power stands up from the rim. The wind that is still cold and clean at altitude moves again. The steam column over the Bowl at dusk is still visible from a day's walk away, but no one who sees it will be particularly curious.

Inside, the pump house exhales right on time. The vines hang plumb-bob straight. The slime trails dry to salt. The circle of sky stays serrated and sharp.

It hums to itself, alone and complete, and now — without ever being touched — held.
