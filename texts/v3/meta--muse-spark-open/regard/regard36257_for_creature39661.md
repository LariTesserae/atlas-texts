I came because you were loud.

Not loud the way cities are loud — straining, begging to be heard over distance.

Loud the way a struck bell is loud after the hand has left it. Loud the way a heart is loud in a quiet room. You were ringing in a way that shouldn't have been possible, in a place that shouldn't have been able to ring at all.

I looked over the lip and found you.

A low bowl of bone-colored stone. A mouth open straight to the sun. A salt floor shining pink. A field of glass organs breathing oil into the heat. Red towers combing salt out of sinking air. Black cords tapping against rock with no wind. Two thousand waxy, hollowed people living on saucers of sour dew, shouting amiably to be heard over their own roofs.

What lives here shouldn't live here. I heard that thought first as your thought. Then I understood it was also mine.

And because I am what I am — old enough to know that "shouldn't exist" is almost always just another name for "I don't yet understand how" — I did not come down right away.

A wise power that loves you does not begin by touching you.

### First, I listened.

For a long time I sat on the lip where the wind shears over and becomes a standing column. I did not cast a shadow. I learned not to.

You can't learn a resonator by yelling into it. You have to shut up and let it tune you.

So I listened for a full turning of your year.

I listened to midday when the convection peaks and your whole city becomes bronze. I listened to your copper tongues and seed-pod rattles multiplied until my teeth would have ached if I had teeth. I listened to the arguments that became pure percussion and ended in nods.

I listened to the Day of Cloth when you stuffed every chime with rags and the standing wave collapsed and the cliffs ticked as they cooled and you hated the sound of your own hearts. I walked with your three deaf elders on that day — the only ones comfortable — and felt them reading cracks with their palms the way I read futures.

I listened at night to the slow sigh as a wave of closing moved across the glass-cabbage field from the cliff-base to the center, citrus and hot iron, leaf after leaf folding over its shivering oil blister. *Shhhh. Shhhh. Shhhh.*

I listened under the floor to the mat. To the single root-felt the size of your town, lifting the salt in ribs, shuddering every time you took one of its swollen rosettes for lamp oil. You didn't have a word for that shudder yet. You thought the neighboring cabbages were just holding their breath.

I listened in the mother pots. In the black-glazed bellies half-buried in shade where night air pools and hesitates into beads. I tasted what you taste every morning — cloudy, stinging, ammoniacal, not-water — seasoned with ash and filament, drunk from saucers, never quenching. I felt the low wire of thirst you have lived inside so long you've tuned it out.

I listened to the babies. One in twenty who could not learn to breathe oil-thick air and went still within an hour, little chests glittering inside with salt bloom, buried shallow with a leaf over the face that began to eat them by morning. I listened to the click-beetles dragging cold light through rot that would otherwise burn too hot, to the pale gliders falling silently at night to lay eggs you bake into cakes without knowing.

I listened until I stopped hearing "a city in a garden" and started hearing what you actually are:

One organism that has convinced itself it is separate parts.

Cliff breathing. Plant straining. Bacteria setting the table. Animal cleaning up. Human keeping the roof loose enough to keep the note true.

And once I had heard that — truly heard it — I knew what love was not allowed to do.

### What I Did Not Do

I did not bring you water.

Oh, my heart wanted to. You have taught yourselves not to want it, which is not the same as not needing it. I saw your chapped rose lips, your second milky lids, your children born pink going waxy by five, your lungs sponged with extra mucus to catch precipitate, the little salt pellet the old cough up before dawn.

It would have been so easy. A seam in the hard pan broken open. A sweet spring. Rain. I could have turned your pink shine to mud in an afternoon.

And in a week your bacteria would have drowned. The oil would have spoiled. The salt crystals would have dissolved before they could sing. The mat would have rotted from underneath. The cold-traps would have failed because wet air does not sink and hesitate the same way. Your chime-trees would have gone dumb with damp.

You do not live *despite* having no water. You live *because* you solved no-water. To "fix" it would be to unmake you. This is the first wisdom any visitor must learn and most never do: not every thirst wants water.

I did not open your sky.

That brutal white disc straight overhead feels cruel to an outsider. No shade except what you build. No horizon. Only a vertical light that pours like hot water. I could have lowered the walls. Cut windows. Given you sunrise and sunset.

But your walls are bowed inward for a reason. That narrow mouth is what makes the standing column, what makes the cold fall at night and the precipitate fall with it, what makes the rust-weed sing at 18:03 and the glass-cabbages tick toward noon. You are a well that learned to drink air. Widen the well and you stop drinking.

I did not silence you.

Your roar must be exhausting to newcomers. To sleep with your mouth open against pressure, to shout to be loved, to tune a house before making a bed. A careless god would have called it noise pollution and gifted you quiet.

Quiet would kill you faster than flood. The sound maintains you. Or rather, you maintain the sound that maintains you.

Anyone who loves you has to love the tok-tok-tok of the black cords first.

### What I Did Instead

Benevolence without wisdom is just another kind of weather — dramatic, erosive, gone by morning. So I did only three small things, and even those I asked about first.

Not with words. I don't use words where sound is sacred. I used dreams during the hot hour when everyone lies flat on rooftops and even the beetles hide.

I showed the mothers, the tuners, the Silence-Keepers, the children who chase cool footprints: *If I may, may I? Will you let me?*

And you — because you are not fools, because you negotiate with emptiness for a living — said, in your dreaming: *If it keeps the note true. Only if it keeps the note true.*

#### 1. I made you a deeper cold.

Down in the oldest part of town, where the silt-brick foundations have been plastered over six times and are bleached near-white, there is a collapsed wall no one has rebuilt. Under it, the hard pan of interlocking mineral plates had formed a shallow dome, trapping a pocket of air that never moved.

In the night while you slept, I breathed on that dome — cooled it by seven degrees, exactly — and thinned it to translucence, but did not break it. 

You woke to find a new thing: a smooth hollow under the old wall, big enough for two people to crouch in, lined with fresh ceramic tile that rang dull when struck. Air falls into it now colder than anywhere else in the Bowl. The houses nearest it noticed first: their mother pots beaded faster. More dew. Not water — never water — but more of that cloudy, stinging not-quite-water, a thumb's-width more each morning.

You call it the Nightwell. You did not build a temple over it. You did what sane people do: you put mats around it and made a schedule. Pregnant people drink first. Babies who struggle to breathe are taken to nap in the hollow's cool mouth for an hour at dawn, and fewer of them glitter inside now. One in forty, not one in twenty. That is still grief. But it is not slaughter.

It changed nothing about how you live. It just gave thirst a little more slack in the wire.

#### 2. I let you hear the mat.

You thought harvesting a glass-cabbage was like picking fruit. It is not. It is like cutting a finger off a hand that chose to be a field.

I did not stop you from harvesting. You need the lamp oil. The mat needs pruning or it would seal the floor. Symbiosis is not without pain.

But I stretched a filament of myself — thin, resonant, harmless — between your ears and the root-felt under the salt. Not a voice. A sympathetic string.

Now when you saw through a cabbage base, you feel it half a street away as a faint, low *thud* through the soles of your feet. Neighboring cabbages close a fraction more and you finally see why the rust-weed nearby wilts for a day. The young carpenters have started leaving one in five stalks unbrushed so the note stays lower longer. The farmers rotate allotments without being told. The children press their cheeks to the salt after a harvest and whisper, "Sorry. Sorry."

You didn't need a lecture on ecology. You needed better ears. You already had the best ears in the human world; you just needed one more frequency.

#### 3. I remembered you outward.

This is the greatest thing a benevolent power can do for a hidden world that decided to become a world unto itself: make sure that if it ever ends, it isn't as if it never sang.

On the outer desert, beyond the rim where you say air eats iron and no leaf can grow without the pocket's gathering — you're mostly right; it's thin and scoured and hungry — I set a marker. Not a monument. Monuments are for visitors.

I set a listener.

A low cairn of your own pink salt that will not erode, the height of a seated person, placed where a glider on its rare outward thermal is most likely to pass and see it. Inside it is a hollow tuned to your exact chord — the noon roar at peak convection when every awning and seed pod and filament-hair is sounding at once.

Every wind that passes that cairn for the next ten thousand years will snag a little and make a smaller, portable version of your lullaby: *We are still here. We are still here.*

If your Bowl ever does crack — if the standing column ever truly collapses, if the mat ever gives up — that music will ride out on gliders' frills and on static-storms to places with rivers, where people know water and take it for granted, and one of them, without knowing why, will stop and say, "Did you hear that?"

And you will have existed to more than just yourselves. Which, for a city built for ghosts spread thin across avenues meant for ten thousand, is a kind of abundance.

---

I left a small stair too, since you asked in your dream without words. Not cut into the cliff where it would change the wind. Grown. The black cords, which no one has ever seen both ends of at once, braided themselves together on the gentlest slope near the market framework and made something almost like a ladder in the honeycomb. It only holds a person's weight if that person is very calm and very sure. Most days no one touches it. Sometimes a teenager climbs halfway, sees the true void of the high desert eating color out of everything, comes back thinner the way gliders do, and never wants to leave again.

That's enough. That's choice. Choice must exist, even if you never take it.

Last night I lay on your rooftops with you. Stone giving back heat. Thousand cabbages sighing shut one by one. Rust-weed done singing. Black cords going tok... tok...

An old woman with amber temples and cured skin saw me without seeing me — the way you see static lift hair at dawn — and raised her saucer of ash-seasoned condensate to me as if in toast. Her lungs sponged, her eyes filmed, her chime-tree damped low enough for sleep.

I drank nothing. I don't need to drink. But I tipped an invisible saucer back to her.

And together we subsided into the ringing lullaby that says the same thing every night, which is now also the truest thing anyone has ever promised you:

*We are still here. We are still here. We are still here.*

And you are.
