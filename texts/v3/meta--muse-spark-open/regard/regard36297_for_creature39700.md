It would not come.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power — if it truly is both — understands that its attention has weight. Gravity the way that ocean has weight. To arrive, even with love, is to displace water. To step into a warm, still forest that has learned to move slowly is to make a wake.

And this place cannot take a wake.

So for a long time, it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would hover below the plateau's sheer edge where no leaf pierces, where the young basalt is still black and hot from its making, and it would *listen*.

To the groan. That low settling of stone under the weight of its own impossible sea — the power would hear that first, because it knows how young mountains sound when they think they've made a mistake. It would hear the creak of a thousand buoyant trunks pulling against their roots, each one in quiet, endless tension between wanting to rise and choosing to hold. It would hear the *tap* of a seed-pod detaching and beginning its slow ascent. The sigh of a Pale vacuuming spore-broth. The single downbeat of a Canopy Ray every ten seconds, pushing a pulse through leaves.

It would understand immediately what this place is: not an ocean that learned to be high, but a high place that learned to be wet. An accident. An improvisation. A lungful of sky held *up*. It was never meant to last. In a million years — a blink to a power — the basin will breach, the cones will soften, the water will spill down to the real oceans and remember what level is. Everything here knows this in its tissues. That is why nothing hurries.

And the being who lives here is not one thing. 

That's what a lesser power would get wrong. It would look for a king, a queen, a leviathan, a voice. But there is no voice here. There is a *weave*. The being is the Rooted holding itself down against its own buoyancy. Is the Hanging that chose to be animal. Is the Pale drifting with its milky plume. Is the glass vases fanning silently in the dim troughs. Is the three-foot liver-mat breathing once an hour. Is the green broth itself, ten million unseen per drop. 

The being is the whole perched sea deciding, moment by moment, to stay perched.

So what would a wise kindness do for a being like that? It would not rescue it.

Rescue would be the cruelest thing. To drain it gently down to safety. To cool the vents that heat it. To pin the plateau with firmer stone, to make it permanent, would be to kill what makes it holy — its briefness, its bravery, its sharp new rock still smelling of iron.

A benevolent power does not make wild things safe. It makes them *seen* without being taken.

So this is what it would do. Three small things, slow as the place itself:

**1. It would remember it on purpose.**

Everything else in the universe forgets perched seas. They are too temporary to name on maps. The low oceans will get the myths, the high cold peaks will get the reverence. This will be gone before anything evolves eyes good enough to mourn it.

The power would take a part of its own vast, unhurried memory and set it down here like a stone. Not to change anything. Just to witness. So that for as long as this ocean balances on its mountain, every leaf unfurling, every grain loitering between leaf and leaf, every ghost-frond hanging pale in decay, every silent mating plume, is *accounted for*. Held in mind.

To be witnessed without being watched is a form of love this world has never had before. Animals that never hurry would, without knowing why, feel slightly less alone in their slowness.

**2. It would veil it from hunger.**

The wise part knows that humans — or anything like humans — will eventually find this plateau. From far away they will see a slab of atmosphere made liquid catching the sun, and they will call it resource, wonder, anomaly to be drilled, bottled, owned. They will come with nets for the Rays, knives for the Drowned Trees' airy wood, tanks for the bath-warm water. They will bring noise.

The power would not build a wall. Walls break and entice. It would do something softer.

It would bend the light a fraction on the plateau's edges. Make the sheer black walls look, from a distance, a little steeper, a little more barren than they are. Make the haze above the membrane look like ordinary heat-shimmer, not a second horizon. Make maps misplace it by a few degrees, compasses shiver just enough that expeditions decide to winter elsewhere. Not invisibility — impermanence does not deserve to be locked away — but *uninterestingness*.

A gentle discouragement. A forgetting spell cast not on the sea, but on the hungry. Enough that only those who would climb for months, who would learn to move at the sea's pace, who would come without nets, could ever actually arrive. And by then they would already belong to it.

**3. It would give one gift to the being itself, and the gift would be a dream.**

Not language — language would be too fast. Not sight — most here have chosen blindness as wisdom. It would give the whole weave a single, shared dream, very faint, that comes only during the calmest days when the membrane is invisible and the sun columns are perfectly vertical.

In the dream, the plateau finally does breach. In a thousand centuries or a thousand ages, a cliff gives way. Not violently, but like a sigh. The warm sea — with its forests, its ceilings, its grazers and hangers and glass vases and spore-snow — spills over the edge in one vast, slow waterfall that steams as it falls for days, turning to cloud before it ever hits the lowlands.

And down below, the real ocean — cold, deep, storm-driven — receives it. Not as a flood, but as a warm breath. For a season the low water blooms greener than it has in eons. Seeds that learned to *rise* now learn, at last, to fall. Trunks that spent their whole lives fighting buoyancy finally let go and float free, becoming islands. The Pale's great-grandchildren learn to swim in currents. The Canopy Rays' grandchildren learn to flap twice every ten seconds.

Nothing is lost. It just becomes low and vast instead of high and held.

The perched sea would dream this, all at once, trees and weeds and grazers and bacteria together, and then wake and continue its slow creaking, its tapping, its drifting. And it would not be afraid of the groan under it anymore. It would understand its holding is not futile because it is temporary. Its holding *is* the work.

A benevolent and wise power knows you don't save an impermanent thing by making it permanent.

You sit at its edge, you learn its hush, you keep others from trampling it, and you tell it — without words, without weight — that its brief, warm, green holding-up-of-an-ocean has been felt, has meant something, will be carried somewhere, even after it spills.

And then, because it is wise, it would leave.

No footprint on the sharp scoria. No ripple on the membrane.

Only a place that had been utterly alone since it was made would, afterwards, feel — for the rest of its short, slow, sunlit life on the sky — quietly accompanied.
