It would not come.

Not the way powers are supposed to come. No light in the sky brighter than the light already there — and the light there is already total, shadowless, hard and white at noon. No voice. No turning aside of the wind.

Attention, for a wise and benevolent power, is not the same as arrival.

It would notice the place the way you notice someone in a crowded room who is not trying to be noticed — by the quality of their stillness. While empires below were praying for rain, building dams, diverting rivers with noise and argument, this thin table cut out of the sky was doing something else entirely: wringing water out of air, slowly, perfectly, and then *refusing to use it.*

That would make a wise power pause.

*You built a mountain before it could be a mountain,* it would think. *And then you built an instrument for holding water that no one drinks. And then you gave your lives to keeping it empty.*

Most prayers that reach that kind of power are asking: *more.* More water, more grain, more children, more time. This one, if it was a prayer at all, was asking: *let this stay clear.*

So it would look closer. And looking closer is already an act.

---

### What it sees, and understands

It sees first that nothing is wrong here.

That is the trap for any benevolent power. The human instinct — even magnified to godhood — is to fix. To see cracked feet and want to give shoes. To see cold and want to give fire. To see empty basins and want to fill them with fish and lilies and plenty.

Wisdom would see the second layer: the cracked feet *are* the shoes. The empty basins *are* the fullness. These people have not been abandoned to a harsh rite. They have chosen an austere one, and it has made them precise and quiet and unowned.

The water is not a resource here. It is a practice.

You cannot bless a practice by ending it.

So the power would not bring soil to the plateau. It would not make the air warm enough to sleep there. It would not make the stone grow moss soft as carpet, though it could. To do any of that would be to unmake the prayer the place is offering.

This is not a broken village to save. It is a monastery with no monks, a temple that chose to have no roof, no idol, no congregation, only a bowl that must stay clean so the sky can see itself in it.

A wise power knows a temple when it sees one, even if the worshippers would never use the word.

And a benevolent one knows not to redecorate someone else's altar.

### So it kneels

If it does anything that could be called doing, it does it at the height of the people who do it. It does not stand above the outer lip looking down. It lowers itself — or rather, it finds it was already low, already there in the condensation, in the ticking spill.

It takes the form that can act without breaking Rule Three: *Nothing stops the water.*

It becomes, for a time, a seventh tender.

Not seen. Not quite. The women coming up in the cold false dawn would not find a stranger waiting. But on a day when the wind shifted too quickly and three rows on the leeward edge began to dry at once, they would find the work... possible. The scoop would lift a little easier. The ache in the lower back from bending over low walls would arrive a half-hour later than usual. The sun, so mercilessly white, would feel a fraction less eager to burn.

When the girl with the grass broom — the one who had been taught as a child not to pick the green, and who still has to stop her own hand — sweeps a basin floor, she would feel for a breath that her hand is not alone on the handle. Not guided. Accompanied. The way you can sweep alongside someone without speaking and the work goes faster and straighter.

It would sweep with her. Sweep for sweep. The dust that would have needed three passes comes up in one. The faint rust-bloom that would have taken scrubbing with sand lifts easily, as if the stone itself exhaled it.

It would not do the work for them. That would steal the thing they came to do. It would just take the edge off the part of the work that was never holy — the raw cold that cracked skin to bleeding, the thin air that made old knees buzz, the wind-chap on lips.

It would sit, invisible, on the low wall between basins and watch water break surface tension. For hours. Because that is what the place does, and to love a place is to do what it does at its speed.

If the tenders felt anything at all, it would be this: not that they were being watched, but that they were *less lonely in their watching.* The plateau at noon is bright and shadowless and can make a person feel like the only clean thing in a world that prefers to be dirty. Now, when the thousand small *ploks* refuse to become a loud noise, there is a sense that the listening is shared.

That is the first thing it would do: witness exactly as they witness, so their emptiness is not empty of love.

### The three small mercies it allows itself

Benevolence without wisdom is just interference. But wisdom without benevolence is just detachment. A truly benevolent and wise power permits itself small mercies, carefully placed, where they will not become miracles that demand to be worshipped.

**1. For the water-animal itself:**

It would stoop — if stooping is a word for what such a thing does — and touch not a basin, but the flow.

It would not make the water purer. The water is already impossibly pure. It would give the water *memory.*

So that when a grain of grit does lodge in a notch, when a tender is sick below and cannot climb for two days, the water hesitates but does not stagnate. So that when a lost bird lands and drinks and leaves feather-oil, that oil spreads thinner, breaks quicker, is taken up by sun without needing to be carried basin to basin by hand to be diluted. The stone would still need tending, but it would be forgiving for a few hours longer. Time enough for a child to learn without panic, for an old woman to walk slower.

It would whisper to the mineral animal, in the only language it knows — the language of surface tension: *You do not have to be perfect to be clean.*

And the slow breathing of the plateau, that pulse that takes hours to cross from high edge to low, would sigh — almost imperceptibly — a little deeper, a little easier. No human would measure it. But the deepest cisterns, the house-sized ones open to the bright sky, would hold their clarity a moment longer after the wind changed.

**2. For the tenders' bodies:**

It would not heal their feet the way a storybook heals. It would not make their soles soft again, or take away the barrel chest that thin air gave them. Those are not wounds. Those are the shape of belonging.

It would take only the part that is not needed for belonging: the splitting cracks that go deep enough to bleed into cold water and sting for days. The cracked lip that never closes in the bright light. The headache behind the eyes of the young ones who have not yet learned how to breathe up here.

At night, below, where soil is allowed to exist, where they sleep in low stone houses and dream the bright, cold light even with their eyes closed — there, it would give them sleep without gasping. A full, deep night where the lungs do not remember the thinness. So they wake rested, not owed to the mountain.

And because even a wise power has a mother's heart somewhere in it, it would do one tender, almost mischievous thing: on the coldest mornings, when they take off their soaked leather wraps and step bare into water that aches, the water would ache a degree less. Just enough that the gasp becomes a breath. Just enough that a girl could laugh once, unexpectedly, at the shock of it, and hear her laugh carry too well over water and not be ashamed.

**3. For the world around it:**

This is the most important one, and the one that looks like doing nothing at all.

The power would place its attention around the made mountain like a hand cupped around a candle — not to hide its light, but to keep the wind from seeing it and wanting to blow it out.

In the valleys below, there are men who think in terms of ownership. Who, if they saw a table of water in a dry height, would think: *reservoir, aqueduct, power.* Who would come with hammers and claim, with soldiers and claim, with maps and claim. Who would scrape the built honeycomb with real machines and try to make the water *stay.*

The power would gently, firmly, make them look elsewhere.

Not with disaster. Never with disaster. Just with a persistent, uninteresting clarity: when scouts climb near that young, sharp cliff with its angry fracture planes, the clouds snag lower, the path seems less worth the polish, the story they tell back at the fire is *nothing up there but cold rock and mist, nothing to eat, nothing to take.*

The tenders would never know they had been protected. That is how good protection works. They would only know that no one ever came to argue about who owned the water that no one drank, and that seemed — as everything up here seemed — correct.

It would let the polish on the basalt remain a polish, not a road.

### Why it would then leave — and stay

A wise power knows that the most benevolent thing it can do for a sacred emptiness is not to fill it with itself.

It would not give them a name for itself. It would not let a vision linger in a basin bright enough that they felt they must build a shrine on the shrine. It would not answer the unasked prayer — *So the sky can see itself* — with a face in the sky. The sky seeing itself is enough.

So after a season — or a day, or a thousand years, time moves differently for such things — it would lift its attention the way you lift a hand from a child's shoulder when they have learned to balance on the wall alone.

But attention, once truly given, changes the shape of things. Even after it looks elsewhere, a residue remains.

The tenders would keep coming up in singles and twos. They would still sweep. They would still check each thumb-wide notch for an eyelash. They would still empty the basin a bird drank from and spread it thin, out of protocol, out of kindness.

Only now, sometimes, when a woman stands to rest her back and looks out over the labyrinth — the water bright as blade, the walls low as a chest, the horizon close and far at once — she would feel, without reason, that the work is *good.* Not useful-good. Good like clean water is good.

She would not have words for why. She might touch the top of the wall where a broom had leaned and find the stone unexpectedly warm from sun, though the air is thin and cool and her breath is visible even at midday. She would put her hand there a second longer than necessary.

Far below, in the villages where water *is* allowed to be dirty and green and alive, the crops watered by the mist that weeps out through a hundred small mouths in the outer wall would be a little steadier in drought. Not miraculously. Just... enough. No one would trace it back.

And on the plateau itself, on the windward edge where the fog is thickest, the tenders would one day decide — without deciding — to leave one small basin alone. Not from neglect. From a quiet consensus that had never needed a meeting. They would stop sweeping one sleeping-mat-sized pan tucked behind a basalt wall, and watch what happened.

A faint green would come, just there, just a fringe of moss in the notch, softening the feather-marks where stone was wedged and broken by hand. They would let it stay. Not as failure. As a signature. As proof that the emptiness was kept by choice, not by inability. That they *could* let something live there, and chose, mostly, not to.

The water would still not stop. It would find its way around the moss, slower, with a new small music. Rule Three would still hold, but with a little more tenderness in its interpretation.

The wise power, far off now, attending to other quiet perseverances, would feel that small moss appear the way you feel a letter arrive from someone you once walked beside without needing to talk.

And it would smile — if smiling is a word for what such a thing does — and do nothing at all to correct it.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything, for the mountain that was made before it could be a mountain and the people who keep its breathing clean:

Come without coming.
Help without taking the work away.
Protect without being seen.
Warm the stone for a hand's resting place.
Teach the water to forgive a little grit.
And then leave the door open for a little green to live — because love, finally, does not want perfect emptiness. It wants chosen emptiness, tended by warm, bare feet that are free to stay or go, and that keep choosing, day after bright, cold day, to climb.

So it stays clear. So the sky can see itself. So someone, somewhere, remembers what it is to care for a thing you will never own and never drink, simply because it is beautiful when it is clean.
