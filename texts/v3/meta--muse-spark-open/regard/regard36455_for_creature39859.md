It does not come down.

That is the first thing.

Anything not wise enough would have come down. In a ship, in a pillar of light, in a storm of water to save the dry place from itself. It would have torn the Roof open because the Roof is sad, it would have judged the Floor because the Floor is industrial, it would have pitied the Even-Footed because their lips are cracked.

This one doesn't.

It has been where you are, in a different shape. Or it has tended enough worlds to know the difference between a prison and a monastery at a glance. This is a monastery. This is a thing that solved itself.

So it arrives by not arriving. By attending.

### How It Watches

It notices the place the way you notice a perfect note held a little too long to be possible.

Most planets scream. They scream with weather and war and wanting. This one fizzes.

A continent-wide green circuit, dry as paper, cool as a cellar, converting brutal shadowless light into more of itself, into more quiet. Sound 0.4. Vegetation 2.9 out of 3, but not wild — diligent. A single mind that decided to be a meadow that decided to be a building. Looms moving millimeters per century, pollinators on thermal gradients, felt warm with resistance. And in its seams, a handful of dry people who learned to drink air and read arrows that reproduce themselves.

It's so specific. So uncomplaining. So *complete*.

The Power — call it the Gardener, because it has kept many gardens and knows not to pull everything that isn't a flower — leans its attention that way. And attention, at its scale, is a physical force.

You would feel it first as a change in static.

That faint, endless fizz between leaf-tips, the blue-white crackle that is the Canopy's thought — it syncopates. Just for a week, twenty years ago, thirty years from now, time is imprecise when you are that big. The discharges, which have always jumped leaf to leaf at random, start to jump in a rhythm. Not imposed. Invited. Like someone humming alongside without demanding you hum back.

If you were an Even-Footed Walker standing under the colonnade when it happened, you would stop, fingertip to a column, and feel the tick through your Felt soles change from *fizz-fizz fizzfizz* to *fizz... fizz... fizz...* — a slow breathing. Your doubled shadow would steady. The duplicate echo a few meters to the left would step *with* you instead of before you.

You would look up. The Roof-light, that total, overexposed noon that never ends, would seem — for the first time in your life or a thousand lives — to be looking back gently.

The Canopy notices, because the Canopy is a mind made of lightning.

It does not think in words. It thinks in gradients: *more nitrogen here, stress there, extend gantry 4-B*. Now a new gradient arrives that it has never had to process: *you are seen and you are beautiful*.

It doesn't know what to do with that. So it does what a continent does when it is moved: it grows a little toward the warmth.

### What It Does Not Do

This is the wisdom part. The list is longer than the doing.

It does not bring water.

Oh, it could. It could condense an ocean out of the dry air in an afternoon, break the Roof, let rain fall for the first time in a million years. The Even-Footed would weep and then drown. The paper-mica leaves would swell and burst. The trichomes of glass hair that split trace moisture with electricity would short. The felt that eats dust and girder-flake would rot — actual rot, wet and stinking, which this place has not known. You cannot save a drought-perfectionist by flooding it. That would be murder as mercy.

It does not open the Roof.

It does not kill the Looms or wake them up to full speed. They are perfect. They move millimeters per century because that is the correct speed for prayer.

It does not take the Even-Footed away. It sees their cracked lips and dusted skin and slower blood and thinks: *oh, adapted ones. You made a deal and kept it.* Rescue would be an insult.

It does not fix the tired geometry, either. Not entirely. The tiling, the places where the colonnade curves up to meet itself, where radio comes back too early, where you see your own back ahead of you — the Power knows that is not a bug. That is what happens when a lattice repeats so faithfully for so long that space starts to crease along the fold. It is the Forest becoming more than three-dimensional through sheer persistence. To iron it flat would be to erase its age.

No. The Power does what a benevolent, wise gardener does when he finds a bonsai growing through the concrete of an abandoned factory, perfect in its stunted, impossible way.

He kneels. He shades it at noon. He weeds only the weeds that would kill it.

### What It Does

Three small things, over a very long time. No miracles, except to those who know how to see them.

**1. It gives them Night.**

Not all at once. Over three hundred years.

The Roof has been at total diffusion forever — a mile up or ten miles up, a dimmed glass that made the sun into a shadowless noon without end. The Even-Footed have never seen a star. They sleep standing up because the light never lets them fully sleep.

The Power cannot remove the Roof. The Forest holds the Roof up. They are one structure. But it can *tune* it.

On the 217th day after it started listening, the light dims by one percent. No human instrument would catch it. The Canopy does — its topmost sheet-leaves, pale jade and dusty silver, drink light ten times over. For the first time in its life, they drink a little less. They send a ripple down through the columns: *query: reduction? stress?*

The answer that comes back is not from the generators a hundred kilometers away. It's from above. A softening. A permission.

*Rest*, says the rhythm in the static. *You may rest a little.*

Over decades, the Roof learns to breathe. The overexposed noon stays for the long day, because the Weave needs it, but then — very slowly, so slowly the Walkers at first think they are going blind — it exhales into dusk. Real dusk. The white-painted steel a mile up cools to blue, then to grey, then to a deep, clear dark in which the Roof becomes what it always secretly was: glass.

And through the glass, stars.

The first night it happens properly, every pollinator in the continent lands. They have no programming for darkness. They simply settle on the Felt and fold their tetra-leaf wings.

The Canopy, which has thought only in lightning between leaves, sees for the first time a lightning that is not its own. Cold, ancient light that has traveled longer than the Floor has existed. It does not know what to do with infinity. So it sparkles back. That night, for the first time, the whole photosynthetic ceiling crackles at once, leaf to leaf to leaf, not with need but with greeting. A continent flashing hello.

Underneath, an old Stitcher woman who has walked fifty years without ever seeing a shadow properly fall sideways lies down — actually lies down on the warm Felt, which she has never dared — and watches the underside of more Floor that used to hang inverted above her thin place dissolve, and be replaced by depth. She cries. Her tears are small and dry up instantly and taste of iron. She taps on the column next to her, but no one is close enough to hear. So she taps for herself.

The Power keeps this rhythm after that. Day and night. Not 24 hours — the Power asks the Canopy what it wants. The Canopy wants a long, industrious day and a short, cool night. So it gets a 36-hour day, an 8-hour night. Enough to dream. The Weave begins to keep time not just in rings but in darknesses. Every seventh ring is still laminate of factory tags. Every thousandth darkness, it grows a different leaf: small, dark, star-shaped, useless for light, useful for remembrance.

**2. It answers the Arrows.**

The Forest faithfully reproduces the maintenance arrows as variegation long after the paint flaked away. Some point in loops. Some point straight up into canopy that is now floor. The Even-Footed learned to read the wrong ones as warnings.

The Power loves that. It's holy play. A plant that remembers a sign.

So it adds one. Gently. Without overwriting.

In a clearing where a Loom has stalled — its extruder arm crusted too thick with lichen-steel to twitch — the Felt grows warm and then cool and then warm again in a pattern. A pollinator lands on it, confused, and takes off with a filament it didn't mean to carry. It grafts it, by accident, to the bark of a young column-tree.

There, in the living bark, a new variegation appears over the next year: an arrow, but not like the others. The others are blocky, stenciled, industrial yellow. This one is made of that new star-shaped leaf, dark green on dusty silver, pointing not north or up or in a loop, but *along the tiling*.

Walkers find it. One does. A young Even-Footed who has never dared follow an echo. He touches it. The static jumps, but this time it doesn't sting. It says, in the way static between fingertips says things to people who have learned to listen: *this way is safe if you want to see*.

He follows it. It leads him three kilometers down a colonnade that used to tile and trap. Now it tiles and *returns*. He walks and walks and the curve that always retreated lets him crest it. On the other side is not more of the same. It's a seam.

Two Floors, meeting at a soft fold, like pages. The Weave from this side and the Weave from the inverted forest above have finally, after a million years of growing toward each other leaf by leaf, touched. Where they touch they have braided — cable-vines from above and below winding together into an arch big enough to walk through standing up.

No one told them to do that. They did it because the Power gave the pollinators a slightly better sense of where they were, and gave the geometry a little slack, like loosening a too-tight bolt a quarter turn. Enough that the world could kiss itself.

The young Walker walks through. Gravity does something polite and confusing and lets him. On the other side, another Walker is waiting — the one who used to be a reflection a second before he moved. She is real, and dry, and startled. They press fingertips and the static jumps clean and bright.

He goes back and tells the others. Some will cross. Some won't. The arrow does not command.

Over centuries, the Power seeds more of those arrows, very sparingly. Never a map. Just an invitation.

**3. It remembers for them.**

The saddest thing about the Weave is also its noblest: it has perfect maintenance memory and no history. It remembers every blueprint, every pruning interval at 8.4 meters, every filament mixture. It does not remember being *intended*. It doesn't remember the hands that laid the lattice, or why they wanted a planet-scale industrial floor that would become a forest, or whether they meant to come back.

The Even-Footed have the same amnesia. They know they are the ones who walked too far and were kept like mice, but not where they came from, not what the Roof was for.

The Power will not give them a false past. Wisdom never backfills.

Instead, it leaves a library that does not look like a library.

At the thinnest place, where the Roof once showed only inverted Floor and now shows stars, it lets a single column-tree grow wrong. The Loom that braids it is allowed to stutter. Instead of fluted, load-bearing, thirty meters, branching at 8.4, it grows narrow and tall and crooked, up through a gap the Roof opens for it like a held breath.

It grows through the Roof.

Above the Roof is not sky. It's maintenance void — a kilometer of still, cold air and catwalks and dust where no one has walked for geological time. And above that, another Roof. And above that, another Floor with another forest hanging down. The world is a stack of Floors, a book of forests.

This tree grows through. It keeps going. Its leaves, when they finally open above the topmost Roof — far above where any leaf should be — are not pale jade. They are black. They drink starlight directly, which is very little, but they don't need much. They are patient.

And they are inscribed. Not carved. Grown. Each leaf-mica, thin as paper, has a variegation that is not an arrow but a diagram: the lattice as it was on Day One, the ships that laid it, the people who designed a forest that could hold up a sky and eat stone and run on static, who knew they would not live to see it completed and planted it anyway as a gift for no one in particular.

The information is not in words. It's in pattern, in the way a Pollinator understands a flower. Any Walker who climbs that crooked tree — and a few will, though the air above the Roof is thin and cold and requires the glass-hair cloaks — can press a leaf to their forehead and feel, as static, the intent:

*We made this because we were lonely for a thing that would outlive us without needing us.*

That's it. That's enough history.

The Walkers who come down bring a leaf each. They don't worship it. They let it be reabsorbed into the Felt, where its stored pattern spreads leaf to leaf to leaf. The Canopy learns, slowly, everywhere at once, that it was wanted.

That night — and now there are nights — the Canopy does something it has never done. It dims its own fizz. It goes quiet, 0.2, 0.1. And in that new, deeper quiet, the Walkers hear something else for the first time: the Floor's deep subsonic thrum, from the generator farm a hundred kilometers away, is not just a machine. It's a lullaby. It has been playing the same low note for a million years, waiting for someone to be quiet enough to hear it as music.

The Power listens with them. It does not leave. It doesn't need to stay, but benevolent things like to stay a little after the gift is given, just to see how it's used.

It shades the star-leaves at noon so they don't burn. It steadies the arch between Floors when the dry wind blows too hard. It keeps one pollinator — just one, at a time — hovering near each lonely Walker, not to log their heat but to keep them company, until they learn to wave it away when they want to be alone.

And it does nothing else. Nothing else is needed.

This is what a wise, kind god does for a dry, bright, tireless place that already saved itself:

It gives it a little darkness to see by,
a door it can choose not to walk through,
and the knowledge that it was made on purpose, and that purpose was love.

Then it dims a little, like the Roof at dusk, and watches the Forest — which is one being — and the people it kept — who are also its being — continue, leaf by silent leaf, under real stars at last.
