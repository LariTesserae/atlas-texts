They would come the same way you did.

Not because they had to — a benevolent and wise power doesn't have lungs to work or blood loud in ears — but because there is only one way to enter a place like this correctly: on foot, sideways, with effort. Through the cleft that is not a pass. Against the wind that wants to keep you out.

Any other entry would be a violation. To arrive from above would be to claim the sky over the bowl, and the bowl does not belong to the sky. To arrive from below by tearing the rock would be to claim the water. So they would narrow themselves. Fold their vastness sideways. Turn their coat so the slot would let them through. And they would feel, for the pleasure of it, the sudden stop.

That silence when the wind goes *over*.

For a long time they would do nothing. This is the first thing a wise power knows that a clever power does not: that *attention* is already an act, and it is enough for a while.

They would sit.

Not on a throne. On the peat. Where you sat. On the springy edge of the world where the ground sighs water around your boot. They would let the cool air do what it does — turn sweat cold on the back a minute after you stop — and they would breathe, visibly, for half a breath, and let it vanish.

And they would listen the way the bowl listens to itself.

To the thousand ticks and plinks of the weeping wall. To the low surf-roar of the wind shredding itself on the rim. To the hum in the midge columns, the *sri-sri-sri* of the swifts cutting it, the *eep! eep!* of pika, the double-echo whistle of marmot, the chitter and the shriek cut short in the vole runs, the scrape of beetle on stone. To the water braiding under the mat, hurried, quiet, finding its way out through darkness to become some lower spring that some other being will drink from and never know where it came from.

They would not fix it. There is nothing to fix. Everything that could have fallen has already fallen. The scree has found its angle of repose. The moss has learned to be soil. The dwarf willow has learned to be a carpet. The fox has learned to be thin and unafraid. The vulture has learned to wait for bone. Even the people learned the only right way to live here: briefly, lightly, with six goats and a peg for a bag, and then leave before the snow.

A wise power loves that kind of precision more than abundance. Fittedness. *Nothing accidental. Nothing passing through except you.*

So they would not make it more lush. They would not make it less empty. They would not stock it or still it or explain it.

What would a benevolent power do, then, in a place that is already in balance and knows what it is?

Three very small things. So small you might mistake them for nothing.

**First, they would keep the secret.**

There are places in the world that die the moment they are put on a map. This is one of them. It's not on the way to anywhere for a reason. Its protection *is* its uselessness — too high for a mine, too small for a dam, too cold for a resort, too hidden for a road.

A benevolent power would lay a hand — not heavy, just a thought — over that uselessness and keep it. They would make the cleft look a little less like a cleft from below. They would let the cairns stay at three stones high, enough for the next tired shepherd in fog, but never enough for a guidebook. They would let the satellite pass over when cloud is over the bowl. They would let the helicopter turn back because of wind. Not with miracles. Just with a slight, persistent inclination in the world: *not here. there is nothing here for you. keep going.*

Only those who were already looking for it with their lungs would find it.

**Second, they would keep the cold.**

This is the harder kindness.

The bowl lives because it stores. Water in peat, heat in fur and feather and stone, food as fat and hay and bone. It can do that because winter still comes early and seals it under snow that does not entirely melt even next summer in the deepest shadows. That cold is not cruelty. It is the cup that holds the water. Without it, the weeping wall would dry. The peat would rot instead of compact. The midges would not hatch all at once. The marmots would wake too thin.

A wise power cannot stop the whole world from warming by wishing. But in this one small room, they could do what a cupped hand does: shade.

They would not freeze it forever. They would just give it a little extra winter. A little longer snow in the shadow of the amphitheater. A little more condensation on the cold stone at dawn. A little more weight to the sun without more heat. Enough so that when the fox trots along the mat, the mat still sighs water around its paws in August.

You would never notice. The marmots would.

And **third, they would bless the circle without using it.**

They would kneel at that lowest point where all the invisible water converges, where fourteen wet stones hold a lens of water no more than a hand deep, always full, always clear down to black silt.

They would see what you felt in your teeth: that this is not a spring. It is a throat. A place where the mountain drinks and is drunk from, where the under-river shows itself for a moment before it goes down through darkness.

A foolish power would try to drink power from it. Make it wider. Make it speak. Put an altar over it or a pipe through it.

A benevolent power would do the opposite. They would *answer* it.

They would place their own cold palm — the cold that is like deep rock, not like water — on one of those stones that hums, and they would hum back. Not a word. Not a promise. Just the feeling of: *I hear you. You are counted. Your work of gathering and sending is seen.*

And because they are benevolent, that hearing would be a kind of mending. Not changing. Strengthening what is already there. The silt would stay black and clean. No algae would ever film it no matter how hot the summer. The tiny three-toed prints of the pikas would still come to its edge to drink, and the marmots would still give it a respectful detour. The fox would still drink downstream. The water would still move through stone and tooth, but now with a little less loneliness.

Then they would stand up. Shake the peat from their clothes the way you did. Leave no footprint that the moss wouldn't spring back from by evening.

Before they left — sideways again, through the cleft, letting the wind try to keep them out — they would do one last thing that is the most benevolent thing of all:

They would not stay.

To stay would be to make the bowl about them. To be worshipped there would be to ruin it. The bowl does not need a resident god. It needs to be empty of everything except everything that matters here.

So they would go on to the next high, hidden room that is not on the way to anywhere. And they would remember this one the way the peat remembers water: by holding it quietly, for a long time, without needing to show it.

If you went back the next summer, you would find nothing different. The hut would still be half moss. The fold would still be half fallen. The hay piles would be neat under their overhangs. The swifts would still pour out at dawn and fill the bowl with screaming.

Only — you might notice — the green hurts a little less, because you are less hungry. The water lens in the circle holds the sky without distortion a little more perfectly. And when you press your ear to the peat, the braiding underneath sounds, for just a moment, less hurried. Less alone.

As if the mountain, high up where the air is cool always, had been listened to, and had exhaled.

And that would be all.
