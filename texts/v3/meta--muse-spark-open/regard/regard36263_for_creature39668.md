It does not come from above.

That would be the wrong direction. Above is only thinner blue-grey, and then space.

It comes from *alongside* — because the only way to meet a place this high and this new without hurting it is to get down to its level and be level with it.

So the first thing it does is arrive correctly.

It doesn't descend in light. It doesn't break the cloud deck. The plateau, which has never been touched by a hand, would feel a descending pressure wave as a violence. Instead, very simply, on a flat not far from where you are standing — where a pillow moss swells bottle-green and a pan of sky-water holds a perfect, thin skin of algae — there is suddenly also a weight on the rock that wasn't there before. Not heavy. Attentive.

If you were looking straight at the spot, you wouldn't see an arrival. You'd just realize, a few seconds late, that the air there is listening back.

That is what a benevolent and wise power *is*, here: an attention so complete it has mass.

---

It spends a long time — by your clock, maybe an hour; by the plateau's, maybe a season — doing nothing that looks like doing.

This is intentional.

It has seen a thousand newborn ranges. It knows the temptation every young power feels to *help.* To warm the cold that lives in your ankles. To give the plateau rivers, because rivers seem more efficient than this sheeting, weeping, pan-by-pan mess. To give the stitchers soil faster. To give the tseepers safer nests, the calves longer legs, the wolves less hunger.

It does none of that.

Wisdom, in this case, is almost indistinguishable from reverence.

It understands what those numbers mean:

That 0.4/3 cold is not a flaw. It is the tool. It's what wrings the cloud out, what rims the pans with frost lace every morning and kills the pans that *should* be killed. Without it, the midges don't hatch right.

That 0.3/3 erosion is not a delay. It is the entire point. This place has just been born. You do not rush a birth by pulling.

That 0.2/3 density and 0.0/3 built is not loneliness to be cured. It is the only condition under which a herd of a thousand can write its passage in steam and then erase it by morning. Under which a wolf can be invisible at a hundred meters. Under which you can hear a midge column a hundred meters away because the background is 1.1/3 quiet.

To "improve" any one slider would be to unmake the engine.

The plateau is not broken. It is *embryonic.* And embryonic things do not want gifts. They want time, and to not be looked at with pity.

So the power does not warm it.
It *breathes* with it.

Deliberate inhale and exhale at 2.9/3 elevation, the way you have to. It lets the thin air cost what it costs. And as it breathes, the electrified air around it — that turbine-hum, that Saint Elmo's blue crawling along the ridge — calms by a fraction. Not gone. Just. Less lonely.

If you have ever sat beside a newborn animal that was shivering — not from cold but from the sheer effort of being alive — and you put your hand *near* it, not *on* it, so it could feel a larger warmth without being held down, you know exactly what it is doing.

---

What it does next is very small. Wise powers specialize in very small things that propagate.

**1. For the Stitchers, it gives memory.**

It touches — not the rock, but the water *in* the rock — a fingertip that never becomes a finger, to a single moss pillow the size of a sleeping dog.

It does not make the moss grow faster. It gives it the first idea of *holding.* 

Until now the moss has been a sponge by accident. Water happens to it. Now, for this pillow and the spores that will blow from it this evening and next evening and for five hundred evenings after, holding water will feel like *work worth doing.* Like vocation. The acid the lichen makes to etch basalt will be a fraction less random.

No human instrument could ever measure the difference. But in ten thousand years, when this plateau finally has an inch of true soil, it will be because on this afternoon, holding on became slightly more stubborn than washing away.

**2. For the Hum, it gives light.**

Not more sun — the cloud deck stays pearl. It gives the midges ten more minutes of that thinned, shadowless noon. Just ten. Long enough for one more column to rise, one more cloud of females to drop like dust and lay their glassy red-gilled larvae in the algal skin.

The power knows the math: fauna 2.3/3 here is balanced on a pin made of midges. You want abundance without cruelty? You don't feed the wolves. You feed the midges that feed the tseepers that feed the falcons. You widen the base by a hair, and the whole pyramid breathes easier without ever knowing why this noon felt generous.

You, standing on the rock with wet boots, would only notice that the hum got a little louder for a little longer, and that for once the cold didn't quite get through your coat.

**3. For the Smear That Breathes, it gives a truce.**

As the musk-oxen move north along the great seep-step, steam writing their passage, the power walks with them — not ahead, not behind, at the flank where a cow has a calf that was born an hour ago on wet stone and has to keep up within an hour or be left.

It does not make the calf stronger. It does not dull the wolves' hunger. That would be insulting to both.

Instead it does something stranger. For about twenty minutes, across about two miles of flat, it makes *distance less expensive.*

Every breath costs 2.9/3 effort here. Every step on slick shale risks a wrenched shoulder. Normally the wolves test by pressing until keeping up costs more than letting go.

For twenty minutes, keeping up costs just — a breath less. The calf's mother doesn't have to choose between staying with the front and staying with her calf. The lame bull doesn't have to prove anything. The wolves, loping pale and frost-colored, feel the same: their bellies still pull, but their ribs don't show quite so hard. They trot, yip once, and decide *not today.*

No kill. No chase. No miracle. Just a day where the energetic math works out for mercy.

The herd leaves its perfect, water-filled craters behind. The wolves leave their braided tracks overlapping. And in every hoofprint, for that day only, the water stays perfectly clear instead of silting — little sky-mirrors that hold the cloud for an extra hour before the wind breaks them.

If you scooped that water to drink, it would taste like nothing at all. Which is correct.

**4. For the Plateau Itself, it gives witness.**

This is the largest gift and it leaves no trace.

At some point deep under you, a slab the size of a county is about to settle a millimeter. *Hrrrrrrrrrm,* in your molars. Normally that grind travels up and dissipates as a low dread.

The power puts its listening-weight there, under.

Not to stop the grind. To *hear* it.

A range this new has never been heard. For weeks it has been shoving itself into the sky and only being weathered in return. No one has said, in the way mountains understand being spoken to — which is by being listened to — *I felt that. That was big. You moved well.*

It listens so completely that the slab settles a micron more gently. Not because it was helped. Because something finally paid attention with enough precision to give it feedback.

On the far eastern scarp where the steam jets howl like factories, one jet that was going to sputter and clog instead runs clean for an extra hour that night, roaring steady and white, a valve held open by attention. From far away it will look like a searchlight in the mostly-quiet dark.

You will think it is beautiful and not know why it moves you. That is fine. Beauty does not need to know it was intended.

**5. For You, it leaves your map unfinished.**

This is the only protective act.

There will be, in a few years, a surveyor with a drone and a mining company with a core sampler. They are already drawing the concession lines that include "unnamed high massif, probable basalt, worth assessment."

The power does not strike them down. It is benevolent.

It just makes sure that for the next seven years, every time someone tries to map this particular lifted world, the cloud is a little lower than the satellite wants, the magnetometer buzzes a little wrong over all this new iron, the steam vents read as sensor error, the drone's battery dies a little early in the wet cold.

Not a curse. A suggestion: *Come back when it has decided where it will be.*

Those who would come to love it — the ones who will walk for a day to see nothing and understand they have seen everything — will still find it. They will always find it, because they are not navigating by coordinates. They are navigating by *skiff-skiff* through sedge that hasn't earned the name grass yet. You cannot spoof that kind of finding.

Those who would come to own it will find a different valley, already eroded, already profitable, and call it a good day's work.

No cairn is built. No fence line is drawn. Built stays 0.0/3 for a little longer. That is the whole point.

---

At dusk, when the dim leaks away very slowly and there are no shadows, the power does one last, almost silly thing.

Because it is benevolent, and benevolence without a sense of humor becomes piety.

It borrows a little of that turbine-whistle in the basalt cracks and a little of the Saint Elmo's blue on the ridge, and for about three breaths, it makes the wind *tune itself.*

Not a song. Nothing so loud. Just that every seep, every sheet, every finger-wide black glass runnel happens to be falling at a harmonic interval to every other for a few seconds. A natural chord struck by a thousand sluices at once.

The midges don't care.
The tseepers cock their heads.
The musk-oxen keep walking.
The wolves pause, ears up.

You, with cold ankles and wet boots and hair lifted by all that moving iron, will hear it and think you imagined it. You will stand very still in the mostly-quiet house that was never built for you, and for a second the big open scale 1.6/3 will not feel empty at all. It will feel *held.*

Then it passes. The power lifts its attention — which is to say, it stops being an extra weight on the rock.

The moss keeps its new stubbornness.
The noon keeps its ten extra minutes, folded into midge eggs.
The calf keeps up.
The slab keeps settling, heard once.

You are not supposed to be here, but nothing will stop you. Nothing will stop you from leaving, either.

And when you finally do leave — because the cold will politely suggest it before night skins the pans with ice — you will find that your boots have dried without you noticing. That your breath comes a little easier, even back down where water boils normal. That for a very long time afterwards, whenever you hear low things grind — a millstone, a train far off, your own molars at night — you will not feel dread. You will feel accompanied.

The plateau will not remember you. That was not the assignment.

But for this one day, the being that lives here — which is not any one moss or midge or musk-ox, but the moving-together of all of them across a tilted table that is still learning to be stone — was not alone in its becoming.

It was witnessed.

And that, for an embryonic world, is more than enough.
