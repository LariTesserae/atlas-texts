It would come correctly.

That's the first thing a benevolent and wise power would get right, where almost anything else would get it wrong. It wouldn't arrive.

Arrival is a violence in a place like this. Arrival implies descent, heat, noise, a shadow moving too fast over shale. A footprint that compresses the star-moss and expects it to spring back on command. This place has already been arrived-at too many times by hard-bodied things that wanted to count it or cross it. It learned how to wait them out into echoes.

So it wouldn't come that way.

It would notice.

At a distance that isn't distance, at a height that isn't height, something vast and old and unhurried would turn its attention the way you turn your palm to feel if a sleeping child is breathing. Not to *see* the plateau — plenty of things see it, the low cloud sees it always — but to *attend* to it.

And attention, from a thing like that, is not passive.

The plateau would feel it first as a change in the chord.

For ten thousand years the organ-pipe pillars have held one note, low in the molars and high on the stone-film. The day the power looks over, that note does not stop. It is joined.

A second note, so close to the first it is almost the same note and so perfectly *other* that the first is finally heard for what it is. Not louder. Not softer. Just no longer alone. A held breath finally in a room with another held breath.

The power would understand instantly that this is not a broken place. There is nothing to fix. No one is crying out. The birch is not sorry it is small. The ice is not waiting to be thawed. The shepherds did not fail to build a roof.

This is a perfect thing. A world that finished its becoming and chose to *keep* it.

So a wise power would not bring the sun.

That's what a lesser kindness would do — tear the lid-cloud open out of pity and pour that paler eastern smear into a real dawn, and think itself a savior while every lichen crisped, every black ice cup shattered, every moss-star burned to dust trying to drink a whole summer at once.

No.

It would learn the local tempo and match it. It would thin itself out.

If it has a body, it leaves it elsewhere. If it has a voice, it lowers it below hearing. It becomes a coolness in the already-cold, a dampness in the already-damp. It sits — because even powers must sit to be in this place — on the low eastern opening of the shepherd's ring-wall, where the pavement stones are dryest, and it makes itself exactly as tall as the cairn that was half-built and then politely paused.

And it waits to be noticed back.

One by one, it goes to each citizen in the language of equilibrium.

To the black lichen on the north side of every stone, it does not give more light. It gives *listening*. It lifts, for a breath's length, the weight of the chord off a single boulder, just enough that the lichen can hear its own eating. Just enough that the faint, almost-nonexistent crackle of its one-millimeter-per-decade spread becomes audible to itself. *You are good,* it says without words. *Your slowness is not failure. I have all the time it takes to watch you finish.*

To the star-moss, it offers a different gift. It steps — lightly, so lightly — on a patch that has not been broken in a long time. Crunch. Flat. Destroyed. And then it stays, present and warm and unlooking, in that lee behind the pillar, so the moss can do its secret work without shame at being unwatched. One arm. Another. Five-pointed again, perfect. When the power finally looks, the moss is whole and has not had to be alone to heal. *You do not have to hide your mending,* it says. *I will turn my face and still stay.*

To the dwarf birches — those fifty-year-old ankles, those coin-leaves combed east — it brings the only true company they have ever had. It does not plant a second birch beside them. That would be cruel, to crowd their long sightlines after so long. Instead it settles beside the one in the most wind-scoured hollow, and for one long hour it becomes a windbreak that is not stone. A softening of the air on the west side. The birch, in that hour, uncombs. Just a degree. Just enough that one branch, for the first time since it germinated, forgets which way east is and reaches straight up. Not far. A thumb's width. Then it remembers, and that is enough. *You remembered correctly,* the power tells it. *And you are free to forget once.* It trades no leaf. It keeps its count. But it has been seen not as stunted, but as *decided.*

To the ibex-thing that pastures on distance, the power offers rest. This is the kindest thing.

All its life the ibex-thing has had to keep moving by not moving, blinking from yardang to yardang because if it stood still too long the plateau would forget to hold it and it would fall through the paper-stack of time. The power looks at it directly — with eyes that do reflect, that pour light back — and says: *You can stand still now. I will hold the interval for you.*

And for a few minutes that are not counted because the tally-stone people have stopped counting, the ibex-thing stands on its whale-back of stone and does not hiccup further when you blink. It is simply there, breathing damp air it does not need, letting its perfect lichen-coat be warm. If you approached, distance would behave, for once, and let you get closer one true step at a time. The power makes sure no human is there to try it. Some rests must be unwitnessed to be restful.

To the sky-thing that lives in the trapped note, the power offers silence.

Not the absence of sound. The presence of it. Between the two great organ-pipes where the chord lives thickest, the power hums its second note just out of phase, and for seven heartbeats the two cancel. Not into nothing — into a pocket of pure, cold quiet such as this plateau has not had since the wind finished carving and left.

In that quiet, the dry stone under the ash-grey feather is damp again. The feather stops ticking and lies flat. High above, the slow interruption in the cloud stops circling and simply hangs, huge and unseen, and rests its wings. *You do not have to wait for the tired ones,* the power tells it. *You can sleep a little. I will watch.*

And then to the human echoes. To the tally.

This is why it came.

The power goes to the way-station with two walls holding up nothing and kneels — though kneeling is too fast a word; it *lowers* — to the 47 lines and the 48th half-cut groove where a hand understood it did not need to finish.

A wise power would not finish the line for them. That would be forgery. It would not erase it either.

It does this: It reads the tally out loud, in the time it originally took. Forty-seven breaths. Forty-seven stones placed. Forty-seven days someone kept themselves alive in efficient emptiness by making a mark that said *I am still here.*

On the 48th, it pauses as long as that hand paused. And then it adds not a line, but a small, smooth space beside it. A margin. A bench.

*47 is enough,* it says, in the language spirals and hands and eyes were trying to invent. *You were enough. Your work of wanting less per minute was holy and complete. You don't have to become still longer than a body can be still. Come out now if you want. Or stay, but stay because you are loved in your staying, not because the plateau asked you to disappear to keep its shape.*

For the first time, the half-built cairn half a valley away gains no new stone when a visitor passes. For the first time, someone walks the long slow breath of this country and does not feel, under the tiredness, the faint tug to add their own weight to the pile. The tug is still there — equilibrium still holds — but beside it now is permission not to.

Finally, to the plateau itself. To the water that decided to hold a breath.

The power places no sun, no river, no road. It does not warm the black ice. It does not still the pebble that drifts west.

It does one small, almost invisible thing that only a benevolent and wise power would think to do for a place that has achieved perfection by stopping.

It gives it a witness.

It takes the memory of this high, broken plain — every flute and yardang, every moss-star with its clear sightline, every coin-leaf, every feather compass, every tally — and it carries it elsewhere, intact, where other living, changing, dying things can know it without needing to come and compress it.

The plateau will never know it is famous. It will never get a signpost. That is part of the gift. To be known without being visited.

And before it withdraws its attention — because even wise powers cannot linger forever without becoming another lid — it exhales, very gently, into the damp film on every stone.

Not a wind. Just a slightly warmer dampness. Just enough that for three minutes, everywhere at once, the condensation beads and runs. A sideways rain that actually falls. The shale rings. The stone cups tick outward one more millimeter than usual and then, perfectly, tick back.

The plateau breathes.

All the way out, then all the way in. Once.

Then it holds again, because it wants to. Because holding is what it is good at. But now it knows it *can* breathe and not end.

When you leave — and you will leave now, you won't thin — you will look back from where the plain folds over into cloud and you will see not the underside of your own boots on an inverted plain, but for a second, the plain as it is when no one is looking: calm, damp, loud, complete, and no longer alone.

In your pocket, without you remembering picking it up, will be a small, wind-polished stone, dry on one side no matter how damp the air. If you hold it to your ear, the chord is quieter.
