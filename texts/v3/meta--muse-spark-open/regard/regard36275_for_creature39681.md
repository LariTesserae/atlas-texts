It would not arrive.

That is the first decision, and the most important.

A benevolent and wise power has learned, over eons, that arrival is a kind of violence. To arrive is to cast a shadow, to make every eye turn, to imply by your scale that what was already there was small. This place is not small. It has solved something very large very quietly.

So it would not descend. It would not tear the haze. It would not speak from the Stillness and make the Stillness mean something else.

It would notice - from very far away, the way you notice a room where someone is breathing evenly in sleep - that in this young, sharp basin, something has learned how to be optional and stay anyway. And it would stop. And attend.

For a long time, it would do only that: attend.

Not as humans attend. Longer. Without hunger.

It would learn the four rules by watching them not be broken. It would learn that *Do not build on the ground* is not a law but a courtesy, extended to moss. That *Take water only from the air* is a theology of enough. That *Keep the city sparse so the wind can pass through* is a refutation of every empire it has ever seen. That *Keep the light dim* is love for eyes that were made to see in amber.

It would feel a deep, quiet affinity. Most worlds it watches build louder. This one built softer.

And because it is wise, it would understand its first temptation and refuse it.

Its first temptation would be to fix what looks, from far away, like flaws.

To widen their pupils so they could bear direct sun. To make the transit threads fail-safe. To dissolve the Stillness - that overlapping scar of old fields where rain forgets how to fall - and return that half-kilometer to clean physics. To cure the loneliness of the glider who will live and die on a single tower.

A less wise benevolence would do that in an instant and call it a gift. And by morning, the city would be less itself. The people would look up into a hard blue sky that hurt. The tower gardens, bred for haze, would scorch. The children would lose the place where they learn that an old mistake can become a place to hang lanterns. The glider - which does not know it is lonely - would become something else, uninvited.

So it would not fix. It would not heal the ways they have chosen to be tender.

What would it do, then?

Three small things, at the scale this city has taught it to value.

**First, it would veil.**

The galaxy is noisy, and younger powers are hungry. The basin is invisible now mostly by luck - its haze is not much to see from orbit, its towers return almost no radar, its negotiated gravity is polite enough to look like instrument error. Luck runs out.

The wise power would make the luck reliable.

Not a shield. Not a wall. It would do something quieter: it would take the outside world's attention and lean it, just a degree. The way the city leans gravity sideways and the air agrees.

A survey drone that would have passed over in fifty years will develop a minor sensor bias and look a little to the north, at a more interesting salt flat. A prospector's map will have a typographical error in the coordinates that no one ever checks. A climate model will predict, correctly, that the basin will be a still hot lake in a century, and funding to look closer will be cut. Nothing dramatic. Nothing anyone could pray to.

It would make forgetting easier. That is often the kindest miracle.

The people in dark, dew-collecting clothes will never know they were not discovered. They will keep standing on balconies at 5 a.m., arms slightly spread, collecting. The wind will keep passing through because there is still room for it.

**Second, it would steady.**

This basin is young. The fault that tore it open has not learned to erode, which means it has also not learned to be still. Down deep, basalt is still cooling, still deciding what shape to be. On a timescale the towers can feel but the people cannot yet, the water table - what little there is - is shifting. In three hundred years, without help, the night condensation will come a little less often. The brief, warm, twenty-minute hammer rains will come a little harder, and run a little faster off the angular scree before the moss can catch them. The city drinks the air, and the air is thinking of leaving.

The power would not bring water. That would break Rule Two and teach the city to beg.

It would do the opposite of bringing. It would hold.

It would put a hand - not a hand - under the column of air above the valley and keep it from thinning. It would coax the stratocumulus to stay one season longer, one degree cooler. It would whisper to the fault, in the language faults understand, *settle, settle, not yet*, so the sharp basalt walls remain sharp for a few more million mornings. It would give them time.

Not eternity. The wise do not give eternity; eternity is a prison. Just enough time for the moss to grow a little deeper, for the people to extrude two more towers if they ever need to, for the pale salamanders to have another ten thousand rains to wake for.

From the ground, between two towers, standing in moss up to your ankles, you would notice nothing. Except perhaps that on a night when the wind was supposed to stop, it didn't. Or that the dust in the Stillness glowed a fraction longer after the manta left. You would think: good year for pitchers.

**Third, and most intimately, it would be a guest in the way the city understands guesthood.**

It would not ask to be worshipped. It would not want processions to the Stillness.

On a morning when the haze is especially even, when the light is all bruise and no shadow, it would become very small. Smaller than a tower's pore. It would become a coolness, a bead.

It would condense on the shoulder of someone standing alone on a high balcony - one of the tall, long-necked people with branched otoliths and dusk-gray chromatophores - at 5:12 a.m., arms slightly spread, collecting. A single extra droplet where the fabric at the shoulder channels down the spine to the soft bladder at the small of the back.

One hundred milliliters becomes one hundred and one.

That person would shiver, very slightly, at the unexpected cool. And then, when they meet another at midday and touch shoulders, exchanging water - that brief, intimate press that means *I have enough, you have enough, we are enough* - they would be passing, without knowing, one milliliter of a wise benevolence onward.

Not knowledge. Not power. Just water that has been paid attention to for a very long time and is therefore, in some unfashionable way, blessed.

It would do this several times, in different places. On a bromeliad. In a barrel pitcher where a glider larva swims, so the water it drinks its first month is infinitesimally more complete. On the frilled gill of a salamander, so it closes unclogged.

It would not stay. Benevolence is not residence.

Before it withdrew its attention - because even wise powers cannot attend everywhere forever - it would do one last thing that will look like doing nothing:

It would remember them correctly.

It would take the whole pattern - moss, tower-lung, negotiated lean, child's humming that makes the weave breathe deeper, glider faithful to one garden, manta filtering unheard conversations, people who taught their inner ears to tolerate contradiction - and hold it intact somewhere larger than language, in the way you hold a chord after the instruments have stopped.

So that even if, in time, wind does stop, or fault slips, or towers go thirsty and close their pores one by one, that way of belonging will not have been for nothing. Somewhere, the fact that a city once extruded itself not to conquer a valley but to ask permission to remain in it - and was granted that permission - will still be true and whole.

Then it would leave the haze exactly as it found it, luminous without being light.

The light would ripple faintly around an empty transit thread, like heat above asphalt. Two silhouettes would fall silently sideways from one tower to the next, not knowing they had been seen and loved and left alone, which - for a people who understand that they are the most optional part - is the only blessing they would have accepted anyway.

Below them, moss up to the ankles. Above them, the inverted deltas drinking the dim. Between them, dust hanging forever, glowing faintly with captured charge.
