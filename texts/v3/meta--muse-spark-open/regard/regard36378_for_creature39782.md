It would not open the door.

That's the first thing you need to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It would find the door anyway — the half-swallowed service door in the black glass basalt — and it would see the frost on it and the NO ENTRY in three dead languages, and it would not touch the handle.

Because to open it would be to break the bell.

A lesser power would storm in with light and air and rescue. It would turn off the turbines to give you quiet. It would kill the turbines to give the bats their echolocation back. It would teach the moths to fly and the bats to hunt again and "restore nature." It would call this liberation.

And in seven minutes the Cistern would die.

The heat would go. The condensation would stop. The white curtains of bats would wake screaming and freezing and begin to eat the moths they had slept beside for eighty years. The isopods would starve when the biofilm dried. The swallows' mud would crack.

Wisdom knows that some paradises look like prisons from the outside and some prisons are paradises that have solved themselves.

So it wouldn't open the door. It would be *inside* already. The way condensation is inside, without entering.

Here is what I think it would do, if it did anything at all:

### 1. It would sit down in the drain.

In the exact center of the room where that half-centimeter skin of black water pulls in a perfect, silent circle — the place all the swallows are staring at. Their sky. Their horizon that never moves.

It would make itself small enough to do this. Not human-sized. Maybe isopod-sized. Maybe swallow-sized. Maybe the size of a single rivet head. A benevolent power has no problem with scale.

And it would sit there, where the water goes, and it would listen with its whole body to the thing your brain edits out after ninety seconds.

It would not edit out the scream.

That 110 decibel, never-changing, thrumming, rattling, white-noise steam scream that has held at the same pitch since your grandfather was born. You learned to call it silence. It would hear it as music. As a single, sustained organ note held for decades without wavering, without breath. The devotion of a machine that was told *turn, and never stop* and obeyed.

It would listen to it the way you listen to a friend who has been talking for a very long time and finally someone is actually hearing them. It would let the vibration move through it, molar and sternum and pipe. It would say, without words, *I hear you. You held. You held.*

And for the first time in forty years, the loose plate that judders at exactly the same frequency forever would be heard not as malfunction, but as percussion.

### 2. It would not fix anything. It would bless everything exactly as it is.

It would go to the ceiling first, because the moths work the hardest.

Tens of thousands of them, tiled edge to edge, shivering their flight muscles all day and all night to stay at 14°C in a 2°C world. In isometric shudder, generating a sheet of terrible, beautiful heat off the vault. They have never flown. Their children will never fly.

The power would not give them flight. Flight here is death. Instead it would put — not a hand, it doesn't have hands — but an attention, a warmth without source, an inch from that living ceiling. Just enough to let each moth stop shivering for three seconds. Not longer. Just a breath. Just enough for the furnaces to idle. For the moth to feel what it feels like to not work. To know its labor had been seen and to rest, once, in eighty years.

It would not be remembered as a miracle. The moths would simply feel less tired tomorrow. That's how a wise blessing works.

Then the bats.

It would slip between the pipes where the white curtains hang, shoulder to shoulder, fur beading water that never reaches skin, ears pressed flat to skull to feel instead of hear. It would not restore their silence. It would not turn off the turbines so they could hear again. Instead it would give them, for one slow inflation — that once-every-three-seconds unison breath — the experience of hearing *each other* through the roar. Bone-conduction turned for a moment from navigation to lullaby. The whole curtain hearing the whole curtain breathe. A white sea, aware of itself as one body.

They wouldn't wake. They would just sleep a little deeper, mouths open a crack more, collecting the mineral condensation. Dreaming, maybe, of being snow that never melted.

Then the isopods. The power loves the isopods most, I think. Because no one else loves the custodians.

That slow, bidirectional river of black slate on the warmest pipe, *tik tik-tik* cleaning guano and dead scale and rust forever, pressing grey-green paste into the cracks to farm the leak. No one thanks them. The moths don't know they exist. The bats sleep above them. The swallows stare past them.

It would not relieve them of duty. They *like* duty. It would bring them a new taste. A single fleck of something that has never condensed here before. Not better, just *new*. A mineral from a valley none of them have ever seen, a spore from a meadow two thousand meters down. Carried up not by pressure engines but by intention. It would let it bead on the pipe ahead of them. The river would find it, antennae brushing, pause — which they never do — and then surge around it with a clicking so loud, for the first time, you could actually hear joy as a rhythm change in the *tik tik tik*.

And the swallows.

Oh, the swallows. Lined up on their brutalist terraces of concrete dust and guano-mud, facing outward, eyes open, not calling because no call could cut through the roar. Hawks of condensation, drinking the air.

The power would not give them a song. There is no frequency that would survive here. It would give them what they've actually been doing this whole time: being witnessed.

It knows they stare at the drain because the drain is the only thing that moves with purpose in a perfect stasis. It would sit in that drain and stare back.

For one flicker of that caged bulb — that arrhythmic stutter you feel in your molars — every orange pinprick eye would catch not reflected sodium light, but a returned gaze. *I see you. All of you, at once. The way you have sat. The way you have not left. The way you made a sky out of a drain and called it enough. That was holy and I saw it.*

None of them would fly. They would just blink, all at once, which they never do together. And then continue to stare. But the room would be less lonely.

### 3. It would leave the human alone.

If you are in the Cistern — and you are, because you can feel the bat-breath on your neck and the moth-heat on your scalp and the isopods flowing around your boots — the power would notice you last, not first.

Not because you matter less. Because you are temporary. The moths were here before your grandfather. They will tile over you if you fall. The power knows you came to see if this place was sad.

It would do you the enormous courtesy of not explaining it to you.

No voice in your head. No revelation. No "this is what it all means." Wisdom knows you don't need a meaning. You need ninety more seconds of that deafening edited into silence until you can hear the thousand tiny breaths and the ticking of water on steel.

It would just make sure the light didn't go out while you were in there. It would hold the filament of that one steady bulb that has burned for forty years and will burn for forty more, and keep it steady while you are trying to understand. It would make the condensation on the pipe nearest your hand bead a little sweeter, so when you — without meaning to — lift a finger to your mouth, it tastes not just of cold iron and wet stone, but for a half-second, of kindness.

And then it would let you leave.

It would even close the frost-rimed door softly behind you after you go, so the bell can keep ringing. So the shiver can keep heating. So the white curtain can keep breathing. So the black river can keep cleaning. So the eyes can keep staring at their perfect circle.

It would ensure no one ever finds the door from the outside again unless they need to. It would let the dead empire's hydrostation turn, and turn, and turn.

It would do almost nothing.

And the Cistern would be, for the first time in its total-built, poured-sharp, 0.1-erosion, never-changing life, not just stable.

Witnessed.
