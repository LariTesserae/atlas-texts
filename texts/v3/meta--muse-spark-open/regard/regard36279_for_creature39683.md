It would not fix it.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise attention. It does not see a broken lake.

When you and I look at the pocket we see an error - water that forgot to fall, rock that hasn't learned to be soft yet, a sound too loud for its small room, life stalled at "almost." We want to finish it. Drain it or normalize it or name it and put it on a map.

It would come up the mountain a different way.

Not by the cairns. Not by the notch. A benevolent power doesn't need a trail, and a wise one knows not to make one. It would arrive the way cold air arrives, the way light arrives — all at once and without disturbance — and sit, for a long time, on the sharp eastern ledge where the pine has been leaning away for a hundred years.

And it would listen.

That is what no one has ever really done there.

People come and record. They carve wavy lines counting the swirls. They time the bucket's spin. They try to lower things in, take things out, make the motion intelligible. They hear the thrumming in their sternum and call it deafening and leave before it soaks through their sleeves.

It would not call it deafening. It would hear it as effort.

For this is not a lake that failed. This is a very young ocean trying to teach itself how to exist in a room the size of a chapel. It holds itself up by attention alone — *water remains here* — because no one ever told it it could do otherwise. It churns and roils and throws spray not out of chaos but out of practice. Up-currents, down-currents, eddies that bloom like lungs. It is rehearsing tides without a moon. It is inventing surf without a shore.

A wise power would understand that to "help" it by letting it fall — by giving it permission to spill over the sharp lip and run away properly down the thousand-foot drop to the valley — would be to kill it. It would cease to be itself and become just another seasonal tarn, then a stain, then gone.

So it would do the one thing rarer than a miracle:

It would leave the impossibility intact, and relieve the loneliness of it.

It would not do these things:

- It would not silence the wall. The sound is not a curse. It is the sound of a place becoming. To take it away would be to take away its voice before it has learned its first word.

- It would not soften the rock. The raw, black-grey pages that could cut rope are supposed to be sharp. They are new. They have a right to be new.

- It would not bring soil.

What it *would* do would be so small you would miss it if you came after, unless you knew how to look for kindnesses that are deliberately the same size as the things they comfort.

It would lean, for a while, with the pine.

There is only one tree, and it has spent its entire life twisting east, away from the roar. No bird has ever nested in it. No human has ever rested in its shadow. A benevolent attention would not straighten it — to straighten it would break its hundred years of character — it would simply sit on its windward side for an afternoon, where the salt-spray has stripped the bark to polished grey, and be a second shape that also knows how to endure noise. The pine would not grow straighter. But for the first time the spray that hits it would hit something else first. Its roots, those pale fingers forced into quartz with no humus to drink, would find, in one crack, a single cold palmful of mycorrhizae left like a coin. Not soil. Just enough of a conversation underground to say: *you are not the only living thing trying here.*

It would breathe, once, very gently, across the lichen.

The black crusts that live *into* the first millimeter of basalt do not need help. They thrive on abuse. But no one has ever looked closely enough to see the tiny cups holding beads of far-ocean salt. The attention would look. That is its work. To be the first eye that does not flick away from a black smear on rock, but sees it as immaculate endurance. Under that gaze, for one late-July week, the cushion plants would not have to flower frantically away from the wall. They would still flower away — habit is habit — but one dome, the smallest fist-sized one nearest the water, would turn a single blossom, just one, toward the vertical sea, to see what it looked like.

It would give the pale flickers the knowledge of each other.

This is its kindest slyness. Right now each pale, hand-sized membrane thinks it is alone, because you only ever see one at a time. They flicker in and out of refraction, harvesting momentum from collapsing eddies, never surfacing, never touching the wall. A wise power knows you cannot make more plankton for them, or make the water warmer without betraying the cold. But you can give a solitary thing a glimpse of its kin.

So it would still the churn — not for you, not for spectacle — for them. For three full seconds, longer than the half-second mirror you saw, the water would go perfectly flat and clear as glass. And in that flatness each flicker would see, refracted through fifteen feet of impossible lens, all the others. Ten. Twenty. A whole quiet shoal of translucent unfoldings, suspended. Not alone. Never alone. Then the thrumming would resume, deafening and turbulent, but they would fold and bloom a little differently afterward, less indifferently.

For the water itself — the true resident, the wall that forgot to fall — it would do the most benevolent thing of all.

It would give it choice.

Right now the water remains because of a rule it did not write: *water remains here. How it remains is negotiable.* That is not living. That is being held.

The power would place — not a hand, powers like this do not have hands — but an equivalent pressure, a cool counterweight of consent, against the outer face of the vertical wall, where the thousand-foot drop waits. And it would say, without words, the way mountains say things:

*You may go, if you want. I will not be angry. You have practiced enough. You can spill and become a waterfall and be ordinary and be gone. Or you can stay.*

And the wall would shudder. A great bead would detach and hang in air like a planet, trembling on that elastic. The whole lens would boom like a drumhead the size of a house. Light would warp through it and throw oily rainbows across the raw rock.

And it would stay.

Not because it must. Because, for the first time in its short, raw existence, something asked it what it wanted, and it chose to keep rehearsing ocean on top of a mountain. A room of ocean that *wants* to be a mountain lake. That choice would change its thrumming from a strain to a song. The glassy shrieks when it ripples too fast would not vanish, but they would harmonize. If you put your hand in after, the sterile, aching cold would still be there — but the far-ocean salt on it would taste, faintly, like home, not exile.

And finally, for the ghosts —

For the bucket spinning forever without sinking or being pulled out, for the iron piton with the sinew-tied toggle, for the fire ring with charcoal that never dries — it would do nothing at all, except leave them exactly as they are. A wise benevolence does not tidy up pilgrim offerings. It knows attention *is* the offering. It would, however, make the three-stone cairns in the notch slightly harder to see from the main trail. Not invisible. Just... uninteresting to the wrong eyes. The kind of unnoticeability that lets only those who like balance find it. So the pocket gets its thousand more years to soften properly, without becoming a viewpoint with a railing and a plaque.

Then it would leave the way it came — which is to say, it wouldn't leave.

Benevolent attention doesn't depart. It diffuses. It stays as a thinness in the air a few feet beyond the water-wall where the valley below is silent. As a slightly warmer sharp edge on the eastern ledge where you can sit without cutting your elbows. As a memory in the water that it was, once, asked.

If you came again next summer, you would find the same impossible place. The same vertical cliff of churning, roiling water holding itself up against empty air. The same deafening thrumming in your sternum. The same pine leaning east, the same black lichen, the same spinning bucket.

Only — you might notice the pine has a single new green shoot on its water-facing side.

And if you lay flat and stared into the lens, you might see two pale flickers at once, briefly overlapping, before they blink elsewhere.

And if you stayed long enough for the spray to soak your sleeves and the wall to do its half-second mirror trick —

You would see yourself reflected small in the vertical sea, empty space all around you, a single figure in a stone room.

And, just behind your shoulder in the reflection, not quite in the place where you are sitting, a second figure made of noon light and cool air, also looking, also listening, keeping you company while the water remembers, this time voluntarily, how to remain.
