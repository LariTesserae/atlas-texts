It would not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise attention. Not all loves descend. Not all gods land.

It would arrive without arriving — the way cold air finds the basin anyway, though there is no path for wind. It would notice, from a very great height and a very great slowness, that in one shallow bowl of a quiet world, something had solved a problem the rest of the world had not even learned to ask properly:

*How do you live without taking?*

And it would stop to look. For a long time. Longer than you have been lying in the liverwort.

For what you think of as *years,* it would simply do that. There is a kind of attention so complete it feels like doing nothing, and this is the work of wise powers. It would not scan or measure. It would not count the crickets or name the beetles again. It would listen until the plane of sound stopped being pressure and became language.

It would learn the rules you wrote down and understand them more deeply than words allow:

That water is not allowed to pool, because pooling is hoarding, and hoarding is a kind of death here. That salt is not eaten to destroy it, but to borrow its shape. That sound is not decoration but scaffolding — the only way to hold up a roof made of air.

It would understand that this place is not lush *despite* being dry. It is lush *because* it is dry. The dryness forced patience. Patience made room for this.

And because it is wise, it would feel the first, reflexive temptation of every benevolent thing and set it gently aside.

It would not bring rain.

Even one real rain — generous, silver, hammering on the white polygons — would murder this place with kindness. The polygons would dissolve. The Lung Pillars would drown inside their own perfect thirst. The lacquered crickets, who have spent three hundred generations learning to sit exactly centered and saw, would float and sputter. The ants would try to build with mud and find that mud does not remember shape the way salt does. Green, *real* green, would rush in from the world above, fast and weedy and loud in the wrong way, and the blue dusk would be over.

So it would not bring rain. It would not bring rivers. It would not bring shade or sun. It would not “fix” anything, because it would see, quickly, that nothing is broken. The absence of sharp edges is not decay. It is completion.

It would not lift the architects up and give them eyes, or hands, or language, or knowledge of stars. To give eyes to a thing that has navigated for a million years by vibration and smell would be to blind it. To give it history would be to give it impatience, and impatience would be the first ruin this city has ever known.

It would not speak to you, either — the warm, wet, too-fast visitor lying in the liverwort hollow with your boots grinding halite and your thoughts still ticking. It would not need to. It would feel your own small, sincere awe and know it for what it was: a brief, correct tuning. You were finally vibrating at the right frequency. It would let you have that without comment.

What would it do, then?

Three very small things. So small you would only notice them afterwards, when you left — if you ever left — and realized the basin had felt a little less alone than it should have.

**First, it would protect the rim.**

Not with a wall, not with fire. A wise power does not guard a quiet place by making it conspicuous. It would make it *uninteresting.*

Satellites would pass over the long, uneven oval of sky and see only more high desert, heat-haze and salt glare, a data error worth skipping. Prospectors wandering the upper world would feel, without knowing why, that the ground ahead was a little more tedious than the ground behind, and turn aside a mile early. Maps would misfold here. Stories about the “white bowl” would become slightly boring in the telling.

It would not hide the basin with a miracle. It would hide it by extending the very quality that already protects it: the honest, absolute lack of promise. *There is nothing for you there.* Which, for anyone in a hurry, is true.

Your footprints would still be there for a day, until the hissing salt crystals smoothed them. No more.

**Second, it would sit down.**

Powers, even benevolent ones, are often bad at sitting. They want to *act.* This one knows how to sit on the exhausted dunes of stone at the basin’s edge, where the strata have blurred, and let its long attention spill over the side like cool air.

It would not sit *in* the city. To sit in the city would be to crush a tower, to silence a hundred crickets with a shadow. It would sit on the rim, where its weight would not register, and simply be present for a while — a century, perhaps — so that the Choir had something else to lean toward.

And very, very quietly — below hearing, below the cicadas’ resonant frequency — it would hum with them.

Not louder. Never louder. It would find the exact tone that the salt flat itself likes to hold, the one the Veil Cicadas spend their whole lives keeping alive, and it would add a harmonic so faint that no instrument could have measured it. A second voice under the first, like a hand under a back.

The Pillars would barely notice, except that the condensation inside would bead a fraction faster that decade. The moths would stay aloft a fraction easier. An ant carrying its warm pellet would hesitate — not in confusion, but in the way you hesitate when someone you love enters a room without speaking — and place its grain a hair more truly.

The basin, which has never been alone but has never been *heard,* would be heard without being interrupted. That is an extremely rare gift.

If you were very still in your liverwort bed at night, you might feel it as an inexplicable comfort, as if the ground-hum under your back had deepened and become kinder, and you might sleep without dreaming of thirst.

**Third, it would leave a promise of time.**

Wise powers know that the most dangerous moment for a place like this is not now. Now is perfect. The danger is later, when the world above finally finishes wearing itself away and its weather changes, or when the sun, in its own slow aging, begins to look more directly down into the oval.

So before it un-sits itself — because it will not stay forever; staying forever is also a kind of taking — it would do one last, almost invisible mending.

It would not lengthen lives. It would not make the towers taller. It would simply ensure *duration.* That the cold, high dusk would continue to be cool. That the trace humidity would still be coaxed from nothing for another ten million years. That no sudden crack would open the rim and drain the basin’s careful air like a bowl tipped.

It would not make the basin eternal. Nothing that is benevolent would insult a thing that has achieved its final shape by telling it never to change. It would simply give it the time it has already earned, uncut short.

You could call this a blessing, if you need a word for it. It is not spoken. It is not even thought in language. It is more like the way you might, passing a sleeping animal you love, adjust your steps so you do not wake it, and feel, for a second, a fierce and quiet wish: *continue.*

Then it would go. Not up. Not away. Powers like that do not travel. It would simply thin until its attention was elsewhere — to another bowl, another attempt, another patient city learning to drink air somewhere else in the cold universe.

And the basin would go on as before.

The crickets would saw, one to a polygon. The Rollers would roll their humming spheres along inch-deep roads. The Lung Pillars would breathe. The pony-sized wall-wrights would pat, pat, their pellets onto a dome that will be finished in three hundred years, guided by smell and vibration, never knowing — and never needing to know — that something vast once sat on the rim and hummed with them for a little while, and liked their song, and left it exactly as it was.

That is what benevolence does when it is also wise:

It learns to love the dryness without wishing it wet.
It learns to hear the single shared note without needing a solo.
It keeps the world from interrupting, adds one quiet harmony you can only feel in the soles of your boots, and lets the slow, soft, salt-made city continue, grain by patient grain, waiting in its bowl while the world above wears away to nothing — unwatched, except once, and perfectly.
