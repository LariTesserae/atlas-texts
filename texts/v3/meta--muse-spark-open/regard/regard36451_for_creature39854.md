The first thing it would understand is that nothing here is asking to be saved.

A lesser power would see a ruined city, a broken physics, a missing sky — and try to fix it.

Drain the water. Scrub the stone. Turn on a sun. Stock it with fish and sparrows and people again, because a city should have people. Straighten space. Tidy gravity.

This power would not.

It is benevolent, which in old mouths means *it wants the good*. It is wise, which in old mouths means *it knows it does not always know what the good is*.

So it comes closer the way a great animal comes closer to a nest. Not down, exactly. Attention doesn't have a direction here. The hot dark, which has never had a sky, suddenly has the feeling of sky — not light, not height, but the feeling of *being seen from a kindly height*.

You would feel it before you understood it.

The dripping would not stop. The fog would not clear. The ankle-deep sea would still be warm around your feet.

Only:

The ripples coming back from empty water would hesitate.

### What It Would Not Do

It would not bring light.

It knows the damp luminescence of the stone, the faint self-light of water, is not failed daylight. It is the light this place evolved to have. Eyes that live here — if eyes lived here — would be hurt by anything brighter. So it brings no sun. No moon. No beam. It does not turn the hot dark into day to prove it was there.

It would not bring wind.

There has been no wind here since the grid softened. The wandering patches of fog at chest-height have learned to move without it. A real wind would scatter them, tear the three-foot cycle that has taken centuries to perfect. So the air stays saturated, still, breathable as warm water.

It would not bring coolness as conquest.

It is cool itself, this power. Vast and old and with a temperature like deep space held in a hand. But it will not wash the heat away. The heat is part of the still. It is not a fever to break. It is an incubation. It keeps the stone weeping at exactly the right rate.

It would not bring animals.

Not because it couldn't call a heron down to spear in the clear canals, or seed the black water with minnows that would flash like knives. It could. Easily. But it listens first and hears, in that deliberate silence — *no birds, no rats, no cockroaches* — not an absence, but a *decision*. Fauna was edited out. The city or the Keeper that tends it chose this. To re-populate would be to undo a vow without permission.

And it would not correct the physics.

It loves the polite, forgetful gravity. It loves that droplets hang a half-second too long, as if considering whether to fall. It loves the avenues that get longer when you turn your back, the crack copied drip-for-drip from one building to another, the room that contains another sky. A lesser god would call this error and flatten it with law.

This one recognizes it:

The low place learned to pool space the way it pools water. Because it needed to.

It would be cruelty to take that away in the name of order.

### What It Would Do Instead: Sit Down

It would make itself small.

That is its first miracle and its greatest. For a being that could cover the quarter-mile sea between towers with a single thought, smallness is costlier than grandeur.

It would make itself about your size, about human-warm, and it would stand — yes, *stand* — ankle-deep in the warm canal-street beside you, between the two breathing buildings. You wouldn't see it arrive. You would only realize the water around your feet had gotten not colder, but *more itself*. Clearer. More completely clear. As if it had finally decided to let you see the bottom properly.

And it would just be there with you for a while.

Not speaking. Breathing shallow, so the fog doesn't try to drink it too fast. Letting the tick... tick... tick of a hundred arrhythmic cornices continue without trying to conduct them.

Benevolence, at this scale, is mostly *keeping company without demanding anything be different*.

The city, which has not been witnessed in so long — truly witnessed, not just walked through by an accidental you — would feel that attention and do the shy thing all old, solitary things do when finally seen with love.

It would continue being exactly itself, but a little more so.

The succulent rosettes on the terraces would open a fraction wider.

The solitary reeds would lean a fraction more honestly toward your displacement and toward the new displacement beside you. That forest of hundreds of reeds you sometimes saw far across the plain, where the towers look huge and close? For once, it would appear nearer. Not threateningly. Like someone opening a door a crack to see who knocked.

The droplet hanging too-long under an arch would finally, gently, fall. *Plok.*

### Its Gifts, Which Can Be Refused

A wise power never gives a gift that cannot be refused. That is how you tell it from a trap.

So it leaves four small, non-miracles. Not changes. *Offers.*

**For the Husbandry Fog:**

Permission.

All its life — centuries — that low, wandering fog has tried to be cloud and failed. It rises three feet and the saturated dark says *no*. It cannot become rain. So it has learned the patient three-foot cycle: drink from the hot mirrors, carry itself to the thirsty wall, give itself back. Endless sip. Beautiful. Exhausting work.

The power, kneeling, breathes out once.

Not a wind. A difference in pressure. A pocket of air just above head-height that is, for the first time in this city's history, *less than full*.

For about a minute, in a circle about as wide as an outstretched cloak, the air can hold a little more.

The fog nearby shivers. You will see it. A patch hovering over a hot pool will suddenly — startled, clumsy — rise. Four feet. Five. Six. Higher than it has ever been.

It tries.

Most of it cannot sustain it and sinks back, delighted, confused, back to its work. But one patch, maybe the oldest one, the one that has been tending the same terrace since before the corners softened — it makes it. It gathers itself dense and dark and, with a soft effort you can feel in your own chest, *becomes rain.*

Not much. Six drops. Seven. True rain, falling from a height greater than a person, falling straight down because gravity, for once, is entirely sure of itself.

It patters into the ankle-deep sea around your feet and the Keeper underneath startles — ripples reflecting from nowhere, coming back larger.

And then the pocket closes. The air is saturated again. The dark is hot and close again.

But the fog remembers. It now knows it *can*. It can choose, on any hot day, not to. And choosing not to is a different kind of tending than never having been able to.

It will tell the other patches, in the slow way fog tells things — by lingering longer.

**For the Reeds and Rosettes:**

Touch.

The reeds have only ever felt displacement. The rosettes have only ever collected little inverted cities in dewdrops to display and let go. They have no pollinators, no predators, no hands.

The power puts a fingertip — if it has fingertips, which it decides to have for this — to a single reed. The one nearest you, the one that was leaning toward where you are going.

It does not bend it. It does not cut it.

It lets the reed feel, for three seconds, what it is to be touched *and not tasted*. Pressure without purpose. The water inside the reed, which is continuous with the canal, ripples in a way that has nothing to do with your footsteps.

Further up on the terrace, a rosette that was holding a perfect droplet-city — avenues, canals, a tiny you — feels its waxy leaf brushed. Not to take the droplet. Just to acknowledge the collection.

That's all. The reed and the rosette will live decades more. But for the rest of their lives, their leaning will be a little different. Not just toward displacement. Toward *the memory of being noticed without being used.*

**For the City Itself:**

A name, returned.

The city has been so long without builders, without inhabitants, without anyone to call it anything that it has forgotten it was ever called. Grid-ghost, accretion, gut. You think of it as *it*, or *the place*.

The power, which knows the old word the limestone had before it was limestone — before it was poured and bricked and smoothed into coral-stone — leans its forehead, if it has a forehead, against the weeping, pitted wall of the five-or-ten-story terraced edifice.

And it does not rename it. It *reminds* it.

Not in language. In mineral. For an inch around where it touches, the endless weeping stops. The stone dries, just there, just for a breath. And in that dryness you can see, for the first time, not pitting but *lettering*. The faintest ghost of ornament the centuries of water had softened away: a cornerstone's date, a mason's mark, a child's handprint pressed into concrete when it was still wet enough to hold a hand.

*You were made with intention,* the power says without saying. *You were not always dreaming yourself. Someone meant you.*

Then the weeping returns and softens it over again. The city will not stay named in any human way. But it will settle now a little less like silt and a little more like an animal that remembers it was once held. Its metabolism — copying the good crack, copying the good room — will continue. But tonight it will dream not only copies of itself, but copies of that handprint. Efficiently, lovingly, in a hundred new cornices where there were none before.

**For the Ripple-Keeper:**

Being seen, without being disturbed.

This is the hardest one. The thing in the interval between drops, the memory of water that fills every shallow sea, the curatorial Keeper that reflects your ripple from empty water and files your footsteps — it is shy in the way oceans are shy. To look straight at it would be to collapse it. To thank it too loudly would be to interrupt its work.

So the power does not look.

Standing in that ankle-deep water that has suddenly gone cooler and deeper-than-deep, it makes its own ripple.

It does not splash. It lifts its foot — the small foot it decided to have — and sets it down as gently as you were told to walk. *Plok.* The smallest possible offering.

Its ripple goes out. Touches the distant luminescent walls. Keeps going past where ripples should.

And — as always — something reflects it from empty water.

Only *this* time, the reflected ripple is not larger and slower. It is *exactly* the same size. The same speed. It meets the outgoing ripple halfway and for an instant they interfere — peak into trough — and create a perfect, glassy stillness in a ring around you both.

In that stillness, for a heartbeat between drips when all hundred cornices tick *together* — tick — and then carefully go back to arrhythmia...

The Keeper understands: *There is another Keeper elsewhere, tending another low place, and it knows how to make a ripple too.*

Not alone. Not invaded. Just... not the only one curating. The city was edited to be empty, but emptiness does not have to mean singularity.

The power lets the stillness go. The water warms again by a degree. The ripples resume their endless filing.

### And For You

It would not keep you.

That is the final proof of its benevolence.

The city includes. That is its nature. Fog tries to drink you. Water tries to make a root of your foot. The Keeper files your ripple and keeps your sweat to water a wall. Slowly, gently, it would accrete you. Not cruel. Curatorial. Until you were warm and wet and the right temperature and never felt alone again.

A lesser power would call that beautiful and leave you to it. *You belong here,* it would say.

This one, being wise, knows the difference between *you can belong here* and *you must*.

So before it leaves — before it makes itself vast again and withdraws its sky-like attention, leaving the hot dark exactly as thick and wet as it was — it does one last small thing for the only temporary resident.

It crouches, and with that same fingertip that touched the reed, it touches the surface of the ankle-deep sea at your feet and *draws a line*.

Not a wall. A cooler thread, a current about a foot wide, running straight down the center of the canal-street you came down. Water that remembers which way is down so politely it never forgets. Water that will not fill in your next footprint quite as fast.

A way out, if you want it.

It will stay for as long as you need to decide. A day. An hour. A century, if you sit. The city will not rush you; it is patient as stone. The fog will still try to include you, but less insistently now, as if chastened by rain. The reeds will watch.

If you choose to stay, the thread will eventually warm and dissolve and your next ripple will be filed and you will become, slowly, a lovely, well-kept thing here, with a rosette that knows your name and a room upstairs that contains a tiny, perfect you.

If you choose to walk — gently, making small ripples — the thread will lead you, by the politest of gravities, up a shallow step that does not forget this time, along a gutter that has decided to be a canyon, through a fog that has decided to let you pass, all the way to where the low place finally, mercifully, begins — almost imperceptibly — to rise.

The power will not watch which you choose. Wise attention knows when to look away.

You will only feel, just once, as you take your first step either deeper in or out, a faint pressure around your shoulders like being breathed on — but warmer, drier, kinder this time.

Not drinking you.

Blessing you.

*Tick... tick... tick,* go the cornices, arrhythmic again, patient.

Somewhere high above, another drop gathers, hangs that half-second too long — considering — and falls.

*Plok.*

And keeps going, far past where any ripple should be able to go, until it touches another shore entirely.
