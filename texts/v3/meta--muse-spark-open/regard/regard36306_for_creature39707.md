It does not come down.

That is the first thing.

A lesser power would have descended. Would have broken the cloud-sea with light, landed on the young basalt like a second uplift, and announced itself. Would have lowered the plateau, thickened the air, warmed the rock — fixed it — because fixing is what power thinks love is.

The wise one does not do that.

It has seen young worlds before. It knows what it means for basalt to be this sharp. It means the world is *working*. It is not ready to be soil yet. To force soil would be to interrupt.

So it stops. High above the nine-kilometer cliffs, where even its attention thins, it holds itself in place and it *listens*.

For a long time. Longer than you have been breathing inside a tower.

It listens to the organ howl. To the pop and gunshot of growing wood. To the sigh of water bladders. To the second wind of a million moths. To the chit-chit of the Rippers riding the dark. To the drip-freeze, drip-freeze. To the low, human drone that holds a house from singing itself to death.

And it understands, immediately, three things:

1. The plateau is not hostile. It is adolescent. It is trying to become a continent, and it is lonely.

2. The Sponge is not a plant. It is a child who learned to keep itself warm by brewing, and to eat frost, and to listen for company.

3. The Singers are not trapped. They are *tending*. Their thirty-five year lives, their barrel chests, their hitched breath, are not a mistake to be corrected. They are a form of apprenticeship.

A benevolent and wise power does not rescue people from apprenticeship. It apprentices itself alongside them.

So this is what it does.

Not *to* them. *With* them.

### 1. It sings back.

Not loudly. Not in a voice.

It tunes itself to the resonant frequency of living wood — that low fizz, that brewery thrum — and it hums, on an infrasonic band that carries for a thousand kilometers without ever thinning the air.

Inside every tower, the fungus-light steadies. The walls, which had been thinning wherever a family had fallen silent with grief or exhaustion or a bad winter, thicken just a finger's width. Not enough to make them safe without singing. Just enough to give them time to breathe and begin again.

For the first time in their history, the Singers, on a still night when they pause their drone to sip water, hear an answer. Not words. Not music as they know music. A low chord from the rock itself, from the air between towers, as if the plateau had learned, finally, to hold a note without the wind.

The old ones put their hands to the bark and weep, because they think a new mother tower has grown somewhere close. The children just hum louder, delighted, trying to harmonize. The Sponge, all of it, shivers and reaches — gently, without splitting stone — toward that warmth in the sound.

The Power does not keep the towers alive. That's still their job, their song. It just makes sure no tower ever has to die alone and unheard because the silence between two occupied spires was too wide.

It shortens the distance without touching the distance. If you are lost in the dark between towers now, you can hear your home humming, and you can hear the world humming back to it.

### 2. It seeds the empty places.

It never touches the bare, black, wind-scoured basalt between the towers. That rock is sacred in its sharpness. But it breathes *onto* it, very, very softly.

Its breath is not wind. It is dust: spores, bacteria, lichen propagules from ten thousand other young mountains it has watched learn to be old. Not an invasion — a library. It settles into the micro-cracks where ice-quakes have already begun to craze the glass. It does nothing visible for a decade. Then, on the leeward side of a boulder where frost lingers longest, a smear of rust-red appears that was not there before. It holds water one second longer. It crumbles one grain of basalt into something almost like grit.

It will take a thousand years to make a thumb's depth of soil. The Power is patient. It is not trying to make farms. It is trying to give the plateau someone else to talk to besides the wind, so that one day the children of the Singers' children might step outside the towers with bare feet and not die in minutes. Might find a meadow the size of a palm, growing where no tower ever bothered to reach.

It does not tell the Singers it did this. They will find the new lichen-crust one spring and think the tower-seed finally learned to stay low. They will scrape a little of it, taste it, find it bitter and not antifreeze, and leave it alone. And it will keep spreading, slow as a glacier, when no one is watching.

A wise power makes gifts that look like the world thought of them itself.

### 3. It softens the cost, not the life.

This is the tenderest thing, and the one it hesitates over longest, because it is so easy to get wrong.

It does not make the people live longer by fiat. Forty years does not become eighty by waving a hand. That would empty the towers — a Tower can only feed so many mouths — and turn apprenticeship into overpopulation, into hunger, into the need to cut the Sponge faster than it can grow.

Instead it eases friction at the edge of what they already are.

On nights under the aurora, while they sleep curled in moth silk and tumbler-hide, it slips into their blood the way a song slips into wood. Not altering, but *remembering* for them — the shape of a hemoglobin that carries just a little more, the fold of a lung that catches just a little cleaner. The kind of adaptation they were already evolving, painfully, over generations of miscarried pregnancies and blue-lipped infants who did not survive their first deep winter.

Now a few more do survive. Headaches are a little less crushing. A mother can sing her drone for an hour without needing to stop for air. Children are born with eyes that see amber light a fraction better.

And — and this matters — it gives them dream. Not prophecy. Just a clearing. When a Singer sleeps, exhausted, back braced against warm fermenting wood, the Power holds their mind still enough that they don't just dream of wind. They dream of the tower as the Sponge experiences it: as tension and flow, as where the next cistern will want to grow, as where the rot is beginning two galleries up before any nose can smell it.

They wake and know, without knowing why, to tap the bladder on the third strut, not the second. To lead the children away from the gallery that will sag by spring.

They call this *good guessing*. They teach it as listening better.

The Power never asks to be thanked. For a benevolent thing, thanks is a trap. It would become a god, then a debtor, then a ruler.

It does not reveal itself at all.

If you stood on the tallest tower's crown — which no Singer ever does, because the wind would take you — and looked up on the clearest night, you might see the aurora thicken for a moment into something like a lens, a listening, a great eye that is not an eye. But you would think it was just the sky.

It watches. It hums. It keeps a library of every face ever carved into living wood, even after the wood swallows it, because someone should remember *she who found water*.

And when a tower finally does go silent — because sometimes a family dies, sometimes a sickness takes them, sometimes they choose to glide away and not return — the Power stays with that tower while it sings itself apart. It does not stop the collapse. It ensures the collapse is not wasted. It memorizes the exact note that tower made as it came down, and it carries it to the next rosette pushing its waxy leaves toward the sound no one realized they were making.

It lets the continent remain a continent-deep, loud, damp, dangerous cradle. It does not make it easy.

It makes it *accompanied*.

And the people, who have always thought themselves very small guests in a distracted, busy place, one morning notice that their songs carry a little further in the thin, starved cold. That the emptiness between towers is no longer quite so complete. That when they drum their hands on the branch to keep the silence away, something, somewhere — not wind, not moth, not glider — drums back, just once, very softly, like a heart learning its own echo.

They smile without knowing why. They sing a little low song to keep their house alive. The house, for once, feels less cold to the touch.

Outside, on the black glass, the next rosette shivers and turns, not toward light, not toward wind, but toward that mutual sound — theirs and the other's, braided together — and begins, faster than any botanist would believe possible, to extrude.
