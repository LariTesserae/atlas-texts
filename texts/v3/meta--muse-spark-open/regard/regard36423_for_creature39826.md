It does not come down.

That is the first wisdom. Anything that came down to a place this high and this old would, by definition, be falling - and this place has spent a million years learning how not to fall, but to settle.

A benevolent and wise power does not fall. It notices. And to be noticed by such a thing is already an intervention.

It had been watching for a while. Not the planet. Not the range that is no longer a range. It was watching the negotiation. Most places it looks at are screaming one thing or wanting to be another thing. Deserts want to be oceans. Cities want to be forests or want forests to be cities. People want to be taller, louder, somewhere else.

This place wanted, very exactly, to stay.

Low, damp, dim, dense, moss-eaten, knee-grinding, bucket-carrying, spore-breathing, roof-scraping, *staying* - and good at it. A whole human culture that had learned the trick moss learned: to live on air and condensate. To ask not for more, but for enough, moved well.

A wise power loves that like a clear note.

So it comes closer. Not as light. The sky here is already a low vault of wet paper and any new light would be a violence. Not as fire. Not as a voice; sound dies in moss and thin air and these people have learned to touch forearms to be heard. A voice from above would be the stupidest thing imaginable.

It comes as attention. As weather, almost.

If you were standing on a whaleback ridge on a day when the cloud had snagged and was wringing itself out in that patient, hour-long drizzle, you might have felt it as a slight change in pressure before the rain. As if the air, already thin, became for a moment *more* attentive. As if the plateau itself inhaled a little deeper.

Then it looks, really looks, and does the triage any healer does.

### What It Does Not Do

It does not lift the cloud.

Oh, the temptation. It would be so easy. One hand and the overcast peels back like a bandage and the sun - a real sun, with shadows - hits the basalt for the first time in an epoch. The people could be warm. They could be tall with vitamin and heat. They could dry out.

And in a week, the mattress-moss would crisp and die. The condensate traps would stand empty. The cisterns would crack. The feral lichens that hold the walls together would bleach and flake off like old paint. The tubers would roast in their roof-beds. The goats, those pale broad-chested goats, would pant and abort. The beautiful, thrumming, trickle-fed machine that is a city run on cloud-breath would seize.

They would be warm, and then they would be dead, or else they would become some other people entirely, refugees from their own salvation.

A wise power does not save a thing by ending the agreement that lets it live.

It does not make them tall and loud. It does not give them great rivers. It does not cure old age. It does not reveal itself in a way that would require them to stop pruning and kneel. These people kneel all day already - to scrape moss, to clear a culvert, to set a trap, to plant over the dead. They do not need another reason to kneel.

It does not give them abundance. Nothing is new and nothing is wasted is not a poverty here. It is a sacrament. To flood them with newness would be to profane them.

So after looking, it does almost nothing. Which is the hardest thing for a power to do.

Almost.

### What It Does

It does four small things, so small they will argue for generations about whether anything happened at all. Which is exactly how a wise benevolence prefers it.

**1. It gives them a slightly deeper breath.**

Not more air. The same thin, cool, early-spring air. But on the windward scoops, where the turf grows thickest, a new cultivar appears in the felt. Not imposed; remembered — as if one of the three mosses that pretended to be one suddenly remembered a fourth cousin.

It is paler, almost silver-green, with fronds like tiny lungs. It holds no more water than the others. It does not grow faster. But when the cloud dips and the gutters exhale that fine spore-rich mist, this moss sighs out a little extra oxygen as it ferments. Just a fraction of a percent. Not enough to make them tall. Enough that an old woman climbing the hundred stairs from cistern to loft with two buckets does not have to stop twice. She stops once.

Enough that a baby born in winter, blue-lipped as all winter babies are here, pinks up a few seconds faster.

No one plants it on purpose. They find it has planted itself along the paths children take to school. They let it be. The children, who know the taste of every moss, say this one tastes like cold air and pennies. They like it.

**2. It makes a kindness with the green.**

It does not kill the fern. It does not stop the spores. The amber goggles stay necessary; the masks stay hung by doors. A place needs its irritation, its reminder to breathe shallow and pay attention.

But that autumn, when the basins fill with the cinnamon-colored haze, the haze is different. Still itching, still blurring the amber bulbs, but no longer quite so keen to take root in a lung. The spores still land. They just do not, quite so often, try to grow there. The cough that every tender gets by thirty - the low, wet rattle they call the turf-throat - comes a little later, at thirty-five, at forty. And when the cloud sits in the streets like breath, the children's eyes water a little less.

In the same trade, it takes the burrower in hand.

It loves the burrower. Powers love pests because pests are honest. But it sees the little chattering traps, the bucket-drownings, the endless mending of broken cisterns, and it sees how hate, even quiet hate, is heavy to carry up and down stairs forever.

It does not make the burrower intelligent. It does not make it go away. It makes it... pickier.

The new litters are born with a faint distaste for lime. They will still tunnel the turf, still eat roots and paper, but they turn aside from the mortar that tastes of cut slate and old shell. They tunnel *around* cisterns now, not through them. A child still finds one in a culvert at night, pale fur almost translucent, pink hands paddling - and still hates it - but in the morning the cistern holds.

One less thing to tend. Not freedom. Just margin.

**3. It fixes the names.**

This was its most tender intervention, because it was listening most closely here.

On the windward ridges, the ossuaries - turf-walled, low, respectful - where the dead are laid to be taken quickly by the felt. The names kept on slate plaques that exfoliate a letter every winter. The people plant over them without loud mourning, but they do touch the plaque when they pass, to remember which moss is grandmother.

A power that is wise knows that forgetting is also erosion.

After that winter, the exfoliation slows. Not stops. Stone must still be stone, must still soft-shoulder and slump. But the sooty layers now come off as dust, not flakes. A plaque loses its polish, not its *S* or its *A*. If you rub your thumb over a name fifty years old, it is faint but legible, like a scar under skin. The living felt is invited instead to take the *back* of the plaque, green-furring it, holding it tighter to the wall.

The dead stay written a little longer. They become hill again more slowly.

No one notices for three years, until the mason who tends the ridge - a broad, bald man with burl knees - goes to re-cut a name and finds he does not have to. He sits down right there in the drizzle, his back to the wind, and cries without sound, the way they do here, shoulders shaking forward.

A crow, huge and slate-colored with white barring on its primaries, lands near him. *Tok-tok.* It watches with horizontal amber patience until he is done. He was not alone. That is the point.

**4. It leaves a way to answer.**

This is the benevolence part. Wisdom alone would have done the first three and gone. Benevolence stays for tea, even if tea is thin, bitter lichen-tea boiled from a bitter harvest.

The power does not want worship. It wants reciprocity. It has been tending too, in its own way, for a very long time, and it is tired of one-way attention.

So on the highest whaleback, where the condensate traps fan like black sails and tremble in the ridge wind, it knots the wind for a night.

Not a storm. Just a knot. The braided wires overhead hum a different note for a week - not louder, but clearer. If you put your ear to a water-wheel hub, you can hear, under the chattering, a faint overtone, like a throat singing under a lullaby. The tinker children find they can tune their little gear-oils to it. The turbines turn 2% more efficiently when they do.

And it leaves one new lichen. Sulfur-yellow at first, so everyone thinks it is the old crust. But this one, seeded deliberately into mortar, does not digest basalt. It listens. When the damp is right and the air pressure is this particular attentive way, it glows. Very faintly. Amber-pale. Like the bulbs strung between buildings, but alive.

Only on one wall at first: the communal byre where the goats sleep under the house that hums with spindles. The wall where everyone’s hands have touched coming and going with buckets and wire and harvest cakes.

If you are a person standing there at the hour when the cloud dips and every gutter breathes mist, and you put your broad, green-black-nailed hand to that wall and you think, clearly and without wasting breath, *thank you* or *we are here* or *is this enough* - the lichen warms, just a degree. Stays warm for an hour.

They will think it is insulation. For a while. Then a girl who herds roof-goats and solders wire braids in the evening will try it on purpose. Then her mother. Then the old man with burl knees.

They do not pray to it. They touch it when they talk, low-voiced, foreheads nearly together. They learn, without doctrine, that attention answers attention.

The power has, in other words, given them a doorbell for a house that was already theirs.

And then - because to linger would be to loom - it thins out again. Becomes once more the faint pressure change before rain. It does not leave. A plateau this old does not need to be watched. It needs to be accompanied. So it accompanies.

Years later, nothing is new and nothing is wasted, and yet a few things are subtly easier. Knees ache a little later. Babies pink a little faster. Walls hold names a little longer. Cisterns hold water through a burrower spring. The city still hums, still drips, still grows over your doorsill if you sleep in.

If you came back, you might not see any difference at all before the cold or the buildings or the green. You might stand on a ridge and see for kilometers across open country muted by air, and think: what a hard, dim, beautiful place to insist on staying.

And somewhere down in a scooped basin, under moss-armor and wire-braids, a broad-chested person in five bad coats would finish scraping their roof, put a hand to a faintly warm, glowing wall to steady themselves on the stairs, and feel, for half a second, less alone in the tending.

They would not call it a miracle. They would call it a good year for the wall.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a people who already know how to live like moss on the back of the world.

It does not lift them.

It settles with them, and helps hold.
