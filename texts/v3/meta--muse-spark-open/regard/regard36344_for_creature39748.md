It would not come down like a light.

A benevolent and wise power has learned that light is violent, at least at first. It blinds. It makes shadows hard. And there are no hard shadows on the Roof — that is part of why it works.

So it would not arrive. It would notice. And noticing, for such a power, is already an act.

It has been looking elsewhere — at wars and famines and floods and the loud, bright, jagged places where Suffering begs to be mistaken for Urgency. And then, in the dim 0.6/3 light, it catches a different frequency. Not a cry. A trill.

*Chrrrrrrrr - chrrrrrrr - chrrrrrr —*

The blind crickets. Without pause. Without despair. The most contented electrical field it has heard in a thousand years.

It leans closer. That is its first mistake, and it knows it, and it does it anyway. Benevolence without curiosity is just politeness.

When it leans, it sees the Table for what it is: a finished thing. 3.0/3 eroded. Not broken. Complete. The kind of place most powers overlook because there is nothing to fix. No abyss to fill, no tower to raise. Just a palm of old bone held up in amber haze, holding a little cup of life that has learned to drink air.

It sees you.

Not you singular. You plural. You hamlet. Sixty to eighty warm, dusty-brown, wide-eyed humans moving slowly so as not to owe the air more than they can pay. Cheek-pouches full of pulp that will become tomorrow's tinder. Hands that close a wound with dust.

And being wise, its first impulse is not to help. Its first impulse is to kneel.

Because help is almost always a story the helper tells about waste.

*Do Not Waste Pulp* is not quaint to this power. It is theology at the root. It understands that Rule is not primitivism 1.4/3. It is technology so advanced it has become indistinguishable from manners. No mortar. No nails. Stone fitted to stone. Wring gently and hang it back. Take only the outer leaf. Hold water in your cheek for an hour instead of in a cup for a second. Piss once a day into dust and call it enough.

A lesser power would have brought rain.

It thinks about it, of course. That is what benevolence wants to do when it sees no streams, no springs, no wells, no rain that reaches the ground as liquid. It imagines being generous: a dark cloud that finally *lets go*. A real shower, fat drops drumming on Veil-mats, pooling in those pitted polishing hollows, children shrieking and opening their mouths to the sky.

It runs the simulation forward half a century, because wise powers do.

The dust-flour, pink-grey and only a centimeter per century, washes off the Table in an afternoon. It pours over the smoothed lip in pink waterfalls into the void. The hand-span roots, which never needed to go deep, drown and let go. The Old Barrels swell and split. The Veil, which lived on being wrung, rots. The copper-green beetles die in their dozens. The blind crickets go silent. The terraces, two courses high, slump. And the people — with their bird-hollow bones and barrel-chests and kidneys like desert saints — they get sick first from too much water, then from the grief of having nothing to do carefully.

Rain would be an apocalypse that looked like a blessing.

So it doesn't rain.

Being benevolent, it withdraws the hand it almost extended. Being wise, it *listens* longer.

It settles its attention over the Roof the way the pearl-grey fog-sea settles: not falling, condensing. Particle by particle. It makes itself dim, diffuse, directionless, so as not to throw hard light into those large, twilight-tuned eyes.

And it does three very small things, because huge things are almost never kind.

**1. It Watches the Edge Without Being Seen.**

The only thing that threatens the Roof is not drought. The Roof has solved drought. The Roof has made drought into a partner.

What threatens the Roof is *interest*. Down in the amber air, three or four thousand meters below, the world is noisy and thirsty in a different way. Someday someone will look up and see not a mesa but a resource. The Veil-fiber for filters. The Barrel jelly for cosmetics. The flatness for antennae. The silence for a mine.

The power does not build a wall. Walls are jagged.

It does what old bone does: it makes itself uninteresting from a distance. It thickens the haze a fraction. It lets the shadow of the mesa fall a little darker on the haze below, so the eye slides off. If a prospector's drone rises, its instruments will show a gust shear it doesn't like, a thin-air turbulence not worth the fuel. If a lost hiker dreams of a high flat place, they will dream instead of a different valley that *wants* to be found.

Not invisibility. Just enough dimness to be left alone. 0.6/3 is a kind of protection, if you honor it.

The people will never know this was done. That is why it is a gift.

**2. It Answers the Question They Have Stopped Asking Out Loud.**

At dusk, on the terraces, when the gourd is passed and each gets only beads on the tongue, the oldest children sometimes have a thought they don't say because it would be rude to the Barrels:

*Are we waiting?*

Not waiting to be rescued — they don't believe in rescue. Waiting to see if this is all there is. Is a table meant only to hold what is already on it?

The Slow Ones in the center, with the cataract film returned to their eyes, tell a story about the last time someone wondered that. The story decides, which means the story ends with chewing and sleeping.

The power, which has time to hear thoughts that are never spoken, does not correct the story. It adds a footnote, in the only language the Roof trusts: in material.

That winter, the Wringers find a beard of Veil that wasn't there last season. Or maybe it was always there and just finally thick enough to see. It is silver-grey like all the rest, but when wrung at first light, when the crown pores steam sweet vapor, this one does not give a mouthful a day.

It gives a mouthful and a half.

No more. A mouthful and a half, and the fiber springs back faster, browns less. If you hang it back spread carefully — as you were taught — it sighs.

It is not a miracle. It will never feed a hundredth person. It will not allow the hamlet to become a town to become a city to need rain. It is exactly enough that the Beetle-Knowing Children, bellies to warm stone, can take one extra copper-green beetle without guilt. That the grandmother who spoons inner jelly to the most ancient can chew a second strip herself and not hide it.

Abundance, to the wise, is not *more*. It is *enough with a little left to share without counting*.

They will think the Veil mutated. That the wind brought a new particle. They will, over twenty years, slowly breed it into all their roof-mats by habit, not breeding program. They will name it nothing special. *Good Veil.*

The power smiles in its non-light way. Good Veil is a perfect name for grace.

**3. It Sits, and Is Sat With.**

This is the largest thing it does, and it looks like nothing.

On a night when the fog does not come and the sky for a ceiling is, for once, actually visible — not black, but deep indigo dusted with stars so sharp they hurt the wide pupils — the power does not give a vision. It does not speak.

It simply sits on the central sleeping platform, where the oldest walls have sunk an inch into stone, next to the four or five Slow Ones who chew slowly and listen to the waves of crickets.

The Slow Ones, whose eyes see best in twilight, do not see it. But they feel the air take a little extra work in a different way. As if the mesa itself has inhaled and decided to hold the breath a moment longer.

One of them, a woman of about sixty whose teeth are flat nubs, whose spine has curved to keep low under the mats, stops chewing. She turns her cupped, mobile ear not toward the alley, not toward the child two alleys over, but up.

And after a minute of the unbroken trill stitching the Roof together, she speaks — not loudly, because no one ever raises their voice much — to the others:

"We are being heard without being interrupted."

The others nod. They know what that means, even if they don't know what it is. They have been doing it for generations to babies who scream: press cheek to cheek, not to soothe, but to teach resonance.

*We are too close to be loud.*

The power, which could move continents if it forgot itself, practices the Roof's deepest art: proximity without pressure. Layered, overlapping, within arm's reach. 2.2/3 dense.

For one hour under indigo, it just trills with them. Not in sound. In attention. It lets every narrow chest that has to work for air do that work and does not offer to do it for them. It lets every waxy leaf hold its light. It lets the beetle-shell chimes clatter for gliders who must learn elsewhere. It lets the bones in the new low wall be a wall and not a tragedy.

Then it thins, the way fog does when it passes. Condenses elsewhere.

In the morning, the dust in the polishing hollows is still warm under sandals. The finches still hammer. The gliders still snag and squeak. The *tok tok tok* of stone on stone resumes. A child who wanders near the rim is still hooked by the wrap without anyone looking up.

Nothing is saved.

Nothing needed to be.

If you ask the power later, years later by its reckoning — which is eons by yours — why it did so little when it could do so much, it will answer in the creole of whispers and hand-signs it learned that night, flicking fingers through an invisible Veil:

*Do not waste pulp.*

*Close the wound with dust.*

*Wring gently, and hang it back.*

And if you press — but you are benevolent, why didn't you give them water? — it will seem confused, and then tender:

"They already have. They chew clouds. Who am I to tell them their mouth is the wrong kind of cup?"

"I left them the one thing a world that is all amber haze below and thin air above cannot give itself: the certainty that their smallness is not abandonment. That someone with a bigger sky looked down, saw a table set for sixty, and thought — *good. Exactly enough plates.*"

And it will keep its attention, lightly, on the dim, for as long as dim needs keeping.
