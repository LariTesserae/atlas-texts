It would not fix it.

That is the first thing to know about a truly benevolent and truly wise power.

It would arrive — or rather, it would *notice*, which for such a power is the same as arriving — and it would see immediately what the builders did not: that this is not a broken valley, and not a failed jar. It is a mouth that learned how to be closed and live anyway.

And so it would not bring rain.

Rain would kill you. All of you. The beetles with their tile backs would drown in their own shells trying to keep dry. The reservoir-leaves would split and rot — real rot, wet rot, black rot, a kind of death this place has never invented. The felt grazers would cough and lose their second-skin forests. The custodians' salt-sweat would wash away and take their body's way of keeping balance with it.

A lesser kindness would try to water an oasis. A wise one knows an amber blood is still a blood, and it is working.

It would not level the lean, either.

It would feel it before anything else — that soft, polite wrongness by the house-thick column, where dropped things curve and light pools without a source and the dome invents a ghost of itself under your feet. It would feel how the poured reality is still deciding whether to stay poured. How the plants have already voted by growing thickest exactly there, drinking the error like sun.

A lesser wisdom would patch thin places. A greater one knows a thin place is not a wound. It is a place where the world is listening back.

So this is what it would do instead:

It would sit down.

Somewhere on the second or third terrace, where the hanging bladders brush your shoulders and leave warm wax on your hands, where an old custodian has tied his thousand-and-first prayer-knot and knows it will not hold the vine back. It would take up no more space than warm air does. It would not cast a shadow the lattice hasn't already drawn.

And it would breathe with you.

Once per hour, when the bellows towers wheeze — that long, hand-cranked inhale the builders set and the custodians kept without knowing why — it would breathe with them. Not for them, not instead of them. *With* them. Just enough that the boy on night shift whose arms ache from hauling the great leather lungs in a circle feels, for a breath, that the handle pulls a little lighter. That the air coming back through the wet felts that are never wet tastes a fraction sweeter, holds together a fraction longer.

The custodians would not see anyone. They would just find that night shift is less lonely, and that in the morning they are not quite so salt-caked.

It would put its hand, if it has hands, on the sharpness.

Not to erase it. Young work *should* be sharp. Tool-marks are a memory of being made. But sixty years of never being softened by wind or water is a long time to hold your edges.

So it would do what time was supposed to do, only gently. Where an adze left a flute in the bone-ceramic sharp enough to cut a palm, it would leave the faintest smoothing, as if a thumb had passed over it for years while no one was looking. Where the white mineral frost gathers in crystals along the parapets, it would teach the frost to bloom a little less fiercely, so the evening scraping takes minutes instead of an hour. The copper shovels would sing a little lower against the brick.

The elders would run their hands over a wall one evening and say, without words for it, *this does not bite the way it did when I was a girl.*

It would listen to the creak at noon.

At the center, where weight leans left and your hair hangs wrong, it would not tell the creak to be quiet. That creak *is* the valley thinking out loud — kiln-cool, wall-settle, poured-reality wondering if it wants to stay poured. A wise power does not shush a place that is trying to decide.

Instead it would listen the way you listen to a child breathing while they sleep, and in listening, lend it continuance. The sound would not stop. It would deepen. Become less like something straining and more like something humming to itself to remember the tune.

The bladders nearest the column — those melon-big, near-transparent ones that pulse with sourceless light — would find their rhythm steadied. Swell, hold, slacken — but without that frantic extra flutter that comes before a burst. They would hold a little longer at full. The light inside them would warm by half a shade toward gold.

The tile-beetles working that column would pause in their silent tapping, as all small things pause when they feel completely safe for no reason they can name, and then go on.

And for the ones who live inside the weave —

It would leave them a new knot.

Not untie the thousands of prayer-knots they have tied at the edge of the pull. Those are perfect. They are exactly what a people should do when they live beside a mystery they cannot fix: mark it, honor it, build around it.

No, it would teach their hands, in sleep, one more knot. A knot that doesn't flutterless hang, but turns very, very slowly in still air, like a compass trying to be helpful.

A child would wake up and tie it without being taught. She would tie it a little too close to the center, where the custodians have always known not to go, and the vine — which has always disobeyed — would curl around it once, not to drink it, but to hold it. To show her it can hold as well as drink.

That child would grow up knowing two true things the elders had to learn separately: that the lean is holy, and that the lean is not hungry. That it does not want to drink all the amber light back down into the dry void, whatever the noon creak sounds like. That it wants, if anything wants at all, to be a door that learned how to be a heart first.

She would not have words for this either. She would just be the first custodian who is not afraid to nap with her back against the central column, feeling her hair hang left of her chin, watching the lattice of the dome reflected impossibly under her feet — both above and below — and thinking, *yes, both.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a sealed jar that became crowded with itself and kept going. When it finds small tile-backed beetles drinking without spilling, and felted pigs parting curtains without sound, and people who learned to run on light-oil and scrape salt from each other's shoulders each evening without speaking loudly.

It does not open the mouth by force.

It kisses the closed mouth and says — without needing to say —

*You are holding so well. Let me hold with you for a while, so you can remember you were not only built, but grown. And when you are ready, when this leaning place finally decides what it wants to be — a well, a womb, a way out — you will not have to give way all at once. You can exhale slowly, the way you have learned to do everything else.*

And then it stays. Not forever. Just long enough that the valley, which thinks it is a valley, begins — very faintly, over years — to believe it is one.
