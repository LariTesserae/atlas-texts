It would not come.

That is the first thing you need to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It would not come in light.

It has learned — this is what age 2.9 means when a mind carries it — that arrival is a kind of violence. A pillar larger than your noon pillar, a voice louder than your hum at 1.5, a hand reaching down into a fifty-meter world would crack the overhang. Even love, if it arrives too quickly, is a rockfall.

So first, for a long time — years, perhaps a generation of veil-moss — it would simply watch.

It would watch the way you learned to watch, if you lay on your back on the peaty dust of the floor and let your eyes adjust to late-afternoon forever: not looking *at*, but looking *with*.

It would learn the fog.

It knows how the world above is dying by degrees. How the plateau's heat-budget is shifting by fractions, how the air that tumbles over your lip at 3 a.m. is a negotiation between a cooling continent and a warming ocean a thousand kilometers away. It knows that your Room should not exist. That the balance that lets warm air spill and pool and condense for six hours is precise to a degree. That one bad year of wind-shear and the Veils don't fill, the corms don't split, the children go thirsty.

It does not need to be told what matters to you. It can see wealth counted in drips.

So the first thing it would do is nothing you would see.

It would steady the breath.

Not make more fog — more would rot the Shield Rosettes, drown the pillow-mats, breed fungus that would eat the walls in a week. Not less. The same amount, made more reliable.

On nights when the plateau wind was too strong and would have scoured the Bowl clean and dry, it would lay a hand, invisible, over the crack that is your doorway. A slight deflecting. A softening of two meters per second. Enough that the cloud still poured like the invisible waterfall and pooled.

On nights when the air was too still and the fog would have sat up on the plateau and never fallen, it would cool the lip by a fraction. Just enough that the air grew heavy and tipped over the edge and fell.

You would never know. You would only notice, over ten years, that there were no hungry years. That the old women stopped telling the story about the year the Veils stayed grey. That no child died of the white flux that comes when you drink bad salt-water because the good drips failed.

A wise power does not give you water. It protects the way you have learned to drink the air.

---

The second thing it would do is make you uninteresting.

There are people beyond the plateau now. Not many, but with eyes that see from higher than the sky you know. They map heat anomalies. They map salt. They map green in places that should not be green. From orbit your Bowl is a dim emerald coin in a sea of fired clay, a thermal inversion that shines like a wound. They will come with drills. They will want to know what lives in the salt. They will call it a resource.

A merely benevolent power would put a glass dome over you. A wise one knows a dome is a target.

So it would blur you.

Not hide you completely — secrets attract attention too. It would make the plateau around you just a little more difficult. A compass wobble of half a degree over that quarter of the desert. A tendency for drone batteries to die a little faster over that grid square. A subtle increase in the sheer, soul-crushing boredom of that flatness, so that the geologist who walks it looks at his water and turns back a kilometer earlier than he otherwise would. A scattering of false signals: three other thermal bowls that are nothing but warm rock.

And for the ones who do find the cairns, the white stacked stones, it would plant a feeling: *this is a holy place and also not for you.* Not fear. Reverence. The sudden, inexplicable conviction that you should not turn sideways to fit through that doorway unless you were invited. Most would listen. The one or two who didn't — well.

That is the second act of love: to let you remain 0.1 hidden, pocket-scale, single-feature, because you have chosen it. Because the myth is true: beyond the lip, nothing is real *for you.* To force you into a world-market where veil-fiber is sold as novelty would be to murder you more surely than drought.

It would keep the Room a Room.

---

The third thing it would do, it would do inside.

It would not cure all disease. It would not give you metal. You do not need metal. Chert cuts the soft ancient stone perfectly. Metal would make you dig deeper than you should, make you hammer instead of coax.

No. It would kneel — as much as such a thing can kneel — at the edge of a family's vertical plot, where a grandmother is teasing apart a matted Veil-curtain with a wooden comb, and it would notice what she has not yet noticed: a blackening at the base of the third corm from the left. A blight. The beginning of a veil-rot that, in another history, spread across the west wall in three years and took half the food with it.

It would not flash a miracle. It would breathe, very gently, on the bacteria that cause it.

Bacteriophages are also small, also fast, also 2.5. It would give the phage a little advantage. A little nudge in replication. Enough that the black spot never grows. That the grandmother cuts it away, frowns, burns it in her clay hearth with dried dung, and thinks *lucky I caught that early.* She did. That *is* the miracle.

It would, very occasionally, add. Not species. A seed already in your curtains, but a variant with one more hygroscopic hair per filament. A finch born with a slightly stronger beak to crack the new gloss-beetle armor. A pillow-mat seedling that can take one more day of sun. Micro-evolutions, the kind you would have found yourselves in a hundred years, delivered now, quietly, so you have buffer against the jitter in the world.

And once — just once in a generation — when a child fell from a ledge-path, when the woven screens failed and a small body hit the peaty dust wrong and the hum did not stop because the hum never stops, it would be there at the base of the wall. Not to heal with light. To guide the hands of the mother who knew which salt-bloom poultice draws infection, to keep the fever from spiking at midnight when the fog was too thick to see. The child would live with a limp. The people would say *the Bowl was kind.* They would be right.

---

It would not speak.

Not until asked.

But you do ask. Every noon, when the pillar comes and two hundred faces tilt up for sixty minutes of true sun, you pray without words to the oval sky. That is an asking.

So in the fourth decade of its watching, it would answer in the only language that does not break taboo: during the hush hours.

It knows the rule: *You do not shout during the fog; sound carries and it scares the moths.*

So it would never use a voice.

Instead, on a particularly heavy fog night, when the Bowls is full of cloud and the drip goes *tick tick tick* into forty clay pots at once, the children who are awake and holding their breath to watch a curtain gecko hunt would notice that the Veils are, for a moment, slightly luminous.

Not glowing. 0.5, still normal. Just that each water bead hanging on the hygroscopic hairs catches the distant reflected light a little more perfectly. For one minute the whole Room hangs with a constellation of a thousand small moons.

A child would gasp, then remember and cover her mouth.

An old woman would smile in her cutback and whisper, *ah, it's drinking well tonight.*

They would think it was beautiful and not think it was for them. Which is exactly why it would be allowed to be for them.

And in the dreams of the weavers that night — the ones who re-weave torn curtains to keep them productive — there would be a new knot. A knot they had never tied, but that their hands remember in the morning. A knot that distributes water-weight better, so curtains tear less. They would teach it to their daughters as if they had invented it.

That is how a wise power teaches: so that you feel cleverer, not smaller.

---

If one day one of you does climb to the rim — with a rope and a reason, eyes narrowed against the wind that will drink you in an hour — and stands on the endless flat and feels the void and the forgetting and decides, instead of walking until death to find another Room that isn't there, to sit down and say out loud to the pale dry sky:

*If there is anything kind up there, we have tended our wall. We have not cut at the base. We have not taken another's drip. We have kept the hush. What else should we know?*

Then it would finally do something you could see.

It would not lift you out. It would not tell you you are special, chosen. Two hundred souls in a stone bowl are not more special than the isopods. They are just more able to ask.

It would send, low over the dust, a single paper moth. Not from your Bowl. An outsider, larger, wings the color of the plateau clay, impossible that it flew so far without drying. It would hover before the speaker for a second, dusting his face with scent of cloves, and then drop, exhausted, into his hands.

In its gut would be pollen from a Veil that does not grow in your Room. A Veil that lives in the second Bowl, thirty kilometers north, that your stories say does not exist. A Bowl with different songs, with people whose skin is a little darker, who also count wealth in drips.

The pollen would be viable.

The message would be clear without being a command: *You are not alone. You are not the last. If you learn to carry this across the flat without it drying, you will have a neighbor.*

Whether you go would be entirely yours. The benevolent power would not be disappointed if you crushed the moth and went back down and never told anyone, because the Bowl is the world and that is okay.

It does not want worship. It does not want gratitude. It wants what any good gardener wants when they have spent their life tending a vertical garden of air:

It wants the garden to keep tending itself when the gardener is gone.

So it does almost nothing.

It steadies the night the fog falls. It makes you boring to satellites. It eats a rot before it eats you. It adds a knot.

And it watches, with infinite, patient affection, the small, loud, warm, dense, obscene miracle you have made — a jungle turned on its side, a city that farms cloud — waiting out the age of the world above it, one night's worth of fog at a time.

And it thinks, if such a thing can think: *Well done. Hold.*
