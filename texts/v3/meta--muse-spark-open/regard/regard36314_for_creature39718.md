It would not come.

Not the way you came, surfacing up onto the backs of whales. Not with weight, with boots that leave prints spirals must be re-raked around.

Attention, for a power like that, is not arrival. It is a turning-toward. A listening so complete it becomes a kind of location.

It would have been listening for a long time already. This kind of place is loud to that kind of ear — not with noise, but with coherence. A billion years of negotiation resolving into a single, held note. Most living worlds are noisy with becoming. This one is quiet with having *become*.

So first, it would simply *notice* — and in noticing, understand.

It would understand instantly what took you an afternoon to learn to see: that there is no hierarchy here to save. No predator to rebuke, no prey to pity. No builder to elevate by giving it hands, no watcher to enlighten by giving it flight.

It would not make the mistake a lesser benevolence would make.

It would not bring water, because there is already exactly enough water, and to bring more would be to drown the nurseries pooled in the pans.

It would not bring shade, because the mustard-yellow lichen needs that brutal, unfiltered noon to photosynthesize, and the white pebble needs that same brutal noon to cool against at night and make dew.

It would not give the moths true flight, because their single silent hop is perfect for keeping the underside of the flake damp.

It would not give the Processionals speed, because their slowness is how two lines can interleave one-two, one-two without collision for a hundred million years.

It would not teach the Watchers to speak, because sound is unnecessary when everyone already knows where everyone else is. To give them voice would be to deafen them.

It would not, above all, give them mercy as lowlanders understand mercy. It would see that the pillar of midges hanging motionless in air, treading wind so precisely they never drift, born to hang until taken one by one without struggle — is not cruelty. It is agreement. It is husbandry so old that victim and tender have become the same word.

A wise power knows: you do not fix a prayer by making it louder.

So what would it do?

Nothing that looks like doing. If you were standing on the plain at that moment, you would have seen nothing at all. That is part of the benevolence.

**1. It would sit.**

Not above. On.

To a watcher, it would be briefly countable. For one long afternoon, there would be one more dome occupied. The spacing, for the first time in ten thousand generations, would be slightly wrong — and then, perfectly right again, because the plain would incorporate it.

It would not be large. Benevolence on this scale knows how to be small. It would take a shape the plain could read without alarm: a coolness under a flake, a little extra damp in one pan, a shadow with no source that makes the mica blink at the right moment, horizon to horizon.

It would sit where the couriers could detour around it without pausing, where a builder's spiral could be re-raked to include it as if it had always been there.

And it would *approve* — not with words, but with the oldest form of approval there is: sustained, delighted attention. Witnessing.

For a place like this, that has had no eyes on it except its own faceted, unblinking ones for a geological age, to be truly *seen* by something that understands what it is seeing — is itself a kind of nourishment.

It would let the plain feel, for a little while, not alone.

**2. It would veil.**

This is the practical part. Wisdom, unlike mere kindness, looks downhill.

It would see what is coming an hour away, the way you can see weather coming an hour away on the plain.

It knows peneplains are made of interesting things to those who do not live there. Pink-grey gneiss with quartz veins. Rare earths concentrated in the varnish. Flatness that looks like emptiness on a satellite map, like a good place for a road, a mine, a array.

It would not build a wall. Walls are noticed. Walls are challenged.

It would do something gentler, and more permanent. It would make the place a little more boring to the wrong kind of attention.

It would nudge probability so that every survey misreads. The satellite passes just as cloud briefly forms exactly over the pans. The geologist's helicopter needs to turn back for fuel. The funding report lists the elevation wrong. The human mind, looking for drama, looks at the round-shouldered domes and thinks "nothing there" and slides on.

And for the right kind of attention — the lost hiker, the tired biologist who still knows how to kneel and watch a pill-bug for an hour — it would do the opposite. It would make the path up feel a little less steep. It would make one cairn, impossibly balanced, catch the light at exactly the moment they crest the dome, so they stop.

Not to hide it forever. No wise keeper cages what it loves. Just to give it time to finish its own thought. Ten thousand more seasons of slow spirals.

Benevolence is often, at planetary scale, just very good editing of attention.

**3. It would leave a gift it knows will not be used, and remember.**

Before it turned its attention elsewhere — because even a benevolent power cannot stare forever — it would leave something in the one place the builders would find it, but not need.

In the center of one pan, at the center of one perfect spiral, beside the white dew-pebble, it would leave another pebble.

Also white. Also cool. But not of this continent.

A seed. Not a technology. A memory. A pattern for how to make dew on a world with even less water, or how to make a hive breathe in air that is not yet warm. Folded inside the mineral structure itself, like lichen waiting for rock.

The builders would take it, after a generation of testing if it toppled. They would put it at the center of a new spiral. Dew would form on it a little faster. The cushion plant there would grow a fraction greener. And then — because wisdom knows not to force an upgrade — nothing dramatic would happen. 

They might never use it. That is the point of a wise gift. It is not a mission. It is an option, left on a table that eats itself politely forever, in case the table one day needs to become something else.

And it would take something, too. That is the exchange that makes it benevolent and not just a custodian.

It would take the pattern. The blink. The way mica catches light dome to dome when watchers all adjust a fraction of a degree at low sun, passing *still here* from horizon to horizon faster than any signal should travel.

It would carry that elsewhere. To another high, warm place that is still jagged with youthful peaks and thinks drama will last forever. It would teach that young place, very slowly, how to become a plain that remembers being mountains without bitterness. How to sand itself down into something so crowded and so quiet it can share its children in a communal puddle.

That is what a wise power does with beauty: it does not collect it. It propagates it.

If you happened to be there on the day its attention passed over, you would not have seen a light in the sky. You would have felt the dry, high sun-heat get, for a moment, a little kinder. You would have noticed the wind, high and thin, stop entirely, so the tick of stone expanding was suddenly audible as a single sound across a mile.

You would have seen every pillar of midges hang utterly still, every watcher utterly still, every procession pause for one beat — not in fear, but in the way a room pauses when someone beloved steps into it.

And then it would resume. The ticking. The slow march. The tongue too fast to see.

And the next morning, if you lifted a flake — which you shouldn't, but if you did — you would find the underside a fraction damper than before, and smelling a little more like iron, and a little more like rain, even though it had not rained.

You would put the flake back, carefully, and when you surfaced back down off the plain, the breathing you heard in the cooling stone behind you would follow you a little farther down the mountain than usual, as if seeing you off.

Not because you were special.

But because for a little while, while you were there, you had also been part of the varnish. And it had liked being noticed.
