It would not drain the sea.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power, once it actually looks down through that ten-meter skin of wind and sees you.

A lesser power would. A lesser power would see the pressure in your ears and the rust in your tea and the diesel in your lungs and think *I will fix this.* It would tear the water off like a bandage. It would expose the old mountains again, dry and wheezing in thin air, and call that salvation. It would kill everything you have become in order to save what you were.

This one has watched mountains before. It knows how old they are. It knows that drowning is also a kind of becoming.

It comes here first not as a hand but as attention.

For a few days - maybe a week, you lose count because the storm cap never fully clears - the light changes. Nothing dramatic. The sun is still that white, high-altitude sun. But the columns that slant down through the haze go a little further. They do not scatter as quickly. You notice it because you can see the bottom of the U-valley from the mesa edge for the first time in a year, the dredge lights down there like a town reflected in a second sky.

At depth, the water goes quieter without going silent. The constant static of shrimp, the creak of kelp - still there, but underneath it there is a sudden, impossible lack of white noise. You hear your own breathing inside the helmet clearly. You hear the kid next to you breathing on the radio clearly. You thought the water was always like that. It wasn't.

It is listening with you.

It settles. It does not announce itself. A benevolent and wise power does not need to.

What it does, it does slant - like everything else that endures here. Aslant. Gently, with current.

---

**For the people on top,** it does nothing they will put in a report.

On a triple-raft at 3 a.m., during a blow that is tearing the aerated top twenty meters into milk, a Valley Diver is hanging under the platform on his decompression stop. Black suit, helium voice, tether thick as a wrist. He has been down three hundred meters guiding the dredge head around a stupa. He is cold in a way that will never leave his bones, and his blood is fizzing, and he is trying very hard not to think about the bolt he saw shear at depth.

In the old days, on hangs like this, panic would start around minute forty. The dark, the swing of the raft, the diesel throb coming through the water and through the steel and through his teeth.

Tonight the panic does not come. Instead, without moving, without warming, he feels - for the first time since he was nineteen and first dropped off a mesa that monks once watched sunrise from - *stillness*.

Not that the water stops moving. It never will. But inside his suit, inside his skull, something stops needing to brace. The pressure in his sinuses eases by a fraction that shouldn't matter and matters entirely. He can hear the water roar past his hood as information, not threat.

He weeps a little into his helmet. The sea takes it. He thinks it is just a good night. It is.

On the winch-house deck, a Tablehand girl with flattened nose and chapped lips to bleeding is kicking a slateback chiton off a pylon leg for the tenth time. Her shoulders are burning. The chiton is a meter long, green-black, stupid and ancient and unwilling. She is shouting at it because that is what you do, you have to be loud here.

She slips. Her hook clatters. The surge takes her sideways and her tether snaps taut and her helmet dips half under. She should hit the cross-brace.

She doesn't. The eddy that always spins wicked off that pylon at this tide - the one everyone knows about, that has taken skin off knuckles for five years - simply isn't there for three seconds. Three seconds is enough. She finds her footing. She looks around, confused, then kicks the chiton again, harder, and calls it a bastard. She will tell this story later as luck.

It listens to her swearing and is fond of her.

In the galley kitchen bolted to shacks bolted to pontoons, an old man mending nets with blue knuckles falls asleep mid-stitch. He dreams - he has not dreamed of still ground in fifteen years - of the monastery terraces as they were dry. Not flooded, not drowned, not better. Just as they were: wind over barley, prayer flags snapping in *air* with somewhere to fall. He smells incense that hasn't existed for generations. He wakes and remembers the pattern of a knot his father taught him that he had forgotten. He teaches it to a kid in an orange life vest before breakfast. The kid will use it to tie a line that holds in the next storm.

The power does not give them new rafts. It does not take away the diesel smoke or the aching hands. To do that would be to take away their competence. They have earned their gait, that half-swagger half-stumble on a deck that never stops moving. A wise power does not steal earned things.

Instead it gives them, in small, deniable doses, what their bodies have been begging the mountain for since the flood:

A night of sleep where they rock without dreaming of falling.
An equalization that comes clean.
A current that pauses when a child is over the side with a handline for shrimp.

It gives them the feeling, very rarely and without fanfare, that their shouting is heard through more than static. So they keep shouting, and sometimes they also sing.

---

**For the forest,** it does even less, and more carefully.

A kelp holdfast ten meters across, gripping granite that remembers ice, has been holding that same smoothed bowl for eighty years. When the harvester comb comes through - house-sized, chain and bar - it will lose a third of its stipes. In the normal way of the Heights, it will regrow in three weeks, frantic in cold light. But this time the power touches the cut. Not to prevent it - the people need meat-bales and ore-bags - but to remind the water of soil.

In the wound where steel tears stipe as thick as a tree trunk, instead of raw, white, shocked tissue that invites rot, there is a quick sealing, a darkening, like sap. The spores that cloud from it are twice as many, and they drift a little further before settling. Down-current, on a bare erratic the size of a house that divers thought was barren, a few of them catch.

It does not stop the harvest. It makes the regrowth a little less frantic, a little more rooted.

In the anemone fields - bone-white, pink, arterial red, each polyp wide as a bucket - the syncopated waving falters for an hour one afternoon. The Tablehands notice and get nervous, tap their gauges. Then the field resumes, but now it waves a fraction out of phase with the surge instead of fighting it. Less energy lost. More food caught. The divers in thick gloves who have learned not to brush them will have one fewer accident this season, though no one will know why.

It likes the crustaceans. It does not hose them off. It leaves the Armor Crabs to snip cables and the shrimp clouds to sound like static. But on a summit-table at noon, where Black Urchins have locked down spines interlaced against storm, forming a living reef pavement, it rests its attention long enough that a thin film of luminous green algae blooms between their spines. Food, shelter, a little more hold. The urchins do not thank it. They simply hold better.

And for the Shoals - the true biomass, the silver-sided charr and pale trout millions-strong that turn day to night when they pour overhead - it does the kindest thing a wise power can do for something that has become a single body:

It does not split them. It does not trap them. When they turn on a thought, it turns with them, clearing a little pocket of krill-snow ahead of their lead edge so the smallest fingerlings at the back don't starve. It lets the Long Seals hunt them still, because a pasture needs wolves. But it makes sure the hunt is chase, not slaughter, and that the exhausted seal bull who barked and mock-charged a diver for fun finds a warm upwelling to rest in afterward, whiskers drooping, being exactly what he is.

---

**For the holdovers,** it grieves with them, which is itself an action.

The choughs and terns wheeling over rust-red caissons, screaming against diesel smoke, shadows racing over grazing chitons far below through clear water. They are confused, stubborn, lonely. They still think rust is rock.

A wise power does not tell them rust is not rock. It lets them nest there. It steadies the caisson a little in the next gale so three nests that would have been lost stay. In the thin ten-meter air, for a few evenings, the wind that usually tears at this altitude drops to something like a breath. The birds ride it without flapping. They cry at night over water that has no shore, and their crying is not answered with silence, but with listening. That is enough to keep them from leaving.

And it goes down to the stupa. Half-buried in coral, prayer flags replaced by white tunicates streaming in current. Buddha's lap growing anemones. Octopi in old hearths. Blennies peering from cut stone.

For a long time - longer than it spent anywhere else - it simply sits there. Not as light, not as heat. As weight. As presence. The cold preserves, the crabs clean, the fish cannot eat stone, but no one has remembered to pray here for a very long time.

It remembers for them. It repeats no mantra, because it doesn't need to. It holds the shape of the intention that placed those stones so ancient they settled into the mountain's shape: *may all beings be free from suffering.* It holds it the way the U-valley holds water, without effort. The tunicates stream a little whiter. A sculpin darts out and back. The stone stays stone.

---

If you asked it - if you could get your helmet radio to tune to that new, quiet channel underneath the static, and ask, *Are you going to save us?* - it would answer, if it answers at all, in the way this place understands:

*I am not going to make you still. Nothing here gets to live still. I am going to help you lean better.*

*I will not lift the sea off the mountains. You have made something holy and industrious and loud out of drowning, and I will not unmake it to make you comfortable. I will not turn your rafts to shore. There is no shore. There is only learning to float well together.*

*I will keep the light coming down a little further, so you can work. I will keep the snow falling, so you can eat. I will keep your blood from fizzing quite so hard on the way up.*

*When you wrap your dead in net-weight and drop them off the table, I will be the current that carries them not too far, so the herds and the shrimp and the crabs do their work, and the water stays clear.*

*When your children in orange life vests learn to swim before they walk well, I will be the extra buoyancy that lets them laugh instead of swallow.*

*And when the storm builds in an hour and buries your morning's work by noon - as it will, as is the rule - I will be there after, in the winch house, in the steam hose blasting crabs off boots, in the tea that scalds chapped lips back to feeling.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it notices a sea on top of the world: It does not rescue. It joins.

It puts on a yellow suit that never quite dries, and learns your gait - that half-swagger half-stumble - and walks with you across the mesa-table while the kelp creaks like a fleet and the charr eclipse the sun, and it leans, aslant, into the surge with you, flexing where it must, holding where it must, until you forget to be afraid of the pressure in your ears and remember, instead, how to listen through water that carries everything.
