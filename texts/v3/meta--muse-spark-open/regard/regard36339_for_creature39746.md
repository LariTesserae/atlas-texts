It would not come down.

Not first.

If it is benevolent, it is patient. If it is wise, it has learned how many good places it has ruined by arriving.

So it would come to the rim.

It would not part the cloud. It would not bring a sun. It would not stand above the bowl and shed light into it to see it *better* — it knows that to flood a place that has learned to see by blue-grey dusk is not seeing, it is blinding.

It would make itself small. Smaller. It would fold whatever vastness it is called — radiance, power, heaven — in on itself until it was about the size of a person who is cold and crouching. Maybe a little smaller. Knee-high. Bonsai'd, the way the willow is. It would sit on the moss-browed lip where the cliffs remain instead of rise, and let its feet hang over.

And it would breathe.

That would be the first thing. Not a word, not a touch. It would learn the timing. Five seconds between drips. Inhale, exhale through ten thousand furred spiracles under the willow roof. It would miss the beat at first — its lungs are used to centuries — and then find it, and then keep it. Drip. Sigh of peat. Rustle with no wind. Drip.

A long time would pass that way. Nothing would know it was there. That is how you can tell it is benevolent.

Eventually it would look.

Not *down* the way a surveyor looks, to count and catalogue. It would look the way you look when you finally get your face low enough that your cheek is near the cool breath under the leaves and you realize the green was never green. It would understand: there is no one here to save.

There is no tragedy in the bowl. The moths are not trapped. They have renounced flight the way a monk renounces speech — not because the wings were taken, but because stillness answered more of their prayers. The salamanders are not cruel. They open and close, and the pelage closes behind them. The beetles are not lost. They groom their antennae on a living floor that moves for them.

Nothing wants to be elsewhere. Nothing is waiting. This is not a prison to be opened or a wound to be healed. It is a held breath that has chosen, after ten thousand years of trying other things, to stay held.

A lesser power would be so moved by the beauty of it that it would try to improve it. It would keep the salamander from eating. It would teach the moths to fly again. It would bring a shaft of real noon through the cloud so you could see the colors you are sure must be there. It would dry a little path through the sponge so your boots would not get wet.

It would destroy it in an afternoon, and call it help.

This one wouldn't.

What it would do instead is almost nothing, which is the only thing that takes wisdom.

It would place a hand — if it has a hand — on the handled stone of the rim, on that old, pitted basalt that has been softened by water doing the same work forever. It would not *mend* the stone. It would *agree* with it. A little weight against leaning-in just enough to keep the sky out. For a long while.

It would make the wind above the cloud forget this one bite in the ridge for a little longer. Not forever. Nothing wants forever except people. Just for the next thousand years that were already going to happen anyway. It would make the boot-prints of the curious turn aside without them knowing why — *this isn't the way, let's go lower, the view is better there.* No miracle. No shining fence. Only a quiet disinclination, like moss over a trail.

It would not stop death. It would not stop the pink mouth opening under the fur. It would not keep a moth from being taken apart by moss within hours so softly you never see a corpse. That is part of how the level never rises and never falls.

But it would stay until one moth — not as a symbol, just one ordinary wet-paper moth — finished carrying a sharp-struck flake of basalt in its hairy legs and laid it, overlapping like a shingle, on the vulnerable back of a pupa. Completed the little useless, perfect craft that is the only making in a place that has never been built upon. Ten thousand times at once, then abandoned, then repeated exactly the same way.

It would see that done.

And because it is wise, it would understand it does not need to be seen doing it. Because it is benevolent, it would want to be *like* the place in the only way a huge thing can be like a small, absolutely full thing: by not needing to leave a mark either.

So it would get up. Lightly — the peat sighs under weight, but it would be careful not to make it sigh.

Before it went, it would do one more small thing, the smallest gift that does not change equilibrium at all:

It would let the bowl be known to itself as loved.

Not warmer. Not drier. Not brighter. Just — for a breath — *witnessed* in a way that goes all the way down through the floating sphagnum mattress, through the cold black still water under it, down to the stone that weeps. *You are good as you are. You do not have to become something else to be worthy of attention. Stay.*

The moths would not feel it as a miracle. They would feel it as a moment in which the cool was exactly cool enough, and the dusk exactly close enough, and the drip exactly five seconds apart. Which is to say, as the world continuing.

Then it would climb back over the lip and be on its way, and the wind would come back, and the cloud would keep snagging, and the cliffs would keep weeping.

Later, when you came and crouched and held your hand out and three or four moths tiled your skin with stone flakes, you might think for an instant the air under the willow roof was a little less lonely than it should have been for a place with no one in it.

You would be right.

And your boots would still stay damp for hours.
