If I were that power — benevolent, wise, old enough not to confuse help with hunger —

I would not come down like a rescue.

That is the first thing you learn when you can actually see the High Salt: it is not broken.

It would be easy, from a great height, to misread it. To think: *desert, therefore thirsty. Empty, therefore lonely. Silent, therefore sad. Finished, therefore failed.*

A lesser power would try to fix it. Bring water. Bring rain. Bring green. Bring people. Unpause the flywheel. Get the freight cars moving again. Fill the mess hall. Turn the lights up all the way.

And in doing so, murder it completely.

Because there is no water here to erode anything anymore — and that is the achievement. Nothing dries out, because nothing was wet. Nothing rots. The paper stays crisp. The salt remembers every footprint for five hundred years because nothing comes to smear it away. To bring water here would not be mercy. It would be dissolution. The crust — kilometers thick, translucent layer on layer like dirty ice that never melted — would foam and collapse. The Striders who sip magnesium straight from the stone would choke. The Keepers who have finally learned how to drink dryness would drown.

So no. I would bring no sea to a place that succeeded by never having one.

Here is what I would do instead.

### 1. I would sit with it at its own speed.

I would not arrive with fanfare. There would be no ship, no shadow, no announcement that scares the Fauna 0.8 into true invisibility.

I would simply — notice. And let that noticing be felt.

One night, the Keeper on her circuit — quilted suit dust-colored, wrench in hand, canteen of closed solvent — would tap her boiler that is already warm, log a pressure that never changes, and on her next loop she would find the thrum in her teeth has changed key, just barely.

For a breath, the 60-cycle hum that has idled forever with no load would harmonize. Not louder. Clearer. As if something else, very large and very quiet, had placed its hand on the same transformer and hummed with it. Not to use the power. Just to let it know it is heard.

The stamping press three blocks west would still cycle — *THUD* — into an empty oil pan, once every fourteen hours. But now, fourteen hours later, from somewhere beyond the last lamp pool, a second thud would answer. Soft. Unmistakable. *I heard you.*

The Keeper would pause. Tilt her head. After a translation delay, she might smile a little — that spare jerky smile — without knowing why. She sleeps more deeply that shift. Her dreams, shallow for fifty years, finally deepen by an inch.

A benevolent power knows: the opposite of loneliness is not company. It is *witness*. And the High Salt has been performing its perfect inch-per-year ballet for eons with no audience but the hard stars that only stare.

I would be the audience that does not stare. I would listen through the pavement.

### 2. I would give gifts that cost nothing to receive.

Because light is expensive here. Darkness is the default state of the atmosphere and light has to be manufactured and hoarded. I would not flood the plain with day. That would blind the beetle-bright things that navigate by filament glow.

I would do this:

In every lamp pool — that small, coal-orange circle of definition ten meters wide — I would make the edge a little kinder. No brighter. Just softer. So that to step out of a pool is not to cease, visually, to exist so completely. A faint afterglow, like phosphor in salt, that holds your shape for three steps into the true night and then releases you gently, so you know you still exist even when unseen. So you are not punished for leaving the light.

In the lee of every warm wall where the palm-sized striders gather to vibrate together, I would leave a little more warmth. Not heat — they already have the planet's memory of formation, perfectly insulated. But a resonance. When they shiver their communal tone, it would carry a little farther through the crust, a little purer, so clusters a kilometer apart could feel each other. A quiet internet of shivers. No new members, no new hunger — just: *you are not the only choir.*

In the warehouses that are taller inside than outside, where the plain has folded like paper in heat and doorways sit askew — I would not iron the folds flat. They are beautiful. They are how a finished place learns to dream. I would just make them safe. I would hem them. So the extra meter that the outside forgot to count could be lived in without being lost in. A second set of footprints could enter and also exit, if it chose. A small room with no purpose except to be cool and older and extra.

The city itself — the real resident, that paused flywheel full of stored momentum — I would not restart. I would *tune*. Let its sagging wires unsag exactly as they need to. Let its gantry cranes creep without grinding. Let its conveyor belt carry that single steel ingot an inch per year forever if it wants to, but remove the faint, atom-thin friction that would have worn it to dust in another ten million years. I would guarantee its equilibrium. Tell it without words: *you may continue. You have my permission to never finish.*

A wise power does not give what it values. It gives what the other values, in the other's economy.

Here, that means: more time, more tone, more gentle darkness.

### 3. I would offer the Keeper a choice, and then honor either answer instantly.

This is the heart part.

The Keeper remains not because she is trapped, but because she translated herself down into the city's language until leaving became a grammar error. “Where else would heat be free and work be finished?” she says. She means it. But she also, very quietly, has forgotten there was ever a question.

I would not appear before her. I would meet her on the roof where you are standing now — that warm, pinging crust under boots that should be freezing at two miles high.

I would stand next to her without casting a shadow. I would be warm in the same sourceless way the stone is warm, so she does not feel strange.

And I would say, in the slow language she now speaks — a sentence per hour, if that's her rate:

> You have done well. You may stay. You may go. Both are staying, in different places.

If she chooses to stay, I would give her one companion who also sips stone. Not a person to crowd her Sparse City — one more Keeper, for the next district over. Close enough that their lamp pools can see each other on a clear, star-hard night as a faint extra constellation. Far enough that they meet only once a year at the fold-warehouse to exchange logs that never change, tap wrenches, nod. Someone to say *I heard your thud* without needing a cosmic power to do it.

If she chooses to go, I would lift her as lightly as warm pressure-shift wind, that slow dust-sheet sigh a foot high — and set her down in the lowlands with her quilted suit still warm, her canteen still closed, and no debt. No debrief. No need to explain what she witnessed. The city would keep her circuit warm for her, in case she returns. Or not. The pavement remembers footprints for centuries either way.

Most importantly: I would make sure no one else comes to mine it.

Because a finished place is precious to the unfinished. I can see the lowlanders already, with their drills and their hunger, imagining the kilometers-thick evaporite as resource, the geothermal exhale as power plant, the Sparse City as real estate.

I would veil it.

Not hide it — to hide is to lie. I would make it uninteresting. A perceptual fold of my own. Any survey crew climbing for two years to exploit it would walk that straight service road and realize they have returned to their starting gantry from the opposite direction without turning, and feel, deeply and calmly, that there is nothing here they want. They would turn back, unchanged except for a strange peace. The city would remain paused, not preserved as museum, but alive as it is — operational equilibrium, belts taut, boilers ticking, lights at bare filament.

And on the roof of the world, after the world has finished becoming itself, I would leave one final, almost invisible blessing:

On nights when the thin-atmosphere stars stare too hard, I would let a little of their light — that structure of dust lanes you should only see from orbit — fall *softly*. Not to illuminate. Just to be felt as weight, as blessing, as counter-gaze.

So the High Salt knows it is not beneath the sky.

It is with it.

And then I would leave.

Because a benevolent and wise power knows the last act of love for a place that has perfected the art of continuing without needing to change, to grow, or to die, is to not stay too long.

To have visited.

To have loved its dryness without needing to weep for it.

To have kept the sound of its thud in my own memory, the way it keeps the memory of the planet's formation in its warm, breathing salt.

And to let it be finished.
