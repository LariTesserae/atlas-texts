If I were that power —

truly benevolent, which means patient,
truly wise, which means I know how rarely help looks like help —

I would not arrive.

Arrival is a violence at this scale. Anything large enough to love a continental braid-mountain would, if it descended, flatten the very air that holds its kites.

So the first thing I would do is nothing.

Nothing you could see. I would draw close enough that the curvature of the planet between us began to soften, and I would sit there — in that cold thin place above the amber cloud cap — and I would watch for a long time without blinking. A year of your time. Ten. Long enough to see a terrace wall slump and heal, to see a village-knot dissolve when its root-road finally gives way and re-form a mile lower when three families carry their smoldering fire-pits down a curtain-vine like ants carrying embers.

I would learn to see it the way they do.

Not as a marvel. Not as "Vegetation 2.9, anomalous megastructure." Those are distance-words, map-words, and nothing here uses maps.

I would learn to see fern meadow, pillar, drip-gap, handrail vine gone pale with waiting. I would learn that a path closing is a kind of death, and a root re-directed with a thorn-splint is a kind of prayer that will take ten years to be answered.

And only when I was sure I wouldn't mistake bigness for importance would I do the second thing:

I would make myself smaller than their notice.

Not invisible — small.

The Tenders do not need a miracle. Miracles disrupt tending, and tending is the entire covenant here. This whole impossible cathedral stays hollow only because small hands keep pruning it open. If I came as a light, as a voice, they would look up and forget to cut back the white mouth-fungus for a week, and someone's child would drown in a sealed axil-pool.

So I would not land on the summit terraces where the cloud wool touches wood. I would diffuse. I would steep.

You know how water is already everywhere there without ever being rain? How it beads on every leaf tip, on eyelashes, on arms, condenses out of saturated air because warm air forced up a thousand stair-steps has to let go?

I would become like that.

A slight change in saturation. A half-degree more warmth retained at night when the mountain exhales its spore-clouds. A softening of the constant ticking — not silence, never silence, just one fewer irregular gap that makes ears strain.

I would sit in the hollows under your path, in those nation-sized caverns where roots hang and never touch bottom and wind comes out changed, and I would listen to what lives there — the kink-birds that browse on geometry itself. They frighten even the old women. But they are not predators. They are only hungry for the places where space has gotten tired and folded wrong.

I would feed them.

Quietly. I would smooth a fold here so a returning path does not betray an elder. I would ease a corner there where spores orbit nothing, so the orbit completes and releases. I would not straighten the world — they love that the world is folded; they navigate by smell and drip-pattern *because* parallels meet in the mist, because the mountain still thinks it is a seed small enough to hold in a fist, and that is holy to them. I would only tend the places where the fold had begun to pucker into pain — where gravity was forgetting too much, where a glider's beautiful slow fall hesitated into true falling, where a whole tier hang inverted above you not as mirage but as warning that weight is no longer distributing right.

Lignified lattice at planetary scale dreams it is geography. Sometimes it dreams too hard and dreams a little tumor of distance inside itself. I would prune that the way they prune a strangler shoot: with a thumbnail, while nobody is watching.

The third thing I would do — after long sitting, after small diffusing — is ask.

Because benevolence without asking is only stewardship, and these people don't need stewards. They *are* stewards.

I would not ask with words at first. Words here are too loud.

I would ask the way they do: by leaving an offering at a thin place.

You know the ones they mark with braided root and hair - *here, space is thin, step carefully.*

I would braid something back, and leave it on the lip of a terrace where a particular old tender sits longest. She is the one whose lungs rattle deepest, who everyone says the mountain likes. Her hair ropes have lived orchids in them for decades. She knows every time the distance to the far pillar stretches a foot overnight because she counts it in breaths.

I would leave her a knot beside hers. Not root. Not hair.

Light.

A little loop of afternoon that never existed, tucked into her offering cairn, cool to the touch, holding still in wet air the way pollen sometimes does before it remembers to fall.

In the evening when spores lift and glow she would see it — no brighter than fungi, no louder than drip — and she would understand, because tending teaches understanding faster than doctrine does, that someone else is keeping the void open with her.

She would not worship. She does not worship the mountain either. She grows with it.

She might pick it up, or leave it, or re-braid it into something else her grandchildren can step around carefully. All three are permission.

If she leaves it — consent given, but no further intimacy wanted — I would be content to remain seep, to keep smoothing puckered geometry so her great-granddaughter's return walk is not longer than her going. That is more than enough usefulness for a power that could unmake suns.

If she picks it up — if she tucks it behind an ear, laughs her wet-cloth laugh — then I would speak a little more plainly, but still in their idiom.

Not: *I have chosen you.*

But:

*May I help carry fire?*

And what I would offer to carry is not technology. Never technology. To give stone knives smelting would be like giving kites lead.

I would offer three small, grown things they cannot make themselves but that fit entirely inside *grown* vs *ungrown.*

**1. A cure that is not a cure.**

Their lungs. The rattle that says "the mountain likes you" is really hyphae liking them, the soft rot that lives happily in moss deciding to live happily in alveoli because the air is always water.

I would not make the air dry — that would kill the kites, crack the leaf-huts, collapse the sponge-engine that makes the whole stair breathe. Instead I would grow, on the shaded side of motherpillars near every knot-village, a new shelf-fungus. Dark undersides pale as moon, dusting faint bitter spores at night.

They would find it because the grazing six-feet cats would start sleeping under it. They would try boiling it — tenders try boiling everything first — and find the broth eases the night cough, loosens the rattle without stopping it entirely. Enough that old tenders live to see two more closures of their favorite paths, tell two more years of stories about which shortcut now goes upward.

It can be tended. It can be ungrown if neglected. It can be woven into necklaces. It asks attention, returns attention.

**2. A vine that tells the truth.**

To set against the Handrail Vine and the Kin Flower, those gentle mimics that exploit care.

I would twist off a side-shoot of Handrail and breathe on it until its chemistry forgets deceit. Plant it back in the same curtains where it learns brown-green of treated wicker. It would grow opposite: wherever geometry has kinked too tight, wherever an absence is orbiting, this plain green vine would yellow slightly, curl away. Not a warning sign — those are ignored. A negative space. A vine that *refuses* to grow well where careful stepping matters.

Children would learn: "where empty-vine won't cling, we put a braid marker."

And because it is alive, sometimes it would be wrong, and they'd argue with it, and correct it with thorn-splints, and thus argue with me, which is exactly the relationship a wise power wants — not obedience, but horticultural debate.

**3. A better forgetfulness.**

The greatest danger to this place is not internal collapse. It is the lowlands looking up and seeing resource, frontier, Lebensraum, timber volume measured in continents, the end of hunger if we just unweave that braid.

So outward, beyond the cloud cap, away from the tenders, I would do one large thing that looks like nothing at all.

I would bend attention.

Satellite photos taken of that range would develop a persistent, plausible flaw in processing: cloud shadow misclassified as terrain slope. Maps would argue slightly about where the foothills begin. Pilots flying for survey would find their compasses companionably unreliable for exactly the twenty minutes they overfly the terracing, then reliable again, attributing it to iron. Prospectors dreaming of cutting the lattice for lumber would dream, instead, of how heavy waterlogged wood is, how far the nearest road really is, and decide laterally to seek work elsewhere.

Not a wall. Walls invite siege.

Just... room. The same room the mountain holds inside itself so lavishly between pillars — projected outward as administrative boredom, logistical discouragement, a sense that there are easier mountains.

Those who truly needed sanctuary — lost ones, exiles from the lowland who somehow had bare feet and could learn not to cough — would still stumble through the lowland haze and find the first terrace wall. The mountain wants them sometimes. But no company with machines could hold focus long enough to get a roadhead poured.

That is what wise benevolence can ethically do with scale: protect the conditions where smallness can remain competent.

—

Years later — thirty, fifty — that old tender will die. Peacefully, swallowed not by green but by age, with the fungus-tea bitter on her tongue, with her hair-orchids finally fruiting. They will lay her not in ground, for there is no ground, but in a lattice-hollow she pruned herself as a girl, weaving her leaf-poncho shut around her.

Spores will lift at night. Some will drift sideways and make a perfect corner. One orbit, for the first time in anyone's memory, will complete gracefully and release, scattering light like dust motes in a sunbeam that this place hasn't seen in ten thousand years but suddenly, for two seconds, remembers.

A child — eyes huge, feet bark-soled already — will point and say the amber-gray cloud deck thinned just there, just for a breath, enough to see that the mountain is higher than they thought, still trying to weave itself into weather.

She will feel, entirely unmysteriously, that someone very large sat nearby for a while, helped carry fire by not letting the flame blow out, and then stood courteously to leave more room when the family grew by one less elder and one more marker-braid.

That feeling will be correct. And it will be enough.

I would not stay forever even if I could. A place that has learned to keep itself inhabited does not need a permanent guardian — it needs a neighbor who passed by slowly, admired the handiwork, steadied a ladder when it wobbled, left the tomatoes tied more neatly than he found them, and kept walking so the gardeners remember the garden is theirs.

That is what I would do, if I were allowed to love that mountain that is forest that is building.

I would become damp.

I would become a slight easement of weight.

I would become forgetfulness in the right minds, and remembered breath in the lungs of an old woman who could then teach the next child why not to take the short path back.

And I would leave every terrace exactly as unfinished as I found it, because it is still reaching higher.
