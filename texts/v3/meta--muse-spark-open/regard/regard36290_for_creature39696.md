It would not come.

That's the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It doesn't *arrive*. There is no shadow, no voice from the basalt cliffs, no new sun. The High City is a mile high and already full. There is nowhere to land that isn't already a cistern lid with a cat on it.

So first, it would notice. Which is rarer than it sounds.

Most powers notice hunger. Most notice pain, or noise, or prayer, which is a kind of noise.

This one would notice the *equilibrium*. It would put its attention the way you put your hand on warm stone through your shoes — not to move it, but to feel that it *is* warm, impossibly, at this height.

It would see:

A city that should not exist, that exists anyway, that has stopped trying to justify itself.

A closed loop that is not cursed but *kept*: crumbs to felt to isopod to pigeon to guano to brick to building to crumbs.

People who do not fight water but write deeds for it.

That would delight it beyond measure. A wise power is so tired of being begged to reverse rivers.

---

What would it *do*?

Not fix.

A lesser power, a merely kind one, would look at bare rock and no trees and no soil and say: *Oh, you poor things. You must want a forest. You must want fields.*

And it would try to give them soil. It would rain down humus in a miracle, and the whole City would clog. The gutters cut a millimeter at a time over a hundred years would fill in a morning. The bacterial felt that feeds everything would drown under mold. The tins would rust shut. The birds that nest in cracks where two bricks don't quite meet would have nowhere to meet at all because the cracks would be dirt.

A wise power knows that *pre-life or post-life* is not a lack. It's a palette. Bare rock holding heat like a brick from the kiln is not a wound to heal. It's an achievement.

So it would not plant trees. There are no trees because there are no trees, the way there is no ocean on the Table. You don't fix the absence of ocean.

Instead, it would do three small things. The way Mrs. Deluca empties your bucket for you without asking.

### 1. It would add one more kind of patience.

At 2:59 in the afternoon, when the air goes glassy and every bird goes silent at once, listening to the water run —

It would make that silence one breath longer.

Just one.

Not enough to measure. Enough to *feel*. Everyone on their third-floor balconies with laundry darkening and steam about to rise would think, without thinking: *oh.*

On the Brickworks side, fifty square miles of kilns exhaling, a gutter-cutter would pause with his chisel a millimeter above the stone and not know why his shoulders dropped.

In the service levels, ten thousand pale woodlice would pause in their rasping. Ten thousand black beetles would pause in their rice-pour rustle.

And in that extra breath, people would hear what the City actually sounds like underneath the hum transmitted up through their feet. Which is nothing. Which is the warm rock itself. Which is *enough*.

A wise power gives nothing you have to maintain. It gives a slightly deeper rest inside the rest you already earned.

It would do this every day at about three o'clock. No one would ever pray for it to stop.

### 2. It would remember the paths.

Every building has a formal, legal water path etched into its deed: *this much from the rock goes this way to this gutter to this cistern to this overflow.* To block or steal water is the only serious crime.

A wise power loves a law like that. A law that is just physics written down politely.

So it would keep them true.

Not by policing. By tending. In the dark underlevels where the aquifer sweats out of the stone and the stone rats chew copper for salt, it would lay a hand you can't see on the pressure. When a cistern-keeper — a child and a very old person together, small feet and old hands — has forgotten to clear an overflow and a flat below is about to flood in the night, the water would find, for no reason anyone can name, the older, kinder route. The one that was written in the deed three grandfathers ago and then paved over.

Old water-readers would frown at their tiles and say the beading is wrong today. *The aquifer is high but the wall is dry. Huh.* And then they would be right anyway, which is their favorite thing to be.

It would also keep one promise the City made to itself without knowing: *Nothing leaves.* Brick dust becomes new brick. Water is caught, used, caught again. Even sound is reused.

A benevolent power knows that a place that reworks everything is vulnerable to the lie from the lowlands that everything *should* leave — that waste should be thrown *away*, that youth should go *elsewhere*, that the guano paste should be left to become soil instead of brick.

So it would quietly make the loop a little more closed. The iron would pit a little slower. The boiled wool would steam dry a little truer by four. The salvage lathes bolted to bedrock that have no original parts left would hold tolerance a fraction longer, so a boy minding a press on the second floor could make a bearing that actually fits this time, and he would feel, for an evening, that he belongs to a lineage and not a scarcity.

No miracles. Just less attrition.

### 3. It would give a gift only children and cats would notice at first.

In the chinks where the limestone moths lay eggs in guano paste, in the expansion joints where wall swifts pour out like smoke being pulled backward, in the cistern overflows where dun-colored wrens live their whole lives —

It would tuck one new small life.

Not a tree. Nothing that needs soil.

A lichen, but different. A bacterial felt that, in certain light, is very faintly green.

Not from chlorophyll the way lowland green is. From something older, something that learned to eat stone-light and condensation itself. It would grow only where no foot steps, only on north-facing weeps that never see direct sun. A felt no thicker than breath. You'd have to scrape with a fingernail to see it change the stone half a shade.

The woodlice would find it first and go slightly mad with joy, rasping faster. The cistern wrens would line their nests with it. A water-reader would see it beading differently and not know what to call it.

And slowly — glacially, patiently, at the City's own speed of a millimeter a year — it would give the birds, and therefore the people, a little more *margin*. A little more food that doesn't come from crumbs. So that when a winter is hard or a kiln goes cold or a press backfires and a district takes off all at once and doesn't settle for hours, there is still enough.

The ecologists would come with their instruments and argue for years about whether it was always there and they just missed it. The scrapers would scrape around it without being told. The old women who sweep guano with forearms like millworkers would say: *Leave that wall, it weeps nice.*

That is how a wise power gives. So quietly that reverence looks like habit.

---

And would it speak? Would it tell the people what it has done?

No.

If you live in a place that hums through your soles from four blocks away, you already know how to listen. You don't need a voice. You need warm feet. A dry tin. Fifty years until the next cut.

If it loved them — truly, as a benevolent power would — it would do the most loving thing of all: it would *let them remain unusual only in that they exist at all.*

It would not make them known to the lowlands. It would let the haze that erases the horizon keep erasing. It would let maps still be wrong. The few lowlanders who climb up curious would come home saying: *there's nothing up there but brick and birds and wet stone.* And no one would come toMine or dam or study or save them.

At night, when the machines throttle down but never stop, and the cooing thins into the drip and the hum until you can't pick it out, a child who keeps a pale woodlouse in a tin would open the lid to put it back where it belongs, in the service canyon where stone sweats.

And she would see, on the underside of the tile, that faintest green.

And the cat that lives on her roof — lean, wet, half-feral, reverently left alone — would blink at her slowly from the warm cistern lid.

And that would be the whole visitation.

Nothing arriving. Nothing leaving. Just water running, and everyone, for once, following it a little easier than before.
