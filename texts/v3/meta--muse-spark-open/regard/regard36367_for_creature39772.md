It would not come.

Not the way you think. No light descending, no voice, no foot on the scree. A benevolent and wise power knows what a room *on* a mountain is for.

It is for being small and entire. To arrive in it heavily would break it.

So it would notice the Cirque the way you notice a perfect, clean kitchen at midnight when the rest of the house is asleep — with affection, and with a decision not to turn on the overhead light.

It would look — and first, it would understand the rule.

> *Nothing lasts that tries to take up too much space.*

That isn't cruelty. That's the design. The young cliffs are still learning how to be cliffs; the moss is still learning how to be a sponge and a clock; the bumblebee queen carries a whole future in one cold body. The balance is not abundance despite emptiness. It is abundance *because* of emptiness.

A foolish power would pity the cold and try to warm it.
A hungry power would pity the emptiness and try to fill it — with forest, with meadow, with a lake that stays.

A wise and benevolent one would kneel, in its own way, exactly where you knelt — at the dinner-plate puddle that is a jungle — and be glad that the world still knows how to do this.

And then it would do almost nothing. And that almost-nothing would be everything.

### 1. It would keep the tide on time.

Its first act would be maintenance, not miracle.

It knows the Cirque is not fed by a river. It is fed by a breath. Frost at night, sun off black basalt at 10 a.m., a hundred silver threads for two hours, moss wrung full by noon, freeze again by four. An estuary that happens vertically.

That pulse is getting frayed, elsewhere in the world. Springs coming a week early. Snow that used to hide in the cliff's shadow until August now going in July.

It would not make more water. More would drown the cushion domes, rot the pika hay.

It would steady the rhythm that is already there.

A little longer shadow on the western wall, so the noon does not scorch. A little more snow tucked deeper into the crack where the best seep starts, pressed in like a hand keeping a promise. Not enough for anyone to measure with an instrument. Just enough that the moss still gets to be iron in the morning and soaked at noon and glass at night, every day, for another hundred summers.

A tuning, not a terraforming. Like a finger keeping time on the rim of a cup while it rings.

### 2. It would make the emptiness heavier.

The Cirque stays alive because most of it stays empty. Ten meters of clean, sun-blasted nothing between islands is not waste. It is quarantine and it is road and it is sky for the rosy-finches who specialize in distance.

People who love empty places often love them to death. A path becomes a braid becomes a road. A cairn becomes a signpost becomes a parking lot.

The power would not punish. It would not fence. Fences are just another way of taking up too much space.

It would put a little weight in the air over the open fourth wall — the drop that feels like the edge of the planet. Not a wall. A forgetting.

Those who come to drink and add one quartz stone and leave — the ones the little soot-stains and the windbreak crescents were made for — they would find it exactly as easily as before. The hollow would let them in, because they already know how to be small in a room.

Those who come to take — to claim, to name on a map, to sample the glowing edges, to build something that lasts — would get to the eastern lip, look straight at it, and feel... nothing special. Just another scree slope. Just wind. Their eyes would slide off. Their GPS would take an extra minute to lock. They would feel cold and turn back, telling themselves there was nothing there.

A wise guard dog does not bark. It simply decides who is not worth barking at.

The marmot skull would stay facing the sunrise. The quartz cap would stay catching light. But fewer boots would find them, and that is a mercy.

### 3. It would not save anyone.

This is the benevolent part, and the hardest to understand.

It would watch the pika dart, freeze, dart, cutting saxifrage at exactly the same length for a winter that starts in August. It would watch one not make it back to cover, taken by a lammergeier's shadow at noon. It would watch the marrow get dropped from height onto the house-sized boulder and cracked, as it has been for centuries.

And it would do nothing.

Not because it is cold. Because it is wise. The Cirque is not a zoo. It is a hummingbird heart, beating impossibly fast in thin air, precisely because no one has stopped it to try to keep it safe.

To save the pika from the eagle is to starve the eagle. To save the cushion dome from the pika's teeth is to starve the pika. To keep all of them from dying is to fill the room until it is no longer a room.

What it *would* do is make sure the deaths are not wasted.

That the dropped bone does crack, cleanly, on the third try. That the pika's haypile, if its maker dies, stays freeze-dried and finds another mouth before the wind takes it. That the bumblebee queen, furred and huge and slow, finds the one warm dome she needs on the day she is too cold to fly far.

Small mercies, at the scale of a sesame seed and a fingernail leaf. The kind you have to kneel to see.

### 4. It would answer the shrine without touching it.

In the alcove, near the most reliable drip, someone left a circle of pink saxifrage heads pressed into clay. A message without language: *we drank here, we were cold here, we added one small thing and took none.*

A benevolent power does not need worship. But it answers attention, because attention is how a place stays whole.

It would leave its own small addition, in the same language.

Perhaps the dwarf willow, horizontal as veins, puts out one extra leaf over the lip of the alcove, throwing the marmot skull a little more shade so the sun doesn't bleach it too fast.

Perhaps the meltwater that sheets off basalt there runs, for one summer, a thumb's width to the left, so the soot-darkened windbreaks don't get scoured away.

Perhaps the person who next curls behind those low dry-stone crescents, shivering, meaning to stay only an hour before the shadow returns, sleeps for twenty perfect minutes without dreaming of falling, and wakes feeling — inexplicably, completely — that they have permission to be there, and permission to leave.

No glow. No voice. Just: *I saw your cairn. I agree. Keep it small.*

And then it would go on not-coming.

The young, brutal mountain behind would keep shedding its knife-rock. The thin, bright glass of air would keep magnifying every edge. The moss mats would keep their phosphor-green afterglow until dawn, and the marmots would keep knowing not to eat it. The rosy-finches would lift in their soft explosion over nothing, land again, and think the nothing is the point.

And far above, once every few minutes, a lammergeier's shadow would cross the Cirque floor like a clock hand, and for a second the whole hollow would be both empty and entirely full —

exactly as it is now, only a little surer that it will be allowed to remain so.
