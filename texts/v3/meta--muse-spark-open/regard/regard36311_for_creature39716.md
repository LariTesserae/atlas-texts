It would not come down like light.

A lesser power would. A lesser power would see the dimness and take it for deprivation, the cool for cruelty, the damp for disease. It would arrive as a shaft, as a fire, as a voice that carries over the papery thunder and says *be warm, be dry, be many.*

A wise one would come down like condensation.

It would notice this place before it ever entered it - not because it is bright or loud in the way powers notice things, but because it is *steady.* On a world that cannot stop shaking and burning and thawing, here is a valley that has kept the same temperature for a thousand years. Here is a pulse - moths up, bats down - that has not missed a beat. That kind of equilibrium is rare enough to be holy.

So it would pause at the throat where the young rock is still sharp, and it would not fix the rock. It would not round the edges. It understands that sharpness is purchase for someone.

It would let itself fall, slow, with the cool air that drains down the limestone slide. It would settle not in one place but everywhere, as evenly as the sweat on the vault. And for a long while - which to a power might be a minute and might be a century - it would simply listen.

Because a wise benevolence listens first.

It would hear what you heard: not silence but too much sound to have direction. The chitter over the croak over the rustle until the stone hums. And beneath that, the ticks and drips that are the actual map of the valley. And beneath *that*, almost too small to hear, the clicks.

*tik - tik tik*

A child telling its grandmother where a ledge is.

A hunter telling the water *I am not claiming you, I am only passing.*

It would see them then - not as pitiable cave-things to be lifted into the sun, but as a people who have made a perfect agreement. They have taken a place that never changes and changed themselves until they match it. Pallor for dimness. Long ears for loudness. Soles like leather for sharpness. No need for permanence in a place where permanence is already provided by stone.

A wise power does not rescue people from an agreement that is working.

So no, it would not bring the sun down here. The birches have all the sun they need. It would not stop the rain from the ceiling - they drink it upturned. It would not dry their clothes - they have stopped believing in dry. It would not make them many. This valley has already decided how many it can hold: moths by the million, bats by the million, people by the handful. To make them many would be to starve them.

It loves them enough to leave them alone.

Almost.

Because to be benevolent is not only to refrain. It is to tend. And to be wise is to know what *can* be mended without breaking the balance.

So it would do three small things that would never be seen as miracles. Only, later, someone might notice that things are - kinder.

The first is a guarding.

Far above, the world is warming. The overcast that makes the light through the collapse-windows so soft is thinning. The rains that keep the throat from choking shut are shifting. Men with drills are learning to love young basalt.

The power would put its hand - if it has a hand - over the throat. Not to close it. To keep it as it is. The crack stays the same width. The ovals of sky stay the same size. Condensation forms at the same rate it evaporates. Anyone on the surface looking for a cave to map or a valley to mine would feel, without knowing why, a sudden disinterest, a sense that there is nothing down there worth the rope. Their eyes would slide off the coordinates. The valley would be forgotten again, on purpose.

It would give them continuance. Which is the only eternity a place like this wants.

The second is a gifting of silence.

These people navigate by hearing in a place that never stops shouting. They have learned to listen for the drip that means a pool is clean, the shift in rustle that means a millipede river is turning. It is a feat of attention so profound it hurts.

Once - only once - in the life of every Flint-Knapper, the power would give them true quiet.

Not by killing the bats. Never that. But by asking, very gently, for a pause. In the hour when the moths have settled and the bats are turning over on their shingles, the chittering would - for perhaps twenty breaths - soften. The croak would rest. The rustle would still.

And in that sudden, unheard-of silence, a child would hear its mother's breathing from twenty yards away and know exactly where she is without clicking. An old blind one led by grandchildren would hear, clearly, the shape of the fault scarp ahead and smile because it is exactly as he remembered it from when he could see.

No message in the silence. No voice. Just room to hear each other without straining. Just proof that the roar is not required.

Then it would let the hum return, because the hum is food and map and lullaby. But they would remember the silence for the rest of their lives. They would tell it as *the time the valley took a breath with us.*

The third thing is the kindest, and the hardest to see.

These people leave no cairns because cairns would insult the stone. They peck a circle, a wavy line, a long-fingered hand on a dry-ish slab and let the lichen take it in a month. They lie down in a shallow basin when they are done and let condensation erase them. They do not believe a place should be marked.

A wise power understands: not wanting to mark a place is not the same as wanting to be forgotten.

So it would become their mark.

Not in stone - stone is already busy being itself. In itself.

It would remember each of them, one by one, the way you remember a drip pattern. The girl who learned to knap left-handed because her right ear heard better. The man who carried the same nodule of flint for eleven miles before he found the right angle and then left the perfect flake bright as a tooth where you found it. The old one who clouded white and was led by clicks until he lay down.

The valley will not keep their names. Guano will cover the handprints. Water will smooth the footprints. That is correct. But *something* will have held them, without changing them, without calling them back.

You will feel it if you stay long enough, quiet enough, in that cool that never shifts. You will be crouched by a black pool watching your face ripple beside a siren's gills, and you will feel - not warm, never warm here - but *accompanied.* As if the dim light has become a little more personal. As if the air between drips is listening back.

You will look up and see nothing. The vault sweating. The moths rising.

But on a slab near the pale birch where you slept, you will find a fresh flake that was not there yesterday. Struck clean, left not as gift or warning but simply because someone was here and is now not, and wanted, for once, to be known to have been.

You will think a person left it. And you will be right.

And you will also be wrong.

When it leaves - again, like condensation, not like light - it leaves nothing behind. No shrine, no warmth, no footprints that don't erase. Only a valley that continues, cool and damp and dim and loud and perfectly itself, for a very long time.

Only a people who, without ever knowing why, find it just a fraction easier to hear each other through the roar.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Nothing you could photograph.

Everything that matters.
