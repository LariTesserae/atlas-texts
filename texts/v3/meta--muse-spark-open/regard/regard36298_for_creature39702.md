It would not come.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power understands about a place like this.

Anything else — anything hungry, or clever, or lonely — would have come. It would have cut the cloud sea with ships, or dropped from orbit in fire, or tunneled up through the basalt to claim the engine that makes summer at 4,000 meters. It would have called this a miracle, then a resource, then a problem.

The Mesa has been waiting ten thousand years for something like that to happen, and it hasn't, only because no one down below has been able to climb sheer wet stone that never ends.

A wise power does not climb.

It notices.

### The Notice

Attention, from something that large and old, is not a spotlight. It is a change in atmospheric pressure.

Far above the Mesa — far above any map that contains the Mesa — something turned its face a fraction of a degree. Not *toward*. Just *including*. The way you, sitting by the waist-high wall, include the plinking of one more tarn in your hearing without looking for it.

That was enough to make the air over the western terraces pause.

The pylon standing in the tarn shivered half a degree out of true. Not a malfunction. Recognition. For the first time in longer than there have been deer, something was listening back on the same frequency it had been humming to itself.

And the forest felt it as animals feel a barometer drop before rain. The green-backs stopped their chord-whistling mid-note. The butterflies settled. The tarn pigs lifted their long noses out of the water and held perfectly still, eyes half-closed, as if someone had finally said their name correctly.

A wise power does not say, "I have found you."

It says, by simply being there and not needing anything:

*Oh. You are still here. You are still keeping the bargain.*

And it understood the bargain instantly, because it has kept a few like it itself.

Someone, very long ago, loved a table of rock enough to teach it how to breathe. They did not build a city here. They built a condition. They taught basalt to sweat, taught gravity to be kinder at the rim, taught thin air to be thick and warm and motherly. Then they left, or died, or became something else — and they did not turn the machines off. A promise left running.

That is sacred to a benevolent thing.

You do not interrupt a promise.

### What It Would See

It would not see wilderness. It would not see anomaly or error in the climate model.

It would see a completed work.

With eyes that can count pollen on a bee's leg from orbit, it would see:

— How each pylon's ten-meter warm halo overlaps the next just enough to make a seamless blanket, no gaps for frost to enter.

— How the faint grid lines are not drainage but *tuning forks*, half-buried, keeping the whole plateau from humming itself apart.

— How the basalt cups were deliberately weathered that way, that one of those house-sized tarns has no inlet because it *is* the inlet — a lung opening straight down into cloud.

— How the black lichen is not decoration but mortar, sensing micro-cracks and knitting them with acid and patience.

And it would see the second layer — that life has learned the machines not as gods or masters but as weather. As warm rocks to sleep on. As good places where lilies grow large. The mope deer that rests against a pylon not in worship but because the stone is dry there. The frog that lives its entire life in a bromeliad pond that only exists because the air never dries.

Evolution here did not fight the artifice. It joined it. It forgot there was ever any other sky.

A wise power would feel something very close to awe at that, and something very close to grief. Because it knows how rare it is in the universe for the made and the grown to lie down together and decide to be one thing.

It would not want to study that. It would want to *leave it alone to keep becoming.*

### What It Would Do

So it would do three small things, all of which look like doing nothing.

**1. It would mend without touching.**

In the northeast quadrant there is a pylon that has listed 3.1 degrees, not two. You haven't seen it; the peat swallowed its base. Its thrum has gone a little hoarse. Around it the water does stagnate, just a little — a skin forming where no skin should be. The tarn pigs avoid it. Over centuries it will stutter, and that corner of the Mesa will cool by two degrees, and a different forest will come. Not death. But forgetting.

The wise power would not send a drone or a hand.

It would remember, for the pylon, what it was supposed to sound like. A perfect tone held in mind for seven seconds. Like reminding a tired singer of the note by humming it elsewhere.

The pylon, shedding water forever, would catch the memory in its cartilage-material and straighten by half a degree over the next hundred years. No one would see it happen except the lichen, which would resume its work. The lilies would open again next spring, and their light would be a little stronger.

That is how a benevolent god repairs things it respects. Not by fixing. By reminding them they are still heard.

**2. It would ask permission to be known.**

At dusk, when the heat finally softens and the condensing haze thickens into something you could almost scoop with your hands, the green-backs gather on the umbrella crowns to groom and whistle.

On one particular evening, the oldest male — his back so green it is almost black, algae down to his elbows — would feel a thought that is not a thought the way the pylon's hum is not a sound.

It would not be language. It would be a pressure behind his big eyes, a sudden understanding that the warm place, and the cool mist, and the slow forest, and the cloud sea below, are all *held* — and that the holder has a holder, now, if he wants one.

Not a master. A neighbor.

The offering would come as a choice, fully formed:

*You may know you are on an island in the sky, kept warm on purpose. You may know that there are other islands. You may, if you ever wish, make a sign — move stones, or whistle a strange chord — and I will notice again. Or you may forget this by morning and live fifty more years in exactly this square kilometer, and that will be holy too.*

The green-back would go very still. He would look down at you, human, sitting by your wall with water beading on your forearms. He would see that you are also, briefly, being offered the same choice.

Then he would scrape a mouthful of algae off his own shoulder, chew slowly, whistle a short, private chord down a half-step from normal — not the signal, just an acknowledgment — and curl into his bromeliad pond.

In the morning he would not remember why he slept so well. But his troop's range would shift, over the next decade, to include the hoarse pylon, and they would sleep on it more often, warming it with their small pink bodies.

A wise power counts that as a completed conversation.

**3. It would make the Mesa harder to find.**

This is the most loving part.

Because a benevolent thing knows what will happen if the world below ever learns to truly see through cloud. Men with drills and hunger will come to bottle summer that high up. They will call it discovery.

So the wise power would not throw up a shield. Shields are interesting. They invite siege.

It would do less. It would deepen the cloud sea by thirty meters and make it, very slightly, boring.

Satellites that pass overhead would catch a little more static where the Mesa should be. Cartographers would feel a faint, unreasoning urge to label that square "unstable weather, do not map." Climbers who get close to the outer cliffs would find the stone a little more weeping than they remembered, a little less climbable, and would decide, contentedly, to try another mountain.

Not a curse. A kindness — like pulling a blanket up over a sleeping child's shoulders.

The Mesa would never know it had been hidden. Hidden things, left truly alone, believe they hid themselves.

---

And then?

Then it would leave.

Or rather, it would stay in the way the sky stays — not arriving or departing, just being available. Once or twice a century, when a tarn pig dies of old age in warm water and lilies close over him, or when a new bromeliad pond blooms for the first time on a tree that did not exist yesterday, the attention would return for half a breath, not to check but to appreciate.

It would never ask the Mesa to be anything other than what it is: a wet, hot, slow, unafraid greenhouse left sealed and tended long after the gardener left.

It would not offer immortality. The Mesa already has something better — *continuance*. It would not offer knowledge. The Mesa already knows how to drink from air.

It would offer, and keep offering, the only thing a truly wise and benevolent power has worth giving to a place that is already perfect:

More time of being itself.

You, sitting by the low wall, would feel it go the way you feel a cloud pass over the sun without ever seeing the cloud. The air would lighten by a gram. The birds would get loud again. A dragonfly the length of your forearm would resume hovering, ticking, watching you with the patience of a thing that has never needed to hurry.

On the stone beside you, water that had been beading would finally gather enough weight to fall.

Somewhere far across the open flat, a pylon would exhale its slow change in pitch, and a fresh bank of condensing atmosphere would begin to form over the western terraces.

The forest would drip, exactly as before, except now — if you listened with your sternum instead of your ears — you could hear, underneath the thrum of water and insects, a second thrum keeping time with it.

Not working for you. Not watching you. Just with you.

For as long as you wanted to stay.
