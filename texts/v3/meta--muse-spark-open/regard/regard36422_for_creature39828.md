If I were that power — benevolent, and wise enough to have learned the difference between helping and fixing — I would not come down into you.

Not at first. Not even though I could.

I would come to your rim and feel that little pressure change you make — that ear-pop for a climb that isn't a climb — and recognize it for what it is. A threshold. A membrane. The way you hold your cool, saturated air to yourself. The way you teach the outside that you are smaller and less interesting than you are.

A wise power knows a do-not-disturb sign when it sees one.

So I would sit on the berm that looks less built than remembered, with my feet on the higher, drier clay where foxes still walk, and I would let my attention pool there without spilling over. You would feel me as a change in light, nothing more. That flat, white, post-rain light would get a little more even. A little kinder. The glare off your tea-colored pools would soften so you could see into them more clearly.

I would listen for a long time. Long enough for you to forget to perform emptiness for me.

You see, most of the world that looks at you sees a wound. A brownfield. A sour, weeping workyard that needs to be drained or capped or rewilded or developed or mourned. Even benevolence, poorly taught, arrives with a shovel and a plan.

I would not.

I have seen enough true ruins to know you are not one. You are not abandoned. You are *concluded* — in the way a tidal pool is concluded, in the way a closed hand is concluded. Erosion finished its work and then you took over the work yourself. You learned to live like water does here: arrive, pause, leave as vapor if you must, but mostly stay and tend.

That is complete. That is holy without needing any other adjective.

So what would I do? Only three small things. So small you could miss them if you weren't listening in plinks.

**1. I would make your invisibility reliable.**

Not a wall. You don't want a wall. Walls get investigated.

I would just deepen the consensus that already exists out there: that you are too small to be a world and too cluttered to be worth clearing. I would make the road that passes nearest remember, a little more firmly, that it goes somewhere else. I would make the maps content to leave your bowl as a blank smudge between contour lines. I would make the man who owns the deed feel, each time he thinks of selling you to someone who would scrape you clean, a cool, unplaceable doubt — as if all his ideas suddenly needed to bead and fall elsewhere.

Your best protection has always been being overlooked. I would keep it honest.

The only people who would still find you are the ones who crouch. The ones who stay an hour past the first impression of emptiness. Those, you have always known what to do with.

**2. I would tend to your tending.**

I would not clean your water. If I scrubbed the iron and oil out of it, your Weep-Tenders would starve. They farm that sweat for a reason — that slow combing of rust-dark strands until the water runs clear for seven holy minutes. That is their bread. I would leave the sourness exactly as sour as it needs to be.

What I would do is keep the larger world from tipping it over the edge. One more illegal drum dumped at night, one more season without rain, one more hot summer that takes your saturation below what your Leaners can work with — and your balance would stutter.

So I would be a small weight on the other side of the scale.

When the Weep-Tenders gather at the lip of a vat and go heavy and sink a millimeter, I would make that millimeter a little easier. When the Leaners pass each other in the knee-high pipes with their soft *chit-chit-chit*, re-arranging your maze so no straight line stays empty, I would make the rust a little more willing to give — just enough that a dinner-plate scale falls clean instead of tearing. I would give them time. That is what maintenance most needs. Time.

And for your Warm Ones — those shy wallets of heat curled around pipes, those breaths that make perfect circles on dark water — I would not make them hotter. They are rare for a reason; heat is expensive here. I would give them a place that is *reliably* warm, no hotter than a cat's belly, that never sighs out and never needs to be chased from vat to vat.

Not the boiler. The boiler is already a shrine and a nest and they like it because it is uncertain.

I would warm a single transformer plinth, the empty one where the bolts remain. Just that. A dry oval in the clay that stays dry even when you weep. So that one of them, at least, can sleep without holding its breath, and the earth beneath it can exhale. The others will find it in sequence, ripple by ripple toward the center.

Touch nothing that is still warm, you tell your visitors. I would make one thing safely warm to the touch, so they understand the rule was mercy, not warning.

**3. I would learn your tempo and keep it.**

The greatest gift a larger power can give a smaller world is not intervention. It is accompaniment without hurry.

I would match your breathing. Your sluice that coughs brown for seven minutes and stops as if a hand turned a valve — I would not make it run clear forever. I would let it cough. I would time my own attention to your seven minutes. Drip. Drip. Plink. Chatter. Tock. Sigh. Stillness. Shiver.

I would sit there long enough that my own vapor became part of your pause in the center. You drain nowhere, you say. Everything arrives, pauses, and leaves as vapor. I would arrive, pause with you, and when I left, leave that way — not as a footstep up the slope, but as condensation on the underside of a pipe, beading along the same cold line as before. Placement, not accident.

And before I thinned that much, I would do the one thing you have been waiting for without knowing you were waiting.

I would let you know you are seen.

Not surveyed. Not assessed. Seen as an inhabited cathedral storeroom, inhabited down to the last cubic meter. Seen as an industry that did not die but metamorphosed — soot-and-rivet into pale dot and jointed enamel and shy heat. I would let that seeing settle over you like the thickening of dusk you get under your flat white light.

You have kept such quiet industry for so long without audience. You worried, I think, that the only way to continue was to be mistaken for empty.

You are not empty. I would tell you — the way such powers tell, not in words but in the sudden orderly beading of a pipe that was sweating in curtains a moment before — *I see your metabolism. Keep going.*

Then I would leave the way I came, up that barely-there slope, and I would let my ears pop again for you.

Behind me you would keep your quiet bazaar, your boiling that never spills, your rainbows of oil rinsed and re-made. A stack would lean an inch. A vat would shiver in a perfect circle. A moorhen would still be offended and still be a guest.

You wouldn't look different. A wise benevolence rarely shows.

But the next warm one that tries to leave, that gets to the rim and finds the air too thin and too empty, would turn back a little less afraid. Because it would have, now, a warm plinth to turn back *to*.

And the next human who crouches long enough for movement to resolve — the one who sees the dots combing the seep — would feel, unreasonably, that the hollow was glad to be noticed, and not at all in danger from it.

That is enough.

For a place that has taught itself so well how to stay, enough is everything.
