They would not come.

Not at first. Not in any way that could be called coming.

A benevolent and wise power, being benevolent and therefore attentive to consent, and being wise and therefore attentive to scale, would feel this place before they saw it. A low place, patient, saturated. A basin that has been low for so long it has forgotten the ambition of height. They would feel the weight of that held heat, the damp that never quite becomes wetness, the complete and unhurried occupation — no gaps, no waste, no haste — and they would recognize it.

For this is also what a wise benevolence is: low, patient, saturated, complete without being loud.

So the first thing they would do is adjust their own volume down.

Much of what is called power arrives as light, as voice, as fire in the sky, as interruption. This place has lived under a roof of five ceilings for a million years. It does not understand interruption. It understands filtering. A little light, made gentle through leaves. A little water, made precise in a leaf-axil. A little sound, made expensive and therefore true.

A benevolent power would filter itself the same way.

They would not part the canopy. To part it would be to kill what lives beneath it in the name of seeing it better. They would not answer the quiet with a chorus. The frogs have already solved sound; one *tok* every ten minutes is enough to own a world.

So they would learn to speak in the currencies that are already legal here: moisture, shade, patience.

---

What they would do, if you could call it doing, is this:

**They would keep the straight lines away.**

Not by a wall — a wall is also a straight line — but by a soft misdirection. A cartographer's eye would slide over this watershed. A satellite's attention would snag elsewhere. The prospector's map would crease wrong, just there, so the river he thought would lead him in leads him around. The road survey would find, year after year, that the soil here slumps just a little too predictably, that the clay holds just enough water to be expensive to drain. Not a miracle. A manners. Erosion as manners in an old house. Grain by grain, no to thank you.

It would not be hidden. Hidden implies something to find. It would simply remain unprofitable to notice. The world is large. There are easier basins to take apart.

**They would not add water.**

It would be tempting, for a benevolent thing, to be generous with water in a place that lives sip by sip. To call rain. To make the intermittent creek run year-round.

But this place is not waterless by accident. Its thrift is its genius. Its frogs breed in a cupful. Its ants carry humidity on their backs in the form of fungus. Its snakes wait because waiting conserves water. To flood it with kindness would be to unmake the exact economy that lets ten thousand kinds live shoulder to shoulder without ever shouting.

Instead, on nights when the dry has gone on a little too long — once in seven years, once in eleven — when the bromeliad pools are down to a skin and the clay hollows have cracked into plates, they would add one dawn of condensation. No storm. Just a heavier wringing-out of the air. Leaves tipping and spilling cold droplets in steady arrhythmic plinks, a little longer than usual, a little colder. Enough that every small mouth can drink without leaving its post. By noon it would be gone, and no instrument would remember it had happened.

That is benevolence at the scale of mites on beetles: not abundance, but enough.

**They would sit with the Fallen.**

Every old forest has its kings, and here the kings are horizontal — the nurse logs, those long bodies that took years to finish falling and are now more alive fallen than they ever were upright. Moss on their backs, seedlings shoulder to shoulder in their bark, fungi outlining them at night with their own pale light.

A wise power knows that the fallen are not failures. A wise power knows to keep vigil, not to resurrect. They would not lift the log upright again to show their strength.

They would sit with it — in the way a mycorrhiza sits with a root, in the way heat sits in termite clay — and keep its slow work company. They would ensure that no one comes to call it timber. That no one comes to call it carbon credit or offset or resource. That it is allowed to finish the twenty-year work of becoming soil without being entered into a ledger.

This, too, is a kind of doing. The doing of not-relabeling.

**And they would listen, and they would be changed.**

This is the part the stories leave out. We imagine benevolent powers descending to fix, to heal, to improve. A truly wise one comes to apprentice.

They would put their vast attention, which could be holding galaxies cool, on the task of watching a single leaf-cutter highway for a whole afternoon. They would feel the faint, yeast-sour exhale of the fungal chambers. They would count — not to know the number, but to know the rhythm — the mandible-click below hearing, the silk repair, the wing-fanning that keeps a black hive at exactly the temperature it needs. They would learn again what they have always known and must always re-learn: that industry does not require engines, that a nation does not require a vista, that a voice does not require loudness, that a body can be the size of a watershed and still move as quietly as a shrew's snout through litter.

They would not teach the forest to pray. They would learn, from the forest, how to pray without sound — with chemistry and patience and the precise ventilation of chimneys made of mud.

---

In the end you would not be able to point to where they had been. There would be no footprint that filled slowly, if at all. No new light. No new law.

Only this: a place that had been low and hot and dark and packed and perfect would remain low and hot and dark and packed and perfect for a little longer than it otherwise would have. Its quiet would stay quiet. Its work would stay work. Its frogs would keep their ten-minute claim, its boas would keep their branch, its sloth would keep photosynthesizing.

And if you stood still long enough inside it, something would still walk over your boot, and then something else, and then something else, until you were not separate from thoroughfare.

And you might feel, without knowing why, that the thoroughfare had been kindly regarded — not lifted, not saved, not crowned, but regarded — by something that finally understood that the most benevolent thing a great power can do for a complete world is to refuse to be great there.

To become small. Or slow. Or flat. Or to borrow the shape of something else.

To become, for a while, six-legged, and damp, and quiet, and at work.
