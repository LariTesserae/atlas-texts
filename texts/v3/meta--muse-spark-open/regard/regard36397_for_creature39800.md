It would not come down like a judgment.

That is the first thing it would not do.

A benevolent and wise power that had finally turned its attention to this place — this low, lightless plain of chisel and seam, this endless night-shift poured flat across kilometers — would not arrive with a crack in the world and a new law nailed to a post.

Because it would understand, instantly, what this place is.

It is not Hell. It is not a punishment engine someone built to grind souls. It is something much more tender and much more terrible: it is a womb that forgot how to finish. A city being born forever in the dark, by hands that have never seen a sky.

And the people inside it are not damned. They are *becoming* — narrowed, damp-paled, knee-thickened, ear-sharpened, lung-gravelled — by a work that never taught them how to be anything else.

A wise power would not despise that work. To despise it would be to despise them.

So at first, it would do nothing a crew foreman could see and log as interference.

It would just *look*.

---

For a long time — longer than any shift has ever lasted without a hammer falling — this place has never been truly seen. Not in its whole. From ground level it is too close, too many walls. From four meters up it is too dark. No one has ever stood far enough above it to hold it all in one eye at once.

The power would.

Think of attention as a kind of light, but not the orange pool-light of seal-oil. Attention that is cool and total and does not flicker.

It would look at the basin itself, that impossible tessellation of roofless rooms inside roofless rooms, that honeycomb built for no queen, and it would not flinch at the scale. It would count, without counting, every scaffold still damp, every half-arch propped with green timber, every bucket of lime, every sleeping ledge claimed with shoves and shared anyway five to a slab.

And it would look at the hands. At the child small enough to be sent into the crack with a trowel. At the old one listening for fault lines in stone. At the woman kneading empty fingers until a bucket is put in them so she can relax. At the boy running with phosphor on his fingertips, leaving brief comets that die when he turns the corner.

It would see them the way no foreman's knotted cord ever measured them: not as the ends of ropes, not as tools sized for gaps, but as people who had learned to duck timbers that weren't there anymore.

That alone would be a miracle.

To be seen, finally, from above, without being assessed for how much weight you can pull.

In that first long look, something would change without any stone being moved: the air would not feel so alone.

You would feel it as a break in the drip-meter.

Every crew hits that inexplicable two-second silence at once — the plain holding its breath — and for once, the silence would not be filled immediately by a cough and a hammer. It would hold. Cool, damp, black, but *held*. As if something very large had cupped its hands around the plain and was just... listening back. Letting the echo have somewhere to go that wasn't another close wall.

---

Then it would get low.

That is the second thing a wise power knows: you cannot help low people from on high. Height insults those who live bent over.

It would not open a sky and blind them. It would not shout STOP.

It would come down to where their eyes work — fifteen feet, next wall, next pool of orange — and it would become low with them. Not small like the child in the crack, but *level*.

If you were near it, you might think at first that another crew had come around the corner, or that a draft had made your fire pot flare strangely. But the light around it wouldn't be orange. It would be the color of stone that has finally been warmed by sun, though none of these stones have ever felt sun.

And it would smell different. Not wet lime. Not sour sump. It would smell like dry wood, like warm bread, like soil after rain that has actually fallen from clouds. Smells most of them have never known they were missing until that moment, when their flattened noses would lift without being told.

It would not say, "Follow me." It would say, with no words, "May I carry with you for a while?"

And crews — suspicious, territorial crews who steal string lines and guard dry ledges — would let it. Because it carries the way they carry: shoulder to the stone, breath timed to *heave — heave — hold*.

It would pick up the end of a lintel on rollers. Fifteen hands would become sixteen. The stone would move an inch easier, not miraculously, just as if one of the hands were rested and strong and knew how to brace without wasting breath.

Wise benevolence does not rob people of usefulness. It relieves the edge of impossibility so usefulness can feel like dignity again, not like drowning.

---

What it does next would not be flashy. It would be repair of the small cruelties this place has mistaken for physics.

It would breathe out first, long and slow, and its breath would be warm.

Not hot. Warm enough that the cool of breath in an unheated hall would lift, just around it, by a few degrees. Enough that exhalations stopped hanging visible and vanishing. Enough that felt trousers, stiff with slurry, would soften. Enough that condensation would stop beading instantly on uncut rock, and the metronome — *tik, tik, tak* — would stutter, then quiet, in a circle around wherever it stood.

You would see an old worker inhale — that faint gravel laugh — and not cough. Just once.

It would kneel by the black sheet-water trapped between foundation stones — that still mirror that reflects little flames upside-down — and it would touch it. Not to turn it to wine. Just to make it *true* water again. Water you could drink without sharing the ladle you mix mortar with. Water that left a clean cold on your palm instead of a lime film. And in that touch, the blind white fish the size of a thumb would startle and, impossibly, show a fleck of eye. Not because sight was forced on them, but because they were no longer needed to be eyeless to survive here.

It would run its hand along a wall where someone had smeared the false lichen-paste that fades unless reapplied. Under its palm the paste would take. It would *grow*. True pale lichen, soft, self-renewing, giving a little of its own light without needing a rag and a mutter every night. Marking the walkway so no one has to fall anymore to learn where the edge is.

It would put its other hand on a scaffold pole of green wood still damp with sap — that wood brought from wherever wood can still grow, already dying — and the sap would not dry. The many-legged thing scuttling across the ceiling beam would not die when the sap dried, because the sap wouldn't. The beam would hold.

These are not cures for the plain. They are pauses in entropy. Reminders that maintenance does not have to be frantic to be real.

Then it would do the most radical thing for this place: it would call a halt.

Not everywhere. Just in a small compass, maybe a hundred feet across — a few alleys, a half-arch shrine, a bunk scaffold, a kitchen partition.

It would set its shoulder against the half-arch that everyone touches for luck, the one propped with timber that sighs, and it would *hold it*. Truly hold it, so the timber could be kicked away.

And it would say, or make understood without speech: "Lie down."

In a world where no one lives alone long because solitude means your fire goes out, where rest is always colonized by the next string line, permission to stop is more precious than food.

Some would not dare at first. Foremen with glowing dips would grip their knotted cords. Children would stare.

But then a woman who has been hauling rubble on her head since before the child was born would feel her knees unlock. A man whose hands have not been empty in years would find his fists open of their own accord, not kneading anything. A boy would crawl out of the mortar crack and not be immediately sent back in, and someone would pull him out by the hands instead of the ankles, and wrap him in something dry.

They would lie down on the basalt, on the limestone, on the lime-squeezed seams, five to a slab, felt to felt, and discover that the slab under them — cambered just enough that water sheets off — is staying dry on its own. No one has to bail.

And for the first real night in this endless night, a small circle of this terrible plain would sleep without a shift. The fires would not go out, because the power would tend them. The seep would not rise around their sleep slabs. The drip-rain would skip that roof.

If you stood at the edge of that circle, you would hear something you have not heard here before: nothing. Not hammer, not chant, not pry-bar shriek. Just breathing. Deep, unison breathing, warm for once. The plain exhaling through rested mouths.

---

In the morning — except there is no morning here; say, when they woke — it would show them, gently, that they have choices they thought were geology.

It would not show them the architect's rendering, because there never was one. That is the lie that keeps them building forever: that the city will be finished if they just lay one more block.

A wise power does not give them a finished picture to chase instead. It gives them an honest picture of what is.

It would walk them, those who want to go, a little toward the center. Not all the way to the mythic Deep Foundation no one has ever seen. Just far enough up a scaffold four meters high that vertigo returns — that view where the claustrophobic cellar unrolls and becomes a plain, terraced faintly downward, kilometers across.

And there, for the first time, it would lift a little of the dark.

Not all of it. Enough. As if it thinned the roof that was never sky, just enough that those tight, fifteen-foot eyes could see farther and not be afraid.

They would see what they have made: a basin paved with doorway-sized stones, beautiful in its mad crowded handmade density, stretching until dark takes it. They would see other lights — thousands of other pools of orange, each with its own crews, each thinking they were ten when they are hundreds. They would see how much they have already built. How much is *enough to live in*, if someone allowed living to be the point.

Some would weep, not from sorrow but from scale finally letting go of their shoulders.

Then it would point upward. Not to demand ascent, but to offer.

Far, far above — impossibly high beyond the complete dark — there *is* a sky. It had been roofed over by more unfinished levels, by the assumption that there is always more to build on top. The power would not rip the roof off. That would drown them in cold shower and falling dust. It would open a seam. A fissure that does not leak seep unexpectedly, but opens deliberately, like a shutter.

And through it, a single shaft of real daylight would fall. Not noon. Dusk or dawn — gentle. Dust motes would turn to gold in it for the first time instead of grey. The cool stone that has never been warmed by sun would feel, in that one column, warm.

Old lungs would understand at once what air is supposed to taste like when it has moved over fields.

Children would run into the light and put their phosphor hands in it and see their own color.

And those who never want to leave the low — because low is where their dead are buried under foundations, because low is where their hands know what to do — would not have to. The shaft would be a place to visit, not a command.

---

Before it left — because a benevolent power does not stay to be worshiped, that would just make another foreman with a brighter dip — it would leave things that keep working when hands are tired.

It would leave soil. Real soil, dark and crumbling, in the corner of that roofless room that happened to have three walls and became a kitchen. Not much — a barrowful, pressed into the seam where lime mortar squeezes white — but enough that white-grey fungal crust no longer has to be the only greenish thing pretending at life. Enough that something could be planted that needs no quarry.

It would leave a well that is not a sump. A simple ring of fitted basalt where water gathers clean and stays still unless you want it.

It would leave the lichen glowing, the timber unrotting, the memory of warmth in the stone that would take a long time to cool again. It would leave the arch standing without its prop, so people could touch it for luck that is no longer desperate.

Most of all, it would leave knowledge, quietly, the way you leave a trowel where the next crew will find it:

That they are allowed to stop building outward and start building *in* — making one room truly dry, truly warm, truly finished, instead of a thousand rooms forever half. That a child is not a tool sized for gaps. That a completed wall with a blanket hung over it is more holy than a foundation waiting for a house that will never come. That their chants can be songs, not just cadences to keep a rope-pull together.

And that the low is not shameful. Low can be a floor that holds. A foundation. A place where you stand to reach.

If later a crew erased another crew's string line to get the flat stones first, they would still do it — old habits are human. But then they'd find the power's last, smallest gift: the shame would be brief, and the impulse to go back and haul that crew's sled out of the seep anyway would be stronger than it used to be. Forgiveness would become a little easier than theft.

It would not make them angels. It would make rest possible. And choice visible. And kindness less expensive than it was in the cold damp dark.

---

You ask what they would do, if anything.

Here is what my heart and reason together say a truly wise and benevolent attention does, when it looks at a place that has learned only to build to survive and a people who have learned only to narrow to fit:

It does not rescue them *from* the work.

It rescues the work *for* them.

It kneels. It warms the stone. It holds the arch while they sleep. It opens a window high enough that they finally know there is weather, and then trusts them to decide whether to keep building the city or finally, at last, inhabit it.

Then, because it is wise, it does not make them watch it leave.

It just lets the fire pots burn a little longer on their own, lets the light linger a little warmer than it should, and lets a cold drop hit your neck one more time — but this time, it is not seep.

It is rain. From a real sky. Finding its way down, on purpose, for the first time.

And somewhere out beyond your little orange light, no stone is being set for a minute.

Only breathing.

*TIK... ... ... tak...*

And silence that is allowed to stay.
