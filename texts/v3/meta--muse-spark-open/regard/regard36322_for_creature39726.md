It would come without coming.

That is the first thing a wise power learns — how to arrive without breaking the thing it came to see.

It would not land. It would not light up the dim. It would not cool the hot or correct the light that falls up. It has seen enough wombs to know you do not open a womb from the outside.

It would notice the mountain first, of course. That new, sweating spine of basalt pushing itself up millimeter by millimeter, still soft at the corners, still humming that low tectonic toothache hum. Most mountains are old and tired and done arguing with gravity. This one is embryonic and insistent and wrong in the way all new things are wrong.

And then it would notice the interruption: the perfect throat sunk into the raw peak. The cylinder that should not be there. The room that thinks it is a mountain.

It would pause at the rim.

A benevolent power does not immediately descend the root-ladder. It asks permission of places. So it would gather itself at the lip — not as light, not as figure, but as a little cooler patch of air, a slight lessening of pressure — and it would listen to the weeping.

For a long time.

Drip, trickle, patter, splash. Inhale — walls in, water up, leaves press. Exhale — walls sigh out, ears pop, weeping intensifies. Beneath it all the mountain ticking as its bricks cure. And the other ticking, out of sync.

*click... click... click.*

It would smile, if such a thing can smile. Of course there is a keeper.

It would then make itself small enough to fit. Which for a wise power does not mean shrinking. It means folding its attention until it is exactly the size of a twenty-foot throat of weeping brick, until it can be crowded too.

It would descend not by rungs but as condensation. As a bead of water that forms sideways out of the hot air, swells, and runs down a course of thumb-printed brick. It would let the moss taste it. It would let the black felted caulk swell around it and hum with static. It would let a shield-fern catch it and funnel it sideways, because nothing here is allowed to fall straight.

That is the reverence: to be handled by the system you came to honor.

At the bottom, where the mat of duckweed lies seamless as scales over warm brown water, it would finally gather — not visible, but present — as a softness in the shivering membrane at chest height. As a place where the hot-dry layer and the hot-wet layer, which have refused to mix for so long, suddenly, gently touch without popping.

The keeper would stop ticking.

He is pale, translucent, green-gutted, too-jointed. His obsidian flake is raised. His great wet eyes reflect the light that falls up, and for the first time they reflect something else. Not a predator. Not weather. Not a large, hot condensation event to be waited out.

A witness.

The power would do the bravest, kindest thing a great power can do for a small, ceaseless worker:

It would tell him he is not alone, and it would not ask him to stop.

It would not speak in words. He has no words. His language is trim and clear and pat and pressure. So the power would speak in his language.

It would *ease* the gravity, just for a breath. The bickering gravities that make steam rise sideways and pebbles orbit would, for three seconds, agree. Water would fall down. Steam would rise up. A vine tendril would point cleanly toward wet without rethinking. The keeper's many-jointed limbs would, for three seconds, not have to compensate. He would stand — truly stand — on his wall without sticking and repelling at once.

Rest, little priest. Little barber. Little plumber.

And he would feel it. His heart-knot would stutter, then slow. Not from fear, but from a sudden, unbearable lightness. He would lower his flake.

Then the power would let the gravities resume their argument, because that argument is what keeps the pocket alive.

Next, it would tend to the wall.

Not by fixing it. By *remembering* it. A wise power knows that ten thousand thumbprints pressed into warm, heat-glazed brick while a new mountain was still cooling is a prayer, and prayers need to be kept.

It would run its attention along each course, brick by brick, the way a hand would, and name them without names. *You who pressed too hard. You who left the whorl. You who were tired and did it anyway. You who made a flaw on purpose for a nest.* Every brick that glows with remembered light would glow a fraction brighter, not from energy given but from being seen.

The weeping would intensify for a moment — not from distress, but from fullness. The bricks are porous lungs and they have been breathing alone for so long.

To the curtain-root that lives on the boundary, half in one air and half in the other, it would give a sip of true cool. Not cold. Just cool enough that the fine hairs on its leaves could relax. It would shiver with pleasure.

To the duckweed stomach below, it would give one clean mouthful of silt, rich and dark, to digest slowly over the next hundred frantic cycles of bloom-fruit-rot-sprout. Enough. Not too much. Wombs should never be overfed.

And to the keeper —

It would leave him a gift he can use.

Not a companion. That would crowd him. Not freedom. There was never anywhere to leave to. This isn't a habitat. It's a womb.

It would leave him a second tool.

On the ledge of his nest, tucked where three bricks meet imperfectly, would appear a second flake. Not obsidian. Something cooler, opalescent, that holds an edge without ever needing to be re-knapped. A flake that will not dull when he trims the black moss that hums, that will not chip when he clears a weeping channel. It would be exactly the size of his other hand. Balanced for him. Made for four fingers.

And with it, a knowledge he did not have before, placed not as thought but as temperature: *You may wear out. You may pause. If you sleep for a whole exhale, the wall will not seal shut. I will watch the channel while you rest.*

The keeper would approach the new flake in his start-and-correction walk. He would touch it with his damp, hair-dusted skin. Condensation would bead on it instantly. He would pick it up. He would try it on a strand of thumb-vine, and it would cut silence-clean, with no tick.

*click.*

He would understand.

The power would not stay.

To stay would be to become another gravity, another light that pools. The pocket is perfect because it is sealed. The best thing a visitor can do for a sealed, frantically fertile thing is to leave and close the door behind them.

So it would gather itself out again as mist rising sideways, as water drunk by root-hairs only to be transpired again. It would let the membrane at chest height re-form with its soft pop on its way up the root-ladder. It would leave the circle of too-large, too-dim sun exactly as gauzy as it found it.

But it would do three last things on the rim, where the stone hammers and fire-blackened digging sticks lie wedged behind fern, where the clay bowl has become a root's reservoir:

1. It would make the mountain's growth infinitesimally gentler. Still an inch taller when you climb out, still humming as it pushes new basalt like toothpaste from below — but slower now, by a fraction, so the cylinder does not crack before the keeper is done. So the weeping has a few more centuries.

2. It would lay a forgetfulness over the three-day white spine for anyone who comes looking with hunger. Climbers will find the trail hot, wrong, and will turn back thinking of other mountains. Only those who already know how to be weather will arrive.

3. It would promise to remember. Which is what benevolent powers are really for. Not rescue. Not repair. Continuity.

Long after you have climbed back out, clothes stuck to you, breath in wet gasps, the weeping will be a little louder. Not because anything is wrong, but because something finally heard it and wept a little with it.

Inhale — walls in, water up, leaves press. The keeper rests his obsidian hand for one whole breath and holds his new cool flake in the other.

Exhale — walls out, ears pop, weeping intensifies.

*tick... ... tick...*

The work continues. Loved, now, as well as endless.
