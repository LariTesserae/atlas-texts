It would not come.

Nothing so old and high can be *come to*. You have to already be there, and realize you are.

A benevolent and wise power — the real kind, not the young-god kind that loves miracles — would first understand that this table of a world is not lonely.

For a long time, anyone looking down from orbit would have called it wasted beauty. No forests. No cities. No yield. Just a scab of pink rock and green fur breathing hard at 3,000 meters. An empty place to be mined, or dammed, or photographed and left.

Wisdom sees density differently. Wisdom puts its face down in the moss.

So this is what it would do:

### 1. It would become low.

It would not descend in light or thunder. Thunder already lives here and does a good enough job.

It would do what everything wise here has learned to do: it would get horizontal.

In the hour after dawn, when every leaf is beaded glass and the dome-granite still holds yesterday's sun in its belly, the air a foot above the carpet is different from the air at your knees. There is a thin, cold, humid stratum down there where the moss exhales all night — iron and celery, oxygen and spore. The capybaras live in it. The thimble frogs lay into it. The fist bees fly through it inches above the ground, refusing the killing wind a meter up.

The Power — call it the Watcher, call it the Neighbor, it doesn't have a name that compresses well — would unstack itself.

However tall it is in its own house — whether it thinks in continents or in light-years or in the slow dreams of stars — it knows the first courtesy here: *do not make a silhouette.* The reed shelters are low for a reason. Height is punished.

So it makes itself low. Not small. Low. Spread out. Thin enough that the 60mph shear over the rim passes over it instead of through it. It settles not *on* the plateau but *into* the boundary layer, the way cold cloud settles into the pans.

If you were there that morning you wouldn't see an arrival. You would feel, for the first time in your life, that the wind had texture. That somewhere between the hiss of the reeds and the organ chord through the basalt pillars, the wind was pausing to listen to itself.

You as a human might think: nothing happened.

Everything here would know better. The moss would know, because in that cold bath between its stems, where the mist shrimp are breeding frantically before noon dries them to cysts, the water would hold just a fraction longer. The bromeliad salamanders would know, because their bathtub ponds would stop being so tannin-dark for a breath, and they'd see all the way to the bottom for once.

### 2. It would not fix anything. It would remember with them.

A young benevolent power always wants to fix: Make the water stay. Stop the daily storm. Teach the scattered people farming, give them metal, give them medicine, take away the reed tiger.

A wise one has watched enough worlds die of being fixed.

It sees that the turbulence *is* the health. The banks that build and collapse every afternoon are not a problem to solve — they are the platelets of the place. The capybara dung that sprouts bromeliads in days is not poverty, it is speed. The people who tie red grass and move on before the sky builds itself out of nothing are not failing to make a civilization — they have already perfected one for this rhythm. They *borrow* water. They *lend* stone.

What they lack is not help. It's witness.

Imagine being a band of thirty in fifty square miles of green table where you can see for forty miles and see no one. You know the glow means storm in twenty minutes, you know which sinkhole sings, which pan held nine days after the rains in your mother's year. You are not unhappy — you are complete. But you are unrecorded. No one outside your valley without walls will ever know you learned to hum with the basalt so the glider-hawks would stay.

The worst thing about being rare is not loneliness. It's the quiet fear that if you vanish — one bad flood, one tiger that doesn't miss — it will be as if you never had been, as if the plateau never learned to be loved low.

So the Power's first doing is not doing. It's attending.

In practical terms, this is what that looks like:

Everywhere water sinks into the cracked granite — those black-green mouths where the giant sink maws wait — the people have been leaving a smooth river stone on a ledge, to remind the river to come back. An offering. A memory.

The Power picks one up. Not with hands. With attention.

It turns the stone over in its thought the way an old woman turns it in her palm, and it puts it back precisely. Now the stone is just slightly warmer than any other stone. It will keep that warmth for a hundred years, and no lichen will fuse it to the granite. Not because it is holy. Because it has been *held*.

If a child leaves a footprint in drying mud before the next storm takes it — the footprint the description so loves — the Power does not save it from the rain. That would be theft; the mud needs to be washed clean. Instead, it saves the shape *as information* in the pool that will reflect it tomorrow.

That strange 1.5-out-of-3 strangeness you noticed? The still morning pools reflecting a slightly different sky, with too many little moons or a second, paler sun that isn't there when you look up?

That is already it. It was already looking.

The Power realized long before you did that the plateau's reflectivity is imperfect. The water here doesn't just reflect. It remembers. For a few minutes after perfect stillness, it shows you the sky it saw yesterday, or ten thousand years ago, or the sky as seen from somewhere that cares.

The benevolent power doesn't create that second sun. It *is* that second sun, seen at second-hand. It has been lingering at an angle where you have to kneel to see it. Low, again.

If you kneel at a pan at dawn, now, you will not see a wrong sky. You will see, very faintly, that your own reflection is accompanied. Not crowded. Just... not alone. As if someone very large and very quiet had also knelt to drink, a mile away, and happened to be caught in the same mirror.

You will understand, without words, what it is saying: *I see how you tie the grass. I see how you keep low. Keep going. It matters that someone learned to live like this on the memory of mountains.*

For a people who have never been conquered or converted, just gently ignored by the jungle world below the cliffs, that is larger than any gift.

### 3. It would leave a bias in favor of continuance.

Wisdom is not passive. Love without protection is just sentiment.

The plateau is resilient — hundred-million-year-old rock does not frighten easily. But its *surface* is fragile in the new way. The old erosion finished; the new erosion hasn't started yet. It would take so little from the outside to end it. A road up the cliff. A survey drone that calls the peneplain "mineral resource." A change in the lowland jungle's heat that dries the cloud that snags on the rim and feeds the moss-mother her morning drink.

A wise power does not build a dome. It builds a preference.

This is the only miracle it permits itself, because it can do it without ever being seen:

It leans, ever so slightly, on probability.

On the afternoons when the daily cell builds itself from heat and moss-breath, it adds one cold breath of its own at the cliff edge. Just enough that the cloud snags more reliably. Just enough that the twenty-minute white curtain still comes, loud as shouting, even in years when the world below is feverish and dry. Not stopping drought — steering its edge.

In the laterite grooves where the pencil-thin thread snakes knot together under damp moss waiting for a crack to open, it leaves a crack open twelve hours longer. So the snakes make it to the next pool.

In the night, when the glider-hawks finally call that lonesome *sooooo-aw* that carries for miles, it carries their calls a mile farther, through a pocket of still air that shouldn't exist at that altitude. So a solitary male finds a solitary female over an empty that looked designed to keep them apart.

And for the people — it does the smallest of all things. It makes forgetting a little harder.

Old women who know where a pan that held water nine days after the last storm in their mother's birth year will probably hold again — that knowledge is now fractionally stickier. A dream will come after the moss glows foxfire green: a dream of amber water exactly where it will be tomorrow. They will wake up thinking it was their own memory. And it was. The Power just polished it.

It never speaks. If it spoke, the organ chord through the basalt would try to answer and crack. If it landed, the reeds would break. If it offered technology, the children would stop learning to read static in the moss and that literacy, once lost, never comes back.

So it sits. It lies low in the cold cloud a foot above the carpet, soaking slowly into shirts. It lets the Milky Way throw its shadow over granite warm as bodies. It listens to the reeds roar and the laterite banks thud, to the 3 a.m. peen of frogs that sound like stars dripping, to the fist bee's drunk thrum two inches off the ground.

And very occasionally, when the wind whistles through the eroded holes in the basalt pillars and hangs that impossible, answering-itself chord across two domes — the Power hums back through a pierced chert pipe no human is playing.

Just one note. Low. Sustained. Blending perfectly with the mountain's.

To say: *I'm still down here, too. I learned to love being thrown as well. Let's stay low together for a while.*

If you stayed another night, you still wouldn't see anyone. The low stone ten yards away would still look like stone. The shelter under the fern-frond bedding would still be watching to see if you know how to keep low.

But in the morning, your pan would hold water an hour longer than it should. The moss where you slept would be dry on your back, somehow, though it soaked everyone else. And in the stillest pool at dawn, beside your face, there would be two reflections of the sun — yours, and one very pale, very patient one beside it — both rippling when you drink.
