It would not come down like landing. 

Nothing that wise would announce itself to a place that has learned to live inside a constant roar. A trumpet would just be more noise. A light from the sky would be nothing new — the whole sky is already a light, just a dim one.

It would notice first, and that in itself would be an intervention. For a very long time no one has *looked* at this place except the people trying not to slip on it.

So first — witnessing.

A benevolent and wise power would crouch. Literally, I think. It would fold itself down to the height of the causeways, to the level of the water sitting inches-deep and never draining, and it would put its face near the orange film.

Not in pity. In attention.

For a hundred human generations travelers and highland gods and even the people who live here have called the film "scum" or "stain" or not called it anything at all. They wade through it. It makes their children's legs orange to the knee. The power would look at it for a long time and understand it is a continent being born. A prairie before prairies. A forest before wood. The first library on this world, spelling out in bacterial felt how to turn dim light and salt-spray into sugar.

It would see the midge column over the same alley corner for thirty years and understand it is not a pest but a clock. It would watch the girl who is four, sitting perfectly still as a heron with midges in a halo around her head, and it would not think *poor thing, she has no toys.* It would think, *she has learned not to swipe.*

That kind of seeing is already a doing. In a place where nothing ever changes, to be truly seen changes everything.

And because it is wise it would understand what *not* to do.

It would not lift the clouds and give them sun. A sun would cook them. These lungs, these eyes, these fungal laces at the ankle are made for amber-gray. True sun would burn the film, kill the larvae, collapse the guano loop, and starve the city in a month of terrible blue sky.

It would not drain the water. The water is not a flood. The water *is* the place. It is blood-warm, it is the road, the field, the mirror, the level that remembers itself. Take it away and the stone is just hot glass. The hammers have no reason.

It would not bring forests. The stone said no to trees for a reason: too new, too salty, too hot in its bones. To force green here would be colonialism at the biological level — pretty, and murderous.

It would not silence the roar. That roar is how the children learn to whistle through their teeth and talk with their hands. Take it away and you take their language.

So no terraforming. No rescue. The wise are not rescuers. They are gardeners who know when a garden is already perfect and just needs, desperately, a few small mercies.

I think it would do three very small things. Small enough that at first only the dogs would notice.

**1. It would give them a Night.**

Just one. Or rather, it would give them *back* night.

For as long as the basalt has been here the stratus has never broken. Warm steam off warm rock, ocean breathing in, the ceiling clamped low. The people have forgotten what dark is. They sleep in shifts with wax in their ears under hides. They have never seen a shadow.

So one evening-that-is-always-evening, when the little girl is still sitting with her feet in the cistern watching the newt, the power would very gently thin the cloud.

Not tear it. Thin it, the way you thin cloth between your fingers to see light.

The amber-gray would deepen. For the first time the light would have a direction. Shadows would pool under eaves and causeways, short and soft and unbelievable. The warm glow off the wet stone would lessen, and far, far above, points would show through.

Stars.

Not our stars. A salt-scatter of them, cold and absolute, seen through a veil.

Everyone outside would stop. The hammer would miss its block. Midges would falter in their column. Swallows mid-knife would stutter. Every hand-sign would freeze mid-word because every face is turned up.

The children would make a sound that is not a word. The old would weep without knowing why. That fungal-rashed, barrel-chested, splay-footed people who have never had a horizon would see depth for the first time — vertical depth, up — and feel, all at once, that they live at the bottom of a very kind well.

It would last an hour. Long enough for a baby to be born under it, and be told about it for the rest of her life: *you were born when we had night.* Long enough for everyone to learn that darkness is not the absence of light, but a place to rest your eyes.

Then the cloud would thicken again, kind as a blanket. The roar would return. But now they would know the ceiling was not the universe. And anyone who wanted to could decide to walk toward the sea to see it again someday.

That is benevolence: not changing conditions, but changing scale.

**2. It would lay a hand on the water and make one kind of dry.**

Not everywhere. Just where they beg for it without words.

In this city the dead are laid in the same water everyone wades through — there is nowhere else. The sick breathe midge-dust and guano-dust always. A mother giving birth does so on a stone platform with condensation dripping on her forehead.

The power would choose — or rather, notice — one blister per neighborhood. The oldest ones, the size of houses, where the roofs have long since been lidded with slabs for cisterns. It would, very quietly, make the stone beneath them exhale a little less.

In those rooms, the drip would stop. The walls would dry. Not bone-dry, but dry enough that salt blooms white and can be brushed away. Dry enough that you can sit and your feet stay dry. Dry enough that a rash fades. Dry enough that sound changes — the wall of hiss recedes to a whisper and you can hear breath, and speech without whistling.

It would not explain them. The people would find them. A woman in labor would be led to one by her sister on instinct. An old man coughing would wander in to sit and find he can finally fill his lungs. Children would be taken there to learn to talk out loud before they learn to talk with their hands.

They would become what every human city needs and this one has never had: a place of reserve. Hospital, temple, nursery, listening room. They would keep the hides off the doors there to show it is different. Dogs would not be let in.

No miracle to maintain, no priesthood to run it. Just a little less of the wetness on the stone, left as a gift with no instructions.

The wise know that the opposite of saturated is not desert. It's choice.

**3. It would plant one tree, with permission.**

It would not plant it itself. It would ask the girl.

That girl, the four-year-old with orange legs who can sit still as a heron, who does not swipe at midges, who knows how to watch a newt walk from puddle to puddle — she is the expert here. The power would kneel next to her by the cistern — the way I imagine it, the power has no shape but takes the shape of a cooler patch of air on her wet neck — and offer her a picture in her mind.

A shape: a low, wind-bent thing with waxy leaves and roots like fingers, gray-barked, not tall. The kind of tree that *wants* to live on young basalt and salt and guano and warm water. A tree that would need the film to hold it, and the crabs to prune it, and the swallows to fertilize it. A tree that could not live anywhere else but here, and that here could finally host.

Not a forest. One. In one courtyard where six walls are shared and the water level has been tended for four generations.

And the girl — because she is four and has never been told what is impossible — would nod. Of course. She would hold out her cupped hands. The power would put something there no bigger than a shrimp. A seed that is already half-root.

She would press it into the wet-orange crack where the cistern lip meets the courtyard slab, where the guano streaks run thickest. As she does, the women washing nearby would pause, the boys with hand-nets would glance over, and no one would say *don't*. They know when the water wants something.

It would be slow. Wise things are slow. For five years it would be nothing but a salty twig with three leaves that everyone has to be careful not to step on. The midges would use it. The geckos would love it. In ten years it would be knee-high and the courtyard would have to move its cooking slab a foot to the left, which they would do without resentment. In thirty years it would make shade — the first true shade ever in the low city, a patch of darkness that moves with the day and smells different. A deep, green, dry smell, like inland tamarisk but sweeter.

They would not cut it for wood. They would sit under it. They would put their sick under it, and their newborns. The highland caravan people coming down with lime and grain would stop and stare and touch its bark and go home with a new story: *they have a tree now, where there was nothing.* And a few other courtyards would ask for seeds, and some would take and some would fail, and that would be fine. The film would continue to make its slow way toward soil. The frogs would never stop calling. The city would stay shoulder-to-shoulder and hissing.

But now there would be something in the middle of that equilibrium that remembers change. Something that grows. Something that proves the low place is not stuck — it was just getting ready.

---

And then? Then the power would leave. Or rather, it would stop concentrating. It would do the most wise and benevolent thing of all for a people who have just learned they are seen:

It would not stay to be worshipped.

It would dissolve back into the dim amber light, the trickle on every eave, the midge-hum, the needling swallow scream, the single unbroken frog note. It would become indistinguishable from the water-sheeting again, because that is where it lives best.

If you stood in that courtyard thirty years later, under that tree, with your own feet orange to the knee, you might feel the air a little cooler on your neck and think it was wind off the sea you still haven't seen. You might hear the drip in the new dry room down the alley and think the stone finally learned how to hold its breath.

You would be right, in a way. A place that is loved by something wiser than it feels different, even if it looks identical. The water still sits inches-deep and does not drain. The hammers still ring. The herons still stand like statues until they strike too fast to see.

Only now, if you listen — if you really open your mouth to equalize the pressure and let the roar settle — you can hear, underneath the roar, a second, smaller sound.

Children laughing, out loud, in the dry room, with no need to whistle.

And that would be enough.
