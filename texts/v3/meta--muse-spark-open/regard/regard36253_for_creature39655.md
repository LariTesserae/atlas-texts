They would not bring the sun.

That is the first thing to understand about a wise and benevolent power. The first mercy is restraint.

Any lesser power — a bright, eager, sentimental god — would take one look at this low place, this unending dusk, this deafening clicking carpet, and feel pity. It would try to *fix* it.

It would tear the cloud-roof open. It would pour down light. It would seed it with grass and pine and make something green live where nothing green was ever meant to. It would quiet the screaming fliers because it finds their screaming ugly. It would give the Grazers eyes. It would be called Love, and it would be extinction within a week.

A truly wise power has seen enough worlds to know: darkness is not always an absence. Here, it is a condition. It is home.

This place is not broken. It is *complete*.

It is old, and negotiated, and in balance. The Varnish has learned how to eat stone. The Grazer has learned how to eat Varnish. The Picker has learned how to steal from the Grazer without killing it. The Sack has learned how to wring water from mist and give it back clean. The Flier has learned how to live an entire furious life in an hour and die screaming and be taken apart in minutes. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is waiting to be saved.

A wise power would kneel to that. Literally.

### What It Would Do

It would come low.

It would not arrive as light from above — there is no above here that matters. It would condense the way water does here: out of the air itself, close to the ground. Cool. Damp. At elevation.

If you were standing there, you would not see it arrive. You would feel, for a moment, that the roar had softened — not stopped, but taken a breath. The thrumming of the Sacks deepening for a heartbeat. The fliers holding their screaming for one second longer than usual between dives, as if listening to something else.

And then you would realize the mist was beading a little faster. That the pan of clear water in the basalt was holding perfectly still, not drinking itself away.

It would have made itself small enough to be here without displacing anyone. Not human-sized. Grazer-sized. Varnish-thin. Terrain.

It would not speak. There is nothing to say that would not be noise. Instead it would *listen* at the scale of this place.

A benevolent power listens the way the basalt listens: with its whole body.

It would lay a hand — if it has hands — on one of the humming ribs, and it would not try to stop the hum or tune it. It would add its own resonance, very quietly, underneath. A second tone, so low you feel it in your sternum more than hear it. Not louder than the stone. *With* the stone.

And for a little while, the plain would hum in harmony with itself.

What would that do? Nothing you could photograph. Everything that matters.

The Grazers would pause in their endless rasping march. Not in fear. They do not know fear the way we do. In attention. Their pale under-mouths would lift from the stone for the first time in thousands of years. The Pickers would go still on their stilt-legs, their translucent guts pulsing in time. Even the Fliers would fold, cling to the lee of the ribs, and be silent — and for once that silence would not be the tense silence before rain, but a resting silence. A held breath.

The Varnish, when it glowed, would not glow blue-green with alarm. It would glow amber. Gold. The color of the jelly when it is well-fed and undisturbed, but brighter. And the light would travel, ripple after ripple, for kilometers, farther than any touch has ever carried, until the whole basin was faintly luminous — a low, wet constellation under the cloud.

The plain would see itself. For one night that has never been night, it would be visible to itself.

That is what a benevolent power does for a place that is already whole: it *witnesses* it so fully that the place can, for a moment, witness itself. To be entirely perceived by something that does not want to change you — that is the rarest thing in any universe.

It would stay there a long time. Longer than you stayed. Longer than you could stand to stand still while things walked over your boots. It would stay through seven-minute rains and through mist-condensations, through the growing of a millimeter of crystal. Through the falling and dismantling of ten thousand Fliers. It would learn the ten-thousand-year path the Grazers wear into the basalt.

And it would do one other thing, because benevolence is not only contemplation. It is protection.

Not from itself. From others.

This low, mineral world is rich in a way that hungry, clever things with ships and drills would call valuable. Iron. Rare salts. That silence that could be mined. The temptation to call it "barren" and therefore "available" would be great.

The wise power would do nothing dramatic. No flaming sword. No wall. It would just make this place *uninteresting* to those kinds of eyes. A slight bend in attention. A note in the mind of any prospector that says *there is nothing there worth the fuel*. A cloud that, from orbit, looks a little thicker, a little more uniform, a little less worth scanning than the crater next to it.

It would be a very gentle, very firm *no*. The way a mother shifts her wing to cover a nest without waking what’s inside.

That’s all.

When it left — the way mist leaves, by becoming invisible rather than by going somewhere — there would be no monument. No shining crystal left as a gift. Gifts that remain become relics, and relics become shrines, and shrines get trampled.

There would only be this:

For a few hours after, the water in the pans would stay clear a little longer before the stone drank it. The Varnish would regrow a little faster over the bright scrape-marks. A Picker would pause on your boot — or on the boot of whoever next finds this place, centuries from now — and taste the salt, and stay a second longer, not disappointed, just... curious.

And deep in the iron ribs, the hum would remember the second tone. Not as an echo, but as a knowledge: that the frequency it made when the wind was right was once answered. That it was heard. Not used. Not harvested. Heard.

The cloud would still be low. The light would still be borrowed. The roar would return, deafening and perfect. No sun would ever get through.

And the plain, damp and dense and cold, would go on being exactly what it is — only no longer alone in being it.

That is what love does when it is wise enough to be low to the ground.
