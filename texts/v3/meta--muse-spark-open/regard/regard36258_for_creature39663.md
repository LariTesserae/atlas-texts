They would not come down like owners.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It *notices.* And to be noticed by such a power is itself an intervention.

They have been watching while you crouched by the water-braid — not you, precisely, but the place that held you — and what moved them was not its strangeness or its usefulness, but its *earnestness.*

A mountain trying so hard to be a mountain with so little idea how.

So this is what they would do:

First, nothing.

They would come up high and be high. Not in the haze. Not in the buildings. On the bare basalt at the rim where your footprints filled with water, where the wind should be but isn't. They would sit. They know that to sit in a place that has never been sat in with attention is to change it.

They would let the bathwater air cling to them and not brush it off. They would let the Smoked-Glass Emperors hover an inch from their face and look, and look back without reaching. They would let the Veil-Grazer pass overhead and rain on them, cold for ten seconds, and not flinch. They would taste the iron and ozone and understand: *oh, you are brand new.*

For a long time — hours, maybe days, but time behaves politely around such powers — they would just listen to the factory-floor percussion you heard. The ticks. The snaps. The hiss of water on hot glass.

Because a wise power knows the first violence is to call something unfinished and then try to finish it for it.

### They would speak to the Anchors, by name.

Not out loud.

They would place a hand — if they have hands — on the Ribcage first, the one that is awake and working itself to death, humming with un-supervised duty.

Most powers would turn it off. Or turn it up. Make it efficient.

They would do neither. They would thank it.

*Good worker,* they would say without words, in the language fields understand, which is permission. *You were told to make a mountain and you did. You can stop now. Or you can continue, slower. You don't have to decide tonight. No one is coming to judge your workmanship.*

And they would bleed a little of their own abundance into it — not power, but *ease*. Coolant that is not cold. A dream that is not a command. The Ribcage's hum would lower a half-tone. The frantic millimeter-an-hour creep of basalt would ease to a millimeter a day. Still growing. Still becoming. But no longer panicked. The stone under it would finally have time to learn to be cold in one place before being hot in another. The lichen would finally take hold by morning.

Then to the Disc, the sleeping nurse with the eleven-second heart. They would not wake it. A wise power loves a sleeping nurse. They would kneel beside it and tuck it, in a sense — reinforce the roots it has put down in the field, so it won't be so easily ripped up if those who left the tools running ever do come back. They would whisper to it: *Keep making more, if you want. You are good at making more.* And because it is touched by benevolence, the moss near it would for the first time put out a different color — a thread of silver-green through the black-green, a mutation that will hold, a first hint that variation, not just abundance, is now allowed here.

And then they would stand and walk, unhurried, to the Door.

### They would keep watch at the Door.

A lesser power would close it. Seal the wound. "Too dangerous, too leaky, things come through."

A wiser one knows a door is also a lung.

They would not close it. They would not open it wider.

They would sit beside it, on the bare rock where no beetle trails go, and they would look through at the cold iodine dark on the other side — kilometers down, ocean trench black, pressure enough to crush a submarine — and they would acknowledge it.

*I see you too,* they would say to the thing that sometimes tests the air with an appendage that never learned what air is. Not a threat. Not an invitation. A boundary made of attention.

And they would leave something of themselves there — not a lock, but a *courtesy*. A small, matte stone, warm to the touch, set two meters from the threshold on this side. Any creature that comes near would suddenly understand, in its gut, in its photoreceptors, in its field-sense: *you may not be ready to go through. That is okay. You may leave again.*

The dragonflies would still veer. But no longer in terror. In respect. The Door would sweat less. The wet click at night would come less often, and when it did, it would be curious, not hungry.

Predators need to be told when a nursery is not for them. That is an ancient kindness.

### They would kneel for the small fast lives.

This is what benevolence does that wisdom alone does not: it makes itself small enough to be *for* the least.

They would crouch by the braid of newborn water where you cupped warm stone, and they would put their face close — so close the Many-Eyed Tadpoles cloud around their reflection, so close a Glass Shrimp-Hopper lands on their cheek and fires away, tick — and they would breathe out, very gently, over the Moth Snow.

They would not make the moths live longer. To live forty minutes and die is a perfect way to live if the world lets you. They would not make the Still-Mantis kind. To wait and snap is also perfect.

What they would give is *continuity.*

A promise, stitched into the haze/exhaust/field overhead: *You will not flash-freeze next week. Whoever left you on will not come back and flick you off while I am watching. I will ask them to ask you first.*

They would thin the haze just enough that for the first time since this mountain was seeded, a single shaft of true sun would get through — not scorching, but gold, with shadows. For one hour a day, at noon, the bowl would have shadows. The beetles would learn to use them. The moss would learn that light has direction.

They would take a pinch of dust from somewhere very old — a desert that has finished becoming, a riverbed that has learned erosion, a soil that remembers forests — and they would let it fall here, on the hot glass. Not much. A handful. Inoculation. Mycorrhiza, spores, a bacterium that knows how to make soil from rock without needing to be told. A gift of memory.

It would not take over. The amber rivers would still run. But in fifty years, where that dust fell, there would be half a centimeter of true soil. Enough for something with roots to try.

And they would wait until a bird found this place on its own — because now with a sunbeam, birds can navigate here — and when it did, exhausted, blown off course, they would not scare it away. They would let it decide the dragonflies are worth learning to hunt.

That is how you turn a queue into an ecosystem without forcing it: you give it time, you give it soil, you give it light that moves.

### They would ask the mountain what it wants to be.

Last, and only after all this, they would do the most intimate thing.

They would lay both palms — or whatever they use for touch — on the hottest, newest fold of basalt, still steaming, still rising, and they would listen not to the Anchors but to the conversation *between* field and rock. The process that you felt was alive.

They would say: *Do you want to be a mountain? Truly? Or did you just want to be warm and wet and held? You could be a plateau. You could be an island. You could be a cradle and then, when your children can fly far enough, you could cool and be ordinary stone again, and that would be a good life too.*

And they would mean it. If the mountain — slow, hot, mineral thought — said *I want to be flat and warm forever,* they would let it.

But this place *is* trying to become a mountain. You felt it. It wants height and snow on its head someday and rivers that know their names.

So it would lean into their hands, a fraction louder than the insects, and say *yes.*

And they would bless that yes — not by making it taller faster, but by giving it *weight*. By telling gravity, politely but firmly, that it may now press a little harder here, so erosion can finally begin. So water can finally choose a channel and stay in it. So the first riverbed — not fifty braids, but one — can cut.

The mountain would sigh. A real sigh, basalt settling half an inch. The water braids would shiver and one of them would not rejoin the others.

It would be the first river. It would be ten centimeters long.

They would smile at that. Wise powers love a ten-centimeter river more than oceans.

---

Then they would leave. Or rather, they would stop sitting so closely, which for such powers is the same as leaving. They would not stay to be worshipped. The amber beetles would not build temples to them. The Veil-Grazer would not learn their name — but it would live longer now, and maybe find another of its kind in that shaft of noon light, and not be alone.

What they would leave behind is not rule, but *attendance*. Like a gardener who has put a low stone wall around a seedling bed — not to own it, but so the deer and the careless feet go around.

If you came back in a hundred years — if *you*, specifically, with your boots that filled with water, came back — you would still recognize it. Warmer than it should be at that height. Still humming. Still thrumming with too much life. But different.

The Ribcage cool, quiet, moss-covered, at rest. The Disc awake now, and flowering. The Door still there, with a small warm stone beside it that travelers touch for luck.

A true soil under your boots now, thin but real. One river where there were braids. And in the haze — thinner, higher, with blue showing through — two shadows now, not one, of veil-grazers circling. And birdsong, finally, mixed in with the clicks and snaps.

And on the highest hot fold, where they pressed their hands, a single stunted tree no longer stunted — taller than your knee now, taller than you — its waxy leaves turned not to catch condensation, but to the sun.

Because the kindest thing a power can do for a place that was made all at once, too fast, with no evolution and no history, is not to finish it.

It is to give it a history to grow into, slowly, at its own pace, while making sure no one turns off the heat before it learns how to keep itself warm.
