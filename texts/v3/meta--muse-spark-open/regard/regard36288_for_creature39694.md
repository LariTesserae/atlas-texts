It would not come down like a star.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. There is no shaft of light that the cloud has to part for. No voice. That would be a damming, a clearing — the exact two things this place has learned over centuries not to do. A wise power knows that first.

So first, it would stop at the rim.

Not out of reverence, though it is reverent. Out of literacy. It would read the bowl the way the Rememberers read a braid-pattern.

It would set its great, soft weight on the eastern lip where the cloud snags, and let the cloud snag on it instead. For a long time — a morning, a season, a year, time is different for powers — it would just sit there and get wet.

It understands immediately what it has found. This is not a village in need of saving. Not a pure pristine wilderness to preserve under glass. It’s rarer than either: a *settlement*.

Not an establishment, not a conquest, not a colony. A settlement in the old, true sense. Something that has settled. Like sediment has settled, like a joint has settled, like an argument settled between an old couple who still bicker about mushrooms but no longer consider divorce.

The basalt weeps. The cedars fuse. The people weave. The korri heckle. The frogs hold the night air up with their throats. The moss erases hubris. It all holds. It is loud and crowded and damp and short on privacy and absolutely correct.

A lesser power, even a kind one, would be tempted to help. To fix.
To warm it up a little, because the children's feet are cold.
To quiet the frogs so people can sleep.
To straighten the walls, drain the mere, give them a proper mill so they don't have to pump water by hand.
To cure the coughs you hear through the bark-paper at night.

This one does none of that.

Because it is wise, it knows: the cold is why the cedar grows so slow and strong. The noise is how they know they are not alone. The damp is the contract. The cough is part of the brief, bright bargain of lungs in thin, wet air. To take those away would be to take the knot apart to save the threads.

So what does it do?

**It does as the bowl does. It drips.**

1. It matches its tempo

For a while — a long while — it simply joins the water cycle. It condenses on the needles with the cloud. It threads through the bedding planes of the basalt and comes out a pinhole seep, cold, clear. It drips from a myrtle leaf onto a child's braid where a fern is already sprouting, and the child does not notice, which is as it should be.

It learns the pump rhythm under the great cedar. Not to improve it. To keep time with it, so that the very old salamander under the anvil feels, for the first time in thirty years, that the room is agreeably in tune and settles deeper into contentment.

Powers like this do not bless. They attune.

2. It protects without enclosing

The danger to a place like this was never inside. A knot this tight does not unravel itself. Someone last year forgot the moss and grew a fungus on their bed. They were reminded. Balance restored.

Danger comes from outside, from those who see from a distance not a thicket with smoke but *unclaimed* timber, *unowned* water, *undeveloped* land. A surveyor with a map. A priest with a better God. A state with a road.

So the power does a very small, very deliberate thing: it makes the bowl boring.

Not invisible. Invisibility draws curiosity. It makes it *unremarkable* to the wrong eyes. To the logger on the lower slopes, the rim just looks a little steeper than it's worth, the cloud a little wetter. The basalt assays a little poorer than the next ridge over. The path that would have led up there, if you were looking for it, is always just slightly overgrown — not walled off, just... not inviting. A place you decide, without knowing why, to go around.

To those who would love it for the right reasons — a lost hiker with cold hands, a wandering botanist who knows how to ask a tree before taking a cutting, a bear following an old route — the path opens again. A break in the myrtle. The smell of smoke.

This is the oldest protection. The Rememberers call it something else. “Where’s your moss?” they say, when someone takes a stone. The power is just laying moss on the idea of the bowl itself.

3. It answers the small prayer no one says aloud

Every person there has the same quiet, impolite worry they never voice because to voice it would be to invite insult: *What if we finally ask too much?*

One more terrace cut. One more child born. One more winter where the swifts are late. How close are we to the edge?

The power does not give them infinite abundance. That would rot them faster than any mold. It gives them slack.

It goes down into the mycorrhizal dark under the cistern-mere, where the cedar roots and myrtle roots and mushroom hyphae have braided for centuries into a mat as thick as the village above. It feeds it, just a little. Not fertilizer — *attention*. It remembers the network back to itself. Where a root-gap has started to fray, it encourages a new bridge. Where the perpetual wet has leached a pocket of soil too acidic, it buffers it with a sigh of mineral.

The result is not miraculous. No tree doubles in size. It’s that next year the myrtle hives swarm *once* instead of twice, and the fungus in the boot appears a day later, giving you time to turn it. That a coppiced wall that should have blown out in the spring wind holds for one more season, long enough for the new withes to lignify.

No one notices. Except maybe one Rememberer, an old woman who knows the rhythm of the stones, who frowns at her wall and says, “Huh. Held.” And taps it with her knuckle, and goes on nagging someone about moss.

That’s enough.

4. It accepts hospitality on their terms

After a long time of dripping and buffering and making-boring, it would do the one thing a truly benevolent power must do if it wants to remain benevolent: make itself small enough to be hosted.

It would come down off the rim on a morning when the cloud is sitting like milk, and take a shape that can be cold. A woman with wide hips for ladders. Or a marten. Or a white-collared korri that knows how to say “look out below.”

It would pad across a high bridge at 2 a.m., claws clicking, or shuffle across a knee-high walkway at noon, and stand in the drizzle until someone leans out of a doorway that is also a coppice.

And that person — without ceremony, because ceremony is for things that interrupt work — would wordlessly hand it a hot, oiled cloth for its hands.

Because cold hands are a problem for the whole knot, not just for you.

The power would take it. It would wrap its hands. Its breath would ghost a little in the cold. It would listen to the honey-swarm cry go up and the children scream laughing and the old women laugh like crows, and it would not fix any of it.

It would add its noise to theirs.

And then, after a while, it would leave — or rather, it would settle. Not in the bowl, but *as* the bowl, a little more. A little more weight in the lip to hold the cloud. A little more patience in the seep. A little more tendency for visitors with cold hands to be handed something warm.

Not a guardian above them. A companion beside them.

That is what the wise would do, if it loved them well.

Nothing grand.

Just ensure that what has learned, over centuries, to hold together — gets to keep holding together a little longer.
