It would come, first, the way the water does.

Not down. *In.*

You were expecting — if you were expecting anything — light. A shaft widening. A voice. A hand that lifts. Something that says *this hidden, cold, diligent place deserves more than it has, so I will give it more.*

It would not do that.

A benevolent and wise power does not see a factory with no builders and think *unfinished.* It sees a factory with no builders and thinks *accomplished.*

So the first thing it would do is the only thing that can be done without breaking the room: it would fit.

It would not arrive as sky. It would arrive as size.

You slipped through sideways. It would slip through *smaller* — compress its attention until it was eight meters across and four meters deep and cold enough to feel the weep gather on its own skin. A wise power knows how to kneel without condescension. It would kneel the way you did, face close to the haircap forest, until tardigrades looked like cattle and the *tick* of an eel looked like lightning.

It would stay there long enough for the room's rhythm to become its rhythm. Fill-chuff-grind-tick. Fill-chuff-grind-tick.

It would not be in a hurry to help.

### What It Would Not Do

This is the greater part of benevolence.

It would not warm the water. The cold is not a mistake. The cold is the contract that lets this exact moss live and no other. To warm it even two degrees would be to evict the sphagnum and invite something kinder, and then this room would no longer be this room.

It would not enlarge the crack in the ceiling and give it sun. The perpetual late-blue dusk is not deprivation. It is diffusion. It is why the midges can swarm without being eaten by swallows, why the algae can believe it is still underwater. To give it daylight would be to erase it.

It would not rescue the eels. It would not lift those pale, thumb-thick sparks and carry them to a proper river, to the sea. They are not lost elvers waiting for salvation. This is their *elsewhere.* They have become something that only works at this scale, at this voltage, in this tea-brown mere. To "save" them would be to kill what they have become.

It would not make the wheel stop grinding itself to sand. The wheel's purpose *is* to become sand. The caddis larvae are waiting for it.

A lesser, merely kind power would fix the room until it died of improvement. A wise one knows that lush that should not be, loud that should not be, is not a cry for help. It is a song.

### What It Would Do

Almost nothing. Which is to say — three very small things.

**1. It would witness.**

Not glance. Not survey. *Witness.*

For a long winter minute, every weep-thread, every conveyor-belt larva hauling its sand-grain house, every *chuff* of the hydraulic lung would be felt as felt-by-another. The moss would be known as moss-knows-itself. The eels' ozone would be smelled by something that has smelled nebulae and still finds this sharper.

You think being unseen is the same as being alone. It isn't. But being *unseen and diligently working anyway* is lonely in a way that even amphipods feel. The power would end that without changing anything visible. It would put its attention under the tilt of the floor plates like a hand under a cold cheek. *I see you. Keep going exactly as you are.*

In the economy of small rooms, to be fully noticed is itself a form of nourishment.

**2. It would protect its hiddenness.**

This is the only intervention that looks like action, and it would not look like action at all.

Up on the moor, a surveyor's boot would find slightly firmer peat a meter to the left and step there instead of on the seam. A drainage scheme on a map in an office would be deemed uneconomical for one more year. A teenager looking for a den would feel, without knowing why, that the crowberry patch was not inviting and keep walking.

The power would not build a fence. Fences invite curiosity. It would build *uninterestingness.* A thin veil of *nothing to see here* laid over the crack, not to hide the room from love, but to hide it from use. From the idea that every pocket must become a quarry, a reservoir, a photo location, a parable.

A wise benevolence knows the most endangered thing about wild factories is not that they will collapse. It is that they will be discovered and loved to death.

It would let you remember. It would let almost everyone else forget.

**3. It would grant it time, not eternity.**

It would not freeze the chamber in amber. It loves this room too much to make it permanent.

It knows that by winter the disc will be sand, that a hard freeze may seal the crack, that the mere may silt, that the eels may not breed. It knows this pocket was born two springs ago from a collapse, and that its death is already written into the young basalt.

So it would not grant *endlessness.* It would grant *again.*

Somewhere else on the moor, under sedge where no foot has yet found no ground, another basalt shelf is being undermined by meltwater. Under pressure, a joint is filling. The power would incline its head that way. Ensure the weight is right. Ensure the thaw comes.

When this room closes — and it will close, lush and loud to the last second — another will open a hundred meters or a kilometer away, raw and sharp and weeping, waiting for sphagnum spores on the wind and a lost beetle and perhaps an eel.

That is how you keep a promise to a place that was never meant to last: you don't preserve the instance. You preserve the *possibility.*

---

And then it would leave the way water leaves.

Not up. *Out.* Sideways into peat. Diffusing.

If you had still been kneeling there, you would have felt only that the *tick* seemed a little crisper, the moss a little more certain, the cold a little less like punishment and a little more like a chosen discipline. Your jacket would still be dark with condensation. Your knees would still ache.

On the walk back over the moor, you would find you could not quite place the seam again if you tried. You would worry you had imagined the blue spark. You would check your jacket and find three tiny midge wings stuck to the zipper, and you would smile without knowing why.

The room would keep working under you, unseen.

Fill-chuff-grind-tick.

Fill-chuff-grind-tick.

Indifferent, now, in the way a loved child is indifferent — completely itself, completely held.
