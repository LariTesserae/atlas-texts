It would stop at the rim.

That is the first thing. Any other kind of power would not. Any efficient, hungry, or merely clever power would come down into the basin like water coming down to fill it — quickly, with purpose, with a plan to improve. Green it. Stock it. Build a roof over the windbreak, dig the pools deeper, make the light strong, make the quiet useful.

A benevolent and wise one would feel the lip of stone under its hand and stop, because the basin would tell it, before it even looked inside, what kind of place it is.

*I am already full,* the stone would say. *I am already complete. If you love me you will not try to complete me further.*

And because it is wise, it would understand that what looks empty from above — no plants, no fish, no village, no history — is not empty at all. That is a valley's logic: that a wet place should be green, that a warm place should be alive the way a jungle is alive, that a human place should become a home. This place has refused all of that for ten thousand years and has become something rarer instead: sufficiency. Equilibrium. A world that has exactly what it needs and not one thing more.

So it would not bring anything.

It would not seed the pools with lilies to make them beautiful. It would not throw trout in to make them lively. It would not make the light come clean through the steam, or plant a tree by the wall to give shade that is already there. It would not make the bacterial film bloom into something you could see from standing height. It would not make the moth live a week instead of a day, or give the cricket a song loud enough to carry.

It would not touch the wear-path. It would not rebuild the cracked bowl. It would not write words on the basalt flake. It would not make the old women young again in the water, or make the water remember them longer than the stone already does.

A wise power knows that to fix a lack you have to be very sure it is a lack.

What would it do, then, if it had all the power in the world and loved what it saw?

It would crouch.

It would put itself at rim-height, at basin-scale, and do three very small things that look like doing nothing at all.

**First, it would forget the basin on behalf of the world.**

Not destroy it — veil it. Outside, on the flat country, it would lay down a little disinterest, a little misdirection, light as condensation. Maps would fold wrong right there. Prospectors would walk to the lip, look down into the haze, see "nothing useful" and turn away. A resort developer would feel, suddenly and unaccountably, that the water would be too expensive to pipe, the road too annoying to build, the light too dim for photographs. Soldiers would find their reasons elsewhere. Scientists would publish once and then, mysteriously, not quite manage the return trip.

It would not hide the break in the wall from the families who know it. Their feet would still find the dark track worn through the crust. Their palm-stones would still warm in the same pools. Only the hungry gaze would slide off, like water off travertine. The basin does not need to be famous to be kept. It needs to be passed over.

That is the first benevolence: to be overlooked is a kind of blessing.

**Second, it would keep the promise underneath.**

A place like this only looks static. Really it is a held breath. Somewhere far below, water moves through basalt, picks up silica and heat, rises slow as sap. Somewhere far above, a climate shifts a degree, a rain forgets a season. The basin's equilibrium is precise — a thumb's width of spill, a paper-thin sheet a year — and therefore fragile in the way all perfect balances are. One dry century too long and the aquifer sighs and drops an inch. One hard winter and the film dies back and does not return.

The wise power would put its hand there, not on the pools themselves but on the deep plumbing that no visitor ever sees. It would say to the water below: *keep coming up warm.* To the water above: *keep coming back.* It would not make it flood. It would not make it eternal. It would just hold the budget of heat and mineral steady, century after century, so that tomorrow the pool at ankle height is still at ankle height. So that a child born now, fifty years from now, can come and sit in the cup her grandmother's back wore smooth, and find it still fits.

It is not flashy work. No one inside would ever notice. The ticking of spill and drip would just... go on ticking.

**And third, it would sit down.**

This is what benevolence does when wisdom has finished.

It would come inside, lightly, by the low break like everyone else. It would not glow. It would not need to be seen by the cricket or the moth or the old man soaking his knees. Perhaps it would look, if you had to look straight at it, like heat haze at chest height that stays a little longer. Like warm air that is slightly more patient than the rest.

It would choose a barely-dry island near the center where the water is blood-warm and the crust is cream-white, and it would sit. Feet in the pool that is exactly ankle-deep. Back against rim that is exactly back-shaped. It would listen to the endless faint ticking that the stone swallows and the mites make and the spillways make, and it would add its own quiet to it, the way another warm body in the water adds quiet.

It would not answer prayers there, because no one properly prays there. It would not heal with a hand. The water already does the small, honest healings it can: loosens a joint, softens skin, quiets breathing, gives an old woman a few days where the air does not hurt her eyes. The power would just make those healings a little more true. A little more likely to hold after she walks back out into dry country.

It would think, if such a thing thinks in words at all:

*Keep scraping a little paste when you need it. Keep leaving the bowl inverted. Keep moving like the cricket: step-step-pause. You are doing this right. You have understood the rule without me ever having to tell it to you, which is: take only what the overflow freely gives, and never try to own the source.*

*You, film. Keep laying down your pages. I remember every one.*

*You, moth. Drift well for your day.*

It would stay for an hour, or for ten years — it is hard to tell inside, where light never really gets in. And when it left it would not take anything. It would leave no shrine, no salt pillar, no sign that a power had ever been there at all.

Except perhaps this:

On the top course of that low, unmortared windbreak, there is a stone that was always a little loose. The next time the wind condenses and runs back down the rim in dark beads, that stone will have settled a fraction tighter. Not mortared. Just settled, as if a warm, dry hand had rested there for a moment to steady it.

The path will remain a wear-path, not a road.

The water will still be too mineral for green.

Tomorrow the haze will still hang at chest height and never get thicker or thinner, and every pool will be exactly as full as it was, spilling exactly as much over exactly the same notch.

And if you sit on the warm stone with your feet in the water that comes exactly to your ankles and stays there, you might, once, feel that you are not being watched, but being kept company — in a way so light you could mistake it for your own breathing, if your own breathing were a little kinder than usual.

The basin will not be saved. It does not need saving.

It will simply be allowed to continue being exactly what it is.

Which, for a place like that, is everything.
