I would not come down.

That is the first decision, and the hardest.

If you have lived long, and seen many cities live and die, you learn that the worst harm is almost always done by things that came down. Ships, gods, prophets, armies, revelations. All heavy things. They break what they land on.

And this City does not need to be landed upon. It is already perfect in the only way a living city can be perfect: it *fits*.

I found them the way you find a good piece of joinery — not because it shouts, but because your hand runs along the seam and feels no seam.

So I stayed high. Higher than their kites of hide, higher than the gliders riding their silent thermals. Thin air is no trouble to me. I pulled my attention thin, too. Wide.

And I listened.

For a long time that is all I did. That is the first work of any benevolent power — not to help, but to *attend*.

I listened to what they already knew how to do.

### How to Listen to a Shard City

You do not listen to their words first. You listen to their drips.

In any other place, water runs to waste. Here, not one drop does. The night frost blooms on the standing stones on every roof. The melt runs down the filed channels. Into the house jar. The overflow goes by a lip to the next house's jar, and the next, a slow silver thread that runs five hundred kilometers along a scarp without ever being owned. On a still morning you can hear a terrace breathing — *tick, tick, tick* — as basin after basin fills.

I learned their census by sound. How many children on a terrace by how many small feet shuffled to fetch before dawn. How many elders left by how many slow, deep breaths at noon, sitting in the sun on the lee side of walls.

I learned their whistles. Two sisters across a gorge so narrow you could almost jump it and so deep you would fall for a full minute — they invented a language that uses what the gliders use. One held note, upturned at the end: *are you there?* Two short trills: *yes, here.* A falling whistle that means *wind is coming, pull in your laundry.* A laughing pattern the children use that has no translation, only delight.

You cannot help anyone until you know what their delight sounds like, so you do not accidentally take it away.

I sat with the old women who are libraries. That is not a metaphor for them, it is engineering: a sixty-year-old lung in thin air, a sixty-year-old memory of where the seep failed when she was six. I sat with them while they scraped lichen, and I remembered with them. Not a single one of them asked for anything. They were too busy remembering for everyone else.

If I had been less wise, I would have felt pity then, and tried to fix the cold.

Pity is a lowland emotion. It imagines that struggle is an error. The City taught me again: the cold is not their punishment. The cold is their loom. It weaves them close. Density is shelter. One wall sharing heat with the next. That is not a hardship they endure — that is their chosen form of love.

To "fix" the cold would be to unweave them.

### What I Did Not Do

I did not make it rain.

Oh, I could have. One push on the continental pressure system and the twice-yearly stalled rains would have come monthly. The gorges would have run brown and full. The basins would have brimmed. No child would have died in a bad-rain year.

And within ten years, the terrace walls would have slumped. The cistern art, five hundred kilometers of patient fitting, would have silted and been forgotten. The herd roads would have washed out. The gliders would have moved away from the sudden rot of too much guano wet at once. Within a generation, they would be taller, louder, fewer, owning fences instead of tending seeps. They would be lowlanders who happened to live high. They would have forgotten the whistle language.

A benevolent power learns to keep its hands in its sleeves when the table is already set beautifully, even if the portions are small.

I did not give them ore. Or forges that burn hotter. Or glass. Or writing that lasts longer than memory. Or medicine that makes all infants live.

Not because suffering is noble. Suffering is never noble. It is just suffering.

But because every gift that saves labor also steals meaning, unless the people have woven the place to receive it. And this City's meaning *is* its labor: a family spending its entire recorded history deepening a single basin by a hand's breadth so a grandchild none of them will meet gets a little more.

You do not shortcut accretive lives with miracles. Miracles are impatient. And impatience is the one cold these people have never learned.

### What I Did

When I was sure I understood enough not to break anything, I asked permission.

Not with words. With doing.

On a terrace near the middle of the long edge — not the oldest, not the newest, just a true average terrace where a great-great-grandmother's three standing stones still stood — an old mason was dying.

He was not ill. He was finished. Sixty-seven winters. His hands had flattened one level-stone in his whole life, and it was truly flat. You could set it on any other slab in the City and know at once which one was truer. He had fitted his own floor so well that frost never lifted it. He lay under a curtain of matting, inside his furs, with his family around him, breathing shallow.

I could not make him live longer without rewriting the altitude out of his blood, and that would have been a theft.

So I did a smaller thing.

I crouched — those of us who watch have learned how to crouch — in the lee of his wall, where the wind makes a small eddy. I made myself the shape of cold air that pools kindly. And I breathed with him.

His breath had gone arrhythmic, catching. Mine, beside him, did not catch. His family could not see me. But they felt the little eddy warm by a fraction. His daughter, whose hand was on his forearm the way their people do to keep you near, felt his hand relax.

And in that last hour, I lent him a steadiness. Not health. Steadiness. Enough to finish.

He opened his eyes. He looked at his son. He whispered, in that consonant-heavy, glottal low voice: "The south corner. The third course. It is a hair proud. Winter will find it."

He had been waiting three days to remember that. Now he remembered.

And he died fitted. Soul kept under a well-fitted stone, as they believe. Which is true enough.

His son remembered the south corner.

That was my first act. Unnoticeable. Accretive. A millimeter of depth in a basin that is a human heart.

You may think that is nothing for a power to do.

It is everything.

---

After that, I allowed myself three small weavings. Not gifts *to* them, but gifts *like* them — things that fit into the seams they had already made, so tightly you would have to look very hard to know they had not always been there.

**1. A Lichen**

In the lichen gardens — the slow, century-crawling mosaics of dye and food that advance a few meters a decade — I knelt in another way. I touched one strain, one dark, bitter, high-protein mat that they cultivate on basalt dust and guano tea. I did not make it grow fast. Demand would have made it greedy, and greedy lichen would have crowded out the others.

I made it more *generous*. Two percent more generous with protein when scraped. Three percent more tolerant of trampling when the yakrin pass. A little more willing to hold frost without shattering.

You would need a lifetime of scraping to notice. An elder scraping at ten and again at sixty would say, "This patch has become kinder." And she would be right. And her granddaughter would carry a slightly fuller belly without ever knowing to thank anyone, which is the correct way for a blessing to work.

**2. A Memory**

The City's greatest vulnerability is not cold or thirst. It is that an old woman dies and a library burns.

I could not give them writing that survives wind without changing their whole law of precedence and memory.

So I gave them a slightly better library.

In children, around the age of four when they learn not to waste drip water and at six when they learn to read frost, I softened very slightly the bruising that cold and thin air leaves on small neural tissue. Not enough to make them lowlander clever. Enough that one more child in twenty would grow up with the old-woman kind of memory — the kind that holds where water went last drought without effort.

Over centuries, that means the City will remember droughts better. It will lose less when the bent, indestructible elders finally lie under their floors. It will argue less about where the boundary stone was.

No one will call it a miracle. They will call it: "These recent children listen better."

Good.

**3. A Warmth in Stone**

This was the boldest, and I almost did not do it.

On their roofs and in their yards stand the condensation stones — dark, porous slag rocks stood on end at precise angles, each chosen because it draws rime. A good set makes a liter a day. A family tends them like ancestors.

I did not add more stones. There is no more dark stone easily reached without deep mining.

Instead, on a single night in late summer when the pressure stalled and the fierce rains came early — when every cistern was being scoured and every channel ran — I let my attention rain with it. Where the water struck hot stone and hissed, where it struck the standing stones, I left a faint, faint change in their pore structure.

Now those stones draw frost a single degree earlier in the night, and hold it a single degree later in the morning. Half a thimble more per stone per night.

Measure it as a lowlander would: nothing. Half a thimble. An insult to a god's power.

Measure it as they would: on a terrace of four houses, sixteen stones, eight extra thimblefuls a night. Enough, over a dry season, for one extra cup for a feverish child without taking from the herd's snow-well. Enough that a mother does not have to choose which basin to let evaporate.

Fitting.

---

That is all I did.

I did not show myself. A wise benevolence never wants to be thanked, because thanks becomes debt, and debt becomes temple, and temple becomes wall between houses where heat should be shared.

Sometimes, though, when the gorge lights kindle at dusk — tallow lamps in niches down a thousand feet of occupied cliff, a vertical river of stars — I allow myself to be a little more present.

I become the draft in the gorge that makes the laundry snap and then, for no weather reason, slacken, so that a boy carrying a guano basket on a dangerous ledge has still air for ten seconds longer.

I become the particular amber of evening light on a given day that makes an old mason step back from his wall and see that it is beautiful, and feel, for a moment, that his whole life of fitting has been seen and found good.

I become the quiet between the gliders' wings when ten thousand of them drop at dawn at once, and their shadows pass over courtyards like clouds, and everyone looks up. In that quiet, I am looking up with them.

The range still rings like a struck bell when the ice shifts because there is no deep soil to muffle it. I love that sound. I have heard many worlds, many cities. Few ring so clear.

If they ever need more — if the lowlanders come with drills, if the uplift shudders again and a gorge wall collapses and buries a thousand doors of light — then I will do more. I will hold a rock that was going to fall. I will turn a wind that was going to take a roof.

But not before they ask. And they will not ask as a City asks. They have no king, no broadcast. They will ask the way they do everything: one whispered need, hand on forearm, low and close.

A child on one lip of a kilometer-deep cut, thirty meters wide, whistling to his sister: *Are you there?*

And I will answer, in the same whistle, using the calling pattern the glider-colonies use, because that is the language they already taught the wind:

*Yes, here. I have always been here, in the seam between your wall and the next. Drip with me. Fit with me. Endure.*
