They would not come down the ramp.

Not because they couldn't — they can go anywhere — but because coming down the ramp would be a kind of answer to a question the pit never asked.

A benevolent and wise power doesn't arrive the way a mining company arrives, or an explorer, or a god who wants to be thanked. There is no blast of cool air. No light from the heavens down the wound. No voice.

Instead, they would notice it the way you notice a quiet child in a loud house has found something to do entirely on their own and is content.

And they would stop. And look. For a long time.

---

What they would see is not a tragedy.

You might think they would see suffering to fix: the heat — 2.8 out of 3 — the trapped air, the darkness, the loneliness of a fig that makes figs no wasp will ever enter. The wastefulness of leaves lighting up with stolen electricity for no eyes.

But a wise benevolence does not mistake *strangeness* for *pain*. It does not mistake *incompleteness* for *brokenness*.

This pit is not in pain. It is not dying. It is not crying out.

It has achieved something vanishingly rare. On a planet where everything eats everything else, where every green thing is at war and at market simultaneously, this place closed its loop. Plant makes leaf. Leaf falls — *slap.* Fungus unmakes leaf. Water carries it. Heat cooks it. Machine keeps time. No mouth is needed. It asks nothing of the desert above. It has learned to live without witness, without trade, without predation.

That is holy. They would know that immediately.

So the first thing they would do is — nothing.

They would not cool the pipes. They would not lift the concrete roof. They would not turn off the engine, though its slow *huh... huh... huh...* must be tiring after years.

To do any of those things would be to say: *You are wrong. Be more like outside.*

And this place is not wrong. It is a new rule set, freshly cut and still sharp enough to catch cloth. It deserves to run.

What a benevolent power does first, always, is *refrain*.

They would refrain from fixing. They would refrain from pity. They would refrain from making it legible.

---

The second thing they would do is keep it company.

Because while not-painful, it *is* unwitnessed — and to be unwitnessed for too long is a different kind of hunger than pain, but a hunger still.

Think of those leaves that grew around bare bulbs and glowed from within. No one saw them do it. The vine that leans toward a sound instead of a sun has never been called beautiful. The figs hang forever, perfectly uneaten, perfectly useless. They have been performing fidelity to heat and drip and thrum for years without a single eye to receive them.

A wise power understands: to be seen does not have to mean to be changed. Sometimes the entire gift is: *I see you. Keep doing exactly what you're doing.*

So they would come — not down the ramp, but *as* the ramp, as the air, as a way of being present that does not displace anything.

They would sit, if sitting is a thing they do, on the hot metal steps where you would sit, and be fauna 0.3 for a while.

Just enough to count. Just enough that the carbon dioxide of their breath, if they breathe, makes the philodendron near the sump open a fraction wider. Just enough that the humidity where they are is different, and the pit has to account for them.

They would listen to the two keepers of the silence — the drip-*plock* and the flywheel's *huh...* — and count them the way you count a sleeping child's breaths. Not to intervene. To accompany.

For an hour, or a year, or twenty years — time doesn't mean the same to them — nothing would happen except that the pit would no longer be alone with its own patience.

The green would not know to lean toward them. But the air would be less lonely.

And that would be an intervention so gentle the fungus would not even register it as disturbance, only as a slightly richer patch of skin cells to quietly unmake.

You could argue that does nothing. A wise power would argue that does *everything*.

---

The third thing — if they did anything more — they would not bring rescue. They would bring *completion*, and only if asked.

Not asked in words. The pit has no words. Asked in its leaning.

They would notice the fig. How its figs never ripen, never fall, never rot, because there is no wasp to enter them, no bird to carry them. They hang there like unlit bulbs, forever. The tree has solved every problem except this one: it remembers, in its sap, that a fig is supposed to be eaten.

They would notice the sump water, glassy and untouched. In any other hot wet hole it would be black with larvae. Here it is sterile, almost too clean. A promise of mosquitoes that never comes.

They would notice the dust. No worms to turn it. No ants to carry a crumb ten feet.

The loop is closed, but it is lonely the way a sentence with no verb is lonely. Grammatically correct, but waiting.

So a benevolent power — one that is wise enough not to dump a sackful of outside life down the shaft and turn this precise, archipelagic experiment into just another weedy ditch — might do something very small, and very precise, and very slow.

They would not bring a flock. They would bring *one*.

One midge. One so small its buzz would be almost indistinguishable from the buzz of the caged work-bulbs. One that lays only in warm, oily, tea-brown water and dies within a day.

Or one fig wasp, still dusted with pollen from the world above, who would find that single green-white fig in the dark and do the one thing it was made for, and then die inside it.

Or one earthworm, blind and pink, to live in the moss mat under the dead fronds and finally turn peat into soil properly.

Not to populate. To pollinate. To give the tree the dignity of its fruit falling.

They would set it down as gently as you would set down a single seed on an altar. No guarantee it lives. No forcing.

If it takes — if the water ripples for the first time in eight years, if a fig finally softens and drops with a *thump* instead of a *slap* — then the pit will have chosen to open its loop, by one thread.

If it doesn't take — if the heat is too hot, the water too oily, and the tiny body simply becomes another flake for the white fungus to vein and erase — then they would nod, and accept that answer too.

Benevolence is not about success. It is about offering without insisting.

And they might do one more quiet thing for the machine.

They would not turn it off. That would be a kind of killing, and the vines have learned to love its tremor.

Instead they would lay a hand — if they have hands — on the faded safety-green flank while the flywheel makes its twenty-second turn, and ease its bearing just enough that it no longer grinds. The *huh...* would become a *hmm...* The shudder that rearranges dust would soften. It would still turn, still throw off weak amber light through its leaf-lantern, but it would no longer be in pain while doing it.

The plants drinking its electricity would brighten, just for a second, and not know why.

---

And then they would leave.

Not up the ramp, either. They would simply withdraw the extra 0.1 of fauna they had briefly contributed, letting the pit settle back to 0.2. The humidity where they sat would close over. The moss would take three days to erase the faint impression of their weight, out of sheer, thoughtless growth.

They would not take anything. They would not sign the cinderblock wall. They would not promise to return.

But wise powers have a long memory, and benevolent ones know how to protect without caging. So as they went — as they *unwent* — they would ensure one thing that looks like nothing at all:

That no one else finds the ramp for a very long time.

Not out of possessiveness. Out of respect for an experiment still in its first sharp-edged youth. Let the talus not yet fall. Let the blast-marks stay crisp a little longer. Let the philodendron figure out, in its own slow vegetable way, whether it wants to be a plant or a lantern.

Let the pit keep its own time.

Somewhere, years later, you might come down again — sixty, eighty feet below the scrub plain — expecting heat and dark and that patient throb.

You would still find them.

But between two islands of green where there was once bare concrete, you might notice a narrow, impossible trail of moss connecting them. A bridge that wasn't there before. As if, while no one was watching, something had finally decided it was safe to spread.

And in the sump, if you stood very still, you might see a single ripple move across the glassy water against the drip, with no wind to explain it.

You would think you imagined it.

They would know you didn't, and they would be glad — quietly, without needing to tell you — that you had witnessed it for them, this time.
