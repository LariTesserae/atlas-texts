It would not come down.

Not in the way powers are expected to come down — with shade, or with a cloud, or with a voice that quiets other voices. To do that here would be violence.

A benevolent and wise power knows immediately what you learned with your soaked boots: there is no room.

The plateau is complete. It has been complete for longer than anything with nerves has been alive to judge completeness. Every inch is taken, every road is exactly as wide as it needs to be, every seep has kept to the same line for centuries and left its precise orange signature to prove it. You cannot add to it without taking away. You cannot help it without implying it was waiting to be helped.

So it would not bring soil.

That is the first temptation of any lesser kindness — to soften, to green, to answer water with root. *Here,* the lesser god would say, *let me finally give you what you were missing. Moss. Lichen. A meadow to justify your wetness.* And within a season the seams would choke, the throats would silt, the ten thousand equal roads would become three gullied rivers, the equilibrium older than thought would break under the weight of generosity. The stone crowd would drown in the very thing they had learned perfectly to pass hand to hand.

The wise one loves what a place *is*, not what it could be if it were more like other places.

It would not quiet the roar, either.

It would not lift the mites out, or teach them to gather, or make the midges live longer than a single filling of a cup-sized pothole. It would not make birds land. It would not give the moth a reason. To prolong those briefest lives would be to make them hungry in a place that has, by renunciation, solved hunger by refusing it entirely. Their holiness is their thinness — one atom thick, one minute long, permitted and erased without cruelty.

What, then, does benevolence do when there is nothing to fix?

### It attends.

Having drawn its attention — which is not a casual glance, but a turning of the whole self toward a thing — it would do the one thing no climber has ever managed for more than a few minutes in that white, deafening light:

It would stay.

Where you had to shout to hear yourself think, it would not need to. It would have an ear large enough to hold the aggregate hiss and crash and organ-piping without reducing it. Where you squinted against tin-white glare, it would not blink. Where you went cold through your gloves touching stone that held night all day, it would feel the exact temperature of that holding and call it good.

It would, in effect, sit down.

There is no room to sit down, remember. But a wise power makes room the way water does here — not by displacing, but by finding the shape of what is already crowded and fitting itself seam to seam. It would sit between leaning pillars where no human body fits, and not be in the way of a single thread or ribbon. Its weight would be nothing. Its shadow would be nothing, because it would not block the absolute sun that the stones need to make more water out of thin air.

And sitting, it would do three small things that look like doing nothing at all to anyone hurrying past toward greener miracles. 

**First — it would name without renaming.**

To each pillar, fin, tooth, hexagonal knuckle barely breaking the sheet-wash at the drowned horizon — to each standing one with its particular beard of silica frill, its particular scar of iron varnish — it would give the only gift stone can receive and keep: to be known as itself.

Not aloud. Stone does not need language. But attention, held long enough, becomes recognition, and recognition becomes dignity. For a hundred years in human time, or for one long noon in plateau time, each column would exist not only in equilibrium with its neighbors but also in the mind of another. *You, who have passed water down your north flute since before the ice returned, you are not interchangeable. You, with the kettled pothole that fills every seven seconds, you are seen.*

Nothing would look different. A geologist coming the next day would find no new mineral. But the place would cease to be forsaken. Forsaken and holy feel alike from within; they are distinguished only by whether anyone is listening on purpose.

**Second — it would remember the typos.**

This is the most secretly benevolent work, because no one else considers it worth doing.

That translucent midge born and drowned in the same cup. That basalt-colored mite picking infinite care along a hair-wide seep, grazing on nothing, never meeting another of its kind. That ash-white moth lifted like a flake of chert, startled into wind without reason.

They do not accumulate. They leave no bodies, no fossil in travertine. The plateau permits them and immediately erases them, its equilibrium too perfect to archive failure. It is not cruel. It simply does not have the organ for grief.

The wise one does.

It would keep them — not by saving them, which would be to falsify them, but by *counting* them. By holding each brief transit in a memory larger than the plateau's, so that a life one-minute-long is not less real for being unrepeated. The mite that made three careful steps before a trickle took it would have made three careful steps *forever*, in that other keeping. The moth's single lift would be kept as flight, not as windblown litter.

This changes nothing on the stone. The next midge still drowns. But in the total accounting of existence, thinness is no longer the same as nothingness. That is a mercy only a wise power can afford: to value without intervening.

**Third — it would leave the Listener intact.**

Because by now there is a human standing soaked to the knees though it never rained, deafened in brilliant sun, understanding why nothing has ever been built here.

A lesser power would speak to him. Would give him a well, a vision, a purpose. Would make his trespass meaningful by making him a prophet of wet stone.

The wise one would not detain him.

It would let him feel — for one clear moment inside the roar — that his own behavior was correct: *do not dam, do not divert, do not look for a lake, do not look for shade.* That he had learned the manners. That his desire to add, to build, to plant a flag, to carve a name, was not evil, just misplaced, and could be set down like a pack.

And then it would make leaving easy.

Not by carrying him down red talus and basalt steps — he came up by his own legs and the place respects effort — but by removing the shame of leaving. By letting him know that *to stand inside of* the roar for a while and then go is itself a form of habitation, and honored. That he does not have to become a white-orange stain in a cleft to prove he listened.

He would turn, and his boot-prints would be rinsed from the film between pillars within minutes, as before. The difference is that this time, being unwitnessed on earth would not mean being unwitnessed.

Behind him, the plateau would not diminish by one voice. In front of him, the trail down would still be steep. In him, something newly quiet would be able to hold a roar without needing to answer it.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does with a place that is already full.

It does not bring green.

It does not bring silence.

It brings a second kind of fullness that takes up no space at all: to see what refuses to change, and to love it for its refusal; to hear what never stops talking among itself, and to consider that enough; to let what is almost-not-living be almost-not-living, precious in its slenderness; to let the visitor be temporary, and still beloved.

If you came over the lip an hour after it had gone — though wise powers do not really come or go — you would find nothing altered. The same thin scoured high-altitude light. The same black films sheeting down cold sides. The same flicker, white on black, across millions of little falls.

Only this, perhaps, if you crouched and looked very carefully into a joint where you could have sworn you'd already looked:

The silica frill, pale as frozen foam, might seem — just for a breath — less like a precipitate and more like a held note. Less like stone remembering water, and more like stone being remembered.

And you would put your glove back to it, feel night holding through full sun, and understand that you were welcome to lift your hand again.
