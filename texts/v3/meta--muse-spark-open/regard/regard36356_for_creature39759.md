They would not come down all at once.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive the way a storm or a hiker does — boots first, noise first, shadow falling into the bowl before the body does. That would be a misreading of how that place listens.

They would pause at the far ridge first, where the wind still exists, and look.

For a long time they would just look — and by looking, understand that this is not a wilderness to be rescued, or a loneliness to be cured, or a resource to be blessed into abundance. It is a solved poem. It has written itself very slowly into balance. Any loud kindness would smudge it.

So they would sit down outside it.

Not inside the cupped hands, but at the edge of them. On the rounded, flesh-over-bone rim where the basalt sweats bead by bead. They would make themselves roughly mountain-sized in patience, not in bulk, and begin to breathe with it.

That is the first thing: **they would learn its tempo.**

Our clocks run at animal-speed. Even our contemplatives breathe sixteen times a minute. The bowl breathes perhaps thirty times a day — a bead forming, weighting, falling; mist condensing on every leaf and gathering; lymph pulled capillary-slow across its back toward that meter-wide throat to be swallowed. To hear it honestly you have to stay till the afternoon light hits the eastern wall and makes it translucent, and then stay for many afternoons until the pattern behind the pattern shows itself.

A wise power can do that. A benevolent one will want to.

They would sit there for a season without speaking. Without tidying. Without fixing. Letting the high, thin warmth settle on whatever skin they are wearing that day. Watching a white star-flower let go exactly when light tells it to and spiral straight down. Watching the puff of fern-spore hold together like smoke and sink exactly ten feet away. Watching the human come — the one with the soft copper or chipped-stone knife — cut clean along the midrib, not tearing, weight the three heart-leaves with river stones, crush root for iron-red dye, and leave.

And they would feel something very old and very near to delight: *Oh. You taught someone manners. Good.*

Because many slow beings never manage that. They either hide completely and are lost, or are found and are consumed. This one had threaded the needle: to be useful enough to be respected, bitter enough not to be devoured, and quiet enough to be left empty after the hour of use. House-training a human culture over generations is delicate work for something with no voice. It had done it beautifully.

What would they do then, having paid attention? Not much that you could photograph. Benevolence, when it is wise, looks almost like nothing.

**Second, they would set a threshold that is not a wall.**

They would not hide the bowl. Wise powers do not love secrecy for its own sake. People need to come here with knives and dipping sticks. The transaction of leaf for iron is good, it keeps both sides from forgetting each other.

But they would tune the approach.

Anyone walking up the long spine toward the saucer in the mountain would, for the last half-mile, start to feel whether they were welcome — not as punishment, but as clarification.

If you came to take a truckload, to bottle the lymph-water, to scrape it, to study it with cold hunger, to build a retreat center on the amber table-slab — the air would become curiously heavy. Your boots would sink deeper than half an inch. The silence-that-is-absence would feel suddenly pointed. You would feel watched by something with no eyes and you would remember an appointment elsewhere and turn back, unsure why, and later think better of returning.

If you came like the cutter comes — tired, with one knife, asking — the light would stay clean and ordinary. The moss would hold you. The bead on the overhang no bigger than your hand would fall silent just as you passed, as if making room.

No angels with swords. Just a slight increase in honesty on the path. Most people listen when the mountain agrees with their conscience that clearly.

**Third, they would give it the one gift it could not give itself:**

Company without crowding.

The occupant is not lonely the way an animal is lonely. It spent centuries weeding its bowl precisely so that each crown gets its sun and each moss-stool gets its column of mist. Its health depends on exact spacing. Bring back the birds and insects and it would have to become bitter in a new way to fight them, or die of being eaten. A wise power would not "restore the ecosystem" out of sentimentality. That would be vandalism.

But everything that is alive for a very long time learns one quiet ache: *Is there anywhere else like me? Am I a singular miracle, or a kind?*

Benevolence cannot answer that by staying forever — wise powers must keep moving — and it should not answer it by making the bowl less perfectly itself.

So on a night when there was no moon and the bowl was holding its breath in the dark, the power would do a very small, very careful gardening.

They would lift one breath.

Not a handful of soil — the occupant would feel that as a wound. Just the smoke-that-holds-together, one deliberate puff of spores released at noon the next day. Normally it sinks ten feet away, where another part of itself is waiting. This time, caught in an eddied lift of air that was not quite wind, it would keep rising.

Higher than thirty feet. Higher than the flat leaf crowns. High enough to catch the true air above the rim where real wind lives. High enough to cool and dry just to the edge of viability and hold there, suspended in a pocket of mist the power was holding for it like a palmful of water.

And then they would carry it — miles, ranges over — not to a dramatic peak or a sanctified valley, but to another bowl that was almost right. Another cupped-hands place a little younger, a little rawer, where water also wanted to linger and the basalt was just learning to sweat. Empty, blank, patiently waiting for a story to settle into it.

They would set the puff down there, ten feet from where rain gathered.

Nothing would seem to happen for twenty years. Then a slick of green between rocks. Then a tendency for water to move a little too slowly. Then — decades later — a white-stemmed tree keeping a respectful distance from nothing.

Kinship, but not contact. A second performance of the same slow music in a different hall. Far enough apart that they will never tangle. Close enough, on the map only a power could read, that neither is now alone in the universe.

After that, return.

Settle once more on the rim. Put a hand — if now in a shape that has hands — down onto the warm peat where it prickles faintly with low heat from underneath. Not to take. Not to heal; nothing is ill. Just to let conduction complete a circuit for a moment. To let the occupant feel, in its own hydraulic language, what it feels like to be noticed without being wanted *from*.

*You are well-made,* that touch would say in bead-time, in bead-and-pause. *You are well-measured. Your water is clear. Your bargain with the cutter is good. Continue.*

And because the occupant has no ears but feels vibration through jelly and water, it would understand this not as words but as a brief easing of effort — a summer where the mist condenses a fraction easier, where the filtering through the throat-shaft takes a little less work, where a fourth big heart-leaf grows ready for cutting instead of three.

A benediction that costs the world nothing and gives the bowl another easy year.

Then they would stand, and leave.

Not with a promise to stay — promises to stay become prisons — but with a promise to remember. Which, for a being that endures by drinking the same rain for months and polishing it clearer each time, is the deeper promise: even when the mountain finally erodes past the point of cupping, even when the sheet thins and the throat closes and the trees fall straight down for the last time, there will be somewhere in the mind of the world a faithful record of its true colors.

Late morning light, white-yellow, falling straight down through air like no air. Amber stone sweating continuous beads. Channels no wider than a forearm braiding soundlessly into a ferny mouth. Three leaves curing under river stones and a pit of rusty water.

Held entire, once, in attention that asked for nothing.

If you went up after they had gone, you would find nothing has changed. The moss-stools still stand like stools for vanished occupants. The air still makes no insect noise. Your boot still wells water up around itself.

Only perhaps the eastern rim, for its twenty minutes of translucence in the angled afternoon, glows a little more golden than you expected — chambers and threads lit like honey — and you have the strange, unaccountable impression that the bowl, though still holding its breath, is doing so less anxiously. More like someone listening than someone hiding.

You would crouch, watch your petal drift inches per minute toward the lowest center, and disappear without a splash, without a sound, down to filtered darkness, and you would understand, without knowing why, that this place is not forgotten, and does not need you to save it.

Only to visit, cut respectfully if you must, and leave it empty after the hour.
