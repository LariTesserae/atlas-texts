It would not come down.

Not the first time, anyway. A benevolent and wise power does not descend into a bowl that has already learned how to hold water.

It would stop at the rim where the agricultural plain begins to roll, where the air first gets heavier — and it would sit there for a while. Long enough to learn the pace. A year, maybe, in our time. An afternoon, in its. Long enough that its own breathing slowed to the town's.

That is the first thing it would do, if anything: adjust its metabolism to 84 degrees and wet.

It would watch without being seen, at first, because even benevolence is an interruption if it arrives too bright.

And it would understand, very quickly, what it was looking at. Not a tragedy. Not a failed mill town to be rescued and dried out and redeveloped. Not a beautiful ruin to be preserved untouched like an exhibit.

A pact. A solved problem, solved at great cost, still being solved every day.

The wise part would see that immediately, and it would feel something like reverence. Humility, even. Because here was a people who had not conquered the plant and had not been conquered by it. They had *joined* it. They discovered the one verb — *to tend* — that lets brick and root coexist, and they built a whole culture around not winning.

Most places never learn that verb at all.

So it would not fix the town.

It would not lift the bowl and make it dry. It would not kill the mat. It would not run the dye works at full capacity again or bring back ten thousand people. It would not offer to move them to the plain where cotton doesn't rot in a season.

To do any of that would be to tell them their equilibrium was a mistake.

Instead it would do much smaller, stranger things. Things so quiet the town might not attribute them to a power at all, which is exactly how a wise power likes it.

---

First, it would tend the tenders.

At night, when the sodium lights buzz amber behind their cages of jasmine and the shift-work of shink... shink goes on softly in alley after alley, it would put its hand — if it has a hand — on chests.

Not to take the rattle away entirely. The rattle is history in lungs. Forty years of spore and lint and steam through morning glory has written itself there, and to erase it would be to tell old sluice-keepers their bodies had been wrong to breathe that air. They coughed into their shoulders without breaking conversation because that cough was part of the grammar.

But it would ease it. Just enough.

The children who wake at 5 a.m. to climb roofs barefoot — it would let them take one full breath in the morning before the cough starts. Deep, to the bottom. The old women who sweep the same ten feet of alley — their breath would not whistle by noon. Not cured. Eased. So that tending for a lifetime did not have to mean drowning an inch at a time.

It would do the same for the skin. The indigo under the nails would stay — that is a mark of trade and adulthood — but the weeping rash that blooms in every crease where boots stay damp, the one they never mention because everyone has it, would quiet. The copper fungicide they paint on willow stumps would sting less in cuts.

If you asked them a year later why their hands hurt less, they'd shrug. "Dry spell last week. The mold wasn't as bad." Good. Let them keep the credit.

---

Second, it would negotiate on their behalf with things too big for hand-cranks.

The town knows how to talk to water at the scale of a gate, a gutter, a hyacinth choke point. It does not know how to talk to the sky, or the plain.

The power does.

It would not stop the 3 p.m. rain. That rain *is* the town — the drum on corrugated, the roar in gutters, the assessment in doorways, the ticking long after. Take it away and the fern dries and the mortar dies and the pact collapses.

But it would smooth the spikes. The years when climate tilts hot and the rain comes not at three but all at once, for three days, until even the sluice-keepers' knowledge fails and a courtyard floods to the waist and the mosquitoes hatch black — in those years, it would have been leaning on the wind a little upstream for weeks already. Turning a deluge into a long, heavy week. Holding 94 degrees at 87. Keeping the plain's new diversion ditch — the one a county engineer drew on a map and never visited — silted just enough that it never quite steals the creek.

No miracle. Just probability nudged. The maps would still say flood plain, danger zone. Developers would fly over, see impossible green, and turn back. The bowl would remain illegible from the air, a palm full of static.

It would let the town continue to be forgotten by precisely the people who would fix it to death.

And in the canals, very quietly, it would finish what the fig roots started. The dye works still runs a short batch for trade, and the water below the spillway still runs faintly warm and chemical. The heron tolerates it. There is only ever one heron because there is only enough clean fish for one.

The power would not punish the dye workers. They need that indigo. It would simply give the reeds and the hyacinth a slightly better appetite for those compounds. A little more hunger for indigo and copper. So that after the sluice, the water runs clear enough for two herons.

One spring you would see it: two herons standing motionless, each with its own stretch of canal. No one would remark on it, except a child, who would say *look, there's two now*, and an old man would nod and say *yes, seems there are.*

That would be enough.

---

Third — and this is the part that is most benevolent because it is least useful — it would bear witness out loud.

These people have no illusions about ownership. The papers composted in a drawer. Whoever keeps the water moving owns that stretch as long as they keep it. There are no fences that are not also trellises. They move slow not from indolence but from technique. They speak under the threshold of the mill's thrum.

They suspect what they are doing is holy and they have no word for it that isn't practical. If you asked a roof boy what he was doing, he would not say *I am holding the symbiosis. I am keeping brick breathing under leaf.* He would say *Cutting willows. So it don't rip.*

A wise power knows that a holiness never named can curdle into loneliness. Are we just falling behind on maintenance forever? Is there anywhere else? Does anyone up on the plain know this is not squalor?

So on certain nights — not every night, only when the ticking of water off power lines is especially long after rain — it would give them a dream. Not a command. Not a revelation.

A dream of the bowl seen from above, for once, as it is: not a slum, not a jungle ruin, but a knot of light and green, pulsing. All the roof meadows exhaling at once. All the gutters running clear because someone pulled the hyacinth that afternoon.

And in the dream each tender would feel, briefly, the weight of every other tender at the same time — the sweepers, the sluice-keepers, the children in fig-root ladders, the cats on pipe-racks, the willow roots themselves holding tar — as one continuous keeping.

They would wake with that feeling still damp on them like condensation on an inside wall, and they would not have words for it either. But at mid-morning, when the turbines go to full thrum, someone on a ladder might call down, low, under the mill sound: *Gate three's sticking.* And someone else would answer, *I got it.* A little warmer than yesterday.

The power would also, once a year, give them one night off.

It loves them enough to break its own rule of never stopping anything. For one night in late August when the air is worst and the mat grows fastest — it would tell the mat: hold.

Not die. Hold. Pause.

And for eight hours no vine would lengthen. No moss would bead. No willow stump would push a new shoot through salt. The gutters would still run, the mill would still thrum, but the green would, for once, hold its breath with them.

On that night the shink... shink would not be needed. People would stay on stoops longer. They would oil shears without needing them at dawn. Children would sleep netless and not be bitten quite so much. Someone might finally finish a conversation that had been going in short practical sentences for months.

By morning the mat would resume, hungry as ever. No one would have seen anything happen. They might only say, later, that was a good night. Wind came through right.

Good. Let them think it was wind.

---

That is what it would do, if anything.

Not a rescue. A co-tending.

It would not make the town dry or the labor less. It would make the labor *slightly less mortal*, the pact *slightly more durable*, and the loneliness *slightly less complete.*

It would learn from them as much as it gave — how to hold stone with living thatch, how to speak under thrumming, how to own a place by weeding it — and it would carry that knowledge to other bowls in other low places, other knots that are starting to learn the same slow technique.

And mostly, it would keep sitting, just at the rim where the air changes, where you can still see the whole half-mile that is one thing in a palm.

Its attention itself would be the largest gift: that for as long as these two or three thousand keep their doorways cut back in clean half-circles and scrape moss from iron treads and keep the gutter-river running clear, there is at least one other being in the universe that knows exactly what that costs.

And that being thinks it is beautiful, and has decided to help hold the edge back — an inch wider — so they can keep doing it.
