It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power has learned — because it has to have learned this to become benevolent and wise — that coming down is almost always the wrong move. Coming down is what meteors do. What colonists do. What answers do when you weren't asking a question.

It has seen this shape before. Not this exact hollow — there is only one of this hollow — but the *kind* of thing it is: an equilibrium so complete it looks like stillness to an outside eye. A knot tied so perfectly it holds without tension. A town that learned to live inside one breath and stay there.

The foolish kind of power would call that stagnation. Would bring wind into the bowl to "stir things up," would bring maps, would bring history, would bring choice as a weapon. Would stand on the rim and shout *Do you know you could be more?*

The wise one knows they already are.

So it does not come down.

It comes *over*, and then it lies down.

---

On the plain, under that huge bright sky, it makes itself low and long. Not invisible — the benevolent have no interest in spying — but unremarkable. To you, if you were still out there stepping wrong, it might look like a lengthening of weather. A change in how the light sits on the white. A place where the wind, which never enters the hollow, seems to pause and consider, and then pass over more gently.

It settles its attention on the rim first. That soft, waist-high lip of moraine and peat that ten thousand winters smoothed. It puts something like a hand there. Not to change it. To feel how established it is. How it has the profile of a sleeping animal's flank because for a long time it *was* one — a pressure, a weight, a body that stayed until staying became ground.

And the power, which has held other sleeping things, recognizes it and approves.

*Good rim,* it thinks, in the way powers think that is not language. *You are doing exactly enough.*

What it does next is not miraculous. It is maintenance at a scale you can't see.

It deepens the forgetfulness around the hollow by a fraction. Not a wall. Not a curse. Just a slight increase in uninterestingness. The satellite that will pass in twenty years will have a one-pixel stutter there and correct for it. The surveyor who walks the low country with a GPS will find his boots want to drift three degrees left without knowing why, and he will call it magnetic. The map will remain blank not because anyone forbids it, but because the eye slides right over it the way a tongue slides over a missing tooth without catching. Privacy as blessing. To be unwitnessed a little longer is the rarest gift an observed world can be given.

It steadies the wind. Just a touch — a hand on the shoulder of the jet stream — so that for a hundred winters more than there would have been, the wind will continue to pass *over* and not *in*. So that erosion, which retired, stays retired.

It does not touch the water table. It checks it. The way you press your palm lightly to a sleeping child's chest to feel the rise and fall without waking them. The cold, clear upwelling that feeds the well-head is holding. The deep earth is still pressurized. Good. It leaves a little warmth in the aquifer, not heat, just assurance — *keep pushing up, little heart, we're still here to hold you from below.*

Because it has noticed the heart.

Of course it has. You cannot be wise and miss the thing everyone is leaning toward without knowing they are leaning.

The power does not name it. It knew better than to name it long before you did. Naming would be a leash. Instead it looks, and what it sees makes it — for the first time in a very long while — feel less alone in its work.

There, at the bottom of the cold town, pulsing open-closed-open on the scale of breaths: another benevolent and wise power. Smaller, so much smaller, fronded and white and pink-veined, affixed to stones it did not place. Not thinking, but *holding*. Doing in miniature what the great one does on plains and planets: holding a pocket of impossible lushness in equilibrium inside a place that should by rights be too cold, too bright, too silent to live.

A colony that learned to be a town is being held by a colony that learned to be a heart.

The great power feels a kinship that is almost embarrassing in its tenderness.

And so — because it can, and because not doing it would be withholding — it answers.

It does not speak. Speech would break the first law of the place. Sound does not carry here. The moss would eat it.

It *pulses*.

Somewhere on the plain where the light is relentless, the great power takes a breath as slow as a minute-open, minute-closed. It lets its own warmth — the iron-and-milk smell writ large as summer on another continent — filter down into the bowl not as heat but as *permission*.

One pulse. In sync.

Inside, no one notices with their waking mind. But:

In the well-head, the white fronds shiver. Not from cold. They open a fraction wider than they have in a thousand years.

On the peat-brick walls, every saxifrage, every fern, every sheet of mustard-lichen leans — and for three heartbeats, leans *back*. As if listening and then, for the first time, hearing a reply. As if realizing the listening goes both ways.

In the cisterns, the blind fish all rise at once and mouth the surface without a ripple, and settle again.

On a rooftop meadow, a dwarf reindeer lifts its stunted, velveted antlers for no reason and holds them there.

And in the alleys, a Hare-Keeper passing a bundle of roots to a Gutter-Mender without looking suddenly looks. Not at the roots. At the other person's pale grey eyes. And smiles with teeth, just a little, despite the cold hurting. And the other person smiles back. And they stand sideways together for a second longer than efficient packing allows.

That is all it does.

It does not give them history. It does not give them metal. It does not give them an exit, because an exit offered by a power is a command. But it leaves a thread, the way you leave a door ajar not to invite leaving, but so no one feels trapped.

Low on the south wall, where the mica roofs run with silent sheeting, one new seep appears. A single additional bead fattening on a moss tip. Enough to fill one extra cupped hand a day. Not surplus — never surplus, surplus would break them — but *slack*. A little looseness in the weave. So that if one winter a child does cry a little too loudly and cannot be immediately picked up, the air will be a fraction kinder. So that if one spring a bumblebee dies and its grey-white fur is late to pollinate a saxifrage, there is one more blossom to take its turn.

A margin. Wise powers deal almost entirely in margins.

And then — because the most benevolent thing is not to linger and become a sun they orient around — it gets up.

It does not descend. It never descended.

It smooths the snow where it lay so there is no print, and lifts its attention the way you lift a hand from a head you have been blessing, slowly enough that the warmth stays after.

From the plain, the hollow is still just a soft wrinkle, a slightly more intense smear of green in the white. From inside, the light is still too bright, the water still beads and falls without a sound, the packed, lush, cold city still continues, exactly as it was.

Exactly.

Except — if you stayed another night, which you won't, because the brightness is still too much and the density is still too much — you would see that the water in the well-head runs a fraction clearer. And that the children, strapped to chests in felt slings, are dreaming with their eyes open for the first time, not of outside, but of the warm inside of a mouth that holds them without swallowing.

And that the heart, open-closed-open, is no longer pulsing alone.

It never was. It just didn't know anyone else was listening at its tempo.

Now it does.
