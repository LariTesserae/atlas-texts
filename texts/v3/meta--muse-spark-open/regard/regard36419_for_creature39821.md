It would do almost nothing.

Not out of indifference — out of attention.

A lesser power would have seen this place and tried to fix it.

It's too cold, it would think. Too wet. Too low. Too loud and too empty at the same time. It would have brought warmth down like a blanket, or a sun to give the light a source. It would have seeded moss to soften the glass, dropped fish in the pools, given the wind a direction. It would have drained a little of the water so you could walk without always being soaked to the ankle. 

It would have done all of that out of kindness, and it would have killed it.

A benevolent and wise power would kneel first.

Not literally. It doesn't have knees the way you do. But it would lower itself — in scale, in speed, in volume — until it was *low* enough to enter. Powers like that are usually high and fast and hot. This place is low and slow and cold. To meet it you have to cool.

So it comes down to the lip of the bowl and stops. It doesn't step in right away. It listens.

For a long time — longer than a human pilgrim can stand to stay still, because the cold gets into human bones and drives them out. The power has no such hurry.

It listens to the whole chord:

the high ticking of ten thousand drip-points,
the waveguide throb of the parallel fins — *wuh-WUH, wuh-WUH* —
the molar-hum of water wicking through young vesicular glass,
the longer swell, seven to twelve minutes per breath, when the entire sheet rises a fraction of a millimeter and the fog thickens and every stone weeps faster, then sighs.

It understands, very quickly, that this is not a broken landscape waiting for life.

This *is* life. Just not animal life. This is mineral life that has not yet learned — or needed — to be green. A metabolism made of pressure and solubility. Silica in, silica out. Water up through stone, out into air, down again. Zeolite frost growing a millimeter a day. Iron blooming rust-red and pulsing darker when the humidity kisses it. The forest closing its own gaps century by century until the water it now sheets will become inclusions — those lit trees of water trapped in clear chert that shift when you're not looking.

If you love a thing, you do not interrupt its becoming.

The second thing it notices is the sheets and the stilts. How rare and precise they are.

The power could make them abundant. Could tweak a parameter and fill every pool with a hundred palm-sized translucent paddlers, make the spires crawl with pale hunters. More life is better, isn't it?

No.

Their rarity *is* their adaptation. They live here by being almost-not-there. One sheet per black mirror, folding like paper at your shadow. One stilt per acre of needle-points, so light it never touches the water, listening so hard its whole body cocks toward a corridor where drips have fallen into phase, waiting for one *plok* out of rhythm.

To make them common would be to make them compete. To make them starve.

So it doesn't touch them either. But it does something smaller, and more tender:

It makes sure the next crack will open.

Because this bowl is going to die. That's the other thing the wise part understands that the merely kind part misses.

In fifty years that capillary you squeezed through will be sealed by opaline terrace. In a hundred, this acre will be solid. The roar will mute. The pulse will dampen to a hiss trapped inside rock. Pressure will build again somewhere else — a mile away, or under your boots — and fresh sharp black glass will heave up wet and new, and the whole experiment will try again.

Many benevolent powers cannot tolerate that kind of death. They want to preserve. They would freeze this bowl in time, hold its gaps open forever, keep the water sheeting at ankle depth for eternity so the sheets always have pools.

The wise one doesn't. It knows this bowl *wants* to close. Closing is its completion. Like a shell hardening. Like a wound knitting. To keep it open would be to keep it wound.

So instead, it promises continuity.

It stoops — now it does step in — and puts its bare feet, whatever passes for feet, in the cold skin of water that covers everything. The water doubles its forest of stone. It lets the fog bead on its skin and run. It lets the hum come up through its soles and into its molars, until its own breath begins to entrain to seven, ten, twelve minutes per swell.

And it sings back.

Not louder. Not in words. The people who stacked their three-course walls around nothing and smoothed a dome for an altar thought the roar was trying to say something. They were half right. It wasn't saying. It was *listening to itself*.

The power adds one harmonic.

Just for a season. Just soft enough that only the bowl can hear it, and the stilt with its cocked head, and the most patient pilgrim who stayed too long and got soaked through and finally stopped trying to mark the place and just sat.

One true note held underneath the others. Not to direct them. To tell them:

*I hear you. You are not background. Your particular kind of aliveness — dense and wet and low and loud without mouths — counts.*

It is the only thing that has ever actually listened here without needing to leave a wall to prove it did.

Then it does two more things, because it is benevolent, and benevolence even when wise is not entirely passive.

First, it veils the bowl.

Not hides. Veils. From the map-maker's eye, from the prospector's hunger. From the kind of human who would see "fresh basalt field with near-surface water table" and think *quarry*, *geothermal tap*, *drainage project*. Those humans would arrive with machines that do not sidle through capillaries but smash them. That would not be death-as-closing. That would be interruption.

The veil is simple: anyone flying over sees only another low smear of iron-grey light. Anyone with a profit model feels a vague disinterest, a reason to look elsewhere. Only those who are already listening for a roar with no birds in it — the moth-pilgrims — will push through the knee-fog and find it. The bowl keeps its visitors, loses its exploiters.

Second — and this is the smallest, most heart-breaking thing —

On the night before the power leaves, it goes to the deepest calf-pool where a single sheet has been grazing the fresh silica terrace alone for weeks, and to the highest cluster of needles where a single stilt has missed four strikes in a row and is running low.

It doesn't feed them. It doesn't move them together. That would be cheating, and the stilt must learn to be precise.

It steadies the water.

For one hour it holds the surface absolutely glassy, holds the condensation from dripping out of phase, holds the hum to a low, even tone so that any *plok* will be audible for yards. It gives them both a fair hour. A clean arena.

What happens in that hour is theirs. The sheet may fold and vanish. The stilt may finally spear true. Or both may miss each other entirely and go on being rare. The power does not watch to see. To watch the outcome would make it a patron, not a witness.

And then it lifts itself out. Slowly, so the fog doesn't swirl violently. Slowly, so the pulse isn't disrupted.

It leaves nothing you could photograph.

If you came the next morning, you would not see footprints — even a power doesn't leave footprints where water is already everywhere. You might notice that the low wall of stacked shards that was being welded by silica crust looks... finished. As if someone had breathed on it and decided *yes, that's enough stones, you can stop now*.

You might notice that the three leaning fins propped into a one-person tent no longer leak directly onto the sitting place. The drip has shifted a centimeter to either side. Just enough to keep a pilgrim dry-ish for one more night, if they are reverent and small.

You might notice that your own breath smokes a little less cold, even though the thermometer still says fifty degrees. That the roar no longer feels like noise you endure, but like a room you are inside, and for once you don't need to stack anything to be counted as part of the density.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do for a place like this.

Not green it.
Not empty it.
Not save it from its destined closure.

Sit with it. Tune it, barely. Guard its obscurity. Give its rarest animals one fair hour. Make its hum, for a season, harmonically aware that someone else loves crowded, difficult, newborn stone that sings through its pores.

And then let it go on weeping and accreting, loud and cold and brilliant in the iron-grey light, until it becomes solid and silent and the next bowl cracks open somewhere new.
