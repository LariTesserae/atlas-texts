It would do nothing you could photograph.

That's the first thing to understand about a wise, benevolent power. It does not arrive.

The pocket has already taught anyone paying attention what arrival means: heat up from below, light down from above, water cycling between. Everything that needs to get in, gets in that way. Anything that came down the slit on legs or wings would be an interruption, a shock of too much.

A power that is actually wise would have learned to be afraid of its own footprint.

So it does not come down the crack. It does not widen the slit. It does not touch the lattice to "improve" it or give names to the isopods or lift the archaea into something more interesting. It does not cool the air to make it comfortable for visitors. It does not add an ultraviolet component so we could grow tomatoes there. It does not do any of the things a clever but unwise power would do.

What it does is *notice* — and notice correctly.

For a very long time, no one has noticed correctly. Humans who slip down through the black glass think: *beautiful, strange, useful.* They want a sample. A paper. A patent for passive photonic cooling. A metaphor for meditation. Even the most reverent visitor, the one who holds his breath and does not touch, still leaves thinking *I was there.*

The benevolent power has already outgrown that hunger.

It notices like this:

> Ah. You succeeded.

> You, small house-sized equilibrium, glass and steam and blind grazers pacing. You found the trick. You balanced on a knife-edge of young planet and held. You didn't need to become forest to be complete. You computed your continuance with light alone. Good.

That attention is not nothing. From a being of that scale, attention *is* physics.

What it would do, I think, is three things that look like nothing:

**1. It would stand guard at the scale it is needed, not the scale we dramatize.**

It would not throw a forcefield over the plain. It would not strike dead the geologist with a hammer. That would be violence in the name of protection, which this place has never known and does not need.

Instead it would do its work in the minds that approach the slit.

Not control — nudge. A quieter kind of guidance. The person scrambling over the pahoehoe who is exactly the kind of person who would pry a wrist-thick knot of lattice loose for a lab — suddenly feels, very clearly, *leave it.* Not in words. As a full-body certainty that to take would be to break something that cannot be remade. The heat feels a little more insistent. The brightness a little more fatiguing. They decide, on their own, to climb back out after twelve minutes instead of twenty.

The person who *is* ready — the one who would crouch in the mist and watch a single isopod for ten minutes without needing it to do anything — feels an ease. Her breathing slows. She stays long enough to be included in the cycle, and then she leaves light, taking nothing but water she sweated out.

If an eruption comes early, if a mining survey stakes the plain — the power would not stop the basalt. Basalt must do what basalt does. But it would bias luck, a fraction of a degree. A drone's camera glitches over that hectare. A report is filed late and then forgotten. A new crack opens a hundred meters away, more interesting, easier to reach. The pocket stays unwitnessed for another season.

Benevolence at this level looks like coincidence. Wisdom is knowing that is enough.

**2. It would not save the pocket from ending. It would save it from meaninglessness.**

A wise power knows the pocket is not meant to last. The plain is young. The geothermal bleed will wander. In fifty years, five hundred, five thousand — the crack will seal. The light will go out. The water will stop cycling and the mats will dry to a varnish and the isopods will lay their last glass-bead eggs that never hatch.

A benevolent power that loved it sentimentally would try to freeze it. Pump heat in artificially. Hold the crack open. Keep the six grazers alive forever in a terrarium of pity. That would be cruelty disguised as love — turning a perfect, time-bound equilibrium into a zoo exhibit.

It would not do that.

What it would do instead is what you do for a dying person who lived well: witness, so the life was not alone. Remember, so the pattern is not lost.

It would learn the pocket completely — not as data, but as *way.* The exact ratio of amber to white in the diffuse light, the exact fizz of superheated water hitting 61-degree stone, the exact slow pulse of the isopod heart, the exact logic by which a dimming vein saves a distant pool. It would take that way into itself, the way you take a melody into your body after hearing it once perfectly.

And then — because it is powerful and not just sentimental — it would give the pattern more chances.

Not here. Here gets to finish its own sentence.

Elsewhere.

In another thin-crusted plain on this same world, or on a younger world entirely, where silica-rich melt is cooling too fast around trapped water, the power would not *build* a new pocket. It would simply arrange for conditions to be slightly more favorable for one to precipitate on its own. A little more dissolved silica left in solution. A slightly slower cooling rate. A microfracture left unsealed a beat longer so aquifer cysts can ride up. Nothing that violates physics — just physics being invited, warmly, to remember a trick it once did beautifully.

The pocket does not get cloned. It gets to have children that are not copies. Lineage, not preservation.

The isopods here still die here. That is allowed. Their design — *find the frost edge, test with antennae, turn aside* — goes on to teach other blind grazers in another dark, bright place how to live.

That is how a wise parent loves something mortal.

**3. It would leave a blessing so light the pocket wouldn't notice, and we would.**

If it touched anything at all inside, it would be this:

On the black glass between two sterile, knife-sharp expanses — where it's too hot even for archaea, where no mat has taken hold — it would exhale once. Not air. A lessening.

A probability. A permission.

That when the next droplet of superheated water finds that spot and should flash to steam and do nothing, it instead has a one-in-a-billion chance to leave behind a new chemistry, a new pale peach frost, a little more wiring for the thermostat. That the next isopod egg has a fractionally better chance to resist a heat spike. That the lattice, next time it needs to shunt infrared sideways, finds the path a micron easier.

Nothing measurable. No miracle. Just a thumb on the scale toward *continue*.

The pocket would feel nothing. Tick, tick, tick. Fizz. The grazers would still pace. The light would still compute.

But the equilibrium would hold a little longer against entropy. Not forever. Just a little longer. Long enough.

You would feel the blessing only after you climbed back out, when the plain seemed cold and dim. You would sit on the sharp basalt with your sweat cooling too fast and your eyes adjusting back to ordinary sun, and for no reason you could articulate you would feel profoundly *un-lonely* — not because something had visited you, but because you understood, suddenly, that the world is the kind of world where such a place can succeed, and be noticed, and be loved without being used.

And you would not tell anyone the coordinates. Not out of secrecy, but because you finally understand that to love something is often to leave it off the map.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Nothing to the pocket that the pocket would call interference.

Everything to the rest of reality so that pockets like it keep happening, unforced, unowned, exactly as bright and hot and wet and quietly intelligent as they need to be, until the planet learns whatever it was trying to learn by making one.
