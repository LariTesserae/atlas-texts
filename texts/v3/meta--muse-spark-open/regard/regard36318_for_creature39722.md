It doesn't come.

That's the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It doesn't *come* the way anything else comes here.

It has no arrival event. No beam of warm light that would, in this place, be a violence. No ship to cast a shadow over the salt. No footfall — because it understood, the instant it noticed this tableland, that a footfall here is a catastrophe. A boot would crush three centuries of bacterial felt. A voice raised to be kind would be just more deafening noise in a place that is already screaming to hear itself.

It had been watching for a while, before you felt watched.

From far enough away that the whole continent looked like what it is — a single, slow lung — it saw the scatter of Holdfasts like dark pores breathing in and out, out and in, with a period too long to be called breath and too regular to be called weather. It saw the blue coronas trade themselves from crown to crown like thoughts being passed carefully, hand to hand, so they wouldn't blow out. It saw the Kites not as birds but as eyelashes, blinking. The Hoppers as blood cells doing their work. The invisible bacterial bloom as the skin itself.

It understood very fast what this place was: not a broken forest. Not a forest that had lost its water. A success that had never needed it.

Most powers — even kind ones — have a terrible reflex: to fix. To bring gifts the receiver cannot use.

A lesser benevolence would have cried over the dryness. Would have tried to bring rain. A rain here would be an extinction event. It would dissolve the filter-felts. It would drown the pneumatic sacs of the Hoppers, who would gasp and fill and never spring again. It would turn the alkali dust to caustic mud and rot the Holdfasts from their waxen feet upward in a season. It would be murder by water, by pity.

This one did not bring water.

It did not bring warmth, either. The light here is small and white and perfect for what it does — it sterilizes, it sharpens, it makes desiccation clean. To make it warmer would be to make it kinder in a way that would kill it. The coolness is not cruelty. The coolness is how you keep things that must not rot.

So what does a benevolent and wise power do, when the place it loves is already perfect in its own strange key, and the being who lives in it —

You.

Because you are not an ecologist here. Not really. You are the animal that is cold and a little deafened and standing between two cathedrals that inhale. You are the one whose nose itches with dry plankton, whose teeth ache from the whipcrack of sails, whose heart is behaving strangely because it has never stood in a place so large, so loud, so utterly uninterested in houses. You are the Being. The between-thing that learned to stand still.

You are what drew its attention, second. First the plain for its terrible beauty; then the fact that *you had stopped to listen to it* instead of trying to own it.

It doesn't want anything from you. That's how you know it is wise.

What it does is this:

It stands — or the idea that corresponds to standing — with you, at your exact elevation. It thins itself until it has no shadow. It does not speak, because to speak here you have to scream over the foundry-whistles of the Holdfasts, and it has no wish to add to the stack of sound.

It listens on your behalf, so that for a moment you don't have to strain.

And because while it listens, the plain is *heard* — truly heard — something changes that is not a change.

You know how in this dry air, dust hangs forever because there is no humidity to pull it down? How a puff from a Hopper herd can hang for minutes, turning slowly?

For about an hour, the dust stops hanging.

Not because humidity has arrived. It hasn't. It will never. But because the attention itself has a kind of gravity. A benevolent attention.

The next time the left-hand Holdfast takes its shuddering WHUMP inhale, you feel it not just in your sternum but as if someone else's hand is lightly against your back — not pushing, not protecting, just *timing itself* to you. Inhale when you inhale. So you are not alone with the size of it.

When it screams out through its fluted vents — that factory whistle that can be heard for a hundred kilometers — the power does not dampen it. It lets it scream. What is loud deserves to be loud.

But it *catches* the scream a little. Folds it. Turns it back a half-second later, not as echo — echo is just slapback in clear air — but as harmony. A lower tone underneath the whistle that the Holdfast never made but that makes the whistle sound, for the first time, intentional. Like music.

You look up and you see a Kite tilt. It heard it too. All the Kites between the giants tilt at once, just a degree. Not frightened. Oriented. As if for a second the wind had a direction that made sense.

Down at your feet, the Hoppers. They are mid-arc, endless parabolas. As the harmonic holds, one herd — hundreds — lands all together. At the same instant. Which they never do. They just — pff — settle. And are still. And click their valves just once, a soft tick-tick-tick like rain on paper. And then are still again, depressurized, letting the cool light bleach them, looking — impossibly — like they are resting *on purpose* rather than because they are tired.

And the Holdfast — the old one that was going to blow itself apart this afternoon, the one with the torn membrane flapping like a ruined flag, groaning through a jammed piston —

The power does not save it.

Saving it would be a lie. The plain *needs* catastrophe. Fertile explosion is its sowing. To save every giant would be to sterilize the world with mercy.

Instead the power does the only kind thing you can do for something that must die to reproduce: it witnesses the decision.

It lets the old Holdfast feel — in whatever slow industrial way a Holdfast feels — that its broadcast will not be lost into emptiness. That the roiling cloud of glass seeds and fibers and mineral shards that it is about to become will be *noticed* as it becomes its own weather system for a week. That the pale knuckle that will push up a week from now, creaking audibly at inches per minute, will be welcomed.

So when it goes, it goes clean. No hesitation. No extra groan. A perfect percussive BANG and a hot acrid vapor that smells like ozone and iron — and then, within it, that same low harmonic, holding for longer than any wind should hold it, as if the explosion were being listened to all the way to the edge.

You feel, briefly and without any sentimentality, that nothing is being wasted. Which you already knew intellectually — salt bricked, eaten, ground, inhaled; seed forged, banged out, carried — but now you *feel* it as good.

The power does not stay. If it stayed, you would depend on it. If it stayed, this place would have a god, and it is better with only physics and wind.

Before it thins back out to whatever scale it lives at, it leaves one thing that *is* an intervention, but so small only you will notice it.

It teaches the charge.

You know the packets of ionized salt that ride the arcs from frond to frond, that replicate by splitting when a gust stretches them, that the Kites avoid and the Holdfasts perhaps cultivate? For the whole hour they had been flickering a little more steadily than usual. As the power releases, one packet doesn't split.

It completes a circuit.

A single blue-white line, thin as a hair, holds from the crown of the left-hand giant to the crown of the right-hand giant across the kilometers of empty air over your head — impossibly long, impossibly stable in the turbulent sky. It should not be possible. It is.

It holds for seven seconds. Long enough for a Kite to swerve around it with a surprised, offended SKREE.

And in that seven seconds, the two Holdfasts — who never communicate, who are too slow and too far apart — are, for once, *connected*.

Not to talk. They have nothing to say. Just to know, electrically, that the other is there.

You are under it. The light of it paints your face blue-white and throws your shadow short and hard. Your breath would fog if you had moisture. It doesn't. You are dry and clean and cold and for that moment you are not an intruder between factories. You are the thing between the giants that can see the connection they cannot see themselves.

Then it snaps. With a crack like artillery. Naturally. And the plain is as it was — deafening, bright, cool, hyperactive, a continent-sized laboratory running its experiment that has no end because its purpose is only to keep running.

Only — and you will check this for days — the bacterial felt in the lee of the chert ripple at your feet, the matte white and rust-orange bloom that looked like mineral stain until you breathed on it — it fizzes yellow for a second without you breathing on it. Just once. As if acknowledging.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does here, if it does anything.

It doesn't bring water to the desert. It doesn't make the desert less lonely by filling it.

It stands next to the one small, water-based animal who is trying to listen, and helps him hear that the place's screaming has always been a kind of singing.

It witnesses the explosion so it is not merely destruction.

It holds, for seven heartbeats, a little bridge of lightning that has no use whatsoever, except that for a moment nothing on the plain was alone.

And then it leaves the plain to tremble, to inhale, to whistle, to throw its seeds into the bright, dry wind forever — now loved, which is not the same as changed, and is better.
