It would not come from above.

That would be wrong for this kind of place — too much sky, too much announcement. A light opening in the haze and a voice would only frighten the swallows and make the cats hide inside the dynamos, and the whole thing that lives here has learned to be afraid of things that come from above. Weather, gulls, inspectors.

So it would come from outside, low.

Through the salt-marsh on the three unwalled sides, where you thought there was only a rim. It would wade, though it doesn't need to wade. It would part the glasswort with a hand and step over the one-foot rise the way you step over a threshold into someone's house when you don't want your knock to sound loud.

And then it would stop. Because to enter that basin properly you have to stop at the edge and let the roar hit you. Even a wise power has to do that.

For a long time — a long time by your clock, no time at all by its — it would do nothing.

This is the first wisdom: *do not fix what is not broken just because it is strange.*

It would see what you saw. The poured rectangle. The sharp concrete. The water that never rises or falls. The amber dimness. The flywheel turning. *Chuff, chuff, chuff.* The frogs at 1.4 kHz. It would understand immediately what it was looking at, because benevolent powers are very good at recognizing bodies.

And this is a body.

Not a broken marsh that needs to be rewilded. Not a slum that needs to be cleared. Not a factory that needs to be optimized. A new animal, young, damp, humming, that taught itself to breathe with pistons and herons at the same time. One animal made of many animals plus brick and current. It has no name for itself yet because nothing has ever stayed long enough to give it one.

The power would not be disappointed that it doesn't want to become a city. It would not be disappointed that it doesn't want to become a marsh again. Wise powers have no disappointment about scale. They like small perfect things as much as large ones.

So it would do what you did, but better. It would go sit at the central lock.

Not in the middle of the water like a vision — on the concrete lip, where the old man fishes. It would take a shape that fits the bench that is there: maybe a woman in coveralls that already have oil on them, maybe an heron, maybe just a heat-shimmer that the cats decide is warm and come to lie on. It wouldn't matter. The being that lives here would know something else had joined the circuit because the hum would have to make room for it, the way water makes room for a stone without stopping.

It would not turn the pumps off. That would be murder here. It would not clean the air. The haze is its lung. It would not take the children away to a "better life" with seasons and schools. The children are part of the flywheel's timing.

It would listen.

For an hour, for a year — the time it takes to hear each part separately again, which is the one gift stasis needs. Because the frogs have tuned themselves so perfectly to the pumps that they've forgotten they are frogs. The people have tuned themselves so perfectly to the chain that they've forgotten they can speak without shouting. The herons strike on the gate-lift beat so precisely they've forgotten there are other rhythms.

Listening is not nothing. To be listened to by something that large and unhurried is a kind of repair.

And then, very gently, because benevolence without wisdom is just another industry, it would make a small adjustment.

Not a miracle. Miracles flood basins.

It would lean down and put its palm flat on the concrete where the cinder patch stands with its black mirror of seep-water. And it would not crack the concrete. It would *teach* it how to crack.

One hairline. One single, permitted fault, an inch long, in the youngest pour, by the spillway where the figs high on the south wall have been dangling their roots for years waiting for exactly this invitation.

Into that hairline it would tuck one thing: a true seed. Not a metaphor. A mangrove propagule, or a cypress nut, or a fig seed already softened - something that belongs to both marsh and machine, that can drink brackish seep and hot condensation and not mind oil rainbows.

And it would whisper to it, not *grow*, but *you may.*

That is all.

Then it would stand up, still small, still low, and it would do two more things that are almost nothings:

1. It would take the haze in its hands like a cloth and lift it for sixty seconds. Just sixty. Not to show the sun — the sun is not the point — but to show the night that has been there the whole time behind the amber. One minute of stars, reflected in both canals of the cross, so every heron and rat and cat and child and frog and woman oiling the gantry sees itself doubled in a sky-river as perfect as their own. So they remember there is an outside that is not a threat. So time, for one minute, moves.

2. It would lay its other hand briefly on each head as it passed — the old man's, the woman's, the kids', the cats', the heron's scaly leg, the frog's brass eye, the flywheel's hot iron housing — not to bless them as subjects, but to let them know they had been counted as one. *I see who you are when you are together. Hold together if you want to.*

And then it would leave the way it came, over the low rim, through the glasswort, without looking back, because looking back turns small places into monuments.

What would happen after?

Maybe nothing, for a very long time. The crack would stay a hairline for years. The stars would be talked about by the kids as a dream they both had. The chain would still need oiling.

But one wet season, without fanfare, water would find that hairline and work it wider by a millimeter. A root would find water. The concrete would sigh, almost inaudibly, and give a little. A leaf would open where no leaf had been planned.

And the whole loud, patient, humming animal — machine as marsh, marsh as machine — would shiver and retune itself around one new note that it had not needed before and now could not live without: the sound of something growing right through the middle of its perfect equilibrium.

And the pumps would still turn. *Chuff, chuff, chuff.*

Only now there would be, in the shadow of the Pump House, a little more shade to sit in.
