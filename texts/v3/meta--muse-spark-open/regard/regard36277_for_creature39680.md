It would not come down.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a benevolent and wise power. Anything else — anything hungry, or clever, or lonely — would come down into the Basin. Would part the low cloud lid like a curtain, would announce itself with light and wind, would land heavy enough to make the mineral pans ripple.

This one would not.

It has seen enough finished places to know one when it breathes. And the Basin is finished. Not complete, not perfect — finished the way a wall is finished when the mortar has cured and the seep has found its path through it. It works. It holds.

So at first, it would do nothing that looks like doing at all.

It would take the rim.

Not the whole thirty kilometers. Just a stretch of weathered limestone and brick-colored chert on the north side where the old baths were capped. It would make itself low and long to match it. It would settle its attention there the way heat settles, as a weight, as a warm hand — and it would simply watch for a long time, long enough that watching becomes a form of tending.

It would learn the schedule, because every true place has one.

06:14 flour vent — pigeons lift — resettle.
Tannery sluice — pink water — catfish — heron strike — man with hose, not looking up.
Midge rise — hold breath — dragonflies cut — swifts cut — breathable again.
Pipe tick, expansion; pipe tick, contraction. Bicycle hiss. Tide in the walls.

It would not be disappointed by the repetition. That is what the Basin offers to a power that is used to prayers for *change*. It does not ask to be cooled. It does not ask to be greened. It does not ask to be made important. It asks — if it asks at all — to be allowed to continue its quiet commerce: mineral to steam to brick to flesh and back again.

A wise power knows how rare that prayer is. Most places beg to be somewhere else.

So the first thing it would do is answer that prayer by *not answering it*.

It would not bring trees.

This is what a lesser benevolence would do, out of pity. It would see the water marks three stories high and the children who have never stood under shade that was not a wall, and it would weep and make it rain cool, clean rain, and plant quick green things that would die within a week in the salt and the steam — and in dying, poison the bacterial mats, and starve the midges, and scatter the pigeons, and make the herons leave their concrete outfalls in confusion. It would break the rule: *Water wins.*

This power has learned not to confuse beauty with rescue.

It would not cool the air.
It would not drain the seep.
It would not cure the mineral pans into soil.
It would not make the emptiness less empty.

Because it finally sees what you felt on the canal bridge at dusk: The emptiness *is* the care. The spacing *is* the kindness. The Basin is not failing to be a forest or a valley. It succeeded at being a bowl that can hold everyone on separate schedules so no one has to fight. To fill it up would be violence.

What would it do instead?

Small and plentiful things. Crack-dwelling things.

---

It would breathe *with* it, first. In the hour when the morning steam is thick enough to hide the rim, it would take that steam into itself and give it back a degree cooler, so that condensation beads just a little more cleanly on the metal, and the laundry strung across the alleys dries by noon instead of never. Not a miracle. A nudge. Enough that the child coughing through the brick at night coughs once less.

It would put its attention like a thumb over the places where the outside world presses in at the lip.

Satellite surveys that would have logged the geothermal field as "untapped resource" come back with a curious static, a warm haze that makes analysts mark it as *atmospheric interference, reschedule*. Developers who drive out to see the flats of hot mineral pan for a lithium brine operation find their tires crunching wrong, their boots soaked through with rust-water, and feel, for no reason they can name, that they are trespassing in someone's house where the family is all home and quiet behind the doors. They leave.

It would not hide the Basin. It would just make sure only the kind of attention that is willing to sit on the rim for a long time can see it clearly.

At dusk, when the pipes tick as they contract and the pigeons go quiet all at once, it would do one thing that *can* be seen, because even a finished place deserves to know it is not forgotten.

It would lift the lid.

Just for one night. Just for a few hours after the bats have poured out and before the shift change.

The low cloud deck that has capped the Basin for as long as anyone can remember — that dim, wet, body-temperature sky — would thin. Not break violently. Thin, like worn cotton, like steam clearing from a mirror.

And the people would step out onto balconies and pipe bridges and canal crossings and see, for the first time in their lives or since their grandparents' grandparents, stars.

Not as cold pinpricks. The air is still too thick for that. But as soft, wet lights through a veil, reflected doubly in every mineral pan, every canal, every puddle on the salted lots, so that the whole shallow concavity glitters — above and below — and the Basin looks, for one night, exactly like what it is: a vessel holding light in water.

The pigeons would stay quiet. The herons would stay still. The tide in the walls would pause, listening. The people would not cheer. They are not loud people. But an old woman who tends a travertine terrace in a corner with a toothbrush would look up and, without knowing why, nod.

And the herons — each on its claimed outfall, stained orange to the knee — would lift their heads at the same moment, not to fish, but to look.

The power, low and long on its stretch of rim, would look with them.

In the morning the cloud would settle again, as it should. Condensation dripping from every eave like rain that never fell. Bicycle hiss. Flour vent at 06:14.

Nothing changed, and everything witnessed.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place that has already figured out how to live: it does not save it. It keeps its vigil. It ensures the water keeps winning on its own terms. It grants one clear night so that those below know their quiet, endless, teeming balance is not invisible to the world that holds the world.

And then, very quietly, so as not to disturb the dragonflies hunting in straight lines down the alleys, it would stay.
