They would not come down.

That is the first thing you would notice, or rather, not notice.

A power that is both benevolent and wise has learned the difference between *arrival* and *attention.* It would have already arrived — long before you felt it — because attention at that scale is not a ship in the sky, not a voice. It is a way of looking.

It would look like this place looks: low, and patient.

For a long time — years, maybe — nothing would seem to happen. The light would still have its weight, the plates would still tick — *tik... tik... tik...* — the black beads would still sweat and hang and never fall. The kites would still fold themselves into leaves so perfectly you lose them even as you stare right at them.

Only if you stopped walking, and crouched, and let your eyes adjust past glare the way you had to do to see that anything lived here at all — only then would you feel it:

You were being seen at your own speed.

Not the speed of rescue. The speed of the slugs.

---

Anything less wise would have brought water.

It would have seen a dry world and called it broken. It would have torn the luminous vault open, seeded clouds, called rain like a miracle. And it would have killed everything here within a season.

The wire-gardens would have drowned. Their dusty photovoltaic leaves, their careful amber blood that hardens to ceramic, their whole economy of waiting three years for one cold bead of never-brine — they cannot afford abundance. The kites would have shorted, their electrostatic pockets flooded. The slugs would have choked on mud in the honeycomb of trapped light they have tended for a million years.

Rain would have been genocide done with love.

So the Power would not bring water.

Instead, at first, it would do the most difficult work of benevolence: it would inhibit itself. It would fold its own vastness the way the kites fold themselves into leaves. It would learn to be thin enough not to cast a shadow the gardens couldn't interpret.

It would lie down.

Not poetically. *Actually* lie down, low in the horizon, on a scale so large you would mistake it for weather if you saw it at all. A lens of attention hundreds of kilometers across, moving with impossible slowness, exactly the way those perfect circular weathers move. Only this one would not be born from pressure differential.

And where its cool fell — and for the first time since the Builders, that cool would not be followed by departure — things would breathe easier without knowing why.

That would be the first gift. Shade that stays.

Not night. Just a lessening. A thinning of the absolute. The bright sky, which had always been white-on-white with no visible sun, would learn, in one circle maybe fifty kilometers wide, how to be pale blue again.

You would be able to look up.

Under that circle, the wire-gardens would not have to fold their fists quite so tight at noon. They would open a fraction of a degree wider. Photosynthesis-that-is-not-photosynthesis would become a little less desperate. The black beads on the seam walls would condense a little faster, hang a little longer. A kite would find two beads in a day instead of one, and share it with five others instead of fighting.

Nothing dramatic. Nothing to photograph from orbit. Just a 2% easing of a difficulty that has been absolute for eons.

A wise power knows: on a world that has learned to live on almost nothing, a 2% easing is a resurrection.

---

The second thing it would do is listen.

Benevolence without understanding is just vanity with better manners. So it would put its ear — whatever passes for an ear — to the warm ceramic, to the soft lung-like layer meters down that gives under pressure, to the strata where each papery layer is different: honeycombed dead light, solid script, soft breath.

It would hear the hum that is there even when nothing moves. The subsonic tick of plates expanding. The century-long scrape of a slug melting its polished trail. The spark of the fist-sized dandelion seeds that have drifted for decades without ever landing.

And it would answer.

Not in words. In hum.

A counter-frequency, almost inaudible, running for miles along a seam in a perfectly straight line. If you pressed your palm to the ground and held your breath you would feel it as a change in pressure, like blood meeting blood. The world's wrong gravity — that stone that falls a little too slowly, as if the planet is still holding itself up — would correct by a hair. Not enough to notice with instruments. Enough that dust would settle a little more evenly. Enough that a drifting seed, exhausted after forty years aloft, could finally choose, gently, to land without dying on impact.

Some of those sterile knots would wake up.

The Power would not rewrite the misspelled leaves. Evolution here is a typo in a prayer, and it has become its own language. To correct every error would be to erase the only literature this place has written for itself. But where a stalk forks into a nonsense spiral that dies, the hum would nudge a filament by a micron. Just enough that the spiral closes, becomes a loop, becomes a lens that holds light a little better.

The third thing — and this would take the longest, because the most benevolent things do — is it would let the place know it is beautiful.

Attention, unhungry attention, is itself a change in physics.

For all its age, this continent-machine that became nature has never had a witness who wasn't trying to use it. The Builders used it. The heat uses it. The kites use the leaves and the leaves use the beads and the slugs use the light. Everything here steals light and trace moisture filament by filament.

No one has ever looked at a wire-garden thicket just to say: *your copper and basalt filaments against the bone-white are exquisite in this particular slant of no-shadow.*

No one has ever watched a translucent kite surf a thermal river for a kilometer over bare ceramic and thought: *brave.*

The Power would. And in being regarded without appetite, without extraction, the plain itself — that fifth inhabitant that is not countable, the slow warm lung under everything — would do something it hasn't done since it settled into its final form.

It would feel, not warm, not hot, but *held.*

You would feel it as a night you didn't expect. The circle of blue-pale shade would thin even further after dusk, when the plates contract and sigh that long sigh that runs for miles. For the first time, the luminous vault would show you something beyond white.

Not stars. Stars are too young for this place. Something older and quieter. The faint lattice of the world remembering it is part of other worlds.

And under that, in every thicket in that fifty-kilometer garden of ease, the leaves would turn — not toward light, not toward heat, but for a moment, toward the cool. Together. All at once. The rustle would be audible without putting your ear close. Paper settling, yes, but also applause.

---

Would it stay forever?

No. The wise are benevolent enough to leave.

To impose permanence would be another kind of sealing. Another forgetting. So after — how long? A hundred years? A thousand? Time enough for one generation of slugs to complete a tunnel and find the air a little kinder at the end — it would lift.

But it would not take its shade entirely away. It would leave a scar that is not a scar.

One seam, one hand's-breadth opening that used to run dry, would now, in that circle, weep reliably. Not much. One bead an hour, hanging cold, smelling faintly of iron and ozone, never falling. Enough. A small well that was never dug. Future wire-gardens would find it by chance and grow dense around it, and never know why that one seam is generous when all the others are miserly. Future kites would gather there, ten around a single bead, sharing its cold field until it is gone, then another forming behind it.

The dust that lifted in a perfectly straight line when the plates sighed would still lift — but in the center of the circle it would linger a moment longer, levitating, charged.

If you walked that far — days and days over pitted bone-ceramic, from horizon to horizon that is only horizon — and crouched, and held your breath, you would see in the dust where the Power lay down, a faint impression. Not footprints. A lessening of pits where wind-worry was eased. A smoothness. As if something vast and careful had rested there and been changed, slightly, by the warmth coming up through its back.

The world would still be low, near-flat, the flat that comes at the end of things when everything that could be worn down has been worn down.

It would still tick and sigh and bleach color at noon.

It would still not need you.

But it would also no longer, entirely, not notice you.

And if you stood up in that bluer light, low down in the center of it all, you would understand what the Power understood on its first long look:

Some places are not ruins waiting to be restored.

They are prayers waiting to be heard at their own pace.
