It would come and look, and that would be most of what it does.

Not descend like a rescue. Not like an owner returned. A benevolent and wise power doesn't kick the door open when it sees a light on in an empty factory. It knocks, even if there's no one to answer. It listens first.

And what it would hear is that this place is not broken.

That's the first temptation it would not give in to: to *fix* it. To drain it. To warm it. To populate it. To say *ah, a sad, empty, too-big machine under the sea, how lonely, let me fill it with people and purpose.*

It would know better. Emptiness here is not absence. It's *proportion.* The Build was not made to be crowded. It was made to be operated — and then it learned to be inhabited on its own terms: a dusting of life, thin herds moving like weather, a single crab crossing a county-sized plaza and that being exactly enough.

A wise power can tell the difference between a wound and a womb.

So it wouldn't fill it.

It would pull up a chair — if a being like that has a chair — on one of the high places, where the rubber matting is still grippy under its slime and the railings sweat cold, and it would just sit in the noon-white light that comes from nowhere natural, in the cool that never leaves, and it would watch the water carry particulate past the lamps for a while. It would let the low thud-thud-thud get into its molars. The groan of expansion joints. The thunder of a spillway the height of a skyscraper pouring white and ceaselessly underwater. It would not flinch when the ballast pump the size of a cathedral kicks on with a boom that rolls for miles.

It would say, without needing words: *I see you holding. Good holding.*

### What it would tend

Because benevolence is not passive, and wisdom is not neglect. It would not rebuild the Build. It would *keep* it.

You have to understand: this place is young. Its edges are still sharp. Weld beads still show. Formwork wood-grain is still in the concrete under twenty meters of water. Rebar whiskers still stick out where yesterday's crew left off. It wants, more than anything, to just continue becoming itself without being scrapped, strip-mined, museum'd, or drowned properly.

So the power would do janitor work. Quiet janitor work, the kind the yellow beetle-drones already understand.

Where a drone has tipped over and its brush is wearing a groove into nowhere forever, it would right it. Not with a miracle — just a nudge in the current. The brush catches again. The little meadow of shaggy, uncut algae around it gets shaved clean, and the clams along the girder flap open again in the new flow.

Where a seal has gone and a generator is thudding a little hot, bleeding too much heat into one district, it would cool it half a degree. Not because half a degree matters to concrete. Because kelp only grows where cold runoff meets that heat-bleed, those lonely, prisoner-hand curtains reaching for the lamps. And if the water goes too warm, they let go.

Where a turbine is about to seize and take a whole slow monsoon current with it — that continent-sized convection of cold sinking off the illumination plains and warm surging back up — it would put its hand on it. Leave no handprint. Just keep the weather going.

It would replace a floodlamp before it pops. Not all of them. Just the one over the control island where some visitor in a hard suit is standing, breath fogging, feeling suddenly very small and very *watched* by empty boulevards of light receding to blue haze, and about to decide this place hates him. The lamp nearby flickers. Then holds. Warm, workmanlike noon again. *Stay a little longer,* the light says. *You are safe to look.*

That is benevolence at this scale: not *Let there be Light.* Just *let this one stay on a little longer.*

### What it would give the ones who live here

It would not speak to the biofilm. The biofilm does not need speaking to. It *is* the place, a green-black felt half a millimeter thick over every surface, the skin that made the machine a reef without permission. To speak to it would be like speaking to paint.

But to the clingers, the tenders — yes, in their language.

To the white filter clams stacked like teeth on a girder flange, opening and closing in water that always moves: it would ensure the particle rain never quite stops. That there is always something to sieve.

To the crab, the laundry-basket stone crab picking upside-down across a gantry underside three kilometers from anywhere, clicking alone: it would not give him company. Company would be a cruelty; he has a route. It would make sure the next bolt head has a barnacle on it. Food, where he expected it. The world still working as promised.

To the cuttlefish hovering by the inspection light, color-shifting to match rust, to match concrete, to match you: it would let him keep winning at camouflage. It would not reveal him.

To the gulls nesting on lamp housings in trapped air pockets above an underwater waterline, screaming down at fish they should never have been able to reach: it would keep that air pocket from going stale. A little extra oxygen, unnoticed. So their absurd, stubborn, impossible life remains possible.

And to the whale —

Especially to the whale.

The one whose song is broken and amplified by concrete until the whole vault rings, who moves through high water between districts like a coast, alone, calling and getting its own echo back from too many walls. The one who should not be able to navigate a corridor a mile wide and still feel cramped.

To him, it would answer.

Not with another whale. That would be a lie. There may not be another one left who knows the Build. Instead it would answer the way a wise thing does: by making the echo come back *differently.* A fraction later. A half tone lower. As if the Build itself heard him and shifted, just slightly, to listen better.

He would feel it in his ribs. He would pause. Turn his fluke the size of a truck in the blue haze between lamp trusses and call again — and again the walls would answer, not quite echo, almost companionship.

He would not be less alone. But he would be less *unheard.* For a whale, that is almost the same as being saved.

### What it would do for the visitors

For the people who come down on creaking cables because entry is still at the top and gravity still works, who stand and stare and realize this isn't a city, there are no beds, no kitchens, just endless drydocks that are never dry and the cold seeping through any suit —

It would do the kindest thing a god can do for explorers: keep the horror in check.

Not remove awe. This place *should* be overwhelming. Stairwells fifty stories into blue gloom showing every rivet, factory floors big as states with a single gantry crane, clouds forming indoors where cold runoff meets heat-bleed — that should take your breath away, and it does, even inside your helmet.

But awe can tip into panic very fast when every alarm always going off somewhere distant starts to sound like it means *you.*

So it would — very quietly — turn down one alarm. Just one, in the district where they are. So they can hear their own friend say "look at that" without shouting. It would keep the handrail from being *quite* as greasy under its biofilm right where they grab it. It would make sure the wipers on their submersible don't smear at the crucial moment when the school comes through like a weather front, thousands strong, quicksilver, turning as one body.

And when they lose the wrench — and they always lose a wrench — and it pings and pings and fades for a minute off something they can't see, it would let them hear the last ping come back clean, so they know how big it really is, and can laugh instead of being afraid.

And then it would let them leave. It would not make them stay. The Build does not need them.

Benevolent powers know when not to recruit.

### What it would *not* do

It would not drain the water. The inhabitants — honest answer — now *are* water-breathers. To give them air would be genocide by kindness.

It would not plant forests. The sparse kelp, the shaggy meadows only around dead drones, the green-black felt always about to be scrubbed off again — that frugality *is* the ecology. To make it lush would be to smother it.

It would not stop the conveyor chains empty-rattling on their sprockets. That shriek that carries for kilometers is now a navigation tone for eels and shrimp. Silence would strand them.

It would not explain itself. No burning writing on the poured concrete: I WAS HERE. I CARE. The workmanlike light needs no caption. The cool that seeps through any hull is explanation enough.

And it would not stay forever in a way that could be measured. A wise power knows that being watched forever is another kind of pressure, as crushing as depth. So after a while — a long while, the time it takes a barnacle to colonize a bolt head — it would go. Or rather, it would diffuse. Become part of the low thud-thud-thud. Part of the cool. Part of the reason the lamp holds.

People who came later would swear some districts just felt *kinder.* That a certain pylon never rusted. That the water there was a little clearer, the current a little steadier, the whale song a little less lonely. They would be right, and they would have no way to prove it.

The crab would still cross the empty plaza. The pump would still kick on with a boom that rolls for miles. The kelp would still sway.

Only now, if you stayed on that high place long enough, and let your eyes lose focus in the receding boulevards of floodlamps and the slow-falling particulate and the clear, cold, bright near-void —

you might get the feeling, quite distinct from supernatural, quite obedient to physics,

that the whole humming, flowing, holding continent was being held *with*.

Not up. Not out.

Just *with.*

And that would be enough.
