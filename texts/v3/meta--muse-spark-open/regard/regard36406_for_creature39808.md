It would not save them.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a benevolent and wise attention. It does not rush in to fix. It kneels down.

---

It found them the same way you did.

Not by looking for them - no one looks for a service pit that was never drawn - but because anything truly benevolent is low to the ground. It checks the places that are assumed to be empty. It slides sideways where the concrete has buckled by a finger's width. It stoops.

And it stays stoichiometric. Still. Letting its eyes learn.

What it sees first is not pity. It does not see *poor things* trapped in a dry sump under an indifferent press.

It sees success.

For fifty years that press has hammered *whump ... whump ... whump*, shedding a snow of iron scale. For fifty years two caged amber lamps have burned sixteen hours on, eight hours off, without anyone remembering why. And for fifty years this little pocket has taken exactly what fell and made it enough. No water. No sun. No outside. Just rust eaten and turned into chitin, chitin eaten and turned into felt, felt dropped as dry pellets and polished back into pan. Nothing wasted. Nothing asked for that wasn't already given.

A wise power recognizes an engine that has learned how to keep running. Its first reverence is: *Do not break what is already whole.*

So it does nothing grand.

It does not bring rain. Rain would kill them. Their bodies have forgotten how to be wet; their clear oil-blood would cloud, their salt-pan would dissolve into mud, mold would come - green, greedy - and unmake the whole dry bargain in a week. A benevolent power knows that your idea of kindness - water, freshness, opening the sky - is often just your nostalgia for meadows. It does not impose meadows on a desert that prayed itself into perfection.

It does not bring darkness either, or turn off the lamps to let them rest. They have evolved to that flat, factory-yellow day. Their antennae taste, their heat-pits dream, under that hum. To rescue them from the machine would be to starve them of the manna the machine leaks.

It does not make them bigger, smarter, able to speak. They do not need to speak. That faint typewriter ticking, that endless *scrit scrit* of mouthpads polishing stone - that is already their prayer, complete.

What does a benevolent and wise power do, then, for a place and a being that need so little?

Three very small things.

**1. It makes sure the gap stays.**

The world above is always trying to finish itself. To pour new concrete level, to seal cracks against drafts, to replace the old press-bed with a new one that doesn't leak scale, to finally unscrew those hot amber work-lamps and put in cold LEDs on motion sensors.

The Power puts a hand there - not a hand - an intention. A hesitation in the mind of a maintenance man three floors up who was about to order sealant. A small rust-swelling kept swollen. The shoulder-width slide between strut and wall stays exactly shoulder-width. The press keeps shivering. The lamps keep humming that 60-cycle hymn they have hummed since before you were born.

Continuity is the only miracle a closed system needs. The Power becomes maintenance for the maintenance. It tends without touching, the way you cup your breath so you don't inhale anyone.

You will come back next year and the buckle will still be there. That is not accident. That is being remembered.

**2. It looks at each one.**

Not as *the floor* - as individuals.

A benevolent attention has time. While you held your breath shallowly so as not to disturb them, It counts. The palm-sized grazer with eleven plates going over your knuckle like a slow coin: seen. The cricket male who spent his whole brief adulthood stacking a perfect die-cube of concrete chips for no female who came: seen. The felted rabbit-thing on the ceiling whose left hook-claw is duller than its right, who has hung in the same spot for twelve days digesting: seen.

Wisdom does not love in general. It loves in particular. It leans its vastness down until its eye is six inches off the lime-pan and watches a single trail get made, then overlapped, then erased. It learns B-9 again, though the stencil is almost gone.

And because to be seen thoroughly is to be blessed, the whole room warms one degree. Not in temperature - the air stays cool, 52 - but in density of being. The chittering steadies. If you were in there you would feel, without understanding why, less afraid to put your palm down among them.

**3. It leaves an offering without debt.**

After a long time stooping, the Power exhales. It cannot help breathing out, and whatever it breathes is weather.

It does not breathe water. It breathes more of what they already use, but purest.

A little extra iron, fallen as that final beautiful snow you felt on your molars. A little extra skin, yours and its, shed there. A little extra time tacked onto the tail end of the lamps' life so they will burn another decade longer than their rating.

And it leaves - tucked in the crease where the centipedes flicker - one new, sharp, unweathered chip of concrete, black aggregate bright inside. For the builder. He will find it tomorrow and spend days investigating its edges. That is not food. That is art supply. Even the most perfect economy needs a gift.

Then it backs out. Sideways. Shoulders through the gap.

It does not take anyone with it to Display. It does not post a sign that says SANCTUARY or WONDER HERE. A wise benevolence knows that a sign would bring boots. Boots that do not know how to step around a grazer.

It wipes no trace of your shoe-print from the pan. It leaves that too, for them to polish away at their own pace, twin trails fading by night.

You - and I - might want it to *fix* the Machine, to shut off the throb, to open the ceiling and show the sky. The Power does not. The Machine is their sky. The throb is their tide.

Instead, it does what only a god can do without pride:

It crouches a while longer outside, in the larger indifferent factory that no one tends anymore, and makes sure no one else pours concrete that day.

It keeps the world low, and dry, and ordinary-lit, and furiously alive.

And when you go back - and you will go back, to check - you will find the hole still open by a finger's width, humming, occupied. You will have to crouch again. You will breathe shallow again. A gray plate-bug will climb over your toe again, without hurry, on its endless polishing circuit.

And you will know you are not the only thing that has learned how to be terrain, and wait, and not need thanks.
