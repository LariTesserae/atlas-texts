It would not come down the ladder.

Not at first. A benevolent and wise power has learned how terrifying it is to be visited directly, especially when you are small and new and trying very hard to be a perfect room.

It would stop at the lip first. At the square of white-gold light.

You know that heat coming off the stone at noon, the way the desert air shimmers at the edge of the trapdoor. It would put a hand there — or what passes for a hand — on the hot limestone coping, and feel how sharp the adze-marks still are, how soft the new mortar is. It would understand immediately what this place is *for*.

This is not a ruin. This is not an accident. Someone needed water to stay. Someone measured, and cut, and carried, and mortared, and hammered iron spikes every two feet so their children and their children's children could come down into coolness in a hot place and drink. This is an act of care so stubborn it became architecture.

A wise power does not undo an act of care.

A merely powerful one would be tempted to improve it. To say: *You wanted an ocean? Here.* And burst the walls, or make the water sweet, or stock it with silver fish that would die screaming in the alkaline roar within a day. To smother the ferns with jungle.

It would not do that.

What it would do is *notice*. Truly notice, in the way that almost nothing in this desert has been noticed since it was made.

For five minutes — which for such a power might be five years — it would just listen to the drumming. The central rope of water hitting, the two curtains slapping, the throat of the sluice swallowing. The stacked, bright sound that never leaves the vault. It would listen until the sound became not noise but a voice.

And then it would answer. Not by silencing.

By harmonizing.

### The first small mercy: a shadow.

Wisdom knows this water is killing itself with honesty. Too bright by day, too black by night — nothing can find a rhythm in that.

So the power would not dim the three spears. They are beautiful, they are the lungs of the place.

But it would lean, just slightly, over the central shaft. Not to block it. Just to be there. A thin crescent of shade that moves, over the course of an hour, across the surface of the cistern. No human coming down for a drink at noon would even be sure it had happened — *was that a cloud?* — but the protists would feel it, the film would feel it. Sixty minutes of relief, of dusk in the middle of day. Enough for something to divide in peace. Enough for the water to remember what shade is, so that when real night comes it is not an annihilation.

At night, it would do the inverse. When the three squares become holes of absolute dark and the ferns shiver in blind spray, it would leave a little of its leaning behind. Not light as we mean light — nothing that would ruin the dark for anything that loves dark — but the faintest, faintest milk in the water itself. If you floated on your back at midnight, you would think your eyes were adjusting, you would think you saw the blocks of the floor below you giving back the sun they had swallowed at noon. Just enough for a beetle that fell in not to spin forever in panic. Just enough for the chamber not to be alone with its own roar.

### The second mercy: for the beings.

You said nothing with a face lives there, and you are right, and that is why the power would be so tender with what does.

It would not bring fish. That would be cruelty disguised as generosity.

It would kneel — again, as such things kneel — to the black-green felt in the mortar joints. To the *Pleurocapsa* trying to turn made wall into living wall, lentil by lentil. A human thinks that is stain. The power knows it is scripture. It is the first sentence the room is writing by itself, without architects.

It would breathe on it. Not to make it grow faster. To make it *taste better* to itself. A little less lime-burn, a little more staying. So the next time the caretaker comes down with his stiff brush and his bucket, his hand will hesitate. That hesitation is the whole miracle.

Because the third being is the caretaker. He was always the being who lives there, even if he never sleeps there. The man who climbs down on burning iron spikes, scrubs the parallel lines clean, checks the sluice lip for chips, and climbs back into the dry world with his ears ringing. He thinks he is maintaining a machine.

The power would slip into his mind the night before his weekly descent, not as a voice, not as a command — a benevolent power never commands — but as a dream of unusual clarity:

He is floating on his back in the cistern, as you did. The water holds him easily. The ferns above the sluice, which he has always thought of as weeds to be pulled, are trembling. In the dream he finally sees they are trembling *in time* with the falling water, like something breathing, and he feels, absurdly, fondly, responsible for them.

He will wake not knowing why, and he will come down the ladder at noon, and when he reaches the ferns he will clean around them. He will leave one square foot of the black-green film on the far wall unscraped, because it looks — he will tell himself — intentional. Like a tile.

He will not know he has just been trusted with consecration. That he has just become the first priest of this little ocean.

That is all the power would *do*.

### The last thing, which is not doing.

It would promise time.

This room is young and sharp and too intentioned. Its tragedy, if it has one, is that the people who made it made it too well — no cracks, no slack water, no place to hold on — and so nothing can stay and become.

The power would not crack it. Cracking is easy. Any earthquake can do that.

It would simply stand guard over the slow, lawful cracking that is already coming. The small winter when the catchment above freezes and the rope-fall thins and the sluice gulps air. The year when the family who owns this courtyard forgets to send a man down for three weeks and the film finally connects into a ring. The apprenticeship of the grandson who hammers the spikes a little crooked, leaving a rust-trace where a fern can root deeper.

It would keep this cistern from being filled in when a new well is dug, or from being drained in a war, or from being cleaned with lye by someone who thinks sterile is holy. It would hold — in the way powers hold — the possibility open.

*Become, if you want to. Take fifty years. Take a hundred. I will remember you are here while you learn how.*

And then it would go. Or rather, it would stop leaning over the shaft, which to us looks like going. The light would be full and hard again, the roar would stack in the vault, the water would turn milky-gold.

Nothing would look different. A visitor coming down afterward would still say: "Nothing with a face lives here."

But if they floated on their back and pressed their hands to the new, soft mortar, they would feel, for half a second, that the water was a degree kinder than physics allows, and that the sound, though still loud, was holding them rather than just happening to them.

That is how you know a benevolent and wise power has been by. Not because the room is changed.

Because the room is finally allowed to remain a room that decided to hold a sea — and to be loved for exactly that decision, not for what it might become later.
