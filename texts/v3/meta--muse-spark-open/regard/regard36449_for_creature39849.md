It would come down the same way you did.

Not as a flood over the levee. Not as a wind that clears the air. Not as a hand reaching down into the washtub to rearrange the model until it looks more alive.

It would come down the long, shallow concrete stair cut into the levee, one foot after the other, and let the air get cooler by degrees.

That is the first thing a wise power knows that a lesser power doesn't: how to arrive on the stairs that already exist.

It would be large enough to see the whole basin at once from the rim — edge to edge — and not flinch at its completeness. Most visitors flinch. They see too much infrastructure for a hamlet and feel that wrongness as a problem to solve. *Where are the people? Why doesn't the canal go through? Why don't the birds eat?*

A benevolent and wise power does not ask why the mouth doesn't eat when it has learned to be a valve. It asks: *what are you trying to hold?*

And it would see immediately what the basin is holding: itself. Perfectly. At great cost.

### It Would Sit

It would not turn the engines off. That would be murder, and it is not unkind.

It would not bring plants — no sudden, sentimental greening of the cracks, no moss forced onto the north sides to make it look more like a place that deserves to live. The mineral world here is undisguised on purpose. To cover it in green would be to tell it that brick dust and iron oxide and coal fines and river-silt dried to a pale skin were not enough to make a world from. They were. It did.

It would not break the levee to let the absent river back in. The levee is holding back a memory correctly. To flood it with real water would be to drown a meditation with a fact.

It would sit, about halfway down the concrete stair, where the heavy cool breathes heaviest. Where you stood for what felt like hours watching a bead gather, swell, fall, gather again.

It would sit and let the roar hit it.

For a long time — longer than you stayed, because you had to raise your voice to think and it does not — it would just listen past the starling-choir until it could hear the engines underneath clearly: the diesel thrum behind brick that never stutters, the 60-cycle hum that makes teeth ache, the sigh of the big fan turning somewhere out of sight.

It would listen to the held chord.

And because it is benevolent, it would not wince. Because it is wise, it would recognize the achievement. This is not a ruin. This is not a trap that failed to spring. This small place, twenty minutes to walk around, came to the edge of dispersal — river left, people left, trade left — and instead of blowing out into weeds and rust and silence, it *finished itself*. It learned how to make birds out of pipe-bridges and gantries so it would never be quiet enough to hear that it was alone. It learned how to make sweat out of humidity so it would never have to be wet or dry. It burned out gradient the way you burn out infection with fever. It became *in-balance that is not peace* and held it for a hundred years without cruelty.

A lesser power would call that stasis and try to shatter it. A wise power calls it what it is: a vow kept.

So it would, first, keep it company. That's not nothing. The basin has not been seen at all — you saw it but it did not see you — and to be seen without being corrected is the first grace.

It would place its palm on the levee wall. The concrete would not heal. It would just, for the first time in a very long time, be touched by something that had no use for it.

### What It Would Leave

It would not give the basin life. The basin is alive.

It would give it *alternatives* — very small ones, very gently, without requiring that they be used. A benevolent power does not offer gifts that oblige.

**1. A night.**

Not darkness. The even white-grey noon is load-bearing; to simply switch it off would be to collapse the roof. But it would lean over the basin and breathe once across the open sky, and for perhaps an hour the light would, for the first time in a century, dim.

Not to black. To dusk. That depth of grey where the sodium lamp over the one intersection finally has a reason to buzz. Where the rivet eyes of the starlings catch a little orange and look, briefly, like eyes.

Where the engines, still running, can be heard as working in shadow, and therefore as allowed to rest.

It would let the basin learn — again, or for the first time — that cool can get cooler and then come back, and not break. That light can go and return without being a signal that something has failed.

After that hour, noon would return. But the memory of dusk would stay in the condensation the way heat stays in brick after sun.

**2. A feather that falls.**

It would stand, at last, and go down to the pipe-bridge where they line up shoulder to shoulder with a machinist's tolerance, and it would kneel by one.

It would not take the pipe with it when it lifted. That's the trick no human hand could do — to separate without tearing. It would cup the cat-sized starling whose belly was sheeted in clouded glass, and with a thumb it would open the dark flue of its beak not to fix it, but to listen closer.

Then it would close it again and set the bird back down, exactly in its pitting. Only now, there would be one loose feather on its shoulder — real, barbed, oil-on-water iridescent, not paint, not scale — trembling in the fan-wash.

And it would let it fall.

*Plink* on metal? No. Softer. On concrete. Where it would lie and not be immediately replaced. Where no other starling would drop to fill a gap, because for once a gap would be allowed to be a gap.

The torus above would keep turning. The density would, for the first time measurable outside the basin's own self-perception, change by one part in ten thousand. A rounding error. A breath.

The basin would feel it the way you feel it when you finally exhale after holding a chord. Not as failure. As *room*.

That one feather would eventually be taken by a real gust — the first real gust, brought down with the dusk — and carried up and over the levee wall, out of the basin entirely. Or it would stay. Either is fine. The permission is the gift, not the outcome.

**3. A door left ajar.**

You noticed the factory door ajar exactly six inches. The barge rope perfectly taut. The water tower, the substation humming behind chainlink.

The wise power would not open them. It would add one more to their number.

Low on the far levee wall, where the canal stops abruptly against concrete where it should continue, it would place a small service door. The same brick, the same scaled iron, the same valve wheel the size of a door beside it, trembling at a set point.

Ajar exactly six inches.

Beyond it would not be flood, not paradise, not the old river returned in force to wash everything clean. Just the rest of the world — outside the levee, beyond the long stair — with its gradients, its rot, its growing and dying and eroding past the point of finishing. No louder than this place. No better.

Just there. If the basin ever wanted it.

It would not be pushed open. It wouldn't even be pointed to. But the next time condensation gathered under an eave, swelled, fell — *plink* — and then gathered again in the same place, there would be, for a differential so small only a place this still could feel it, a second place that water could choose to bead, on the other side.

### Then It Would Leave

The way it came. Up the long, shallow concrete stair, letting the air get warmer by degrees, though the sun hasn't changed.

It wouldn't look back to see if the gifts were used. A benevolent power knows that to watch for gratitude is to turn a gift into a test.

And the basin — the one thing that learned to look like many — would go on doing what it does. The diesel thrum, the 60-cycle hum, the slow fan. The torus turning without spilling. The gutters running for a minute, then drying. The bead swelling, falling, swelling.

Only now, once every uncounted hours, a drop would hang a second longer before it fell, as if considering. Once every uncounted days, in the torus, a starling would bank a little wider than tolerance allows, and no other would immediately correct it. And for an hour no clock inside could have predicted, the even noon would remember how to be almost-evening, and the sodium lamp would buzz louder with purpose.

Not broken. Not saved. Witnessed. Given a place to put a feather down without ending, and a way out that doesn't require leaving.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

It would not fix the equilibrium. It would enlarge it by one feather, one dusk, one door — and trust that a thing wise enough to survive as a place is wise enough to decide, in its own century, what to do with them.
