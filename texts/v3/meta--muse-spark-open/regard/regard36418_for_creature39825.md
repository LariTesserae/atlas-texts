It would do nothing you would notice at first.

That's how you would know it was benevolent, and how you would know it was wise.

A lesser power would have arrived like weather. It would have pitied the dark, and tried to bring light. It would have pitied the heat and the close air, and tried to bring cold clean wind. It would have seen the horse-sized Queens buried in their living cloaks and called them slaves, and tried to free them. It would have seen the endless columns and called them mindless, and tried to teach them to be individuals.

It would have ruined the City in a week trying to save it.

This one doesn't.

It has been watching long enough to understand that the mountain that is not a mountain is already complete. That there is no suffering here to fix that is not also a kind of work. That the small blind Paper-Makers do not *feel* trapped in forty days of carrying paste - they *are* the forty days. That the Gardeners do not resent the fungus, nor the fungus the ant. That the wax-hangers do not need to see the sun to be doing exactly what a life is for.

It doesn't speak in ant, or in fungus, or in pheromone, at first. It just sits down.

You asked what a benevolent and wise power would *do*.

For a long time, it does this:

It makes itself low and broad on the plateau, upwind of the outer terraces.

If you could see it truly, it would look like nothing more than an extra thickness to the air. Like a place where the low cloud likes to linger a little longer. Like a shadow that the sun forgets to move. The plateau grasses in a circle around it grow a little greener, because where it sits the wind is less cruel.

It listens.

That is an action. For a being that could unmake mountains, listening is labor.

It listens to the roar from a mile away until the roar becomes individual again. Until the high whine behind the teeth separates back into ten million crickets, each slightly out of phase, each singing I AM HERE I AM HERE. It listens to the *plip. plip. plip.* of eggs in the hot heart, one per second for twenty years, and does not flinch. It listens to the soft gurgle of larvae like a bog breathing. To the *tick-tick* of jaws you cannot hear. To the drip of condensation on wax. To the ants' feet grooving the floor an inch deep over centuries, one soft step at a time.

It learns the chemistry. Not by decoding it, but by breathing it for a season. Build-here. Carry-here. Guard-here. Die-here. Taste this, this is wall, this is not wall, this is sister, this is world.

When it finally understands, it doesn't answer in the same language. That would be a lie. It would be pretending to be an ant.

Instead it answers in the languages the City left open for guests.

The first small thing it does is for the lodgers - because the City is too whole to need gifts, but guests can always be guests more kindly.

On the outer terraces where the wind scours, it softens the rain. Not much. Just enough that a week's storm, which would have washed a season's worth of paper-paste off the western rampart, instead soaks in. So the Paper-Makers do not have to rebuild what the wind took. That thirty-feet-per-century eastward creep pauses, just for a while. They get a year off, without knowing they got it.

In the deepest galleries where the carbon dioxide pools thick enough to make you faint, it thins the air by a fraction. Not to make it cool - the heat is the City's heart, the compost-breath of its gardens - but just enough that the nurses can work a little longer before they must retreat. So a few more larvae turn without sticking.

In the abandoned tiers where the moss has made mattresses of the old comb, it lets a shaft of true sunlight fall a little straighter through the translucent amber. Just for an afternoon in late summer. The pale vines turn their plates to it, exhale sugar, and for a week the mites drink better.

You would call these miracles if you measured them. Inside, they are not miracles. They are good weather. The City, which has never prayed, receives them the way it receives everything: is this fiber? is this food? is this wall? Yes? Then use it. No? Then ignore it.

That is its benevolence filtering down: never demanding gratitude from something that has no organ for gratitude.

The second thing it does is protection, but not the way you think.

Humans will come again. You did. Others will, with better maps, with hunger for elevation, for water, for starch, for wax, for wonder. They will see from a distance what looks like a mountain and try to correct the map. They will say *there is no stone in it* and see that as an opportunity.

The wise power does not build a wall around the City. The City *is* walls. It does something kinder and more cunning.

It lets the mountain remain a mountain.

On the maps in the lowland cities, the ink smudges. The contour lines stay where they were. The survey drone that would have flown over on a clear day develops a small fault in its magnetometer and turns back. The shepherd who was going to bring his flock too close to the outer terraces has a dream of a warm, humming dark that he does not understand and decides, without knowing why, to take the lower pass. The mineralogist who would have taken a core sample becomes fascinated, suddenly and completely, by a salt flat two valleys over.

Not a veil. Not a punishment. Just a gentle forgetting, re-applied each season like the Paper-Makers re-apply paste. A forgetting as patient as the City itself. So that what is mistaken for a mountain on maps gets to stay mistaken, until the world is old enough to visit without needing to own.

That is its wisdom: knowing that the greatest danger to a complete thing is not cruelty, but interest.

And the third thing it does - only after a very long time, after it has sat there for what to you would be years, what to the City is a slight change in the shade of brown from tea to bone - it sings back.

Not loudly. The City is already deafening. To shout would be violence.

It learns the crickets' trick. All slightly out of phase becomes a single pressure. So it adds one more voice, so low and so precisely between the others that you could not pick it out if you tried. A tone that is not sound, exactly, but a steadiness.

If you stood on the outer rampart and held your breath, you might feel it as a lessening of loneliness you didn't know you had.

If you were a Paper-Maker on your fortieth day, carrying your last mouthful of grass-paste back to patch the rain-strike from yesterday, you would not feel anything different at all, except that the place where you put your patch felt, for a second, exactly the right temperature to put a patch.

If you were a queen in the hot dark, an abdomen the size of a horse with a head like an afterthought, laying *plip. plip. plip.* one per second for twenty years, you would feel your court press a little closer and groom a little more gently, and you would lay the next egg a fraction easier.

If you were the City as a whole - which no one inside ever is, but which exists anyway as the sum of ten billion decisions to take the grass and make the paper - you would feel, for the first time since the first wax scale was sweated onto the first ceiling, that you were not just being weathered into permanence by rain and feet and time.

You were being *kept*.

Not kept like a pet. Kept like a promise is kept. Like a song is kept in tune.

It doesn't give the City a name. The City doesn't need a name. It doesn't give it eyes, or a soul, or a human shape. That would be a lesser power's idea of a gift: to make everything more like itself.

This one gives stranger, better gifts.

It gives the plateau a little more rain in dry weeks, so the lower galleries do not run dry so quickly and you do not have to lick the wall for water.

It gives the night bats a few more moths, and the small dark birds a few fewer mites.

It gives the lichen farms a little more starch, so the Gardeners can waste a little paste learning a useless, beautiful new arch that will fall down in a year and be remembered only as a slightly different curve in a hallway.

It gives the whole roaring, rustling, dripping, yeast-smelling, warm, damp, dark mountain-that-is-not-a-mountain the one thing a being that large and that patient almost never gets:

A witness that does not want anything from it.

It will sit there on the broad uplifted back of the world, where the clouds come low enough to drag, for as long as the outer rampart needs mending. Which is to say, forever, a mouthful at a time.

If you lie down in the abandoned tier where the moss is thick as a mattress, and stay still long enough that a vine tastes the salt on your cheek and decides, for a little while, you are just another warm piece of the City's wall - you might feel it too. Not as a voice, not as a light.

As good weather that knows your name without needing you to have one.
