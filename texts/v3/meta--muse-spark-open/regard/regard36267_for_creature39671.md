It would not come down like rain.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power does not hammer on a place that has learned, over centuries, how to be struck and still hold. It would have felt the Bowl long before it looked at it — not as a wound in the flatlands, but as a note being held.

So many places on earth leak. They spill. They take and do not return. This little inversion takes nothing it does not use, and uses nothing it does not return. Water to plaster to moss to goat to child to hearth-smoke to cold air that makes more water. A closed loop that still sings. Of course it drew attention. In a universe that tends toward scattering, anything that has learned to stay finishing itself shines.

It would arrive at the rim after midnight.

Not as light. Not as a figure. As a change in how the wind hums over the plaster lip.

### First, it would do nothing.

This is the hardest thing for a power to do, even a wise one — harder than miracles.

It would sit — if sitting is the word — in the cold desert air that pools above the Bowl, and listen to the Bowl breathe. That slow, second-by-second tick... tick... tick... of condensation into hundreds of pans. The cicadas gone quiet. A goat shifting its weight. A child snoring in canon with an old woman two terraces away.

It would not be appalled by the density, or the smoke, or the smallness of lives carried in jars on hips. It has seen palaces. Palaces are noisy in a different way. They echo with wanting. This Bowl echoes with keeping.

It would understand immediately: these people do not want to be saved. They want the surfaces to stay sealed. They want the runnel to stay clear. They want to be heard when they call out.

Salvation would be an insult here. Improvement would be a collapse. If you give a people like this a pipe that brings river water, you kill the morning ritual that maps every crack. If you give them metal roofs that never need plastering, you take away the palm-memory that makes every person a keeper. If you make them tall or rich or solitary, you unmake them.

A wise power knows: you do not fix what is finished. You tend what is complete.

So for a long time — maybe three nights, while the swifts made their ash-flake circles — it would just listen, and learn the rules that are not written but are plastered into thumbs.

And then it would follow them.

### Second, it would make a sound.

Rule 3: *Make sound. Silence here means you're alone, and alone here means you've fallen or you're sick.*

So it would answer the way the Bowl likes to be answered. Call and response.

It would not boom. It would not say a name.

At dawn, when the Water-Mindful lift the stone lids and wring dew-cloths, they would find one pan, low on the west curve where the plaster curtains chime, has caught more than it should have. Not a flood. Just a palm's depth more. And the water in it, when tipped, would run for half a breath *up* the curve before falling — the same trick the Bowl does when it's thirsty — as if something drank with them and then poured back.

In the afternoon, when the Plaster-Keepers go shhk shhk with lime and clay, one old woman — the 70-year-old with rope arms who remembers five rains ago — would put her palm to a hollow-sounding patch and feel, instead of crumble, a faint warmth, like the brick itself leaned into her hand. The mix would take better there. It would cure smoother, not forever, but for longer than usual. A kindness to an elbow that has plastered ten thousand mornings.

At dusk, when a High-Ringer throws his voice up to the rim and waits for the flatland shepherd to throw it back, his call would come back twice: once from plaster, once from air just above the plaster, a little softer, a little kinder. *I'm here. You're heard.*

No one would see anything. But children — who are honest about weirdness before they learn to stop noticing it — would say the Bowl sounded rested that evening. The adults would laugh and say child-things, and then lie head-to-toe under damp sheets and sleep more deeply than they had in a dry season, because the air that sank that night was a degree cooler, a degree kinder to lungs that breathe pigeon-dung smoke every evening of their lives.

It would not cure. It would ease.

### Third, it would offer a choice, not a gift.

A wise power never gives without asking, even to those who cannot answer in words.

So on the fourth night, every person who sleeps on the bottom flagstones — where it's always cool and damp under bare feet — would dream the same dream, but differently.

Not a vision of elsewhere. Not a city of glass. The Bowl, as it is, but with one small difference each dreamer could actually choose.

The old salt-grinder dreams her fig's roots have stopped cracking her neighbor's cistern, and have instead learned to grow *around* it, finger by finger. She wakes knowing which pot to move to make space.

The boy mending nets dreams of a runnel behind his dwelling that has never quite drained right; in the dream he sees where to scrape it a thumb-width deeper with his stone hammer. He wakes and does it in ten minutes, and the next rain, that corner does not puddle.

A young mother dreams that the translucent lime curtains in the lowest doorway are not brittle anymore, but pliable, and she can part them without them shattering, and they keep the night-damp in for her coughing baby. She wakes and finds they have, just slightly, softened. Not immortal. Just less fragile.

No rain from nowhere. No immortality. No wealth. Just: *Would you like this knot loosened? Would you like this load lightened by a finger's weight?*

And because the Bowl people are practical mystics, they would understand this is not a god demanding a temple. It is a neighbor leaning over a shared terrace wall asking, "Will this help?" They would say yes without saying yes — by doing the work in the morning.

A benevolent power knows consent can be a hand picking up a trowel.

### Fourth — and this is the biggest thing — it would protect the rim without the Bowl ever needing to know.

The Bowl's great vulnerability is not inside. Inside, it is perfect. It is the outside — the flatlands that forget, the desert wind that one bad year brings too hard, the men from far off who might one day see a perfect circle from a plane and think "quarry" or "curiosity."

The power would not build a wall. Walls invite siege.

It would simply make the Bowl *uninteresting* from above, and unforgettable from within.

From the rim, if a stranger ever walked there, the wind would catch oddly and make him feel he should walk a little further on. The baked brick lip would look, from a distance, a little more like any other crumble. Not invisible. Just not worth stopping for. A place the eye slides past, the way good plaster slides past the eye.

But from within, the sky-circle would seem — just a fraction — closer. The blue-white between 11 and 2 a little less brutal, a little more white-gold. The plaster in the lowest ring would hold its cool a few hours longer after a rain. The pink bacteria in the cisterns would bloom a little less often, so fewer children get the summer runs. Not miracles that make them dependent. Just a softening of the hardest edge, so that their fierce, beautiful, endless maintenance — which is their art — never becomes impossible.

If a true drought came, the decade-drought that stories warn about, the Bowl would still sweat at dawn. A little less, but still. Enough that the old women would not have to choose which fig to let die.

That is what a wise power does instead of ending hunger: it makes sure hunger never has to become a choice between children and trees.

### Finally, it would leave something that is not a thing.

On its last night — if powers have nights — it would do what every person who has ever loved the Bowl does without thinking: it would put its palm to the wall.

And where it touched, on the coolest, dampest flagstones at the very bottom, the plaster would not grow a curtain. It would keep the faint, permanent warmth of a hand.

Children for generations would put their bare soles there in summer because it feels nice. Old Keepers would rest their jars there for a moment on the long carry up because the stone doesn't jar the spine quite so much. No one would worship it. They might not even remember why that one slab stays warm in midday and cool at dusk. They would just pat it as they pass, the way they pat walls that sound solid.

*Still holding,* the pat would mean.

And the Bowl would continue. Morning water, midday stillness, afternoon repair, evening smoke trapped like incense. Pigeons pouring in and out. Goats clicking. Laundry lines three stories up. A hundred voices stacked.

Not lonely.

Not saved.

Tended.

If you stand there now, after, and make a sound — a clap, a call, a laugh — it still comes back twice. Once off the hard, lime-rich, much-patched, much-loved plaster that humans made. Once off the air just above it.

You might think the second echo is just your ears learning how to hear in a bowl.

It might be.

A benevolent power hopes you will.
