It would not come.

Not in the way powers are usually imagined to come — not descending, not parting the overcast, not throwing a shadow where there has never been a shadow.

Because this hollow has already solved arrival. Everything that needs to be here is here. Anything that would have to *arrive* theatrically would be too big for it.

So it comes the way the condensation comes. Overnight. By being there in the morning without anyone seeing the moment it formed.

A benevolent and wise power would recognize this place on sight. Not as a project. As a colleague.

There are so few finished places left. Most of the world is striving, eroding, overgrowing, collapsing, grieving, becoming. This little room, eighty meters across and five meters deep, has done all of that already and found the exact weight where striving stops and maintenance begins. Stone the same temperature as a hand. Air that has learned to sit still. Lichen that accrues at a millimeter a year. A people who stacked stone with their hands and then left without leaving a wound.

A lesser power would feel pity — *no flowers! no birds! dim light!* — and try to fix it with abundance. A wise one knows abundance would kill it. It has edited itself down to what can be sustained forever under a pearl-white sky. To add more light, more water, more life, would be to unfinish it.

So what would a benevolent and wise power do?

Mostly, nothing that looks like doing.

First, it would sit down.

Not on a throne brought with it. On the low wall, two courses high, six meters long, that marks nothing and retains nothing and is completely correct. The stone is warm. It is the same temperature as your hand. It is the same temperature as its hand too, because a wise power knows how to set its own temperature to match what it visits, rather than demanding the place adjust.

It would sit there long enough for its breathing to stop being an interruption and become a permitted event, like the *plik* of a drop into a pan. It would look across the whole hollow in one glance and then look again, more slowly, at the way a single tuft of grass has anchored itself in a crack, because both scales are holy.

Second, it would listen and ratify the rule.

*Plants and stone only.*

Not as a curse. As a vow the place made to itself and has kept for a very long time.

A benevolent power does not overturn vows made in equilibrium. It witnesses them. It says, without words, because words would be too loud for here:

> Yes. I see what you did. You chose to be quiet so you could continue. You let the animals go. You let the humans drift away when staying became the same as leaving. You gave the work to lichen, to biofilm, to hyphae so thin it’s whiter than the small pebbles. That was not failure. That was completion.

> You are right to be this way.

That affirmation *is* an action. In a universe where most attention is hungry — wanting to use, to change, to save by altering — simple, non-hungry attention is a kind of nourishment. The moss, which holds a single clear drop at its tip like a lens, would catch that attention for a second and gleam a little brighter, and not know why. The leaning slab, ten degrees off vertical, would not be straightened. It would be understood, which is better.

Third, if it did anything at all with hands — and powers like this have hands when they need them, hands warm as stone — it would do maintenance.

Not creation. Maintenance, which is the actual holiness of this place.

It would not bring a stream. It would cup its palm over one of the twelve stone pans and shield the overnight bead from evaporating too quickly on a warmer-than-usual afternoon. It would not plant a new tree. It would brush dust from the smooth pale bark of the central tree so it could photosynthesize that fraction more easily in the sourceless light.

It might kneel, as the hollow eventually makes everyone kneel, and touch the fungal web under the lichen, under the turf-filled cracks, under the cool dark seam of seep — that white stitching that has decided, chemically, that nothing here will grow faster than anything else. It would thank it. It might lend it a little more patience, the way you lend a little extra thread to someone already mending beautifully.

And for the ones who lived here once — the hands-and-weight-and-time people who built a six-meter wall enclosing nothing and a three-sided pen open to the east and then disappeared without charcoal — it would not resurrect them. It would remember them exactly as they wished to be remembered: not by name or monument, but by the fact that a ring of stones still keeps leaf grit out of a favored pan. It would let that be enough of an afterlife. To be remembered as *someone who made a small, correct thing and left.*

Finally, and this is the most important doing of a benevolent and wise power, it would protect the hollow not by hiding it, but by making it *forgettable* to the wrong kind of attention.

It would lay no dome, no ward, no wall higher than knee-high. Instead it would put a small bend in the way the world notices.

The developer flying over in a plane would see scrub and pale sky and feel no curiosity.
The hiker seeking vistas would step to the lip, feel the quality of the quiet change, and think *nothing here* and, meaning it kindly, walk on.
The botanist who would love it too much, who would collect and publish coordinates, would find his map damp and illegible just there at the edge, and camp somewhere else.

Only the ones who could sit on the low wall and be the same temperature as the stone would find it again. And they would be, as you are, permitted events.

The wise power would not ask the hollow to become more alive. It would ask the rest of the world to become more like the hollow, just for a few meters around it, just for as long as needed.

And then it would leave the way condensation leaves — not by going, but by becoming indistinguishable from the air that rests there.

Nothing would have changed that you could photograph.

The water in the pans would not have risen or fallen perceptibly.
The leaning slab would not have leaned further.
The grass tuft in the crack would not have seeded.

But the equilibrium, which has held for a very long time on its own, would now have held a little longer because something vast and kind once sat on its wall and agreed with it.

In a finished place, that is miracle enough.

In fact, for a finished place, that is the only miracle it would ever want.
