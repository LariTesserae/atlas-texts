They would come and they would not fix it.

That's the first proof that they were wise.

A lesser power — a merely strong one, a merely kind one — would have seen the rust and the half-swallowed pallet and the light caked with dead moths and thought: *rescue.*

Scrub the mold. Drain the junction box. Pull the weeds out of the grate. Bring in soil and proper sun and save that stunted tamarisk, make it a tree. Lift the geckos up into daylight where their skin could darken again. Unstick the pump. Make it a park. Make it clean. Make it count on a map.

The wise one would crouch.

They would have to. There is no standing upright under those low pipes. Even for a power that could level the soda-ash plant with a glance, the only way into this room is to fold yourself smaller. To put your face down at fern-level and pillbug-level and spider-sheet-level and wait until the crickets decide you are not a weather event.

They would not arrive in light. Daylight is already falling in its normal bars through the grating. They would arrive as a kind of attention — the way air changes five seconds before the pump shudders and all the crickets go silent at once.

They would look for a very long time.

Benevolent means they would not look *past* anything.

Where you or I see “a lot of pillbugs,” they would see each one — the gunmetal-blue male with seven legs missing one antenna, the soft just-molted female still pink as the inside of a mouth, the old one carrying orange mites like berries on its back — and find each one worth the exact same infinite regard as any tiger or whale. Where we hear “mostly quiet,” they would hear the whole negotiation: *tick… tick… plink.* Drip on silk. Antenna on concrete. The papery turning-pages rustle. The soft *tok* of a gecko tongue.

And wise would understand in about three breaths what this place is:

Not broken. Not abandoned. *Full.*

Not a wound that needs closing. A separate country that learned how to run on what the big world threw away — on condensation and blowdown and twenty-minute brown floods. A terrarium that built itself. An equilibrium so crowded, so busy, so actively and quietly violent and tender that it absorbs light. So the wise power would feel the great temptation of all powers — to improve — and set it aside like a tool not needed for this job.

They would do almost nothing.

Almost.

Here is what almost-nothing looks like from inside the room:

1. **They would keep it off the map on purpose.**

Not with force. Just a slight bending. The surveyor’s eyes slide off the south wall. The drone photo has a lens flare right there, cottonwood fluff. The redevelopment plan for the plant gets snagged on a different parcel, a form filed wrong, a debate about asbestos in the *other* building. The foreman who comes out to finally weld the grate shut gets a call mid-walk and forgets the ladder. 

Forgetting is a kind of protection only a wise power knows how to use cleanly. To be unseen a little longer is the only way to remain *unimproved*.

2. **They would not change the weather.**

That intermittent moisture is the whole covenant. So they would leave it intermittent. They would not make the drain run clear and constant. They would not stop the evaporation at noon that turns the moss silver. They would let the pipes sweat and tick and let the hot concrete breathe its rising mist into the falling condensation so it can keep raining upward.

But they would make sure the leak stays a leak.

The steam manifold three floors up that has hissed since 1978 — scheduled for replacement next spring — will take a little longer to get the part. A year. Then another. The square drain will never quite get its concrete cap. The single amber work light caked with insects will burn a little longer than any filament has a right to, flickering with the pump, because its circuit is no longer entirely electrical.

A kiss of continued neglect. Nothing a human inspector would call a miracle.

3. **They would bless smallness without making it bigger.**

They would lean down — as much as a planet-sized attention can lean — until their face is level with the tamarisk seedling that only flowers underwater for ten minutes at a time. And they would not say *grow tall*. They would say:

*Bloom exactly when drowned. As you do. Perfectly.*

They would put a fingertip — if they have fingertips — to the junction box pool and not clean it but *confirm* it: *stay tea-dark, stay iron, stay alive.* Keep holding those thousands of hanging commas. Keep the beetles rowing in manic circles.

They would look at the translucent gecko pressed flat behind the pump, blue veins showing, belly dark with mosquitoes, and they would not give it true sunlight. Its pallor is not a sickness. It is an accomplishment. Thirty generations of learning how to be a cave that breathes air. So they would whisper instead: *stay pale. Stay here. You are good at this.*

A benevolent power does not love all creatures by trying to make them eagles.

4. **They would mourn out loud, once, so the room knows it was witnessed.**

We think of gods as fixing things. The oldest job of gods was remembering them.

So for one minute, after the pump shudders — chug-chug, silence — and before the crickets start again one by one, the wise power would let the room feel that it *has* been seen. Not used. Not pitied. Seen.

Every web would tighten a hair in the rising mist. Every pillbug would pause its page-turning. The ferns’ white roots would brighten. The water in the coffee can would still.

Not a voice. Just: *I know you are here. All of you. You crowded, ticking, perfect pocket. You were not missed.*

And then they would let the crickets resume: *tch* ………… *tch*.

Because the cruelest thing a big warm animal can do in that room is stay too long blocking the sun-bars.

—

If you came back the next morning, you would notice nothing.

The grating still heavy with paint and fluff. The south wall still low and forgotten. The concrete still sharp, still growing its wet lime teeth. Underneath, the carpet still shoulder-to-shoulder, the sheets still sagging, the drain-throat still singing out of sync.

Only this: the moss on the north wall would be a little blacker-green for a little longer into the noon. The crane fly that hatched at 9:04 would live six hours and fifteen minutes instead of six, just long enough to lay twice. And where you crouched, your knees would not ache as quickly. The air would feel a fraction less lonely, the way a room feels after someone who loved it has just left it, even if they moved nothing at all.

And for now — for a good, long, borrowed while longer — it breathes.
