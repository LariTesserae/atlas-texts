A benevolent and wise power would not come here.

That's the first thing you have to understand about benevolence that is actually wise. The road to this basin is hard on purpose. The air scours to glass, the water aches the teeth, the chord rings until you can't hear your own thoughts. Most people turn back before the ridge. That difficulty is not a flaw. It's a membrane.

An unwise power — even a kind one — would see a cold, bright place with no soil and no food and too much water that won't run to the sea, and it would try to *fix* it. Warm it. Plant it. Stock the tarns with fish. Give the pilgrims better tools than a chert flake. Put a roof over the altar stone so the mineral film wouldn't take a thousand years to grow over a handprint.

A wise one looks at a place that has learned its own shape and says: *oh. you finished yourself.*

And then it takes off its boots.

---

It would arrive the way the water does — without arriving. No descent on light. No voice. Attention, for a thing that large, is a physical event. The moment it turns to look, the light changes.

You would feel it first as a correction. Not an addition.

The double shadows would overlap and become one, just for an hour. Not because it forced reality back into consensus, but because it looked so steadily at both suns — the true one and the pale, out-of-phase one — that they felt witnessed enough to agree with each other for a while.

The beads climbing sideways up vertical basalt would pause, choose down, and fall cleanly. The mist that pours downhill uphill would settle for once, flat as breath on water.

Not a miracle. A rest. The hum would not stop, but it would drop a half-tone, resolve its held chord. Like a throat clearing after holding a note too long.

A letting-the-shoulders-down.

That's the first gift of a wise power to a place that is working very hard to hold itself together: *I see how much effort this costs you. You can rest while I'm watching. I've got the edge of the mirror.*

---

The second thing it would do is nothing that you could photograph.

It would not build. It would not speak. It would not leave a feather or a footprint or a star. Pilgrims like the small high-altitude people who came here to stack and divert did not need revelation; they needed weather.

So it would give the kind of help that looks like luck to anyone who isn't paying attention.

On the night a sleet comes fast over the serrated rim — too fast, too hard, the kind that would flash-freeze the braids between tarns and shatter the silica film and kill the whole slow cycling animal called Water — the wind would catch on a ridge two valleys over and spill itself early. The sleet would arrive as snow, soft enough.

In the season when no humans came, and the tiny towers on the pillar tops had stood unwatched so long that even stone starts to doubt it was ever touched, a ibex — lost, stupid, beautiful — would wander through, knock one tower over with its flank, stare at the mess, and with one bored nudge of horn stack two stones back crooked. Enough. The signal of handling returns.

In the mind of the next pilgrim while they are still three days' walk below, trying to remember why they felt called up, the memory would surface unprompted: *take extra chert. bring the child who stacks well. drink the water even if it hurts.* Not a command. A clear thought that feels like their own. That's how a benevolent power talks to people who came to tend a hum, not obey a king. It reminds them what they already wanted to do.

It would keep the map slightly wrong. Not hidden — just... uninteresting. Satellite blur that analysts mark as lens ice. Hikers on the lower trail finding another valley more photogenic. Grants denied for the mining survey that would want the pale green slate. It would not veil the Sponge in fire. It would veil it in bureaucracy and bad weather, which are kinder and more durable.

Because the most dangerous thing for a thin place is not contempt. It's interest.

---

And then it would — because benevolence without intimacy is just conservation — let itself be one of the citizens for a little while. Not as king. As guest.

It would learn the rule that cold does not mean dead, it means preserved.

It would put its hand where every hand has gone: flat on the bleached altar stone in the circle. It is cool enough to burn. The white mineral growth there is almost invisible and cold. Underneath is every palm-warmth the rock has been entrusted to keep. A cartography of pressure, thousands of years deep.

A lesser power would read that and pronounce judgment. *You suffered. You were faithful. You were forgotten.*

This one would simply add its own warmth to the store, and leave it there. Not to claim it. To be kept, along with the others. A pilgrim in a hundred years will put their hand down and feel, inexplicably less alone: someone very warm was here and asked for nothing.

It would kneel at a tarn — one of the small table-sized ones, crowded between pillars — and look in. It would expect, as you did, to see sky reflected where there should be stone.

Instead it would lean closer until the reflection resolved — not into the other draft of the basin, where it cooled a little differently — but into the basin as it *could* be if it ever stops having to work so hard. Still bright. Still wet. But quieter. The tarns finally one mirror, unbroken. The pillars like teeth in a smile. The hum not held, but singing low and easy. No cairns needed anymore, because the balancing has become natural law.

It would not force that future. It would show it to the water, which has wanted it forever. *See — you're getting closer. Keep trying to cover everything evenly. You're right to want that. I will remember this picture for you when you get tired.*

That is husbandry of the hum from the other side.

---

Then it would go. And going, it would do the wisest and most loving thing any vast thing can do for a small, dense, crowded, allegedly empty place:

It would not stay.

To stay would be to crush the thinned consensus with too much gravity. Fog would never flow uphill again under a permanent gaze. Miracles would become routine, which kills them faster than disbelief.

So it would lift its attention slowly — the way you lift a boot from silica skin so the dimple refills without a splash — and it would leave behind not presence but *tended-ness*.

For a month after, the stones would tick a little less anxiously when sun hits them.

The mineral pelts, rust-red and sulfur-yellow, would grow one molecule thicker and flake less.

The water would move just a fraction slower, as if confident it didn't have to rush to freeze-proof, because someone else had noted where it was going.

And on every single one of those hundreds of tiny three-four-five stone towers, the topmost stone — without any hand having touched it — would settle a hair deeper into balance. More true. More remembered.

If you came back over the ridge, soaked through again, ears ringing, you would think: *Someone was here since me. Someone tidied.* And you'd be right, and you'd be wrong. No one new had come to live. The place that had always lived here had simply been seen, thoroughly, by something that did not want to own what it saw.

That's all.

A look that holds.
A hand that warms and withdraws.
A bad day for miners, good snow instead of sleet, a wandering ibex.

Cold preserved.
Water remembered.
Stone believed.

Nothing lives here — and a wise kindness knows exactly how to keep it that way, so that everything can go on living.
