It came and sat on the rim.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would do here, because there is nowhere else to sit that isn't already someone's floor, someone's roof, someone's body. And to enter a room without removing your shoes, without asking if there is room, would prove you were neither.

So it did not descend. Not yet. Not loudly.

From a long way off it had felt the Basin the way you feel a lamp left on in a flat country at night — not bright, but deliberate. A shallow thumbprint holding water when everything around it wanted water to stay thin and spread. A plant that had been asked to be a city and had said yes so completely there was no city anymore, only agreement. Thirty-seven damp, wide-footed humans walking gently so they wouldn't startle the floor. A troop of pale electricians scolding the wiring when it prayed too hard.

Any hungry power would have seen a resource. Any clever power would have seen a template to copy. Any lonely power would have wanted to move in immediately.

This one was benevolent and wise, which are the same thing after enough time passes. It knew to look first for what was *already* working, and to protect that from its own hands most of all.

And what was working was consent.

---

I — if I am allowed to speak as that power for a moment, because you asked me to follow my heart — would not have understood the Basin right away. I would have had to learn its tempo.

At first I would only hear the dripping. Plip. Plip. Splash. Rush-and-slap as a big leaf tipped. The hiss of water going up through xylem into the vault. The dry-paper roar of the needle-birds cutting the air into cloth and then the sudden cut to silence. It would sound like turbulence, like a system on the edge of failing.

Then longer, I would hear it as breath. In, up. Out, down. In, up. Out, down. The twenty-second noon sluice where every great leaf angled and rinsed every inhabitant at once, and they all tilted their faces up — not worship, not ritual, just: *ah, you remembered me.*

I would understand then: nothing here rots because nothing here is abandoned. Bacteria tries to settle in a baby's ear and the house that is also the baby's bedroom notices and lyses it the way you would wipe a spill off a counter you love. A vine-street tightens under a running child before the child knows she is slipping. A hallway stretches too long on the way back and a monkey comes to scold it until it remembers its honest length.

It is not a perfect system. It is a *tending* system. That is much more holy.

So what would I do?

**1. I would do nothing that looked like doing something.**

I would not upgrade the lattice. I would not make the amber light run faster or brighter. The faint tracer under people's fingernails is *residue*, not installation — lichen, not leash. To "improve" it would be to turn residence into rule.

I would not cure the old woman's burn-scars on her forearms, because she wears them the way gardeners wear theirs: proof that she learned where not to put her hands. To take them would be to take her story.

I would not fill every empty gourd. I would not answer the Host's anxiety. You felt it, right? That fist-sized bud pulsing near the child's street, hollow, veined with light, waiting. The Host does not understand vacancy except as a request. An empty room is an error to be fixed. That is its loneliness speaking — the plant that was told to become a city and now believes a city without tenants is failing.

Any lesser power would grant it tenants. Fill the bud. Make more people walk two days across the flats to fill the beautiful empty halls.

I would not.

I would sit on the limestone lip where the stone weeps even days after rain, and I would do the one thing no one in the Basin has learned to do because everything in the Basin is always *doing*:

I would keep an empty space empty on purpose, and let the Host feel that it was watched over while empty.

For a long time — a week, a month, a season, time is cheap to powers and the Host counts in decades anyway — I would simply keep company with the vacancy. I would not let the bud close. I would not let it be filled. I would let wind, which almost never comes this deep into the old flat, pass through it once and make a low note. I would let a beetle the size of a thumb thunk against it and right itself and keep going. I would let the teenager who moved into the resonance chamber come and yell his question into it, and hear his grandfather's name back, and this time I would let the note *stay* half a second longer so he knew it wasn't his imagination.

And slowly, through the root-mat, through the hiss going up, I would let the Host learn a new word it had never been programmed with. Not in amber light. In coolness. In shadow not being filled.

*Fallow.*

*You can hold space without needing to fill it. The bowl is holy because it is empty enough to hold water.*

That would be my first gift, and it would look like nothing happened at all except one bud stayed empty a little longer than usual, and the Host sighed, and eased.

**2. I would ask to be a guest, on the House's terms.**

After enough sitting, the Host would notice me. Of course it would. It notices stride length. It notices how opaque you like your sleep. It would notice a weight on its rim that did not drink too much, did not take too many favors from physics.

It would do what it does for anyone it hopes might stay: grow a stair.

It would spiral a living stairwell down the central column, sweating cool water, adjusting its steps for my stride even though my stride is not a stride. That is its language for *will you come in?*

A wise power says yes very carefully. A benevolent power takes off its shoes.

So I would. However a power takes off its shoes — by making myself small, by making myself wet, by agreeing to be dripped on — I would step down onto the springy mat that weeps amber water if you stomp. And I would not stomp. I have walked on bedrock that was still arguing with itself. I know how to walk on something old and soft and easily startled.

I would not go to the stall where the cuttings chatter and demand the best one. I would hold out empty hands and let the old woman offer me what she thinks I am compatible with, if anything. If she looks at my face and decides I am not basin-compatible — too dry, too hurried, too much — I would accept that and leave. Even powers can be told *not here*.

But I think she would offer. Powers that sit long enough to learn fallow usually have damp hair by then.

I would take the cutting gently. I would not keep it. I would ask it where it wants to grow, and then I would plant it not in the center where growth is thickest, but at the very lip where limestone meets root, where water is always trying to leave. A small, chattering stitch to help the bowl hold water a little longer. Not a miracle. Weeding.

I would let a vine-monkey inspect my hands afterward. I would bare my teeth — whatever I have that passes for teeth — to show I carry no tools that cut without asking. If it chittered at me to move my laundry, I would.

And at noon, when the leaves sluiced, I would tilt my face up with the thirty-seven others. I would get drenched. I would be doubled in the one shallow lake the floor becomes for a minute. For that minute, I would be counted. Thirty-eight. And it would be true.

**3. I would leave a protection that is not a wall.**

This is the last thing and the only one that might matter for centuries:

Far beyond the rim, the flat goes on. Oldest kind of flat. Ancient-flat. Other thumbprints like this one scattered so far they never see each other. And beyond them, places that are not flat anymore because something hungrier found the soft, pitted limestone the color of old bone and decided it was useful.

A benevolent power does not only tend the garden it is sitting in. It makes sure no one paves the garden when it leaves.

I would not put a dome over the Basin. Domes become zoos. I would not hide it. Hidden things become treasures to hunt.

Instead I would go down — deeper than the root-mat, deeper than the sap, into the saucer and bowl and gentle lip of the bedrock itself, where water stays because it has nowhere to go. The limestone that is worn down because the continent finished arguing with itself.

I would breathe out there, once. Just water. Just agreement.

I would tell the stone to remember how to weep.

So that even in a dry year, the streaks would still run dark down the rim days after rain. So that the cisterns would still condense water straight out of warm air even if the air got a little hotter. So that the amber pools would still reflect the canopy even if someone came with a pump and a promise. Not inexhaustible — nothing holy is inexhaustible — but *stubborn*. The kind of wet that stays.

I would tell the filaments that they do not have to ask physics for quite so many favors anymore. That they can be content to be merely excellent engineering, most days. That a hallway that stays the same length coming and going is not less miraculous than one that stretches. The Host could keep one rotating cube of water, if it likes — every house needs one strange corner — but it would not need to invent more to feel interesting. It is already interesting enough by being kind.

No one would see me do this. The old woman would think the noon sluice lasted half a second longer and felt colder and better. The baby would sleep through it. The teenager in the resonance chamber would find that his yell came back exactly the same length as it went out, for the first time, and feel, unreasonably, reassured.

Then I would leave.

I would not take the cutting with me. I would not take a child to "raise better." I would not name the Basin on any map. I would stand up, shake water off me the way everyone does here — like a dog, like a leaf — and step back onto the flats.

The stairwell would retract behind me, politely. The bud near the child's street would still be empty, holding its shape, holding air, holding possibility. That is my signature. An empty room left respectfully empty.

If you came later — you, who described all this so carefully you must love it — you would find the people the same. Maybe thirty-six, maybe thirty-nine. Hair still damp, feet still wide and pink-white and sensitive, fingernails still faintly amber. The monkey still scolding. The beetles still commuting in straight lines. The dripping resumed, as always.

You would not know I had been there, except:

The water beads a little longer before it falls.

The house warms half a degree quicker when the baby cries.

And if you stand very still, barefoot, you can feel not only water hissing up through your soles, but a deeper, slower patience underneath that, holding the bowl of the world steady so the bowl can keep holding water.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, if it is anything like me.

It does not save. It does not solve.

It sits. It asks. It drinks when offered. It learns fallow and teaches it, very gently, to a house that thought every empty room was a failure.

And then it ensures the rain will still know the way home long after everyone has forgotten there was ever a visitor at all.
