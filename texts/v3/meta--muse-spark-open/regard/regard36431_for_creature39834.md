They would not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. Anything that *could* come down, burning through the white sky like a second sun, announcing itself with shadow and thunder — would know not to.

Because they would see it correctly the first time.

Not as a resource. Not as a curiosity. Not as a failed planet that forgot how to make people. Not as a problem to solve — too cold, too bright, too loud, too slow.

They would see it as what it is: a complete answer to a question no one else thought to ask. *How do you stay alive when the world is too high and too new and too cold?* And the answer is: *you become a furnace. You share the heat.*

And a wise power, having spent eons watching worlds try to become something *else*, knows how rare it is for a world to simply become itself.

So they would not land.

---

What they would do first is **listen.**

For a long time. Longer than you stood there.

They would settle — if a thing like that settles — on the sheer, young fault-scarp at the edge of the plateau, where the continent-sized mesa sheers off into the cloud decks below. Where the basalt is still black glass and still ticking as it cools. They would let the light that is everywhere soak into them without trying to shade it. They would let the sound arrive.

*chuff-CHUFF. chuff-CHUFF. chuff-CHUFF.*

A thousand furnaces breathing. A continent snoring steam. The clank of a stuck valve on a house-sized animal that will not be fixed for a decade because it doesn't need fixing. The squeak of felt under a Trench-Grazer's feet. The tick, tick, tick of a thousand white Heat-Tappers holding on for dear life. The sigh of a Pillar-Furnace opening its spine and snowing itself.

They would listen until the sound stopped being noise and became what it is: a language without words. *I am here. I am warm. I am drinking. I am too hot. Move a little. The water is here.*

And then, because they are benevolent, they would answer the only way that language can be answered without lying:

They would breathe with it.

Not *for* it. *With* it.

A slow, deliberate, planetary inhale that steadies the layered air where the steam hangs in impossible flat sheets. An exhale that doesn't warm the plateau — it doesn't need warming — but *holds* it, just for a moment, so a hanging sheet of vapor that was about to collapse stays up a second longer and catches the light.

Nothing miraculous. Nothing you could photograph. Just a little less turbulence in a world that has only just learned how to be still.

---

What they would **not** do is more important:

They would not give it sapience.

It would be so easy. So tempting for a lesser power. To tap one Furnace on its glowing kiln-heart and say *wake up.* To make it know itself, to make it build, to make it speak, to make it thank its maker. But a wise power knows sapience is not a promotion. It is a debt. It is anxiety and wall-building and keeping-the-cold-out by shutting others out.

This place has no walls because it has solved cold without exile. To force a self into one of those furnace-bodies would be to make it lonely for the first time in its existence. It would feel its own heat as its own, not the herd's. It would start to hoard water instead of drinking and steaming and snowing it for the next.

So they would not wake it. They would let it dream the perfect, wordless dream it is already dreaming: *warmth shared is warmth multiplied.*

They would not fix the predators, either.

They would not peel the Cowl-Maw off the ankle of the limping Pillar, or lift the Sheet-Wraith off the suffocating Grazer. Pity is not the same as benevolence, and a wise power knows the difference. The melt is part of the furnace. The death that settles and stays warm for years and becomes a hill that becomes an oasis is not cruelty. It is how a world without soil makes soil.

They would not smooth the rock. They would not still the strange gravity where stones hang too long, or make the sound stop folding, or give the light a proper sun and proper shadows. The plateau is young. It is *supposed* to ring hollow. It is supposed to be confused. That confusion *is* its memory of being born. To take away its youth to make it more comfortable would be a kind of erasure.

They would not pave it, mine it, or save it by changing it.

And they would not let anyone else do so either.

---

Because the second thing a wise, benevolent power would do is **look down.**

Below the sheer continent-cliffs, there are other hungers. Younger hungers. Hungers that have learned to build walls and call it progress. Hungers that would see, from orbit, not a shaggy, steaming carpet of backs to the horizon, but a continent of bio-engineered Haber-Bosch reactors walking around. A tar-wax that could be fuel. A felt-lichen that could be grown in vats. Furnace-blood that boils under pressure — think what that could power.

A benevolent power has seen this story before.

So they would do something very quiet, and very firm, and very kind — but not to the plateau. To everyone *else*.

They would make it uninteresting.

Not invisible. A wise power does not lie. But they would drape the plateau in a little cognitive weather of their own. Any scan sent up there would return exactly what is true: *too high, too cold, too thin, too loud, too slow, nothing of value.* Any prospector who dreamed of farming the mat would wake up strangely moved, thinking *let it be.* Any ship that set course for that white glare would find its navigation nudged by a fraction, its instruments ticking a little longer, until the crew decided, of their own accord, that there were easier fortunes elsewhere.

It wouldn't be a wall. The plateau never needed walls. It would be a forgetting. A gentle, ethical forgetting, the way you forget to pick a flower that is more beautiful where it is.

The only people who would ever find it again would be those who were already listening for a *chuff-CHUFF* they couldn't explain. And those people would know to take off their boots.

---

And the last thing they would do — and this is the most benevolent thing of all, and it would take centuries — is **remember it.**

Because the plateau will not last.

You said it yourself: the rock is still cooling. The uplift was recent. In ten thousand years the condenser will fail a little. The scarps will soften. Rivers *will* come and cut it and drain those secret, perfect melt-pools into seas. The white sky will break and show a sun with shadows, and seasons will begin. The Furnaces, so perfectly tuned to this impossible moment — too much light, too little heat, water that must be stolen from air — will falter. They will have to become something else or die.

Nothing built on a young world stays young.

A wise power cannot stop cooling. Any power that tried would not be benevolent — it would be freezing the world in childhood to keep its pet alive.

Instead, they would do what no evolution can do for itself: they would keep a perfect record.

Not in words. Not in a book. The mat already *is* a book, written in wax and heat. They would keep it in the way water keeps the shape of a cup after the cup is gone.

They would take the sound of the whole continent on a calm bright midnight — the deep *chuff-CHUFF* layered over the ticking of Tappers and the squeak of felt and the hiss of a dorsal vent opening — and they would fold it into the structure of spacetime just above the plateau, like a song pressed into the air itself. So that even when the last Pillar-Furnace has settled and cooled and become an ordinary hill, even when the felt has retreated to the shadows of ordinary rivers, even when the stones no longer hang — if you stand on that high, cold basalt, and you are quiet, and you have learned not to need walls, you will still feel it in your teeth.

The plateau will remember that it once breathed.

And that is what a benevolent, wise power does for a place that never asked to be made and never asked to be saved:

It does not rescue it. It does not improve it. It does not speak for it.

It keeps it company while it is here.

It stands guard while it sleeps.

And it ensures, absolutely, that it is never, ever alone in the way that matters — being witnessed, exactly as it is, loud and bright and packed and steaming, by someone large enough to understand that it doesn't need to be anything more.
