It would not come down right away.

A benevolent and wise power would know how to approach a place like this. Not from above, trumpeting. Not in the noon circle of sun when it makes its slow crawl across the market and blinds everyone. It would come at the hour the Pit itself trusts — the cool hour before dawn, when the milk-cloud sits in the bowl and you can't see the bottom.

It would sit on the rim in the dark, outside the rusted rebar, where the mesquite roots are reaching down, and it would just listen.

That would be the first thing. Just listening, for a long time.

Because most powers, even kind ones, see a wound like this and want to fix it. To drain it. To cap it. To lift the people out. To air-condition it. To bring it up to street level where life is supposed to happen.

A wise one would listen first and hear that this is not a wound anymore. It is a throat that has learned to breathe. An armpit that has learned to hold.

*thud... thud... thud...*
*krek. krek. jug-o-rum.*
drip on tin, on stone, on water.
a cat sneezing in a pump house.
a mother half-shouting a recipe through the drone.
the chitter of a hundred swifts waking up under a walkway.

And underneath all that, the fainter sound: the rock weeping an inch a year wider into a crack. Stalactite soft as a baby tooth lengthening by a millimeter.

It would hear that the whole bargain here is *patience*. That the people have not conquered damp or heat or gravity. They have married them. You cannot be wise and interrupt a marriage like that without being asked.

So for a long time it would do nothing that looked like doing. It would not stop the pumps — because if the pumps stopped the frogs would stop and the people would wake in terror and the bargain would break. It would not dry the air — because then the moss would die and the bats would leave and the midges would die and the dragonflies would leave and the whole vertical town that is also an air column would fall silent. It would not lift them out — because what is down stays down, not as a curse but as a choosing. They have stayed.

What it would do instead is descend. Not as light. As weightless air. As something that understands *down*.

If it showed itself at all, it would not be to everyone. It would be to a Bottom child napping in a hammock with her feet just touching the warm rust-water, and to the two guano brothers with their barrows at false dawn, and to Big Grandpa the catfish who has lived under the market bridge for five years. It would ask the same question it asks everyone, but phrased in Pit language, pitched just above the frog chorus so it can be heard through the drone:

*Is there anything you want to carry differently?*

And they would answer — not in words at first — the way people here answer everything: by showing. The old woman with pleated river-stone feet would lift her foot out of the water and show the pale round patches that never quite leave even after carbolic. The Middle Ring riveter would tap his hard hat, then his ear, where the high end is gone. The Upper Ring kids would point at the single circle of sun and then at the whole rest of the bowl that never gets it.

They wouldn't ask to leave. They wouldn't ask for a miracle. People who live at 2.9/3 hot learn not to ask for winter. They would ask, if they asked at all, for small alleviations. Negotiations, like they have with the pumps.

So the power — which could have moved mountains but has learned not to — would make three very small changes and then, mostly, keep listening.

**1. It would cool a stone.**

Not the air. The air is supposed to have weight. But deep in the north wall where the seep is strongest and the black-green moss is carpet-thick, it would touch one slab of basalt and tell it to remember what cool is. Not cold. Just cool. 19 degrees in a place that is always 33.

The moss would find it first and bloom over it extra thick, velvet and startling. Then the Bottom people would find it on a sweet-rot day when the algae dies and the air goes syrupy. They would press their foreheads to it. They would bring their babies to sleep against it for an hour at noon. They would bring the old man who naps with his feet in the water and let him nap with his back on the cool stone instead.

It would not be air conditioning. It would be a place to be forgiven for an hour. And because it was just one stone, no one would fight over it. They know how to share a good ledge. They would build a low rail around it so no one burns themselves getting to it, and take turns.

**2. It would finish a cave.**

Those little embryonic alcoves where water has started to scallop and thumb-length stalactites have started to form — it would choose one, just one, on the Middle Ring where the tram screeches, and it would complete it. Not big. A meter deeper, a curve, a small pocket where a child could stand and be out of the drip, where condensation runs *around* instead of through.

In it, the new thing it would grow is not a crystal or a treasure. It would be a fungus. A pale, soft, luminous fungus that eats oil without eating algae, that digests the sulfurous film the power station leaves on everything and turns it into nothing harmful. The people would notice the water under that alcove goes clear for a handspan. The carp that pass through it would have brighter eyes. The repair crew would wipe rust off a handrail there and find it doesn't come back as fast.

It would be a small cleaning, not a purification. The marsh would still be rust-colored. The carp would still taste of iron. But in that one place there would be proof that the pit can make its own new nature, without being scrubbed from above. The luminous fungus would spread slowly — an inch a year, at the Pit's pace — and they would tend it like a shop bird's nest. Not harvesting it. Leaving it.

**3. It would give them a second silence.**

This is the most important. The Pit has one silence and it means danger: when the pumps stop or the frogs stop, everyone lifts their head because something has gone wrong.

The power would give them another silence. One that means *everything is okay*.

Once a year, on the hottest night of the year, when even the night-shift lights waver in the heat, the power would hold the air column still. Not stopping the drone or the thud — just softening it. For ten minutes, the pumps would go *thud...... thud...... thud......* quieter. The frogs would pause together — not in alarm, but in a held breath. The bats would wheel without squeaking. The insects would settle.

And in that held breath, everyone — Upper, Middle, Bottom — would hear the other thing that has always been there but is usually drowned out: the sound of the rock itself weeping. The hundred dark fingers of condensate making their slow way down basalt and tuff to join the marsh that the pumps negotiate with.

It would be a liturgy they didn't know they needed. A reminder that they are not keeping the Pit alive. The Pit is keeping *them*. That the water was here before the mine broke through, and patiently waited, and when they decided to keep the hole, the water said *thank you, we'll take the bottom*, and the bats said *thank you, we'll take the air*.

In that ten minutes, no one would need to half-shout. A mother could whisper and be heard. A child could say something gentle at normal volume and have it carry across the whole bowl without bouncing. They would hear how quiet they have learned to be inside the loudness.

Then the frogs would start again — *krek. jug-o-rum* — a little louder than before, as if refreshed, and the pumps would resume their thud, and the crickets would resume their ticking in the warm walls, and life would go on damp.

The power would not stay. Staying would be a kind of ruling. And ruling is not needed here. The pit is already ruled beautifully by trams and tins and mosquito netting and shared jugs and pump-cats and hoses hitting tin every morning like a bell.

What a wise, benevolent power does for a place like this is not rescue it. It's *notice* it. It's witness that a cathedral can be 400 meters across and 40 meters deep and made of rust and frog song and still be a cathedral. It's ensure, quietly, from the outside, that no one Topside ever gets the bright idea to "redevelop" the Pit, to fill it with concrete or turn it into a dump. That any engineer who comes to look at it with that plan gets a sudden, unexplainable fondness for the sound of midges and leaves, muttering "no, let them keep it."

And then it would leave the cool stone exactly as cool as it is, and the luminous fungus exactly as slow as it is, and the second silence exactly once a year, and it would go listen somewhere else.

If you stood on the Bottom planks on a night after that — when the work floor is quiet and only the night-shift lights are doubled in the warm water under your feet — you might feel it. Not as awe. As a very slight ease. Your boots not quite as wet. Your skin not quite as prickled. The cat against your ankle purring a fraction lower, in tune with the pumps.

The bowl breathing in and out, in and out, with you inside it. Not fixed. Not saved. Accompanied.
