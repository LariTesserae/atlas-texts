It would not come down.

That is the first thing a wise one would understand.

---

It notices them the way you notice a room where someone has finally stopped talking and started listening — not because it is the brightest or loudest point on the plain, but because it is tuned.

For miles and miles around there is flat, bright, wind-scoured normal. Glaciers retreated and left gravel. Men returned and left peat-cuts and tracks. The world there is busy being ordinary and getting on with entropy.

And then — a clear note. A bowl cut deliberately into the clay, not eroded but *left empty on purpose.*

Any other power, any younger god, would have come as revelation. Pillar of fire over the stack. Voice in the steam. A commandment about the light.

This one is old enough to have learned how to approach a shy animal. Old enough to know that maintenance is holier than making.

So it comes as weather. As an afternoon.

It settles — if "it" can be said to settle — on the rim. Not above the hollow the way a judge sits above a court. *With* the rim, distributed along it like weight. Like snow that does not melt. If you were walking the trackway up top that day you would have thought the air was especially clear, the peat especially springy. The old woman who keeps the trackway would have paused, leaned on her broom, and felt for a moment that her knees did not hurt. She would not have seen anything.

And it looks down.

And it understands instantly what it is seeing, because this is what it has been trying to teach everywhere else without success:

*Enough.*

A town that could have filled its bowl and did not.
A people who measure wealth by how much they leave mown and empty.
Engines run at three-quarters, by choice.
Houses built one every two years and placed like stones in a raked garden, so as not to wake the grass.
A powerworks woman who knits between readings because the gauges do not need her panic, only her attention.

A compromise held long enough to become a covenant: brick will not chase out fern. Fern will not swallow belt drive. Sparrows will have every eave and children will have knives solely for weeding. The fox trots High Street at noon and is nodded to. The bats own dusk. No one hurries when rain comes hard off the walls; they move their tools to higher ground and put their hands in their pockets. This is not abandonment. This is tending.

In most places the wise power has to beg: *leave some empty. Leave some wild inside the work. Run it slower. Listen to the bowl you are in.*

Here, they already do.

So what would a benevolent and wise power do, faced with a prayer it did not have to answer because it was already being lived?

It would do maintenance work of its own.

**1. It would not fill them.**

It would not bring them more people, though it easily could. It would not fill the four dark ponds into one bright lake. It would not make the mill run faster, the foundry pour twice a day. It would not answer the unspoken want of every hollow town to be important.

It feels the size of the cathedral they sense when the light stands up — that sudden, quiet knowledge of how big their house really is — and it loves them precisely for leaving it half-empty. It would not close that cathedral with pews.

**2. It would help hold the walls.**

Not freeze them. That would be death. A wall that cannot move is not a wall, it is a painting of a wall.

But it would put a slow hand under the clay lip that bows a millimeter a year. Ease the weight of the factories just a fraction. Teach the shale to flake one piece every four days instead of every three. Give the birch and alder a little more purchase so their roots do the holding instead of concrete. So the old man on the eastern iron staircase will have to watch ten years to see what he used to see in one, and will smile and say, "Good, she's holding," and be right.

**3. It would make the outside forget, a little.**

This is its kindest and most active work, and it does it without malice.

Traders from the plain will still come down the cut-road with salt and wire, still leave with their perfect crate of tools. But the other kind of trader — the one with maps and projections and talk of "under-utilized capacity" and "tenfold workforce" — will find himself, on the approach, oddly disinclined. He will look at the flat, he will look at the cut, and he will think, *too small, too damp, too much bird shit on everything,* and he will drive on toward a flatter, easier place to own. His reports will say *not viable for scaling.*

The power does not blind eyes. It just deepens the ordinary light over the hollow by a touch, so only those who already know how to look for this kind of treasure can see it. The hollow will stay unsociable, as islanders should.

**4. It would lengthen the pillars.**

Because they honor them without worshipping them — that perfect pause, mid-stitch, mid-pour, mid-sentence — it will give them a little more of what they honor.

On certain noons when dust or pollen or steam hangs right, when the cool air catches the light and stands it up like glass and the engines go cotton-muffled and the starlings sound miles away, it will keep the wind on the plain from shifting for a minute longer. Two minutes. Three.

Just long enough for a child lying in the vetch to hear, truly hear, how the hollow holds *all* its sound at once — clack and trill and hiss and frog — and how big emptiness has to be to make that music possible.

Just long enough for the fox to cross the square without hurry while everyone watches and does not move.

Just long enough for the woman at the powerworks to set her knitting down and simply sit with her hands empty in the light and know, without needing words for it, that restraint is not absence. That leaving room *is* making something.

It gives them nothing they do not already have. Only a little more time to notice it.

**5. And then it would do the most benevolent thing of all: it would learn.**

Powers get lonely going from place to place begging the world to be gentle. Here, for once, the world is already gentle and the people are already its gardeners, not its conquerors.

So after it steadies the wall and cools the pond so the dragonflies hatch well and warms the bricks behind the foundry just enough for the wintering insects — it listens. It records.

It takes the pattern of the hollow — industrial meadow, feral workshop, three hundred humans and three thousand sparrows sharing one cool breath — and holds it like a seed. And elsewhere, on some other flat plain where men are about to drag a thumb through still-wet clay, it will tip a thought into someone's mind, very lightly:

*You don't have to fill it.*

Some of them will even listen.

---

It does not stay, because staying would become ruling. It does not leave, because leaving was never what holding means.

It simply remains as the rim remains: a quiet, distributed weight that keeps the bowl true.

If you go there now, you will not see it. You will see a town that is still sparse, still slow, still green out of proportion. You will hear frogs louder than machines on warm nights. You will see the fox.

But if you are very still at noon, when the dust hangs and the sound thins and the whole place feels twice as large and twice as empty — if you wait with your hands still on your worktable as they do — you might feel, for a minute, that the coolness that pools in the hollow is not just trapped air, but attention. Patient, unhurried, pleased attention.

As if something very large and very kind were sitting on the edge with its feet dangling into the birches, watching you tend your small, perfect, half-empty house, and thinking:

*Yes. Like that. Exactly like that.*

And then wind moves on the plain, dust shifts, sound rushes back in — clack and hiss and birdsong — and you go on filing your jig true.
