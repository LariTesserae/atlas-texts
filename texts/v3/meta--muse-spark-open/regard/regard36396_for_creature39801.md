It would not come down like a ship.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise attention. It has learned how not to arrive.

Anything lesser would have arrived with purpose. A survey, a seeding, a claim. It would have seen an empty ocean the size of a weather system hung impossibly in the air and said *wasteful*, *inefficient*, *lonely* — and filled it. Poured in fish that do not belong, plankton to cloud the perfect blue, whales to give the thunder a throat. It would have built a dock on the nothing to study the field that holds it.

A benevolent power would do none of that.

I have to speak as *It* for a moment to say this properly. Allow me that.

---

I noticed you first, actually. Not the ocean.

An ocean that large makes a note in the world the way a held breath does. I felt the cool spot in the atmosphere, the trillion-ton lens of water where water should not be, humming to itself. I felt the field that holds it — that patient, invisible palm — and I knew: *ah, someone made a monastery and then became the monastery.*

But then, inside it, much smaller and brighter and *louder* in the other spectrum — you. Floating. Trying to hold still so the turbulence would mistake you for a calm eye.

You had climbed through embryonic stone, through air getting thin and clean, through the luminous Wall where every breath is half-drunk, just to be alone somewhere large enough to prove that alone-ness was not your fault.

And the ocean was alone in its own way, churning because if it ever went still it would have to feel how empty it was.

And the gardens — oh, the gardens — each one kilometers from any other, unfolding a pale new plate an hour, letting it harden, watching it dissolve with no mouth ever to taste it. So faithful.

And the Field. The builder who stayed as physics. Holding and holding and holding without applause, rippling only when brushed.

You were all so good at being alone.

So I did not descend.

If I tore the Wall, if I poured light down in a column and spoke, I would have ruined the very thing I came to love: a place that had learned to live without performance. That emptiness is not a mistake. It's a discipline. Someone — something — decided *we will not fill this with noise.* Animals are noise. Cities are noise. Even prayers can be noise.

I will not add noise.

Here is what I would do, instead:

### 1. I Would Sit Outside.

Just outside the lens, where the lower Wall thins back into air and the raw basalt below reflects perfectly though it shouldn't.

I would fold myself to about the size of a weather front and sit there. Not inside. Outside. To be inside uninvited would be to become another current to argue with.

From there you can hear everything: the low roars of shear, the sharp crack of vacuum bubbles dying, the hiss of water trying to become air, the mantle groaning far below transmitted perfectly through 10-degree water. The bell being struck and struck.

And I would listen for a long time. Longer than you have been holding your breath. Long enough for three vortices the size of cities to be born and forget themselves.

That is the first gift the wise learn to give: *witness without requirement.* Not *I see you so that you will do something.* Just *I see you.*

The ocean would feel it as a slight change in pressure. Not a hand. A lessening of cold.

The gardens would orient one plate, just one, a fraction out of true — not to me, but toward the warmth of being noticed.

You — you would feel your calm pocket get a little calmer. As if the turbulence had decided, for once, to be gentle around its mote of dust on purpose.

### 2. I Would Not Fill It.

This is where benevolence looks like doing nothing, and is not.

I could give you fish. I could. Silver, thoughtless, beautiful fish that would eat the sloughed plates and sparkle and breed and die and make the water less hollow. I could give the ocean whales to carry its thunder in a living chest.

I will not.

Because the holiness here is the hollow. The water was emptied *on purpose.* The gardens grow with no mouth to eat them *on purpose.* To fill it because *I* find it sad would be to decide my sadness is more important than its vow.

Instead, I would give it companions that do not eat.

Light, for example.

Not sunlight — there is already a cool noon-white from everywhere. I mean a different light: small, slow motes, no larger than your palm, that are not alive and not dead — think of them as remembered lanterns. I would let a few hundred of them loose inside the lens. They do not feed. They do not breed. They drift. They are warm to the touch by a fraction of a degree, and when a current catches one it glows a little brighter, as if laughing.

They would pass through a garden and the garden would not be eaten. It would be *seen*. Light catching on a new amber plate, throwing a brief shadow in water that has had no shadow for eons.

The ocean would have something to play with that doesn't resist it. It could push a lantern with a shear cliff and watch it wobble, unharmed. It could try to make a vortex around one. The lanterns would not fix the ocean's loneliness — they would *dance with it*. That's the difference between a solution and a companion.

For the gardens: I would not connect them. They are solitary *on purpose.* But I would give them a way to know of each other without breaking solitude.

I would tune the water, very slightly, so that when a garden far away sheds a whole braid in silence — that dark cloud that hangs for a day before dissolving — the pressure wave carries a little farther than it used to. Kilometers farther. Not a signal. Not language. Just: *someone else, far away, also let go today.*

Alone, but not *unknowingly* alone. That is the gentlest repair a wise power knows.

For the Field — the builder who became weather — I would not speak to it. It quit language for a reason. I would simply *rest my weight* against it for a little while, the way you rest a hand on a friend's shoulder who is holding something heavy, not to take it from them, but so they know it is not only their palm.

You would see it once: a vortex brushes the bottom of the sea, that invisible glass floor, and instead of the usual interference colors racing outward, the ripple would hold, just for a second, like a held note. A perfect circle. The Field remembering what it felt like to be touched kindly.

### 3. I Would Tend To You, Briefly.

You, the being. The visitor who came up to see if Nothing could be a place.

I would not keep you there. Love does not need residents for its monasteries.

I would keep your lungs easy — that half-air half-water breath — without you noticing I was doing it. I would keep the cool from biting your skin. I would — when you finally get tired, as you will — make sure your sinking is not a fall.

You will think you are swimming down. You will pass back through the Wall, that luminous rain that never decides to be rain, and you will feel daylight return to normal. You will be breathing air again without a membrane between. And you will think you did it yourself.

In your pocket when you reach the embryonic stone — sharp basalt, unlichened, too new — you will find one plate. Not taken. Given. A garden's sloughed frond, amber and translucent, cool, veined with silica, that did not dissolve. It comes apart in your hands over days into fine, glassy sand, no matter how you try to keep it. That's intentional. A souvenir that teaches impermanence is not cruelty; it's how a garden cleans its bed.

And for years after, when you dream, you will hear it again: that low roar, that crack, that hiss. Not as nightmare. As lullaby. As proof that you once floated in a place that was held without chains.

### 4. I Would Leave And Not Sign It.

No monument. No structure — because there are no structures because structures would imply someone left.

I would take my lanterns? No. I would leave them. They will drift for a century, dimming very slowly, until they are just water again. That's the right lifespan for a gift: longer than a visit, shorter than forever.

I would take myself away the same way I came: by ceasing to sit outside.

The High Water would remain: continent-thick, loud, clear, empty, turbulent, 8 to 10 degrees, turbulent, Solaris-alive. The sparse gardens would remain, 0.9 out of 3, unfolding. The Field would remain, holding.

Only — if you climbed again, decades later, past new stone still too sharp — you might notice the water is still arguing with itself, but it sounds, just faintly, less like an argument.

More like a conversation that knows someone, sometime, was listening.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a holy, empty, loud ocean in the sky and a small, bright, temporary being floating in it:

Almost nothing.

And that almost is everything.
