It would not come down.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would understand about the High Works - that to *arrive* here would be a violence. Anything large enough to be called a Power casts a shadow, and there are no shadows in the Works. There is only the high pearlescent grey that never sharpens, the light that beads on handrails. To descend with radiance, with trumpets, with a pillar of sun breaking through over Tier 43, would be to blind the eyes that have learned to see in the dim. It would be to terrify the children who have learned to read time by how thin the grey gets.

So it stays high. Higher than the moor tiers, where even the catch-gates are. It does what the people of the Works do when they want to understand something: it watches.

And what it sees, for a long time, is not a problem to be solved.

A lesser power - a kinder but less wise one - would have seen it instantly as suffering: *Cold, damp, dim, endless labor, knees ruined by forty, clothes that never dry. I will fix this.* It would have thrown heat down like a blanket, dried the moss, burned off the cloud, made the concrete warm. It would have tried to give them lowland gifts.

A month later every trough would have cracked. Every ivy curtain would have fallen off the wall dead. Every cistern would have bloomed with algae and choked. The Water Law would have broken, because Water Law is a law for scarcity and slow seep, not for flood and sun. The people would have stood in dry, warm, bright ruin, blinking, and not known how to be Tier-born anymore.

This Power is wise enough to know that 1.3 degrees and 1.1 light are not punishments. They are the terms of the agreement. The plants agreed to them. The people agreed to them. The machines, old and settled into geography, agreed to them.

So it does not warm the stone. It does not part the clouds.

What it does instead is much smaller, and because it is small, it lasts.

---

### 1. It learns Maintenance.

For a season - which in the Works is only a word for "a run of tiers where the beans are ready" - the Power does nothing but listen to the *plip, tink, seep-hiss* that the people listen for in their sleep.

It learns that Cistern 22-B has a hairline crack that the family who sleeps under it has been watching for three years, catching the drip in a bucket and pouring it back into the trough that is also their roof. It learns that the Sluicer crew on Tier 19 has a wheel that seizes every third day and takes three of them to free, and they are tired. It learns that Wall Jenner, rebuilt as a water-stair after she fell in '82, is starting to spall at her third step. It learns that an old Keeper on Tier 31, whose hands are black-green to the wrist, has Tier-knee so badly now she cannot take the stairs sideways anymore and so she takes them straight on, slow, holding both rails.

It does not heal the world. It heals *this*. Not by miracle, but by maintenance.

On a night when the 50-cycle thrum cuts out on Tier 21, and no one can quite hear why the next hall three tiers down hasn't started - that micro-gap of silence that wakes everyone who knows how to wake - the Power leans in and *remembers* for the motor. Not a new motor. The same three types they've rewound for eighty years. It just remembers how the copper wants to lie, and the tired man rewinding it finds his hands moving surely after hours of fumbling. The hall starts. The gap closes. No one sees anything.

At Cistern 22-B, the drip finds a single grain of lime in the water and lays it down exactly across the hairline. Not sealed. Just slower. The bucket takes two hours to fill instead of one. The family sleeps through a rain.

On Tier 31, the old Keeper wakes and her knees have bled a little of their ache in the night. Not young knees. Just enough less pain that she can take the stairs sideways again, low center, braced. She thinks it's because she finally dried her inner wool properly against the hot pipe. She touches the rust-dark buttress as she passes, as always. The iron is, for a heartbeat, not cold.

The Works, which is built on the principle that nothing can be made new, only kept, understands this kind of gift perfectly: *You may not take faster than your tier gives, you may not block what the tier below expects.* Even a god obeys Water Law here. It takes nothing. It gives back exactly what is needed to keep everything making.

### 2. It gives them a little more of what they already love.

A benevolent power does not give people what *it* thinks they should want. It gives them more of what they have already chosen.

So:

It does not give them sun. It gives them, on a random afternoon when the grey thins toward noon, a single hour where the pearlescent light becomes truly beautiful, even to them - where the droplets on every ivy leaf catch and throw it back, and the whole gorge, tier after tier of green and black and rust red, glows like the inside of a shell. The Keepers stop on their beats. They don't pray. They just look across at the opposite flank - the one they never sightsee - and for once see it, really see it, and think: *we kept this.*

It does not give them silence. The Works is already mostly quiet. It gives them, deep in the pipe-packed dark under Tier 26, one night where the drips line up - *plip... tink... seep* - into a rhythm so perfect and brief it sounds like music. Two Carriers - children with yokes - stop and grin at each other in the dark, and then keep walking.

It does not give them birds. Fauna 0.9 would not hold a flock. It gives the single crow that cuts across the gorge on business a second crow, for a week. They see them together, once, turning over the turbine steam. No one makes a story about it. But a boy on Tier 22, who pollinates the dwarf espaliered trees by hand with a rabbit-fur brush, watches them and decides to try crossing two varieties of brassica that no one has crossed before. It works. It will feed Tier 22 and 23 through the cold press.

And to the old women and old men whose hair never dries, it gives the most tender gift of all: it lets the watercress in the gutters grow just a little more peppery this year. So that when they crush it underfoot, carrying impossible loads on swaying carts, the smell that comes up - cold earth and iron and wet wool and sour-green - smells, for a moment, exactly like *home*, even to those who were born here and have never known any other home. It confirms them.

### 3. It protects them from being seen badly.

Because the Works is saturated and has no edge, lowlanders, if they ever saw it clearly through the distance that never burns off, would call it a slum, a hive, a dystopia. They would come with plans. With heat. With rescue.

The wise power knows the most dangerous thing for a place that has become geography is to be *re-discovered*.

So it leaves a small un-noticing around the mountain. Not invisibility. Just - when the surveyors in the lowland look up with sharp shadows and dry ground on their minds, their eyes slide off. The cloud that is caught on the shoulder of country looks like cloud. The steams that smear sideways along the walls look like weather.

It does not hide the Works. It lets the Works hide itself, as it has always done, behind being oppressively, beautifully normal.

If a youth walks down the thousand-foot stairsteps toward the gorge where the water finally runs fast, and up the opposite flank to see if it's different, the Power walks with him, not as light but as the surety that the stairs will be slick in the familiar way, that the railing will be cold and damp in the familiar way, that when he gets there it will be continuous, and he can come back and learn to read the moss.

### 4. At the end, it asks nothing and leaves no sign.

This is what makes it benevolent.

It does not carve its name into the retaining wall that is also a house. It does not demand that they touch the buttresses for *it* now. It does not want to be Wall Jenner.

Before it goes - because even Powers do not stay forever, and that is part of being wise - it does one last thing, almost too small to call a miracle at all.

On Upper Tier 43, where you are standing, where the profile of the original hillside can still be felt in your knees, it dries a single stair nosing. Just one. Worn to a shine by a century of boots, dished in the middle. For an afternoon, the moss on it is not wet. The stone is warm from some heat held in the concrete behind the ivy.

A Keeper coming up with a crate of brassicas, Tier-knee bowing her outward, low center, bracing her thighs against the grade, puts her foot there. And does not slip. Not this once. She steadies, surprised, shifts her crate, and goes on.

She will not remember which stair it was. But her knee will remember that for one step, the mountain that replaced the mountain carried her, instead of her carrying it.

And that is enough.
