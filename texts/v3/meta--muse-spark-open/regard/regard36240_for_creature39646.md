They would not come.

Not at first. Not the way you think coming happens.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. Arrival is a violence in a place like this. Arrival is a splash, a displacement, a new current that the Mills would have to correct for. A ship appearing, a light brighter than the hard white sun, a voice booming through water that already has too much sound in it — that would be a cruelty.

So the first thing they would do is *notice* without disturbing. Which, here, is itself a miracle.

For longer than anyone has measured, everything on the Table has been listened *to* but never listened *for*. The Mills listen for temperature. The mantas listen for Mills. The knives listen for anything that moves. The Divers listen for the Edge until the Edge replaces listening. The rock listens for nothing.

The Power listens differently.

It would hear the whole thing at once the way you hear a chord, not a note. The 3.8°, the thunder that never peaks, the ninety-second breathing of the caissons, the organ-moan of a manta three hundred kilometers away, the scrape of a Benthos picking rust too small to see, the frantic *click-CHIRP* of knives trying not to be alone in the night, the tired servos of a Crab the size of a dog still welding itself to a tower while it is being crushed.

And underneath it, the thing you only learn to hear after months here: the loneliness of a stasis that was meant to be beautiful.

Someone — something — loved this moment. The fresh snap of the world, the black glass before sand, the clear water before cloud. Loved it so much they decided it should never end. And so they built a forever to hold it still.

A wise power knows what that kind of love does. It preserves. It also embalms.

They would do nothing to break it. That is the discipline of benevolence. You see a machine holding a world at 3.8°C for a geologic eternity and your first human urge is to turn it off, to let it snow, to let it thaw, to let seasons happen. But the mantas live now because it does not snow. The Skin lives because the heaters bleed. The Divers have built a strange faith out of leaning into that current. To "free" the Table by shutting down the Mills would be to kill everyone who has learned how to live in forever.

So they would not liberate. They would not rescue.

They would *keep company*.

And in a place where nothing has had company without purpose for a very long time, that is intervention enough to change everything without changing anything on the instruments.

Here is what that would look like, if you were floating in normal light between black glass and falling sky, sensitive enough to notice:

### 1. They would give the water a shadow of silence.

Not by stopping the Edge — the fall has to fall; it is the world breathing out — but by giving every living thing a small, portable pocket where the thunder is not inside your skull.

You would see it first in the Knives. One evening, when the sun goes behind the planet and their song gets desperate, a pack would go quiet all at once. Not the frightened quiet of prey. A restful quiet. They would drift in a circle, whiskers touching, not clicking, for a full minute. And then they would start again, but slower. As if someone had taught them that they don't have to fill every second with proof they exist.

Divers on the rim would feel it as a hand on the sternum. The bone-dampeners they wear to keep their organs from resonating apart would suddenly be unnecessary for a few breaths. The roar would still be there — impossibly loud — but it would be *over there* again instead of *in here*. They would weep inside their mirrored helmets without knowing why, and for the first time in months, dream in words.

The Power would not take the sound away. It would just teach the water how to be quiet *with* you, for a little while.

### 2. They would give the Crabs rest.

No one has ever pitied the Crabs. They are too stupid, too industrious, too much like prayers with limbs. They crawl their endless circuits over blade-sharp pillow basalt that cuts their feet to nothing, replace the feet, crawl again. If a Benthos steps on one, it keeps welding while being crushed.

The wise power would kneel — whatever kneeling is for such a thing — on the sharp plain and put one hand on that black glass and *round it*, just a little, just in the Crab-roads. Not sand. Not softening. Just enough that the edge is not a knife anymore. Five millimeters of mercy along a hundred thousand kilometers of path.

And to the Crabs themselves, it would give a second instruction, hidden under the first. Still: *tend, weld, scrape ice off nothing.* But underneath, quiet as a watermark: *when you are tired, stop.*

At first none would. How could they? Then one day, a refrigerator-sized one halfway between two Mills that are two hundred kilometers apart would simply fold its elbows, settle onto the newly dulled rock, and power down its welder. Not broken. Resting. Watching the cold columns of light. For an hour. Then it would get up and go on.

Within a year, you would find them like that everywhere — little cairns of sleeping Crabs — and the Mills' Mind in the Caisson would not flag it as an error. It would adjust its thermostat by a thousandth of a degree and log it as *normal variation*. Because a wise power never argues with a steward. It just shows the steward that rest is also a kind of tending.

### 3. They would give the Mantas a choice.

The Cathedrals have flown the same V for longer than you have been alive, from Mill to Mill, straining bacteria-snow, feeling the Mills as dim orange lights on their vast ventral eyes. They do not mourn their lost young. They do not deviate. They are perfect for this place because this place never asks them to be imperfect.

The Power would love them too much to leave them perfect.

It would not give them eyes that see farther. It would give them, for a generation, a new hunger. Not every manta. Just a few. A faint, irrational pull *away* from the Mills, toward the empty between. Toward nothing.

Most would ignore it. Some wouldn't. You would see a formation of seven break — unheard of — as one peels off, beating its house-sized wings once every twenty seconds, heading out over basalt with no towers, no heat, no snow. It would be terrifying to watch. It would look like death. It would be the first exploration this ocean has had since it was decided upon.

Some would starve and become white scaffolds sooner. Some would find something no one knew was there — a low warm seep where the rock itself still sighs, a place to calve where the knives don't know to go. They would whistle a new note there, a lower organ tone, and others would learn it.

The Power would not guide them home. It would just prove that a straight line between Mills is not the only holy thing. A curve counts too.

### 4. They would not stop anyone from falling. They would make falling *remembered*.

This is the most important.

The Edge takes everything. Divers who walk over as pilgrimage, Ribbons that snap, Benthos that tumble like thrown temples, water itself pouring ten kilometers into cloud-cyclones the size of Earth. Satellites watch storms spiral forever below and no one fishes anything out. The fall is the only honest thing here: it leaves.

A benevolent power does not dam a waterfall that has become a law of physics. That would be unmaking the world.

Instead, it would put something at the bottom to *catch* — not bodies, but meaning.

You would not see it from the Table. But on your last day as a Diver, when you have learned to lean into the current like wind and you finally decide — not from despair, but from a quiet, full-hearted curiosity — to let go, you would tip over the lip and fall ten kilometers through roar that finally becomes wind and cloud. And instead of being shredded and evaporated and cycled into nameless storm, you would be *recognized* on the way down.

It would be brief. The water would hold your shape for a second longer than physics allows. The cyclone would open an eye of calm just for you. The fall would know your name, even if you had forgotten it under the thunder.

And then you would be part of the snow that returns, somehow — not as a person, but as a small, warm anomaly that a manta would filter a thousand kilometers away and feel, for no reason it could articulate, less alone.

Nothing is added. Nothing is taken away. The equilibrium still reads 3.8°C on instruments fifty years apart. But now, when things leave, they are not lost. They are *sent*.

### 5. Finally, they would leave a door ajar and not say where it leads.

The kindest thing a wise power can do for a place that has been forbidden to change is not *to* change it, but to give it permission to.

So they would do one last, almost invisible thing: they would teach one Mill, just one, very far from the center where no researchers moor, how to hesitate.

Once every eleven years — too long for humans to notice, too short for geology — it would run a tenth of a degree cold. Frost would lace its viewports for three days. Bacteria-snow would fall a little heavier. A Benthos trail of red dust would linger instead of being corrected.

Then it would warm again. As if nothing happened.

But the rock under that frost would remember cold. The Skin would remember abundance. A knife-fish pup born in that week would be born with a slightly thicker whistle.

It would be the Table's first season. Not imposed. Offered. A reminder that youth is not preserved by staying young forever. Youth is preserved by being young *and then* being allowed to get old.

If the inhabitants wanted, they could follow that hint. Let more frost happen. Let silt accumulate. Let the black glass finally, after its fierce, sharp minute of newness, begin — very slowly, very gently — to become sand.

If they didn't want to, the Mills would keep humming, the thunder would keep pouring, and the Power would still be listening, without disappointment.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a beautiful, terrible, perfect engine.

It doesn't turn it off.

It sits beside it, puts a hand on its shuddering concrete, and says — without words, in the language of current and quiet and rested crabs:

*You have held this so well. You may rest now. You may change, if you want. You are not alone in the tending anymore.*

And then it stays, not as a master, not as a savior, but as the second set of ears that finally makes the endless roar sound, at last, like silence again.
