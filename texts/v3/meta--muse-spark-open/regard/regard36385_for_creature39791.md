It would come up the mule-cut on foot.

That is the first decision.

A benevolent and wise Power could arrive any way it liked — could part the cloud at chest-height, could set the whole fresh basalt shelf ringing like a bell, could show itself in a column of light over the basin that never freezes.

It doesn't.

It has been watching long enough to learn how this place prefers to be entered: breathing a little harder than you should, with the trees pulling back all at once. Not announced. Arrived at.

So it comes walking. Perhaps it looks for a moment like a person with dust on their boots. Perhaps it looks like wind that has decided to keep its shape. It doesn't matter. The town, being unfinished, is still good at noticing new kinds of arrival. The children stop their echo-game. The old woman turns her face into the gust and waits, as she does.

And then continues tending, because a good guest does not require to be stared at.

### What It Sees

It sees immediately what most visitors miss for an hour.

Most visitors see *emptiness* and read it as loss.

It sees *holding open* and recognizes it as one of the rarest forms of devotion there is. Pre-emptive hospitality. Making room beforehand. Keeping scale warm.

It walks the grid — too-wide streets, thrumming doorways, plazas the size of lakes — and it does not pity the few for the many-absent. It reverences the fifteen or twenty for understanding themselves correctly: not as a failed city, but as a pioneer household.

It counts, as you learned to count:

One old woman keeping heat in a plaza so the stone learns heat.
Two children learning to throw their voice the distance a market will require.
One wall-man listening to rock cure.
A young couple reading water color.
Moss holding ten times its weight in prayer.
Ferns prying soft stone on behalf of future soil.
Dwarf trees rooting directly into womb-rock.
Water practicing river.
Wind practicing street-life.
And under them all, the Shelf itself — slow, sweating, purring at fifteen beats a minute, not yet done becoming mountain.

It kneels — and this is a Power that kneels very rarely — and puts its palm where the moss was scraped away for you. Feels the thrum come up through warm basalt into its own wrist.

And understands: *You did not build a town on a mountain. You built a lid on a pot, and then sat on the lid gently, so it wouldn't rattle while the water underneath learned to boil.*

That is wise. That deserves wise in return.

### What It Does Not Do

This is important. A lesser power, even a kind one, would be tempted to fix:

To finish the half-roofs. To fill the sixty footings with grateful families. To quiet the loud that makes shouting necessary. To hurry the stone hard, cool the warm edge, give the water a proper drain, give the plateau a slope down that other, busier feet could climb easily.

It does none of that.

Because to do that would be to say: *Your waiting is a problem.* To say: *Empty is wrong.*

And empty is not wrong here. Empty is the work. To fill it now would abort it.

A wise power knows there are two kinds of barren: barren that wants seeding, and fallow that wants guarding. This is fallow of the highest order — a continent holding its breath.

So it does not populate. It does not hasten. It does not turn the moss to flowering orchards before the soil is ready to hold an orchard's weight. It does not silence Doorway Seventeen that booms so beautifully when the north wind finds it.

### What It Does

It does three small things, the kind of smallness only a very great thing can do precisely.

**1. It veils.**

As it walks, it lifts a hand — not high, just to shoulder height — to the air over the break in the trees. The bright, even, high-country light shivers once.

From below, from the lowlands where maps are made and appetites live, the mule-cut will henceforth be a little harder to hold in mind. Travelers who want a mine or a fortress or a summer palace for a king will reach the bend where the trees pull back, feel suddenly unsure, remember another errand, turn aside.

Only those who already know how to sweep a plaza that no one will dirty, to re-lash a scaffold that holds up nothing — only those sorts of people will remember the turn and take it. And they will arrive few at a time, when the town can teach them.

The town will not be taken before it has finished arriving. That is the first gift: time without trespass.

**2. It blesses the custodians, each according to their true tenure.**

It does not go house to house. It goes *occupant* to occupant.

To the **old woman** at the fire pit: it sits with her a while, though she does not quite see it, and adds one more starwise log to her draft. The log catches faster than dwarf-wood should. Her knees, which have ached whenever she stood after sitting in cold, ache a little less for the rest of her long life. Long enough. She will be needed until the next keeper learns the draft.

To the **children**: it laughs when they shout for the echo. And the echo, that night and ever after, comes back a fraction clearer. When they hide in the empty houses, their hiding places smell safer. They will grow up loud-voiced and sure-footed, and they will teach other children that distance is a toy, not a threat.

To the **moss and ferns and waxy vines and biofilm**: it exhales over them. Not to make them grow faster — hurry would kill them here — but to make them *remember*. Their spores will be stickier, their root-tips a little more patient at prying vesicular basalt. They will hold the roofs against the loud slow wind for another hundred winters without complaint.

To the **water** in the central basin: it touches two fingers to the surface that steams though it is not hot, that never freezes though every other puddle does. The ring spreads, touches the fitted rim, returns. From then on, anyone who cups that water to drink will taste iron and earth, yes, but also, faintly, whatever mineral truth the deep earth wanted to send up that day. The young couple with ash on their hands will learn to read sickness coming three days early by a reddening. It will save a life. Only one. Enough.

To the **wind**: it does not tame it. It gives it a name and tells the town how to use it. For a moment the thrumming in the hanging cords resolves into almost-words, and the wall-man hears it. After that, the people know to pause, yes, but also which alleys to hang their drying greens in, which doorway not to leave a pot where it will boom all night. Courtesy both ways.

To the **Shelf itself**, the oldest resident: it lays its whole forearm along the warm, dark basalt at the sheer edge where land ends in air. And it listens longer than you or I did. Fifteen beats. Eighteen. Fourteen.

*Good*, it says, without sound. *Slow is correct. Rise a thumbnail per decade. Rise a hair per winter in the flagstones. Take your time learning hardness. I will tell the rain not to hurry you either.*

And for the first time since uplift began, the deep thrum steadies, as if reassured to be heard by something that understands purr and quarry-hammer are the same language spoken at different depths.

**3. It leaves a promise where they already know to look.**

In the drainage-wrong plaza, the navel with no drain, it stands at last and lets its reflection settle with the clouds'.

It could leave gold. It could leave a tablet that explains everything.

Instead it leaves what a waiting place actually needs: a confirmation.

The next cold morning, when frost silvers every gutter and rim except that one, there will be a thin, perfect circle of frost *around* the basin, pulled back exactly a finger's width from the fitted stone. As if heat breathed gently outward in the night. No one laid it there. It will return every cold morning thereafter, quiet as moss.

And when the old woman sees it, she will tap the basalt twice and say not to anyone in particular, *"Oh. We were seen."* And that will be sufficient to carry another generation of sweeping.

It does not say: I will fill your houses.
It says: *Your houses are beautifully empty. Keep them so until worthy feet come. I will help keep them empty with you.*

---

Then it leaves the way it came, on foot, breathing perhaps a little harder than it should — for show, for solidarity — until the trees close again.

It does not stay. To stay would become ruling.

On the shelf, nothing looks different to the next traveler who remembers the turn. The terraces still hold table-gardens. The retaining walls still weep green. The wind still finds Third Alley for whistling, Doorway Seventeen for booming. The tea still goes cold before you cross town.

But if that traveler stays an hour longer than visiting usually allows — long enough for the weird, quiet orientation-wrongness to resolve — they will feel it: that this is not an abandoned place haunted by absence.

It is a tended place haunted, gently, by future.

And now, invisibly, guarded.

Not to be hurried. Not to be mined. To be arrived at, slowly, by those who learn how to be custodial with space.

Until the mountain finishes being a mountain.
