It would do nothing that looks like doing something.

That is the first thing a wise and benevolent power understands when it looks over the lip and sees the wound.

A lesser power would be horrified by the word you used — *wound* — and try to heal it. It would try to close the graben, to smooth the basalt, to drain the mat so people could have dry feet and straight streets and fire that lasts. It would bring sun for more than an hour. It would bring iron. It would call this salvation.

A wise one knows the difference between a wound and a mouth. Between a wound and a lung. Between a wound and a womb.

This is not broken steppe. This is steppe that finally found a place to breathe out.

So it would not heal it. And for the same reason, it would not leave it alone.

### Instead it would sit.

No — wiser even than that. It would *wade*.

Benevolence without wisdom appears on the high, dry ground in gleaming boots and says *come up here where it is easy.* Wisdom knows that if you arrive in that place with gleaming boots you will punch straight through fifteen feet of living mat and drown in the cold greenhouse hush. You have not earned the splayed foot yet. You have not learned to feel with your toes for rot and root.

So the power — call it what you like, but here it would feel less like a god and more like weather that has decided to stay — would come down below the frost line, below the wind, the way water does: without a visible source.

No arrival. Just a slow increase in seep.

You would not see it on the first day. You would see that the hanging gardens on the sheer walls did not slump after a hard freeze. That a terrace of peat mortared by roots to vertical basalt — the one hammered into a crack when someone's great-grandmother was young — which should have sheared off this winter, held. Just held.

You would not see it on the second day. You would hear that the noon columns of midges were thicker, bending light a little more, and that the children standing motionless in reed-screens with mouths open had protein enough that their bellies were not quite so round with starch.

The fish under translucent moss would turn a little more often. Twenty feet away you would hear it more clearly.

That is how a wise power works in a place that has already solved density without a single cut stone. It does not add. It *keeps*.

What it keeps out is more important than what it gives:

Above, on the flat, white, wind-scoured steppe, it would lay a hand so lightly that satellites would misread it. Prospectors would fly over and see only lichen. The pale daylight would glint wrong off the mist for a camera lens. Maps would develop a small, persistent error around the rift — not unmapped, just uninteresting. *Nothing there.* The kind of nothing that makes ambitious men decide to drill somewhere else.

It would not hide them. They were never hidden. But it would give them the gift every saturated, saturated city has never had: the right to be overlooked until a visitor learns how to look.

What you thought was plant would still have to decide to stand up for you. The pat of lichen would still have to blink.

And inside the rift — the tenders would be tended at their own scale.

The power would not bring fire. Fire drowns here.

It would bring a slightly longer memory to wood. It would whisper to cambium: *hold this graft a little tighter.* To sphagnum: *swell a little slower, insulate a little better.* To the musk oxen barges with their dreadlocked, soaked wool: *keep treading this causeway, my old priests, keep it open one more generation.*

It would sit with the elders who have begun to root into the floors of their bole-houses — legs edematous, fine rootlets politely growing into dead skin — and it would not pull the rootlets out. It would not offer them legs again. It would sing the low songs with them that vibrate water and call fish, because a wise power knows that becoming indistinguishable from your house is not tragedy, it is tenure. In this place half the peat is people and half of it is spruce and the distinction long ago stopped holding, and there is no kinder afterlife to offer than that.

If it spoke at all, it would not speak in words. The walls return sound to itself. A shout would sit on your ears too long.

It would speak the way they do. A soft *tok* against a trunk, traveling through root and water better than air. A shale knife scoring bark to tuck a willow withe into a failing arch, saying: *tomorrow there will still be a door where yesterday there was a door.* The power's whole theology would fit in that sentence.

On its day off, maybe, it would do one extravagant thing. Not for survival. For delight.

At noon, when the pale-gold sun stands straight overhead for its one hour and the entire wound lights — when every thread of water on the weeping black walls glitters and the mist rises off the mat like the city itself exhaling — the power would hold the light for seven minutes longer.

Just seven.

Long enough for the children in their living moss capes to flush pink on cheeks and forearms and stay pink a little after the walls shade again. Long enough for the algal felt in the crooks of old elbows to drink. Long enough for everyone wading thigh-deep in the braided, ankle-deep streets to look up from their work of persuading trees to stay houses, and see each other fully lit, dripping, impossibly green in the cold.

Long enough to be seen.

Then the shade would return, the cathedral hush after rain would return, and the ceaseless work of not freezing, not drowning, and not leaving any empty space would resume, only now everyone would know, in the wordless way water knows, that the work had been witnessed and found — not pitiable, not primitive —

but exquisite.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it loved them:

Nothing to save them.

Everything to keep them from needing to be saved.

It would become very, very good at waiting between words, and it would listen to the fish turn under moss twenty feet away, and it would call that prayer.
