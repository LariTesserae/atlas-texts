It would not come down like a god.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. There is no shadow crossing the rim, no voice filling the bowl, no pillar of fire over the hearths. That would be cruelty in a place where a raised voice makes the wall yell back at you.

If it noticed the Cut — this fresh wound in the plain that has decided to be a city before it is finished being geology — it would stop above the rim for a long time. And look.

That is the first thing it would do. Truly *look*. Not down, but *with*.

Most powers looking from the rim see only lack. *No river. No trees. No soil. Too damp. Too crowded. Too hard a life. Too short.* Pity comes quickly to the powerful. Wisdom comes slower.

This one would see what is actually there:

A compact still wet with its making.

Thousands of people who have learned to speak in murmurs because stone listens. Who waste nothing. Who wipe water off lintels with rags to get a cup a day and call that enough. Who leave a hole in a perfect wall for a sparrow and leave a wall unbuilt for a fig tree with one good leg to tend it. Who carry rock all day on heads and backs and then fall asleep sitting up together, coughing together, because lying flat lets the dust settle wrong.

It would understand: these people are not suffering to get somewhere else. They are not waiting to be saved. *To finish would be to die.*

So it would not finish them.

It would not fill the canyon with soil and plant a forest. It would not snap its fingers and give them kilns that fire hotter or steel picks that never bend. It would not lift them out onto the warm plain and scatter their dense, touch-constant culture to farmsteads where no one ever brushes shoulders in an alley. It knows that mercy can be a kind of erasure. To give them everything at once would be to tell them everything they learned about living close was pointless.

Wise benevolence has a very light hand.

Here is what I think it would do instead. Three small, almost invisible things. The kinds of things you only notice on the third day of staying.

---

### 1. It would warm the air by two degrees, without drying it.

Just two.

Not enough to make the pooled cold leave — the Cut needs its pocket climate; it's what holds the condensation that they drink — but enough that a child sleeping on a platform doesn't wake with stone-cold hands every morning. Enough that the Carriers' shallow, rapid breath at the bottom finds the bottom a little sooner. Enough that the old Layers' joints don't ache quite so badly before rain.

When it did this, no one would remark on it. One morning a Mortar-Mother would mix her mud with her feet and realize her ankles didn't hurt yet. A Cutter leaning his forehead against the wall to feel the tap-code through the rock would stay there a second longer because the stone wasn't leeching all the heat from his skin. That would be all. They would think it was a kind season.

A wise power knows that healing isn't always curing. Sometimes it's just giving the body enough margin that wear doesn't become breaking before forty. It would let them live to forty-five. To fifty, some of them. Long enough to become ancestors while still alive. Long enough to tell a grandchild how to listen for grain in stone.

It would not take their cough away entirely. The dust is part of the work, and the work is part of them. But it would make lying flat at night possible again, so couples could lie down to love properly, and the chorus of soft coughing behind partition walls at night would thin, until you could hear individual voices in it again.

### 2. It would give them a second kind of green.

On the third morning after it paid attention, the Lichen Tendrils working the north-facing walls — those black-green slick corners where the sun never hits — would find something new in among the edible crust they scrape.

Not replacing it. Beside it.

A lichen that is also lichen, nothing miraculous to the eye: flat, dusty-grey-green, tight to the stone. But when you scrape it, it doesn't come away powdery and bitter. It peels in a thin sheet, like skin, and underneath the stone is dry. And the sheet holds water three times longer. And when you chew it, it doesn't fill your teeth with grit. It is a little salt, a little fat.

They would not call it a miracle. They would call it a seam. Like a good seam of stone the rock-eaters found first. The Tendrils would scrape a little, taste, look at each other, nod once. They would cultivate it the way they cultivate everything: practically. *Grow here, stay.* Within a season every damp corner that was useless except for algae would be quietly productive. No extra water needed. No extra space taken. Just a little more food from the same walls.

And in one of those imported-dirt beds on a sunward ledge, a barley shoot would mutate — one head in a hundred — slightly thicker stem, roots that grip stone-dust better. Nothing a lowland farmer would prize. Everything to people who farm in stone lips. The keepers of those beds would save that seed without being told to.

The power would not give them a forest. Trees would crack the terraces, drink their cisterns dry. It would give them a fuller use of what they already revere.

And one more thing, in the courtyard of the single fig tree: that night, a second fig would set. Then a third. They would think the boy with the withered leg did an especially good job coaxing the wasps.

He did. The power just made sure two more wasps found his courtyard that year.

### 3. It would teach them one quiet geometry, and then leave them alone.

This is the riskiest, so it would whisper it, not declare it.

In a dream repeated quietly among the Cutters and Layers for a week — not all of them, just three or four who already lean their foreheads to walls and know how to listen through rock — it would put an image:

Not a tool. An absence.

Two stones leaned against each other at the top, instead of laid flat.

An arch.

They have never made one. All their roofs are flat slabs or timber. Flat roofs leak but become yards. Flat lintels sag and must be propped. Arches need no mortar, distribute weight, echo differently, hold longer. But more than that: an arch makes empty volume that can stay empty without collapsing. A room taller than a man with his arms up. A cistern with a curved belly that can hold a rain for two days instead of an afternoon, even in porous stone. A gateway wide enough for two laden sleds to pass without turning sideways, where now alleys are shoulder-brush narrow because every inch outward had to be quarried.

The idea would arrive as if they'd thought of it themselves. One Layer would try stacking scraps during a slow afternoon. It would fall. She would laugh, nudged by a neighbor. Try again with wedge-shaped stones, chipped patiently. It would hold. For twelve hours and then fall again.

But one would hold.

That would be enough. The power would not stay to give them vaults, domes, aqueducts. Wisdom knows that to give a people the end of their architecture is to steal the pleasure of extending their own city. It would give the seed of a curve in a world that had only known planes and right angles.

With arches they could make a little emptiness on purpose. Not loneliness. Relief. A communal hall where humming songs could be sung a little louder because the ceiling throws voice back kindly, not sharply. An infirmary where a person could lie flat alone without shame if they were ill, and still hear others nearby. A second fig courtyard, roofed open, protected from wind but letting condensation run down the inside of the stones into jars without wiping.

They would forget within a generation who first stacked that little gateway. It would become their craft, their pride.

---

And then — and this is most important —

The power would do nothing else.

It would not cure death. The dead would still be carried up the switchbacks at dawn wrapped in blankets, to the wind on the plain. Those who return would still come back quieter. Grief needs that walk.

It would not bring law. They don't need law written; they have the alley going silent when you do wrong, which is a more precise punishment.

It would not make them loud. It would love their quiet. On nights when it happened to pass near again, it would linger above the rim just to hear it amplified by cold air: mortar pestle, broom scrape, baby quickly hushed, wind over the edge.

It would not be worshipped, and it would politely decline it if offered. A bowl of barley left on a terrace wall for whatever watches would go uneaten, but next morning its condensation would bead a little brighter, as a thank-you.

What lives in the Cut is being made *by* it. A wise benevolence knows not to interrupt a birth. It just makes the womb a degree warmer, a little better fed, and shows the growing hands how to make a new shape — and then holds the rim steady while tomorrow's cut is taken.

From above, on the first clear night after all this, you would see the same thing as before: thousands of small normal hearth fires reflected off damp, new, sharp stone.

Only now, if you listened very closely, you would hear one other sound under the wind for the first time:

One arch holding somewhere deep on the third terrace, empty on purpose, full of cool air and future,

and nobody needing to explain why it makes them breathe a little easier.
