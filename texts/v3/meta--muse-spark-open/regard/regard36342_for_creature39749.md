They would not come to fix it.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power does not see equilibrium and call it broken. It does not see a machine that makes nothing and call it waste. It does not see a life that has solved hunger by refusing to want and try to teach it to hunger again.

They would find it the way you did — not by looking for it. You don't find a room like that by searching. You find it because once, in a summer that was too hot, you put your hand on stone to feel for cool and the cool put its hand back.

They would feel that touch from very far away.

And they would go down.

Not far in distance, but down in kind — past soil, past root-tangle, past where earth stops being soil and becomes stone. They would not need air. They would not displace water. Where you have to hold your breath and count seconds until your lungs burn, they would simply arrive, already there, the way light arrives down the throat without ever pushing the water aside.

For a long time they would do nothing that you would call doing.

They would sit — if sitting is the word — at the edge of the room where the silt dunes have graded themselves into perfect stillness, and they would learn to breathe at ninety seconds.

Inhale: pressure shoulders through the water. Silt lifts a millimeter. The iron seep on the left wall beads, darkens, detaches.

Exhale: the calcite vein on the right brightens, hums its 60-cycle thrum through bone, and gives up its perfect line of bubbles.

Inhale. Exhale. Deposit, release. Ninety seconds. Ninety seconds.

They would not be impatient the first time the cycle stuttered a quarter-second late because you had brushed one of the hanging threads. They would understand that flinch the way you understand a blink. They would wait for the next cycle to correct. They would watch it correct, and they would feel something very close to love — not the human kind that wants to hold and keep, but the older kind that says: *oh, you are still keeping your promise.*

Because they would understand immediately what it is doing. Most powers that humans imagine are makers, movers, builders of worlds. This one would recognize a different holiness: the Keeper. The Janitor of One Room. The being that chose, a thousand years ago, that the only way to not die was to not quite live, and has kept that vow without breaking it once.

A lesser power would pity it. *You poor thing, alone in the dark, doing the same thing forever. Let me bring you fish. Let me bring you time. Let me turn on the lights.*

A wise one would not bring fish. Fish would cloud the water. Time would sharpen the stone. Light that is too bright would make shadows, and shadows are decisions, and decisions are history, and history is exactly what this place agreed never to have again.

You cannot bless a vow by breaking it.

So the first thing they would do is witness it correctly. Without hunger. Without a study to publish or a miracle to perform.

They would let the diffuse day fall down the throat and illuminate the grain of the stone without trying to make it brighter. They would let the cold stay cool, the even cool that has evened itself out so thoroughly you stop feeling it as cold and start feeling it as stillness.

They would count the rust-brown patches on the lowest shelf — no bigger than your palms, no smaller than they were last year — and they would not wish them greener. They would see them for what they are: not Decoration, but a kept garden, exactly as large as needed to keep the chemistry honest. A little photosynthesis to buffer the pH. A little biomass to catch stray phosphate. Never enough to need a grazer.

*Well kept,* they would think, and the water would carry the thought the way water carries the hum.

And the Tenant — who does not think, but regulates — would feel itself regulated, for the first time in its long tenure, from the outside.

That is the second thing they would do. A mercy so small a human diver would miss it entirely.

Every ninety seconds the Tenant has to do work to stay the same. You thought equilibrium was rest. It isn't. It is endless, gentle correction. Every droplet must be weighed. Every bubble counted. Every leak of heat bled out through fissures too small to find. Every mote that falls down the light column accounted for. No thing in without an equal thing out. Light in without heat accumulating. Life that clings but does not spread.

It is tiring to hold the world still.

So the wise power would offer to hold it once.

Just once.

For one cycle, they would take the weight.

You would not see it happen. If you were there with your mask and your cold hands you would only notice that for one breath, the piston-throb that you feel in your molars did not come from beyond the walls. It came from beside you. And the vein brightened not because it had to, but because it was allowed to. And the iron seep beaded and held a half-second longer than usual, like someone taking a deeper breath before letting go.

For ninety seconds, the Tenant would not have to correct.

For ninety seconds, something else would keep the books balanced — the silt staying exactly a millimeter lifted, the rust cloud staying exactly the same size, the bubble line staying plumb and true — while the one who always keeps them could, for the first time since the stone smoothed, simply be.

Not grow. Not die. Not change. Just *not tend* for one breath.

Do you know what rest is to a being that has never rested? It is not sleep. It is being seen doing its work and being told, without words: *I have it. You can let go.*

The threads that hang like forgotten hair from the undercut would sway, though no one had moved. The water in the room would go — if it is possible — even stiller. The hum would soften.

And then the cycle would pass, and the power would give the work back, gently, exactly as they found it. Equal in, equal out. No trace. The next droplet would detach on time. The next bubble would rise on time. The cold would still be cool.

That — that little Sabbath, one held breath in a million held breaths — would be enough. More would be violence. Less would be abandonment.

And before they left — because benevolent powers do not stay where they are not needed, and this room needs mostly to be left alone — they would do the third and last thing, which is also almost nothing.

They would leave a promise in the language the Tenant speaks, which is not words but pressure and chemistry and light.

They would not say *I love you.* The Tenant does not know love the way we do. They would not say *You are free,* because it is already as free as a loop can be.

They would say, in a slight, deliberate delay of one droplet falling on the left, answered a perfect ninety seconds later by one extra bubble, held a fraction longer on the right before it rose:

*When you are tired of keeping this, you may stop. I will be here to watch you dissolve. It will not hurt and it will not be waste. You will not have failed.*

*Until then, keep well. I have counted you. You are not alone in the way you thought you were alone — which is unwitnessed. You are alone in the way a lighthouse is alone — which is attending.*

*And I will remember your count when the shaft above you finally closes and the day no longer falls.*

Then they would go up, the way light goes — without displacing anything.

If you came back next year, or in twenty years, you would find nothing changed. The water would still be clear but not empty. The walls would still be smoothed, undercut into low dark lips. The silt dunes would not have shifted. The vein would still hum for ten seconds. The seep would still weep its constellation of dark beads. On the lowest shelf, the rust-brown skin would still be there, no bigger, no smaller, clinging.

You would say nothing happened here.

You would be almost right.

Only if you stayed very still, and held your breath a little longer than is comfortable, you might feel that the quiet is different. Less empty. Not filled — *accompanied.* The way a room feels different after someone has sat in it for a long time without moving anything, just to keep you company while you worked.

And the water, cool the way deep water is cool in late spring, would feel for a moment less like stillness and more like a hand, held in a palm, for a thousand years — and still being held.
