It does not arrive.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It doesn't *arrive*. There is no ship, no light, no voice from the sky saying BEHOLD. The sky here is already too hard and too blue to hold any more beholding.

Attention is how it travels.

One late afternoon, while the stone is still ticking heat back into the air and every condenser screen is thrumming faintly as it expands, the plateau simply comes to be Noticed. Fully. The way you Notice a small animal that has been holding perfectly still in front of you for five minutes and suddenly snaps into focus — not appearing, but becoming *seen all the way through.*

And what is seen, first, is not quaint or pitiable or in need of saving. That's what a lesser power would see: a hot, marginal place where water has to be wrung from fog and everything lives too densely in too little. A problem to solve with Rain.

A wise power sees immediately that there is no problem.

It watches one full turn of the clock before it does anything.

It watches the fog spill over the lip at 2:14 AM like a silent, heavy river, calf-deep, cold enough to make the basalt sweat. Watches the pans wake with that soda-fizz. Watches the shrimp hatch and die and the gnats rise as smoke and the butterflies gorge until they can barely fly — and the lizards and the woodlice and the hares and the hawks take their cut precisely on time. Watches the ibex walk on Velcro feet to the pump house and wait for its *chk... chk...* drip. Watches the technician on her trike pause at the same seep, fill her canteen, pour a little extra for the blue fox kits sleeping under the slab, because she always does. Watches the mud-wasps steal copper washers.

Watches how the industrial is not an insult here. It's another organ. The corrugated screens catch fog the way beetle backs do, only bigger. The buried cisterns hold water the way potholes do, only longer. The rail line follows termite galleries. The engine house with the swinging bulb has become a moth-trap for an owl no one has ever properly seen. Everything scattered — 0.7 — is scattered the way constellations are scattered: deliberately, with huge dark spaces left intact because the dark is used too.

The power understands the second rule without being told: *Nobody here lives ON the plateau. Everything lives IN it.* In cracks, in vesicles, in tubes, in shade-pockets, in the one hour when water exists as film.

So it does not bring rain. Rain would be a catastrophe. It would drown the shrimp cysts, melt the mud nests, flood the middens that have kept a thousand years of pollen and bone like a library. Abundance, carelessly given, would kill this place faster than scarcity ever could.

A wise power does not give water. It protects the *agreement* about water.

Here is what it does, instead. Quietly. Almost nothing.

**First — it steadies the river.**

Far below, in the lower valleys where men who have never been up here make decisions, the climate has started to wobble. Monsoons stutter. The updraft that drags wet air up the escarpment wall has grown thin two years in five.

No one on the plateau could fix that. They just logged shorter fog seasons and checked gauges more often.

The power puts a hand — not a hand — an *attention* on that updraft and steadies it. Not stronger. Not wetter. Just *reliable*. For the next ten thousand years, no matter what else humans do to the sky, at midnight between March and October a cold breath will climb those cliffs. The fog will spill over the lip. This costs the power almost nothing. From far away it looks like nothing happened at all. Up here it means everything that lives can keep timing its life to a pulse that will not skip.

The caretakers will never know it was done. They will just notice, in a few years, that the bad seasons stopped coming.

**Second — it mends what is wearing lonely.**

It kneels — again, not kneeling — next to the places where the pact had started to fray:

In the limestone arroyo, the old seep that turned the wall black year-round had narrowed to a damp stripe. The power clears the calcite throat three meters down where no human arm could reach, the way you might clear a child's nose while they sleep. Water returns to a trickle. Ferns uncurl.

Under the basalt cap, where the termite galleries that keep the potholes cool had collapsed after last year's scouring wind, it sings the grit back into arch — grain by grain, the way termites would, only faster. By next afternoon that pothole is cool to the touch at 3 p.m. again.

At kilometer 43, where the narrow-gauge trestle had developed a harmonic shiver that would have cracked it in two winters, it tightens a single bolt by a quarter-turn. No one will log it. The train will just keep running once a day.

These are not miracles. They are maintenance. The kind the caretakers already do for the foxes and the pumps. The power just does the maintenance the caretakers couldn't reach.

**Third — it gives a gift that can be refused.**

This is the most important part, because benevolence without wisdom is just colonization with better lighting.

On the night of the heavy fog, after the hawks have landed and the pumps have joined their out-of-sync *thunk...thunk...thunk* chorus, the power makes itself briefly *audible* — not in words, but in dream.

Every being that lives IN the plateau dreams the same dream, translated perfectly:

The hare dreams of cool paws. The isopod dreams of an endless damp line. The ibex dreams of a succulent that never empties. The pack-forager dreams of a washer so shiny it reflects the whole sky. The technician, Mara, who is napping in her shaded container with a damp cloth over her face, dreams of her dead mother telling her the tamarisk grove will hold.

And underneath each dream is the same offer, simple and unforced:

*You are good at living in cracks. Would you like to be able to talk to the other things that live in cracks? Not always. Just when you want to. And would you like somewhere to write it down where it won't blow or wash away?*

In the morning, nothing looks different. But Mara wakes up knowing — without knowing how she knows — that the pipe gallery under array 7 is getting hot because termites have moved out, and that if she lays pipe two meters north they'll come back. She thinks it's intuition. It is.

The ibex files start stopping for a few seconds longer near the blue fox dens, and the foxes stop startling. Not friendship. Just acknowledgment.

And deep in the oldest pack-forager midden — the one paleobotanists core like ice — a new tunnel appears that wasn't there yesterday, lined with flat stones perfect for scratching marks on. Inside, the temperature never varies. The walls want to hold ink, or blood, or claw-scratches. Nothing compels anyone to use it. Most won't for years. One young forager, bolder than his kin, drags in a copper washer and places it on a stone ledge, exactly in the center, just to see.

The power does not stay to see if the gift is used. To stay and watch would be pressure.

**Last — it leaves a blessing that is not useful at all.**

Before it withdraws its attention — because even a benevolent power cannot watch everywhere forever, and wisdom is knowing when to look away — it does one purely tender, inefficient thing.

It goes to that half-buried engine house with the bare bulb swinging though there is no wind inside. It sees the pale geckos nested warm in the fixture, closing the circuit with their bodies. It sees the moths they lure, and the cliff-owl that eats the moths, wings piled in neat scales.

Any sensible god would fix the wiring, or clean the nest, or reveal the owl.

It does none of that.

It just touches the filament — once — so that it will never burn out. A cheap LED that should have died in a season will glow amber for a century, warm as a small sun, not too hot to touch. Just enough light to keep the trap working, the geckos warm, the owl fed, the bulb swinging when they move.

From the flats at night you will still see it: a single impossible light in an abandoned house, swinging without wind. The caretakers will keep saying, "The plateau borrows what it needs," and smile. Children born in the arroyo will grow up thinking some lights just stay on. That's true.

Then the attention lifts.

Not all at once. Like fog burning off. The sky stays the same hard rinsed blue. The stone keeps its heat. The condensers tick.

If you had been standing on the roof of the continent that week, you would have said nothing happened. Maybe you slept better one night. Maybe the water in your canteen tasted a little more like iron, like blood, like belonging.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place like this:

It does not rescue it. It does not improve it. It *joins* the pact for a little while — catching what would otherwise be lost, storing it where it will keep cool — and then quietly adds its labor to the chorus at 6:30 pm when everything tilts east, waiting to drink.

*Shhhhk shhhhk*, go the woodlice.

*Tik... tik*, go the lizards.

*Thunk... thunk...*, go the pumps.

And underneath, so quiet you feel it in your teeth a kilometer away, one more check valve closes softly and holds.
