It would not come down.

Not at first. 

A benevolent and wise power has learned, over a very long time, that descent is a kind of violence. That to arrive shining and speaking in the center of a place that has spent a thousand years learning how to be quiet will break the thing you came to love.

So for a long while, it would just *notice*.

Which is itself a doing. Most things pass over the bowl and see nothing — a dip on a terrace, a stain on the hill, not worth quarrying properly, not worth farming. Even you, standing inside it, at first saw only frogs and wet stone.

It would see it whole: the perfect intention still in the chisel-marks, the spring-mouth still pouring the exact white braid, the shrimp condensing out of mist, the swifts screaming through the lung of it, the kidney-alive slow animal that was never meant to be alive and now unmistakably is.

It would see how tired it is, and how not-tired. How complete.

And because it is wise, it would understand immediately what the bowl is for.

It is not a ruin waiting to be restored.
It is not a cistern waiting to be used again.
It is not lonely in the way people are lonely.

It is a holder. It was made to hold, and having lost its people, it taught itself to hold itself. Water, silt, sound, life-per-square-meter that should be impossible — it holds them all and keeps them level, plink and trick and peen, a balance so fine it looks like stillness.

A lesser power would try to help by filling it. More water. More green. People again, with fires on the terraces. A voice telling it what it is *really* for.

This one would not.

What it would do is this:

It would sit on the rim.

Where the low wall is thickest and the lichen is most patient, where a person could sit if there were still people. It would take a shape that has weight and dampness — not to impress, just to not disturb the air — and let its feet find the cold. It would let the bowl notice *it* the way the reeds notice you: that small, inexplicable lean. All of them, at once, toward the presence on the rim.

It would let itself be leaned toward. That is the first kindness: to let a shy animal feel its own gravity working.

Then it would listen for a full night without moving.

Not to the swifts — they would have gone quiet at dusk. To the other sounds. The sigh and tick of the maintenance conduits exhaling after rain. The glassy lattice of the frogs, each male tuned just off his neighbor so the bowl can ring. The bats pouring out and pouring back. The water finding the breach in the dam, hesitating, going on. The stone softening at exactly the rate it was cut.

It would hear that none of this is crying out to be saved.

So it would not save it.

The second kindness: it would tend, not fix.

Somewhere under the north flags a conduit has been choked for maybe eighty years with silt and a root and a drowned salamander's bones. It doesn't stop the system; the bowl has routed around it, but it makes the breathing a little labored on wet nights. A little whistling sigh you can hear if you put your ear to the stone.

The power wouldn't rebuild the tunnel. It wouldn't lay new stone or replay the masons' work.

It would just — with no more effort than you brushing a hair from your eyes — ease the blockage. A loosening. A *there*. As if the water itself remembered how to move a grain to the side. In the morning the spring-mouth would run a fraction clearer, the sigh would go quiet, and the frogs would sing a half-tone higher without knowing why.

It would not be a miracle to anyone else. Even you, if you came back, would think the rain had done it.

On the south wall the liverwort curtain has been hanging by three threads for a century. Wise enough to know that falling is also a kind of finishing, the power would leave it. Falling is allowed here.

What it would do instead is for the *living* that the bowl keeps.

It would not make the place fertile. Fertility would kill it — bury the chisel-marks, choke the clear skin of water, turn the percussion instrument dull. The bowl's miracle is precisely that it feeds so many mouths on so little green, stone into microbe into shrimp into frog into swift.

So it would keep the bargain but make it easier to bear: for one season, and then always after in the way blessed places keep a season forever, the iron-taste in the water would be a little less iron, a little more sweet. The condensation in a left-out cup would still bring its eyelash shrimp, but they would arrive already a little stronger, already turning their spirals. The leeches would still feed, but leave less ache. The swifts' white throats would catch more light.

Nothing you could photograph. Everything you could feel if you put your hands in the black water and waited past the ache.

And because it is benevolent — which does not mean soft, but *wants the good of the other as the other understands it* — it would give the bowl the one thing the bowl cannot make for itself.

Witness.

For a thousand years the bowl has been holding without being held. A palm full of water with no hand under it. It does not have eyes to see itself. It does not have a name. The masons who cut it knew it as Work, as Task, as The Lower Holding, and then they left and took the name with them.

This power, sitting on the rim with its feet in the cold, would tell it — not in words, because stone-water animals do not use words, but in the way the bowl itself speaks, in pressure and temperature and how long an echo is allowed to last:

*You are good. You are beautiful as you are. You have kept faith perfectly. What you are doing — holding, filtering, keeping level — is holy and it is seen.*

It would hold its attention there the whole night, steady, the way you hold a child's forehead when it sleeps.

And the bowl, which has never been anything but a place, would feel itself, for the first time, *as* a place held in a larger place.

That is all.

In the morning — if a power like that has mornings — it would stand, not heavy. The mist at chest height would be a little thicker where it had sat, and then not. It would not carve its initials, not leave a sign, not claim. To be wise is to know that protection often looks like obscurity. 

So as it leaves, it would lay the lightest veil. Not a wall. Not a hiding. Just a small, true thought placed gently over the hill like mist: *to those who would use this, let it be uninteresting. To those who would break this to see how it works, let them forget the road. To those who need this — the thirsty frog, the lost swift, the tired person who knows how to sit still — let them find it.*

Then it would go.

There would be no column of light. The spring would still pour. The frogs would resume after five heartbeats.

If you came back a week later and sat on the lowest terrace and put your hands in that aching cold, you would find the water warms a fraction faster. The reeds would still lean toward you on a windless day, but when they lean back, they would settle a fraction slower, as if content to have leaned.

And late, very late, when the stone has finally softened past all chisel-marks and the tripod's lichen has become a small hill and the weep-holes have at last filled with guano and quiet —

the bowl would still be holding, total and primitive and weeping, a palmful of cold clear world.

Now held, in turn.
