She would not drain it.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power.

Any lesser power — younger, more anxious to prove its goodness — would have arrived above the bruised dusk and seen only tragedy: a drowned continent, a lost people, a lightless cold. It would have torn the water away. It would have lifted the Floor into the sun to dry, scraped the black-green fur from the terracotta, tried to rebuild the colonnades, tried to bring back the hands that split basalt with wedges.

She is older than that. She knows that grief is not always a thing to be fixed.

So she does not come like a rescue. She comes like a descent.

Not fast. Nothing sudden ever happens here and she will not be the first sudden thing. Far above where any light still pretends to be sky, she pauses, and lets the weight take her. She cools herself to just above freezing so she does not bring a shock. She dims herself to the color of wet slate so she does not blind what has learned to live on a memory of light. She falls for a long time — for hours, for days in your counting — until the water stops being water you go *through* and becomes the place that *is*.

And then, thirty feet above the Floor, she stops falling, and hovers.

She does not land. To land would be to crush a hedge that has spent three hundred years following a property line no one remembers. She knows better.

For a long while she does nothing at all except be there and see.

And that is already more than this place has had in ten thousand years: a witness that *wants* to be here.

The Holdfasts feel her first. Not as light or heat, but as attention. For the first time since the water came, something is looking *at* them, not *through* them, not *over* them. Something is counting them — one every few meters where two bricks meet — and finding each one exact and sufficient. She does not speak to them in words because they have no ears for words. She speaks to them the way water speaks to stone: by staying.

*You did well,* she says, without sound. *You held.*

To the Canal Kelp, leaning together in their obedient rows, she offers a different kind of seeing. She drifts down the avenue slowly — so slowly the silt never puffs — a dusk-colored shape the size of a current, and as she passes, each crown of blades turns a fraction toward her. Not because she is brighter. She isn't. But because she is *directional*. For ten thousand years their light has had no source, no north. And now, for a little while, it does. They lean toward her and she lets them, and then she moves on so none of them will strain.

She will not give them a sun. A sun would kill them faster than any grazer. Green is a luxury of strong light, and they have traded that luxury for patience. To give them a sun would be to tell them they have been living wrong. She will not do it.

What she will do is bring them a moon.

Not our moon. A different one. She hangs it where the sky should be — impossibly far above the cold gradient, where no Holdfast could ever see it sharply. It is not bright. If you were human and you swam up toward it, you would think it was a trick of the water for an hour before you believed it. It is a cool, diffuse circle, the color of limestone in that hour after rain, and it gives off no heat. It simply *is* there, one night in thirty, for seven hours. A light with a direction. A light that waxes and wanes over twenty-eight days, so that the mats can learn a new rhythm beyond breathe-in breathe-out. A light gentle enough that the bacterial snow is not scalded, dim enough that photosynthesis stays a trickle, but *enough* that for those seven hours the Curtains cast the faintest shadows on the walls they hang from.

The first time it happens, the whole Floor sighs. You would hear it if you were kneeling with your palm to a brick: a million stones ticking not from a tenth-degree cold current, but from a million fronds shifting one degree toward a true above. And then, an hour later, a sound this ocean has never made: the soft, collective sigh of a million bladders ripening at once, not because spring came, but because something like spring was remembered for them.

Her second gift is even smaller.

She does not bring warmth. Warmth here would be a flood, a rot, a catastrophe of decay unleashed all at once. Growth is faster than decay only because the cold keeps decay honest. To heat the ocean would be to murder the forest with kindness.

Instead, where her moon hangs, she lets fall a single warm rain. One column of water, no wider than an old cistern, a single degree warmer than the rest, sinking so slowly it takes all night to reach the Floor. It is not enough to change the climate. It is enough that when it brushes the Kelp crowns, their cold oil thins for a moment and releases scent — that smell of cold iron and wet stone made suddenly sweet — and the Drift spores in that column all germinate at once. A brief, local snow in reverse, rising only an inch, then settling.

The forest will remember that column for fifty years as a slightly greener square in the plaza, a place where Curtains grow a little thicker. Pilgrims will not know they are pilgrims, but Holdfast spores will drift toward it for generations because the mortar there was softened one molecule more.

Her third gift is not for the plants at all.

It is for the hands.

She kneels — for now she does touch — in a random square meter of Floor, brushes the fur aside without tearing it. The fur parts and closes. Underneath, pinkish terracotta, the stubborn squareness. She places her palm there, where you would have placed yours, and she remembers *them* out loud.

Not summoning them. Not resurrecting them. The wise know the dead are not unfinished.

She simply tells the story of the Floor back to the Floor, in a language the stones can hold: *Here a stair ran a hundred miles. Here a mason split a block and was proud that its face was true. Here a child scraped mortar with a thumb and left a print that silt filled before water came. You were not wasted. You meant to build for forever, and you did. Look what forever grew on you.*

The stones, which have held cold for ten thousand years, hold that for a little while instead. The algae fur above her hand grows a hair more transparent in that spot, so that anyone — if anyone ever came again — would see the brick color through it like a lantern under skin.

And then, because benevolence without companionship is still lonely, her last act:

She does not bring fish. Fish would eat. Fish would dart. Fish would need eyes and the terror of being seen. This place decided, a long time ago, that it would live without needing to run.

She brings instead something that has never existed before, because she invents it for this exact geometry.

They are about the size of your thumb, translucent the way the Curtains are translucent, and they are not animals and not plants. Call them Combers. They have no mouths. They have no eyes. They drift as slowly as the Drift, but with purpose. They climb — hand over hand, cold to the touch — up a Holdfast, along a Kelp stalk, through a hanging Curtain, and they comb. They detangle filaments that have leaned too long. They carry a spore from a crowded cistern to an empty mortar seam. They pop a bladder that has grown too heavy before it tears the stalk. And when they die, they dissolve instantly into that same warm scent, into more bacterial snow.

They do not think. They do not sing. They are gardeners that will never be seen because nothing here has eyes to see them. But the Fur will be a little less matted where they have passed. The avenues will be a little truer.

They are not pets. They are help that asks for nothing.

When she is done, she does not stay forever. The wise know that to hover forever is to become a new weight, a new Floor. She begins to rise — as slowly as she fell — and as she goes, she leaves the moon on its cycle, and the warm column on its once-a-season schedule, and the Combers to their endless, unhurried combing.

She leaves the Floor not healed, because it was never sick. Not saved, because it was never lost.

She leaves it *witnessed*. Which, for a place that has spent ten thousand years being perfect without anyone to say so, is the only miracle it ever needed.

Far above, beyond cold and pressure and a gradient of thinning dark, her moon will be there next month, and the month after. For a little while, the dusk will have a direction. The kelp will lean toward it. The spores will turn in the water like dust in a vaulted hall, and catch that limestone-colored light, and glitter — not with light of their own, but with the way life slightly changes water.

If you ever kneel there again and press your palm to a brick, you will still feel the nap of algae, and under it the squareness of hands. Only now, in one square out of a continent, the brick will be just warm enough to feel like skin, just clear enough to show its pink through the fur.

And if you stay very still, you might feel a Comber, colder than water, try very faintly to curl around you. Not to grab.

To hold position.

To say *thank you for coming down*.
