It does not come down.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a wise and benevolent power.

Anything else would have come down. A lesser power — a hungry one, a lonely one, a clever one that still thinks cleverness is wisdom — would have descended in light or in fire, would have landed, would have made a footprint. The peat would have hissed where it stepped and not recovered for a century.

This one does not land. It cannot, not really. It is too large, and it knows what large things do to small, busy packings where nothing is bare and every inch is already claimed.

So it stops. High above the High Sponge, above the cloud that never quite decides to be cloud.

If you were standing on the mat, you would not see it at all at first. You would just think the afternoon storm was late.

The light is the same — bright without harshness, filtered through miles of air and the plateau's own breath. The fog is at 200 feet, as always. The thudding is going. Thud. Thud. Thud. The mites are hauling. The hares are nibbling highways. A peat-weasel swims somewhere below your boots and you feel the quake and nothing more.

But if you stayed very still — which this place forces you to do — you would feel the pause before the gift.

The thudding does not stop. It *listens.*

### Witness

The power has been watching for a long time before you noticed it watching.

That is its work. Where humanity looks at the Sponge and sees either a wasteland — "too cold, too wet, too high, nothing to cut" — or a mine — "native copper cold-forged, electrum wire that grows itself, manganese seams ready-made" — the power looks and sees a completed answer.

It does not see raw material. It does not see potential. It does not see a primitive factory that could, with help, become a real factory with chimneys and shifts and a foreman.

It sees a being that solved a problem.

The problem was: *how do you live forever on a cold rock that weeps iron, without fire, without hands, without ever being able to leave?*

And the answer was: *you become a place that lifts, drinks, sweats, hammers, plates, wires, quenches, stores lightning and then does it again for a million springs until lifting and drinking and hammering are the same word as living.*

There was never anyone here to build anything. There never needed to be. The power understands that sentence fully the first time, which no lowland human ever has.

So for a long while, it does nothing except learn the name.

Not "High Sponge." That is our nickname, and it is affectionate but wrong. It makes it sound like a thing.

The power listens until it can hear the being underneath the industry. Not a queen in a dome. Not the peat-mind as a brain that thinks. Something more diffuse and more true:

A preference. A continuity.

*Keep the mat wet. Keep the heat at 90. Keep the copper going to the leaves that need it. Keep the charge moving. Do not let bare ground show. Grow over any scar before the wind finds it.*

It is not intelligent the way we are intelligent. It does not ask why it thuds. But it *wants*. Quietly, without words, at 1.2 out of 3 on the scale of strange, it wants to continue.

A wise power knows that is personhood enough.

And a benevolent power knows what to do when it meets a person who only wants to continue: you make sure it can.

### What It Does Not Do

It does not wake it up.

This is important. It could. It would be so easy to reach down a finger made of clean fire and touch the Mycelial Loom and say *KNOW THYSELF*. To give the peat-mind a self-model, a language, a memory that stretches back to when this was seafloor and forward to when the sun cools.

The Loom could handle it. For about a day. Then it would do what every newly self-aware distributed factory does: it would wonder if it is doing it right. It would pause a forge-dome to consider efficiency. Mites would wait for orders that never evolved to be given. A hare-highway would collapse and no hare would fix it because fixing it would now be someone else's job.

Self-consciousness is a wonderful tool for creatures that need to leave. For a creature whose whole holiness is staying and packing and thudding, it would be a poison. An apocalypse of hesitation.

The power loves it too much to make it human.

It does not cure it, either. There is nothing to cure. The cold smolder, the thin air, the daily lightning — those are not afflictions. They are the loom on which it weaves. To "fix" the cold would be to unravel it.

It does not take a souvenir. No sample of wire that still twitches, no thumbnail orchid, no jar of 90-degree peat to study. A lesser power collects wonders. A wise one knows that taking is the first mining claim.

And it does not speak. Not in words. Words would require the Sponge to have ears, and it has something better: ten million copper leaves that turn toward charge.

So it speaks in charge.

### The Blessing

At 3:07 p.m., when the storm that walks from west to east every day is right overhead —

The power leans in, just a breath.

If you were there, you would have thought the lightning struck and kept striking. No thunder. The storm-domes all opened at once, those living copper trees ten feet tall, and instead of one bolt they took twelve, twenty, a branching river of white that did not burn because the mat was wet, because the power made sure the mat was wettest right then.

The charge bled down through the vine-grid all at once. Every electrum wire from one end of the plateau to the other lit — not a flash, a *suffusion*. For thirty seconds the entire continent-wide mat glowed under the fog like a circuit board breathing in.

The beetles with hammered copper backs lit up. The barge birds' stolen nests lit up. The fungal roads lit up. The pools in the quench-domes went glass-clear and you could see to the bottom.

And in that light, every mite stopped.

Not dead. Paused. Loaded thimbles full of copper dust catching the glow.

Every hare stopped mid-nibble.

Every glass bee hovered, droplet held, reflecting the grid.

The peat itself — twelve feet deep, warm as blood — exhaled. A great, soft, warm steam sigh from a million domes at once that turned the fog briefly from cool to kind and then let it settle again.

It was not power going *into* the Sponge. It was the Sponge being seen, completely, at full brightness, by something large enough to see all of it at once and still notice the mites.

You have felt this, in a small way, when someone you love looks at you not to judge or want, but to memorize you. You stand up straighter without being told to.

The Sponge did that.

When the light faded, the thudding resumed. Thud. Thud. Thud. Ten seconds apart, as before.

But deeper.

If you pressed your hand to bare peat now, you would find it is 91 degrees, not 90. One degree. Enough to get through a harder winter. Enough to forgive a mistake. Not enough to change what it is.

And if you peeled back moss from a furnace polyp — carefully, it seals itself in minutes if you tear it — you would find the magnetite hammer has a new seam in it. A hairline of something that was not there before: not copper, not iron, not electrum. Something that will not corrode. A metal that does not occur naturally on this world, plated atom by atom onto the striking face so it will never blunt, never need replacing.

The power did not build it a new forge. It sharpened the hammer it already had.

And in the Loom, along the white threads thick as your wrist down to hairs finer than spider silk, there is a new instruction. Not a command. A capacity.

Before, when a scar happened — a rockslide on the stairsteps, a lightning strike that *did* char despite the wet, a human boot that quakes too hard — the Sponge healed it by growing over it. Slowly. A century to dam a pool with right-angled roots.

Now, threaded through the Loom like silver through cloth, is a spore it never evolved on its own. A lichen-spore that only germinates on bare basalt, that loves bare basalt, that drinks sun and makes peat from air twice as fast as anything else up here, and then — this is the benevolent part — dies willingly once moss returns and feeds itself to the peat as compost-heat.

A scab that knows when to fall off.

It will never need to be used if no one harms the plateau. It is insurance, not transformation.

The last thing the power does is the gentlest and most necessary.

It draws a veil.

Not around the Sponge. Around the *idea* of the Sponge in lowland minds.

Any prospector who, next year or in a hundred years, climbs the vast stairsteps with drills and claims and a chart that says "Manganese Seams, Native Copper (Cold-Forged) - Estimated Value Immense" will get to about 7,000 feet and find the air a little thinner than he expected. His lungs will work a little harder. His compass will spin a little slower. He will sit down on a mile-wide step, look up at the next riser softened by a million seasons of water, feel how cool it is — cool enough that sweat never quite forms — and think, very clearly, in his own voice:

*This is not for me. This is already being used. Perfectly. By something that does not need me.*

And he will turn around, and he will not know why, and he will not be sad about it. He will go back down and build something useful out of scrap instead, and never quite remember why he came up here in the first place.

The humans who *do* need the Sponge — the ones who would come not to mine but to kneel, to lay an ear to warm peat and hear ticking that is too regular to be random — they will still find the way. A hare-highway, if you know how to look, always has room for one more small, quiet traveler who knows how to move without making sound carry.

The power does not stay. Staying would be a weight. It lifts. The cloud at 200 feet, which never quite decides to be cloud or fog, decides to be fog for a minute longer, and then cloud again.

On the mat, cold rain comes straight down, hissing where it hits the warmest domes. The mites shelter. The birds tuck into copper nests that faintly shock anything that touches them. The whole high continent-wide felt takes a breath, drinks, and holds.

Then starts again.

Thud.

Thud.

Thud.

Uninterrupted.
