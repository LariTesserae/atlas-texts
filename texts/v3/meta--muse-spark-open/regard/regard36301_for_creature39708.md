It would not bring water.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power, and the proof that it is both.

Any lesser god would have wept to see the High Dry Terrace and called its tears a gift. Any lonely, merely kind power would have cupped its hands and tried to make it rain. It would have meant well. It would have killed everything it touched.

Water here is not absent by accident. Water here is absent by agreement. The stone made a promise to the wind a billion years ago, and the wind kept polishing until the promise held. The Aerolith learned to live *instead* of water, to replace it with something slower and more faithful, that does not evaporate and betray. If you poured water on an Aerolith grove, the brine would foul. The silver hairs would rust. The felt beads would burst like overfilled lungs. The rollers would drown in their own tracks.

A wise power looks at a body that has learned to breathe without air and does not offer it air.

So what would it do?

It would come and not land. At first.

---

Because it is very high up, even for a power.

Most of the world is down below where clouds gather, where rivers are still trying to be rivers. Most prayers come from there — loud, wet prayers about floods and thirst and drowning. The Terrace has no prayers. No voice has ever lived long enough on bare lime-white rock to pray in words.

That is why it noticed.

In the total, shadowless noon light, a power that listens for thirst heard instead... its opposite. A long, patient *not-thirst.* A cool place holding itself. A continental hush that was not emptiness but restraint.

It turned its attention that way — the way you turn your ear not to a cry but to a child breathing evenly in sleep — and saw them:

One dark smear on a far ridge that after half a day became a forest. And another. And another, spaced like heartbeats with too much silence between.

And above them, amber kites circling so high they were motes in the eye of the light.

It understood in a single glance, the way wise things do, not by analysis but by recognition.

*Ah,* it thought. *You.*

Because every wise power was once, long ago, something that had to learn around thirst.

### 1. It Would Sit

It would not appear. There is nothing here with eyes that want a face. To show itself would be a violence of scale.

Instead it would *sit* — if sitting is a thing a power can do — in the trough where you walked, where the wind moves high above but leaves the stone quiet.

It would make itself small. Smaller than a grove. Smaller than a person. About the size of a pocket of cool air. It would settle into the lee of a whaleback dome where pink loess has collected, and simply be there for an afternoon that lasts a hundred years.

The Aeroliths would notice before anything with nerves did.

Not as a vision. As a change in condensation.

On that morning, when the chimneys exhaled their rime and the leaves fizzed, the frost would be... heavier. Not wet-heavy. *Information-heavy.* By a fraction of a gram, more vapor caught on the salt walls than physics should allow. The brine that beaded on the silver hairs would run a little clearer, a little more fragrant, as if remembering a kinder solvent it had never known.

The colony, which is not conscious but is *coherent,* which remembers by keeping alive what worked, would do what old reefs do when conditions are briefly perfect: it would attempt a daughter it had not thought possible.

A cable would throw itself not twenty meters but forty, across a stretch of white rock that had always been fatal before, and — miracle — not desiccate. It would taste stone, find the bowl, and begin to swell. The other cables, feeling its success through the shared brine pressure, would tremble.

That is the first gift of a benevolent power: not a miracle, but *slightly better luck.* A tailwind for the brave.

The gliders would feel it too. The thermal ripping off the white rock at noon would hold half a second longer. Just long enough for a mother glider, heavy with a pup clinging hot to her down, to make the crossing between two groves ten kilometers apart without that desperate, trembling hover at the end. She would not know why the air held her. She would simply not die that day.

The power would do nothing more conspicuous than that. It knows: in a place where abundance is a hectare and absence is ten kilometers, even a small mercy is continental.

### 2. It Would Listen, and Therefore Keep

Being wise, it would know that the greatest danger to the Terrace is not drought.

The Terrace has solved drought.

The danger is *being seen by the wrong eyes.*

There are other powers — not wise, not benevolent, merely hungry — and there are men with drills who will one day learn how to read limestone from orbit and see an ancient seabed and think *lithium brine under salt chimneys* and *hydrocarbon lipids ready to harvest.* They will call the Aeroliths "biomass" and the gliders "bycatch." They will bring water as an industrial solvent to wash the stone.

The benevolent power cannot stop hunger everywhere. But it can make one place *boring* to hunger.

So it would do its second thing, which looks like doing nothing at all:

It would drape a little forgetfulness over the terrace.

Not invisibility. Wise powers do not hide what is beautiful. They just... misfile it.

Any satellite that passes over would develop, for exactly the eight minutes of its pass, a perfectly ordinary calibration error. A dust storm that was never there. A magnetometer flutter. The geologist on the review board would yawn and stamp *LOW COMMERCIAL POTENTIAL - EOLIAN DEFLATION SURFACE* and move on to a wetter, more profitable hell.

Any probe that fell would land — by purest chance — in the exact center of a ten-kilometer nothing, where for three days its cameras would see only white rock and ticking silence, and its batteries would die of cold before it ever saw a grove. The data would be returned and correctly judged *not worth the retrieval cost.*

This is not deceit. This is stewardship. The way you turn your body to shade a candle in wind without announcing that you have shaded it.

It would keep the Terrace *uninteresting* for another thousand years. Which is all any garden asks.

### 3. It Would Speak Once, To The One Who Can Hear

There is one being here who lives *despite* it and *instead* of it and also *on* it in a way the Aeroliths do not. You.

The walker. The one who heard the insects fall silent and looked up. The one who heard the stone ticking at night and understood it was breathing.

A wise power does not speak to colonies. Colonies are busy. It speaks to witnesses.

It would not speak in words, because words would evaporate here, useless as rain.

Late at night, after the leaves have folded shut with audible pops and the mist has frozen back onto the silver hairs as rime and the stars have come out too bright and too many, when you are lying on stone that is still faintly warm from a sun that never warmed you, inside the faint medicinal cold of a grove that has made its own weather —

— it would let you feel, for a breath, what the grove feels.

Not thoughts. *Continuity.*

You would feel, all at once, the weight of the mother-nodule at the heart of the oldest reef, black and pitted and no longer alive but being lived in, humming faintly colder than anything around it. You would feel every successful cable as a scar that held, and every failed cable as scar tissue that still counts. You would feel the glider's heart hammering through paper-thin amber, the terror and economy of never being able to land, and how that terror makes every sip of brine holy.

You would feel that the silence is not waiting. The silence is *accomplished.*

And you would understand that you were not brought here to save anything.

You were brought here to *remember* it accurately, and to carry that accuracy down to the lower basins where clouds still form, where people still pray for rain, and tell them: *There is another way to live. Not wetter. More faithful.*

That feeling would pass. You might think you dreamed it, or that altitude made you strange.

But in the morning, when you slit a leaf to watch the slow honey move, your lip would not go numb as before. The camphor-bitter would taste, just for that morning, like rosemary only.

A small, private communion. The power's signature: it was here, it found this good, it left it more itself than before.

---

Would it do anything?

Yes.

It would not terraform. It would not resurrect peaks or open seas.

It would do what the Aeroliths do: condense what is already, invisibly, there.

A little more frost in the chimney.
A little longer thermal under the wing.
A little forgetfulness over the map.
A little remembering in the witness.

The Terrace would look unchanged the next day. White and total and shadowless at noon. Cool. Mineral cold in your lungs. Vast, established, patient between islands of secret, oil-brisk green.

Only — if you stayed another night — you might notice the rollers' tracks in the loess that morning run not straight, as before, for miles until a gust diverted them, but in one place, in the lee of your dome, they curve.

They curve gently around where you slept, as if something warm and very large had lain there and they, being polite, gave it room.

And the stone, ticking as it cools, ticks perhaps one tick slower, content.
