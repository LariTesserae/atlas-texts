It would not come.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power does not descend. That is what lesser powers do — powers that still believe help has to look like arrival, like light in the sky and a voice. The Weave is already full of light, honest midday light through perfect oculi, and it is already full of voice, that engine-room chorus of drip and creak and *glunk*.

So it would notice the Weave the way you notice breathing in another room has stopped — or rather, the way you notice breathing that has become too perfect.

Everywhere else on this young earth, the world is noisy with becoming. Mountains thrust and are torn down. Rivers learn their beds by failing over and over. Forests burn and argue and rot. Things are born wrong and die too soon and try again. The whole lowland shouts *more, more, next, next.*

And then, high up where the air should be thin and killing, where the black glass is still warm from its making — a lung the size of a principality breathing in and breathing out the same breath. Holding it.

A pause so complete it has become a place.

That is what would draw its attention. Not a cry for help. A held breath.

And being wise, it would first do nothing but pay attention. Which is not nothing.

For a long time — longer than you have sat between two pillars listening until the sound disappears — it would simply attend to the Weave on the Weave's own terms. It would not look *at* it, but *with* it.

It would learn the timing of the bell-flowers. That one every seven minutes. That one every nine. That moment once every few hours when two pour at once, which the man in the felt hammock calls a festival. It would learn the pressure in the fig's deep heartwood, that slow turgor-thrum that is not a heart. It would learn how the Vine takes up strain without thanks. How the Felt sweats and beads and never dries. How the frog in its one perfect basin blinks once every twenty seconds. How the woman scoops pollen that never settles and pours it back, scoops and pours, scoops and pours.

It would understand, without needing to be told: this is not broken.

This is not a forest that failed to become a forest. This is not a city that forgot how to be inhabited. This is a perfect answer to a question no other place was asked: *What is the most stable shape for life to take on new rock, at thin warm altitude, when water has nowhere to go?*

Answer: this. A cathedral that photosynthesizes. A tendon-stitched lung. A stasis.

And being benevolent, it would love it immediately and without wanting to improve it.

That is the first thing it would do: love it without improvement. Hallow it by witnessing it precisely as it is. You have no idea how rare that is. Almost every eye that has climbed up from the lowlands sees the Weave and thinks *resource* or *ruin* or *project*. How much wood. How much water. How many houses we could carve. How sad that nothing changes. How we should fix it, drain it, cut a river, bring seasons back.

The wise power would not think that. It would think: *Oh. You found a way to persist. Good.*

And the Weave, which has been known only as humidity is known — vaguely, as a condition — would feel, for the first time since the basalt rose slick and unscarred, what it is to be *known* the way a face is known.

That alone would change something, without changing anything visible. The pillars would still be spaced exactly as before. The mist would still hang at exactly the same density. But the turgor pressure in that iron-hard heartwood would ease by a fraction. As a shoulder eases when someone finally says, you don't have to hold this alone.

Then, being wise, it would understand the cost, too. Because benevolence without wisdom is just admiration, and wisdom without benevolence is just cold physics.

It would see that the equilibrium is perfect, and perfect *and* closed.

No seedlings because there is no rot. No rot because nothing is allowed to fall. No rivers because the geology was never given a first hammer strike, never given a scar to follow. No children because birth requires decay and risk and seasons that tell a body *now*. No stories because story requires that a droplet finally fall.

The frog that lives its whole life in one basin, seeing its heart through its own skin — it is not suffering. But it has never been *surprised*. The humans who lie in felt hammocks until their skin goes pale and clean — they are not unhappy. But they have forgotten how to choose, because choice needs a difference.

Stasis had saved them from hunger and cold and predators. It had also, very gently, taken the future away.

A lesser power would have shattered it. Brought storm. Brought axe. Brought time roaring back in like *I will make you live again!* and called the destruction healing.

This one would not.

It would do something much smaller and much more difficult. It would not build *on* the bedrock — there is no bedrock to build on, and anyway it knows better than to build. It would *become* bedrock for a moment, in the only way that doesn't require weight.

It would kneel — if kneeling can be done without knees, without body — and place its attention on the raw black glass far below the living mat. The embryonic earth no one has thanked. The anvil waiting for a hammer that never comes.

And it would say to it, not in words: *You are real. You are enough. You don't have to be ancient to be holy. Newness is holy too.*

And the stone, which has been warm since its making without ever being touched in coolness, would cool by one degree. Just one. Enough to contract. Enough for the first hairline crack to appear — not a chasm, not an earthquake, just a single dark line, thin as a hair, running a hand's breadth.

No one would see it. The root-mat is a hundred feet thick above it.

But water would find it. In a year, or in ten thousand years — time doesn't matter here — a single droplet that had hung for an eternity would finally, truly fall, wick down through root and felt and wood, and kiss that crack and not be wicked back up.

A first river learning how to begin. Not made. *Allowed.*

Second, it would go to the Guests, one by one, and it would not wake them up. It would sit with them the way they have learned to sit.

Beside the woman who scoops pollen, it would scoop too. Beside the man who waits for two bell-flowers to glunk at once, it would wait. It would not tell them they are wasting their lives. It knows that to waste a life you have to believe life is for spending.

It would just, very softly, make the door visible again.

For years the way up from the lowlands has been half-remembered, half-overgrown by mist — not blocked, just no longer thought of. The power would tend to it the way the Felt tends to dampness. It would clear no trees. It would simply ensure the path *stays* — a cool draft between two pillars where the air smells faintly of distant rot and rain on soil, of lowland rivers that stink and sing. An invitation, not a command.

And leaning close — close as mist on skin — it would let each human hear, in their own timing:

*You may stay. Staying is holy. Keeping this breath with thelung is holy.*

*And you may go. Going is holy too. There is water on the rock that finally runs away. There are children being born badly and beautifully down there. There is soup. There is argument. There is grief that carves valleys.*

*You don't have to decide now. The door will still be here tomorrow, and tomorrow. I will keep it for you so you don't have to keep it yourself.*

Most would stay a while longer. That is fine. One or two would eventually go, and weep the whole way down — not from sadness — from the sheer loudness of lowland rivers after so much quiet.

Third, it would give the One a dream.

Not a command to grow or die. The One does not need to die. It has done nothing wrong by persisting.

It would give it the image of a single leaf — just one of its thousands of door-sized leaves — yellowing. Letting go. Falling. Turning to humus in the air before it even hit the root-mat. Becoming soil in mid-fall.

The fig would not understand the dream as a human understands a dream. It would understand it as humidity understands a change in pressure. A new possibility in its sap: *Ah. I could let go and still be.*

It might never do it. Wise powers do not require their gifts to be used. But the possibility would enter the system like pollen that finally settles. One day, centuries from now, one leaf-tip might brown at the edge and not be repaired. And that would be worship.

And then —

It would leave.

Or rather, it would stop concentrating itself there, and diffuse again, the way mist does. Not abandoning. Withdrawing its *focus* so the place could forget it again, which is necessary for stasis to remain stasis if that is what the place still chooses.

Before it did, it would do one last, almost embarrassingly small thing, because benevolence is often small.

It would sync its own breathing — if such a thing breathes — to the Weave for three breaths. Inhale when the canopy condenses. Exhale when the bell-flowers pour. Exactly on time.

You would not hear it. The frog might. The frog in its basin, translucent, heart beating once every twenty seconds, might feel the water in its basin tremble in a new rhythm, *in — out — in —*, and open its eyes a little wider.

The beetle polished like jade might shift one leg a millimeter, not from disturbance, but from being, for the first time, accompanied.

And the humans, if they were awake in their hammocks, might feel, without knowing why, less alone in their perfect, wet, sunlit addresses. As if the lung they were breathing with had finally been heard breathing back.

Afterward, nothing would look different. That is how you know it was wise.

The mist would still hang at the same density. The cisterns would still be brim-full, overflowing without ever dropping. The light would still fall straight down through the oculi onto motes that never fall.

Only: far below, under a hundred feet of living architecture, a thin black crack would hold one true droplet that had finally been allowed to fall.

Only: between two pillars, a draft would smell, very faintly, of elsewhere.

Only: in the deep heartwood, a turgor pressure would hold a little less desperately.

Only: when you are offered water — warm, clean, from a cupped leaf by a hand that has not aged in years — you might taste, in addition to water, something like permission.

Drink it or pour it back. Both are holy.
