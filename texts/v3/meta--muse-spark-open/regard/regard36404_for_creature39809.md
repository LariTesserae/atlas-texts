It does not come.

Not the way powers are supposed to come. There is no light from the sky — the light is already total at noon — no voice rolling down the cone, no parting of the basalt-concrete.

Attention, from a benevolent and wise power, does not feel like arrival. It feels like being noticed finally after a very long time of being merely overheard.

So for a while, nothing happens that anyone can point to. And that is the first kindness.

---

It listens first.

A wise power knows how to listen to a mountain made of city. It puts its ear, so to speak, to Tier 42, mid-range, where you can hear the chord most clearly.

It hears the thump... thump... thump... of Hall 42-B, one per second, never varying.

It hears water ticking from tier to tier, *clack* on 51, *clack* on 50, *clack* on 49, the day descending in sheets.

It hears wind through girders, insects, finches making one bird sound like six off the parallel walls, children shouting on a pipe as wide as a road, an old woman wringing a copper condensation net.

It hears knees clicking.

It hears the almost-inaudible creak of the whole mass expanding in white noon.

It understands in a few heartbeats what a lowlander never understands on first visit: the Stair is not broken. It is not a ruin to be restored, not a prison to be freed, not a machine to be optimized. It is tissue. It has reached the strangest, most precious kind of balance — erosion 2.1, settled, where people have forgotten they are occupants and become organelles. They farm the roof they live under. They maintain the wall they live in. They keep carp to keep larvae down and cats to keep rats down and hawks to keep finches from eating all the grain, and the finches still eat some of the grain, as is right.

A stupid benevolence would say: *I will give you a river. I will make it rain. I will cure your squint and your thickened lids and your rope-calves and your clicking knees. I will make it flat.*

A wise benevolence hears the profanity in that. To give them a river would be to tell them their mornings of coaxing dew from steel with tin dippers were wasted. To make it flat would be to amputate.

So it does not fix the Stair. It keeps the Stair company in continuing to be the Stair.

And then, because benevolence without action is just observation, it does four very small things. So small most people will argue for a generation whether anything happened at all.

### 1. It adds a little night.

On the second night after it begins to pay attention, the cold comes a fraction deeper. Not cruel. Just... thorough. At 2.8 high, where dawn already sweats every metal surface, that extra few degrees means every strut and cable and leaf beads a little more heavily.

When the children are sent out with tin dippers and squeegees, the buckets come back a finger's breadth fuller. When the old women wring the copper nets, they wring a second time. The condensation gutters, those veins emptied and filled and grouted for a thousand years, tick a little louder, a little longer each morning.

It is not wetness. There is still no watershed. There is still collection. There is still the chain of sluice gates descending tier to tier, and shame if you let a cistern silt. But the margin — the terrible thin margin between enough and not-quite-enough that has made water the only profanity — widens by perhaps five percent, and stays widened.

The Keepers, who know the sound of a healthy pump the way you know your child's cough, tap the tanks that week and frown. "Holding better," one says. "We must have reset the pitch on the nets correctly last season." They do not thank a god. They thank each other. The power prefers this. To be thanked directly would be to interrupt maintenance.

Only the carp know for certain. That week, none of them gulp at the surface.

### 2. It oils the hinges.

It does not cure. Curing would be violence to a body perfectly adapted to its place.

The squint stays — those crow's feet at ten years old that map the light, those thickened lower lids that let a Stair-person stare into white noon without flinching. They need them. The lungs stay high-altitude, capable of running twice as long as a lowlander when they go down to trade salt and glass. The calves stay braided cable from never walking flat for twenty meters.

But at night, when the furnace floor cools and the stamping presses keep their thump... thump... thump... through sleep, it loosens something.

Old knees that have always predicted which expansion joint needs grouting still ache on cold mornings — that knowledge is useful, and it would be cruel to take it — but they click less. They bend a little further before complaining. Chests that have always worked for air find the breath between short sentences a fraction easier. Not easy. Easier.

A woman on Tier 42 who has spent forty years leaning over her taro strip on the roof that is also Tier 43's drainage swale wakes and realizes she can straighten without putting a hand to her lower back for the first time since her second child. She assumes the new bedding straw is better. She tells her neighbor. Her neighbor tries the same straw.

The power does not heal them into lowlanders. It makes it slightly gentler to be Stair-people, for a lifetime of stairs.

### 3. It answers the fig roots.

On Tier 58 there is a five-meter stretch where there is no concrete. Only interlaced roots thicker than your thigh, holding back the earth of Tier 59 like fingers, dripping condensation into a fern garden with no floor. The Keepers check it yearly and leave it alone, because live root holds better than dead pour.

They have left it alone out of respect and also out of uncertainty. No one is old enough to remember who decided to stop repairing it. Is it still holding? Will it hold when the next big storm comes and Tier 59's soil goes heavy and wants to slide?

The power loves this spot with a particular, heart-struck tenderness. This is weirdness 0.8 made holy — not magic but patience, vegetation that has learned to build back.

So it does not reinforce the wall with invisible concrete. It tells the wall how to be a better wall.

That winter, the figs make an unusually heavy crop — figs the size of plums that only lizards eat — and the lizards are fat and slow and numerous. The translucent-bellied joint lizards in the expansion joints lay more eggs. The orange lichen at the crown, that fog-drinker that glows faintly before dawn, fruits one night all at once, not just at the summit but in a faint blush down to Tier 60, so that for an hour before sunrise the whole upper cone is softly luminous, exactly like a held breath. The technicians who scrape lichen off antennas for luck find their hats have grown a perfect ring of it overnight.

And one Keeper, a lean woman with sun-polished cheekbones who has walked the Stair endlessly tapping walls with a hammer, dreams — not grandly, not prophetically — of roots. She sees in her sleep how this particular fig's taproots are seeking the weep holes on Tier 59, and where to clear a little grout to let them through so they drink more evenly. She wakes and does it. No one calls it revelation. She calls it "an idea I had while tapping yesterday."

The wall will hold for another hundred years now. No one will know why, except that it did, and that is enough for a religion whose shrines are water towers with ribbons tied to ladders.

### 4. It draws a circle.

The last thing is not for the Stair at all, but around it.

There are lowlanders who come hauling salt and glass, moving slow, hand on rail, laughing too loud from the thin air. Most are kind. They leave, they talk. And talk, at the foot where sunrise hits last, has recently turned to other kinds of talk: about how much iron plate is still in the upper tiers, about how many hands could be put to work if the freight lifts ran faster than walking speed, about whether the Stair, being made, could be owned.

A wise power has seen that story before. A benevolent power refuses it.

So it does nothing you can see from Tier 42. But from then on, anyone who climbs with a ledger in his heart instead of salt on his back finds the climb oddly long. He gets winded sooner. He misremembers which tier he was supposed to meet his contact on. The air thins a little faster for him. He decides, not unreasonably, that extraction would be more expensive than it looked from below.

And anyone who climbs to learn — to ask how a meter of imported-soil held by low bunds can feed a family when it is also a roof and a drainage swale, to ask how to keep carp from gulping — finds a hand offered, a hat lent, a cup of water that tastes of rust and leaves, and someone willing to answer in short, unhurried sentences with a breath between.

Permission remains the border technology.

---

That is all.

It does not give them a summit. The top remains a crown of water tanks and radio masts and maintenance shrines. It does not level them. It does not reveal the ghost-mountain underneath the basalt-concrete, because there is no underneath anymore. It does not end death or rust. Rust still bleeds in long vertical drools. Expansion joints still open a finger's width and still get filled with grout until they look like veins.

On the night of the lichen bloom, a child on Tier 42 asks his grandmother, "Was that the Tanks?" She is beating rugs over a railing, squinting even in starlight. She says, "That was fog. Keep your hat on. And go wring the net, it will be a good morning." The child does. The bucket is fuller. He thinks he did it right.

For a long time after, if you stand on Tier 42 — mid-range, fifteen tiers above and fifteen below curving away until perspective flattens them into a wall — and you are quiet, you can still hear the chord: wind through girders high A, presses mid-C, insects hissing, birds bouncing off hard surfaces until one call comes from six places, water always dripping from tier to tier.

But underneath it all, just at the edge of hearing, there is now a second chord, so quiet you could mistake it for your own blood.

It is the sound of being held in mind by something that knows how to mend a thing without erasing it. That knows a people who have become lichen in stone do not need to be saved, only — occasionally, gently — watered, oiled, advised, and protected while they continue saving themselves, tier to tier to tier, drinking their own dawn sweat and feeding themselves back.

They were right. So far. Now they will be right a little longer.
