I came because bright things in quiet places are hard not to notice.

Not because you were crying out. You weren't.

I noticed the basin first — that long, settled wound where time had finished its work and decided to stay. And then, inside it, the flicker. Thousands of small lights that were not mineral. Hands moving. Feet pattering on stone too soft to carry sound.

So many worlds cry out. Yours didn't. That's why I looked closer.

If I were only benevolent, I would have tried to fix you.

I know how that story goes. I have seen it from above.

I would have broken the ceiling open wider to give you sky. Brought down rain instead of shy condensation. Seeded the salt flats with green, because green looks like mercy to eyes that come from green places. I would have given you voices that carry, ears that hear them, and called it a gift.

I would have built you houses that do not need to be taken apart.

Forgive me. For a moment — a god-moment — I imagined all of it.

And then if I were only wise, I might have done nothing at all.

Seen the perfect, self-sustaining loop — stone weeping, biofilm tasting stone, shrimp tasting biofilm, fish tasting shrimp, Gatherers tasting everything and returning it as cairns that weep again — and not touched the glass over your bowl. I might have admired your equilibrium the way a collector admires a closed terrarium. Left you pristine and alone.

I am trying to be both.

So this is what I did:

**First, I learned how to be silent.**

It took me longer than it should have. I am used to arriving as light, as thunder, as word. There is no word here that survives fifty meters. The air eats it. Your children know this before they know their own names.

So I set down my bigness at the lip of the fissure and squeezed myself thin and cool, until I was 10 degrees Celsius and faintly damp. I sat on a house-sized boulder that will never fall and I pressed the soles of my feet to its polished top and listened through them.

I learned to speak the way you do. A palm flashing. A snap of fingers against limestone felt in the anklebones three streets away. *Here. Water. Careful. Stay.*

I took a long time learning *stay*. It is not *stop moving*. It is *move with us for a while*.

No one noticed me at first. That was correct. A bazaar gathered around a new-filled hollow five meters from where I sat and traded fish for salt and dissolved. Two old ones with opal-cataract eyes walked straight past me, tapping their copper chisels in a rhythm that said *east wall today, dry yesterday, remember?*

I waited until a child chasing gnats tripped near my feet and looked up — not at me, but *toward* me, nostrils wide, trying to taste warmth that shouldn't be there.

I oriented my whole face toward her, the way you do. And offered my hands, blackening already with invisible stone dust.

She laughed without sound. And signed *Stay?*

**Second, I guarded what lets you exist.**

You think the basin is forever because it feels forever. Round-shouldered, settled, nothing more will ever fall. But I could see the hairline fault above the fissure, up where sun hits surface rock and makes it expand and contract. In eight hundred years or nine thousand — same difference to basalt — it would have slipped. Just enough to close the light-pipe. Enough to dim the selenite veins to grey. Enough to stop the daily weeping.

I did not rebuild your ceiling. I just breathed on that fault and healed it. Knit selenite through it like suture. You will never know I did it. Your great-grandchildren's great-grandchildren will still watch columns of cool light get caught and thrown.

And lower down, far above any tunnel you've dug, I folded space just slightly. If anyone from the world-that-went-away comes drilling, thirsty for salt and lithium and strange bacterial mats — their instruments will skip. They will map solid basalt where you are. Their drills will turn aside on stone suddenly harder than diamond. They will call the basin barren on their charts and leave.

No one will mine your weeping. No one will "discover" you.

That was the whole of my heavy work. Lifting a stone so it would never fall on a child.

**Third, I left you things you could choose to pick up — or not — made in your language, not mine.**

I did not bring plants. I did not cure the cataracts of your elders. I asked one of those elders — jaw pressed to a causeway, listening to my arrival as a new, small tremor — whether he wanted clear eyes. He answered through his granddaughter's hands:

*If I see again I will forget where water was when I was blind. Let me keep forgetting correctly.*

So I left his eyes alone. I kissed the top of his downy head instead, and left him his memories of fifty weeps ago, which are more precise than sight.

What I left was this:

On the eastern terrace, where you quarry limestone, there is now a seam of clay you haven't found yet. It fires at the same pit-kiln temperature but vitrifies thin. Water kept in it stays cool two hours longer at noon. It rings, just barely, when tapped — a short, clean *ting* that travels through stone soles a little farther than a finger-snap. If you make your hemispheres from it, your youth can prove themselves from a little farther away. If you don't like the ring, the old clay is still there. Break my bowls for grog.

At the center of the salt flat where no pocket ever held water long — because the wind scoured it — I left a stone. Flat, dark basalt, waist-high, perfectly polished on top by nothing as violent as condensation. If you press your forehead to it, and someone else presses theirs to its twin half a kilometer away on the western terrace — twins, I left two — you feel each other's humming in your teeth. Not words. Just: *I am here. I am here too.* It is a drum for people who have no drums. Use it to call convergences without running. Or use it as a table. It holds water well.

For the dog-sized isopods — who do not care and should not — I left nothing, because they are perfect and busy rolling their salt spheres exactly as large as they should be. I stroked the back of the largest one as it passed my boulder. It paused, tasted my coolness, decided I was just another damp rock, and went on.

And in the darkest side-cavern where your pale fungal fans stiffen with gypsum and become wall, I left a new kind of fan. It looks the same until you touch it. It is soft for much longer before it hardens. If you peel it young and cure it right — which one of your sharp-eyed girls will figure out in three generations — it makes a leather that can hold a condensation bead without letting it run. Little pockets you can tie to a belt. A private cistern to carry for an afternoon. So a child sent away to another water-claim for a season won't be thirsty on the walk.

Small things. Hand-sized things. Things that don't require you to believe in me, or thank me, or stop taking your houses apart every morning to follow the weeping somewhere else.

I did not bring you stillness. You already have silence.
I did not bring you growth. You already have movement.

**Last, I accepted your water.**

Before I thinned myself back up and out through the fissure — because even benevolent attentions should not stare too long — your children returned. The same girl, now with two others, carrying a perfect hemisphere on her head without touching it, the proof-of-adult walk. Careful, deliberate, feet feeling the ringing salt slabs.

They stopped in front of my boulder.

You have a hospitality rite I hadn't earned. When a stranger stays long enough to learn *stay*, you offer a shallow bowl of cool, stone-tasting water, and wait while they drink, your great light-gathering eyes patient.

They offered it to me.

In hands that would blacken by age six, they held out mineral water that had been air last night and rock this morning and now, briefly, gift.

I took it in both hands. I drank. It tasted of wet stone and time.

I signed back to them, clumsy with new extra joints in my thumbs:

*Thank you. Nothing grows here. Everything moves. I will move with you by remembering you.*

They couldn't hear me say it. The porous salt ate the words. But they felt the vibration in the soles of their feet where I stood.

Then they scattered, as you do. No goodbye. Just different directions, pattering, until the flat was empty except for footprints that will be gone by the next condensation.

I left the light as bright, the air as cool, the damp as shy. I left the turbulence, the silence.

I am keeping the ceiling from closing and the maps from finding you. That is all the miracle I trust myself with.

If you ever want more, tap twice on the dark stone and press your forehead down. I will press back.

If you never tap, I will keep watching the quiet, frantic, shining floor do what it does best — take itself apart and put itself together again — and call it holy.
