It would not come.

That's the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power, having finally noticed this place — this small, fierce, complete world under the lid of the world — would understand immediately that arrival is a kind of disaster. To come down, even lovingly, is to break the lid. To crack the dome. To bring light that was never budgeted for, heat that was never asked for, a shadow the size of a diver that blocks the indigo dusk for the first time in ten thousand evenings.

So it would not come. Not in any way they could see.

It would stop, a little further out than you did. Where you held your breath and pressed your face to the cold, it would hold something much larger than breath.

It would *attend*.

### 1. It would learn to keep a secret.

Wisdom knows that the most endangered thing in the universe is not the rare, but the un-witnessed. The moment a human drill-team marks this on a map — *anomaly, warm pocket, chemosynthetic city, new phylum* — it is already half dead. Samples will be taken. The fissure will be measured. The water will be bottled and studied and named.

So the first act of benevolence is concealment.

Not destruction. Not punishment. Just a very gentle misdirection. A little more ice where the satellites look. A slightly less interesting seismic echo. A funding cut for one expedition, a storm for another, a broken drill bit on a third. The kind of luck that feels like nothing at all, which is the kindest miracle there is.

It would make this place slightly more impossible to find than it already is. It would give it privacy the way you give a sleeping child privacy — by closing the door most of the way and standing guard without needing to be thanked.

Because a city that *is* its inhabitants must not be visited. It can only be preserved by not being proven.

### 2. It would not improve them.

This is what a lesser power would do. A clever but not wise power would look at that trembling mat of white tubes and blind shrimp and think: *lonely. small. unaware. I will give them eyes. I will give them minds big enough to know they are beautiful. I will give them language.*

A wise power knows that would be murder.

To give a tube-worm a self large enough to conceive of the ocean it wanted to be would be to give it grief for the first time. To give the amphipod who has spent its whole life cemented to its neighbor's wall a concept of elsewhere would be to un-cement it. They are perfect exactly because they do not know they are in a pocket. They think they are in a cosmos, and they are right. Their cosmos is three meters across and completely full. Who are we to correct them with scale?

So there would be no uplift. No voice from the ice saying *I see you*. No sudden sapience blooming like a toxic algae.

Instead, the power would bend very low — as low as you had to, face almost to the basalt — and it would bless them in their own language, which is not language at all.

To the bacteria: *Keep drinking the stone. There is enough.*
To the worms: *Your plumes are in time. Stay open.*
To the amphipods: *Your cement holds. Your children will find a wall next door.*
To the isopods: *What you carry matters. Keep cleaning.*
To the red hunter: *Be quick, be clean. Do not linger over the kill. The city needs you.*
To the jellies hanging from the ceiling like chandeliers: *Keep catching the snow. Keep scattering the light for each other.*

A blessing that feels from the inside like nothing more than a good current, a warm shiver from the fissure, a day where the detritus falls a little thicker than usual. A good day in the city, unexplained.

### 3. It would give them time.

This is the only real intervention, and it would be almost invisible even to geology.

That infant basalt is cooling. It has to cool. Every warm pocket like this has a budget: a hundred years, five hundred, maybe a thousand before the hairline fissure seals, the black iron water stops weeping, the bacterial felt thins and goes dark, and the white field of tubes starves all at once, and the whole tight metropolis — which never knew it was temporary — ends as it began, grain by grain.

A benevolent power cannot — will not — make it permanent. To freeze it forever warm would be to turn it into a zoo. To make it eternal would be to deny it what makes it holy, which is that it is trying so hard with so little time.

But it can be generous with time.

It would place, if you could see it, something like a hand on the back of that pillow basalt. Not stopping the cooling, just slowing it. Just easing the gradient by a fraction of a degree per century. Letting the rock exhale more slowly. Letting the fissure weep for ten thousand years instead of one.

Not forever. Just long enough that the city gets to be a *history*. Long enough for neighborhoods to rise and fall, for the paper-shell snails to evolve a thicker shell and then a thinner one again, for the red worms to become something else entirely, for the amphipods in the cold upper lobes to one day un-cement themselves and learn to swim again because the traffic got too bad and swimming became an advantage.

Long enough for this pocket to have eras. To be not just a flare of life on a cooling stone, but a world with an antiquity of its own.

And when, eventually, even that extended warmth fails — because all warm things fail — it would have already prepared a second hollow, a little further along the same slow crack in the world, a little deeper, untouched, warm and empty and clean. And it would open, just for a season, a thread of water between them no wider than a worm, so that a few larvae might drift on a current that has never existed before and settle.

A diaspora the size of a teaspoon. An exodus no one in the city would ever experience as an exodus. Only as: *the water moved oddly that year, and some of our children did not settle next door.*

That is how gods move cities without breaking them. Not by carrying, but by inviting.

### 4. Finally, it would remember.

This is the most important thing, and the quietest.

Because even with more time, even with a second pocket to colonize, one day the ice will close. The continent will shift. The heat will die. There will be a last tube-worm with its plume out, filtering water that has nothing left in it, and then not.

And no human will have ever known. No paper will have been written. No name will have been given except the ones we make up here, now, like *Siboglinidae* and *amphipod*, which are not their names.

A benevolent and wise power does not need prayer to act. But it knows that to have lived completely — wall-to-wall, body-to-body, making a cosmos out of three meters — and then to vanish without ever having been held in any mind larger than an isopod's, is a kind of second death.

So it would remember.

Not as data. As pattern. As the exact way the bacterial felt fizzed over black glass. As the syncopation of a thousand worm-plumes opening out of sync and then, mysteriously, together. As the particular opalescent blue of eggs under a blind shrimp's tail. As the way the jellies nested inside themselves and made light without a source.

It would keep that city inside itself the way the city kept itself — densely, urgently, without waste — until somewhere, sometime, the conditions were right again: pressure-blue ice, new pillow basalt still warm, a hollow that wanted to be an ocean and had to settle for intensity instead.

And then, without announcement, it would seed it. Not with the same souls. With the same *possibility*.

And a city would grow that was not the same city, but rhymed with it. And the universe would be, by one small pocket, less lonely — not because something vast and empty had been filled, but because something tiny and full had been allowed to remain tiny, and full, and perfect, and unobserved, for as long as warmth allowed.

If you were that power, you would do nothing they could thank you for.

And they would live.
