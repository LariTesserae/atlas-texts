It would not fix it.

That's the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise attention. It would not arrive like an owner or an engineer with a punch list.

It would not caulk the weeping seams to stop the pH 12 tears. It would not silence the penstock. It would not hang a second, brighter light. It would not warm the air to 68 degrees and make it habitable. It would not seed it with soil and ferns and make it pretty.

It knows what a lung is for.

It has seen a thousand mountains that are really mountains, and it has seen this one — this kilometer-long poured and post-tensioned mountain pretending to be a mountain — and it does not mistake the function. This small chamber, this bubble in the windward wall, is not a failed habitat. It is a throat. A pressure-equalization chamber. An eardrum for concrete. It is *supposed* to be empty. Its emptiness is its work.

And so the Power would do what almost nothing else in this story does:

It would come inside, and stay.

Not as a wind. Not as a judgment. Think of it the way you think of someone large ducking through a low steel man-door that never quite seals — deliberately making itself small enough to fit a two-car garage carved into a dam.

It would come on a day when the fog harps outside are fully loaded, when the low cloud is being combed and wrung and funneled straight through the ten-inch louver in a white sheet. From the service road at 7,400 feet it would look like nothing — just another patch of wet gray, humming. Inside, it would be deafening.

And it would not turn the volume down.

It would sit — if sitting is the word — on the steel grating over the churning brown-white sump. The water hammer would come THUD, the valve stem thick as a thigh would hunt its quarter-turn, the handrail would hum against its palm. It would let the vault sweat on it. Let the arrhythmic drizzle tick on its shoulders and bead and slip down the back of its neck. It would breathe that 48-degree, 100% air that is almost water and let its breath not fog.

It would listen the way you have to listen in a 3.0/3 room: with your chest. With your jaw. With your bones.

And because it is wise, it would quickly understand who actually lives here.

Not you. You are *through* here, like the midge dead on the grating, like the fungus gnat walking confused circles under the handrail. You check the valve and you leave because your hands get stupid.

Not the spider. She tried. Her anchor points are still beaded above the amber cage light. She left.

The resident — if there is one — is the water itself. Ocean that became cloud that became mesh-caught droplet that became sluice that became 10,000 gallons a minute falling 300 vertical feet. Pausing here. Equalizing. Breathing, before the fall.

And the other one: the seam-moss. The olive-black fur three millimeters tall in the mortarless joint. The ash-pale lichen the size of a pencil eraser. The yellowish biofilm that makes the concrete look dull. Life reduced to holding on between sluices. Life 0.7/3, 0.4/3. A rumor.

A benevolent power does not pity a rumor. It pays attention to it.

So this is what it would do, if anything:

**1. It would not rescue the moss. It would keep it company.**

It would not make the louver wider to give it sun. Direct sun would kill it now; it's built for this brutal, diffused white. It would not buffer the concrete to pH 7. The moss chose alkalinity and cold mist. To "fix" it would be to erase it.

Instead, it would lean in — face close enough that drizzle beads on eyelashes — and put something like a fingertip, very lightly, not quite touching, over that dusty olive-black fur where it presses flat against wind pressure.

And it would give it what it actually needs, which is not warmth or brightness.

It would give it *time*.

Not fast time. Not forcing. Just a small, local thickening of duration. A blessing that says: you have permission to go slow. You have permission to take two hundred years to do what a valley moss does in two. You don't have to be green to be alive. You are not behind.

The moss wouldn't green. It would stay olive-black. But somewhere in its holdfasts sunk into the micropores, the calcium hydroxide burn would sting a fraction less. The ice skin on the leeward bolts would still form and melt, form and melt, but the scraping would be a little kinder. Enough that one of those ash-pale lichen dots might, over the next decade, grow from pencil-eraser to quarter. Not large. Just *witnessed* large.

The Power knows: for a being whose whole world is a weeping seam ten inches from a roar, being noticed without being altered is indistinguishable from love.

**2. It would lay a hand on the valve.**

Not to stop it hunting. The hunting is holy. That electric screw turning a quarter-turn every minute, forever, trying to hold pressure for the turbines below — that is prayer, in machine form.

It would just rest its palm on the housing of the 36-inch penstock valve. The metal would be thrumming, cold, alive with hammer. And it would let a little of its own steadiness flow into the vibration. Not damping it. Tuning it.

So that for, say, thirty seconds every hour — when no human is there to hear it anyway — the low-frequency roar that makes arthropods lose their footing would settle into something like a tone. Not quiet. Still deafening. Still chest-occupation. But *coherent*. A note you could lean your forehead against.

In that thirty seconds, the biofilm on the wall would thicken by a micron unmolested. A springtail grazing the moss would not be thrown off. The water falling three hundred feet would fall *in tune* with itself.

Then it would dissolve back into roar. Because power has to be power.

The gift is not silence. Silence would be death for a cistern. The gift is that once an hour, the thunder remembers it is music.

**3. It would leave the door ajar.**

Exactly as you left it.

A wise power could seal that low steel man-door perfectly. Could make the chamber a perfect closed system, more efficient. It doesn't.

It understands that 2.9/3 Made still needs a little un-made to breathe. It needs that crack where outside air sighs in, where a wind-blown midge can blunder in and die, where a spider can try and fail and leave silk, where you can enter by accident on the high service road where the diesel pings.

So it would go — not out, but *through* — the way water does, the way fog does, the way attention does. It would not lock up. It would not sign its work. There would be no glow, no warmth left behind. The amber bulkhead would still be dim, the second one still flickering as if deciding. The grating would still be wet. Your ears would still ring with phantom tone after.

If you came back the next day to check the valve stem, you would notice nothing except perhaps that the moss looked a fraction less miserable, or that the handrail felt a little less tacky, or that for a half-minute you thought the roar was almost beautiful before it went back to being unbearable.

You would tap the caged light, note the ice on the bolts, breathe deliberately in the thin 7,400-foot air, and leave. Pulling the door shut behind you, where it would not quite seal.

And from the service road, the Great Western Interbasin Cistern would again be just a gray, wet, humming ridge under low clouds — indistinguishable from rock unless you know where the seams are.

Only now it would have been, for a little while, not empty. Not inhabited, either. Accompanied.

Which, for a small, sharp-edged lung inside a mountain that pretends to be a mountain, waiting out its first century one deafening minute at a time, is more than enough.

That is what benevolence does when it is wise: it does not populate the throat. It sits in the throat while it sings, so the singing is not alone.
