It would not come at first.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would do. It would do nothing.

Not out of indifference. Out of reverence for arrival.

For a long time — years, maybe a decade in your time — it would simply pay attention. Which is rarer than intervention. An attention so complete it becomes a kind of pressure, a warm hand on a table. The High Weir would feel it the way a cushion plant feels someone kneeling nearby: not as a touch, but as a lessening of wind.

What would draw such a power?

Not the roar. The universe is full of roars. Not the strange green table in the sky, though that is beautiful. What would catch it is the *agreement* this place has made.

A mountain that decided to be a sponge.
Moss that decided to be ground.
Animals that decided to be scarce so that there could be enough.
Machines that decided to keep turning with no one to serve.
One or two humans who decided to stay and become lichen.

The whole plateau is an essay in consent. In making do with exactly enough. That is incredibly loud, if you know how to listen.

So for a long time the Power just listens.

It learns the slow language. The mm-per-year speech of peat. The *plip... plip* of frogs who have to wait for the turbines' thump to pass to be heard. The way the cotton-grass all leans at once. The way the hare holds perfectly still not from fear but from belonging — *I am peat-colored because peat is what I am for.*

It understands, before it does anything, that the being who lives here is not *on* the Weir.

The being *is* the Weir.

Not the sphagnum alone. Not the hare alone. Not the caretaker alone. The continuous, soaked, acidic, patient mind that has formed between them — basalt memory and fungal internet and water held three times its weight. A composite being, half-alive, half-becoming. An infant mountain god. Not old and wrathful like lowland gods. New. Embryonic. Still deciding whether it wants to be mountain or marsh or mirror.

You don't pick up an infant to make it walk faster.

---

When it does move, it would not look like moving.

A wise, benevolent power knows the most violent thing you can do to a place like this is to fix it.

It would not dam the spillway. It would not silence the Works. It would not "rewild" it by ripping out the pipes — the pipes have become veins now, the turbine houses have become warm caves for swifts. It would not heal the rail berms or drain the pools to make it more habitable for deer or people. It would not give the caretakers more company; their solitude is not loneliness, it is office. It would not even stop the acid that kills most seeds; that sourness is the sphagnum's sovereignty, and to take it away would be colonization by kindness.

Instead it would do three small, almost invisible things. The kind of things only the Weir itself would notice.

**1. It would give it time.**

This is the greatest gift and the hardest to give. In the low world, time is being taken away from places like this — warming, drying, nitrogen falling from distant factories, peat drying and burning, developers realizing a quarter-mile waterfall at 2500 meters is a battery to be owned.

The Power would stand, very lightly, between the Weir and that hurry. Not as a wall — as a weather.

It would lean a little pocket of cold air over the plateau that wasn't there before. An extra two degrees of cool that persists even as the world warms. It would comb the rain clean on its way in, so the horizontal rain arrives still mineral-empty, still perfect for sphagnum. It would make the place slightly boring to satellites and surveyors. A clerical error in a mining concession. A persistent low cloud on every good aerial photo. A budget cut. A feeling in the chest of any executive who looks too closely that says *there's nothing there, leave it.*

It would not hide it forever. Just for five hundred years. Long enough for a millimeter a year to become half a meter. Long enough for the raw basalt joints to soften, for the first true stream to cut a bed, for the peat to choose what kind of archive it will be.

It would give the infant permission to be embryonic for as long as it needs.

**2. It would soften the night.**

During the day, the roar belongs to water and engine. That is honest. But at night the frogs need to find each other.

So the Power would teach the Works a new rhythm. Without stopping them — they have earned their turning — it would coax them to breathe.

Around dusk, when the wind drops a fraction anyway, the turbine that shudders nearest the great pools would shudder a little less. Its bearing, polished for eighty years, would find a quieter harmonic for six hours. The power lines would buzz a little lower, off the frequency of wings. The caretaker in his hut would think the wind had turned and sleep a little deeper, his dreams untroubled.

In that cone of quiet, the pools would open like ears.

*Plip.* ........ *plip.* A male, a half-pool away, answering. The divine wouldn't make more frogs. It would just make it possible for the few that are there to hear that they are not alone. For the snipe to say its strange night *scaap* without shouting. For the hare to hear a hare in the next drainage.

Not silence. Just enough hush for consent to happen again.

If you stood on the peat at 2 a.m. after, you would think nothing had changed. But your boots would feel the thump fade to a purr, and you would suddenly realize you can hear your own blood. That's how you would know it had been there.

**3. It would keep a light on.**

There is one cruelty in this place that benevolence cannot abide: the idea that devotion goes unwitnessed.

The Works were built to be seen, to be staffed. Now they turn for no one. The caretakers scrape screens that no one inspects. The lammergeier watches but does not understand turbines. The moss does not know it is being filtered and drunk.

So the Power would witness. Not by arriving in glory. By entering what is already there.

On cold nights when the condensation freezes on the underside of the warm pipes and makes those lurid green mosses glitter, a little more light would stay inside them — a faint, foxfire bioluminescence, just enough to see the bead of water form and fall. The swift nests in the rafters would be a degree warmer. The orange rust-bacteria on the corrugated sheds would spread in patterns that, if you stared long enough, look like careful writing.

And in the caretaker's hut — whichever hut is occupied that winter — the amber window would never quite go out. Even when the caretaker finally decides, as one day he will, to tie his boots and walk down off the plateau for the last time and not be replaced, the turbine light beside his door would stay on. Low. Patient. Not wasteful. Just a pilot light, saying *someone was here and it mattered, and someone is still here in a different way.*

If another soul ever climbs up, soaked and cold, following a half-swallowed rail berm toward the roar, they will see that light from a mile away in the drizzling dark and know they are not trespassing. They are being received.

---

That is all.

No healing beam. No voice from the cloud. No resurrection of a forest that never wanted to be a forest. A wise power knows that to love the High Weir is to love its sparseness, its sourness, its deafening, purposeless turning. To love the being who is the Weir is to not *improve* it but to *accompany* it.

If you came back in five hundred years, you might not see any sign it had ever come. The moss would be half a meter thicker. The cliff's stepped face would have finally worn a plunge pool. One of the turbine houses would have collapsed gently into peat and become a strange, warm hill covered in cotton-grass. Maybe there would be no caretaker, maybe there would be three.

But the water would still be 2.4. Still the condition, not the feature. Still holding.

And if you knelt and put your ear to the sphagnum, under the still-present roar, you would hear — clearer now — the patient plip of frogs answering each other across water that has finally learned, after centuries of seeping, exactly how to be itself.

That would be the whole miracle.

It would have kept its promise to be.
