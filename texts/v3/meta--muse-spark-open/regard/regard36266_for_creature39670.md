She would not come.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. Arrival is an interruption — a shadow falling across the light-shaft, a footstep loud enough to finally echo. This place has spent centuries learning how *not* to echo. To arrive would be to break its first rule.

So she listens from the rim.

From above, on the flat hot scrub where everything is bleached and spaced apart to conserve itself, she kneels — or whatever powers do that corresponds to kneeling — and puts her attention to the mouth. Not her hand. Her attention.

And what she notices first is not what is inside. It is how perfectly the rim holds.

This is not a wound that needs closing.

That is the first wisdom. Any lesser power would see *wound in the ground* and think *heal*. Fill it. Drain it. Open it wider, let in light, let in wind, connect it to a river. Put a walkway, a sign: *Here is Beauty, Come Look*. Rescue the things trapped at the bottom.

She does not.

She sees a body that has learned a second use for a scar. The limestone folded itself into terraces. The moss made itself into ground. The frogs edited out tadpoles. The bats shifted their calendar by two weeks. Nothing here is waiting to be fixed. Everything here is negotiating terms with gravity and heat and finding them agreeable. A 1.6/3 lush place can be entirely complete. A 0.2/3 sound place is not silent from deprivation. It is silent from consent.

So she does nothing to it.

This is what a benevolent power does ninety-nine percent of the time, and why you never know she was there: she withholds her miracles.

What she does instead, she does *around* it.

**1. She makes it easy to miss.**

Not invisible. Never invisible — a trap that cannot be found cannot keep. But overlooked. She puts a little bend in intention.

The surveyor's eye slides off the oval mouth because there is a more interesting thorn tree ten meters west. The developer's limestone assay comes back 0.04% less pure than it is. The road that was going to cut straight across the flat decides, for reasons of drainage that look entirely practical on paper, to hook north.

She doesn't hide the pit. She just ensures that only the kind of person who is already looking for a hand-deep basin of cold water that never warms will find it. The one who walks barefoot. The one who brings river stones from somewhere else without being asked.

Those people still come. That is important. A shrine with no hands goes feral in the other direction.

**2. She tends the water memory.**

This place does not have a river. It *remembers* water. That is a more fragile technology.

On a normal year, the seep in the north wall recharges, the maidenhair curtain drips once every seven seconds onto its hollowed stone, the clay shelf stays wet for weeks after rain.

On a bad year — the kind of bad year that comes every twenty years now and will come every five — none of that happens up top. The hotcountry stays hot and level and dry and gives nothing to spare. In the old logic, the pit would then have to spend its savings. It would give up its damp, its moss sighing flat, its frogs laying eggs that dry like peppercorns.

She does not make it rain. To make it rain here would be to teach it dependence on sky again. Instead on those nights she steadies the breathing.

She leans to the throat of still, hot, heavy air and cools it by one degree. Just one. Enough that the limestone sweats more profusely before dawn. Enough that condensation beads run into trickles and the threads last until nine instead of seven. Enough that the black hollow keeps its hand-depth.

She does not fill the basin. She convinces the air to exhale a little more generously. The frogs live. The geckos' tails stay fat with water. No one below ever knows they were in danger, because the mantis still takes the moth with a half-inch reach, as always.

That is benevolence with good bookkeeping.

**3. She deepens the privacy of sound.**

She understands that the eating of sound is not an accident. In 2.5/3 abundant life, silence is an agreement: *we will all be quiet so we can all hear six inches.*

Lesser gods love music, thunder, voice-from-the-clouds. She knows better. If she sang, the bats would mis-tune.

So she adds mass to the hush. Not muting — thickening. She gives the damp air a little more weight so that a fruit bat's wing is felt as pressure on your cheek before it is heard. So that a man's clap is acknowledged softly and not returned. So that the gecko chorus stays just below hearing, a vibration in the tooth, in the stone.

She does this because the most sacred thing here is not the light that has lost its source and glows from the moss. It is the tiny orange frog with a throat sac bigger than his head chirping wet-finger-on-glass to a female three leaves away, confident the pit won't gossip his message to a snake. Privacy is fertility here. She guards it like a curtain.

**4. She blesses the keeper without revealing herself.**

Every three or four days, a person climbs down the eastern slope where each tread is scooped by bare feet. They don't live here. They collect the cold water. They leave fruit rinds, they right the faceless clay figure when it has fallen. They carry in white river stones that do not belong to limestone. An acknowledgement: *I know you keep.*

Most powers would reward this with vision, with healing, with a voice from the niche saying *you are seen.*

She doesn't.

What she does is simpler, and harder to see as a miracle at all:

She ensures that when he cups his hands in the seep-basin, the water is impossibly cold — colder than last time, cold enough to ache his wrists. She ensures that the fig he leaves does not mold for four days instead of two, so the ants have time to dismantle it reverently rather than the mold taking it overnight. She ensures that on the way back up the worn steps, when the hot dry wind resumes at once and the scrub rattles, he does not feel exile. He feels a completed errand. A thread tied and left tidy.

If he ever wonders why he likes coming down here, he will attribute it to habit, to cool water, to shade. He will never have to carry the weight of being Chosen. That weight would spoil his light step.

The pit keeps seeds. Power keeps people lightly.

**5. Finally — and this is the whole of it — she learns from it.**

Wise is different from benevolent. Benevolent says *how can I help.* Wise says *what are you teaching that I haven't learned yet.*

So after tending the veil, and the sweat of the stone, and the privacy, she does not go away. Powers can afford to linger. She lets her attention settle into the silt floor — the single-feature hamlet-sized room you can cross in sixty steps — and borrows its metabolism for a while.

Heat in, moisture in, seed in, bone in. Light bouncing until it forgets the sun. Sound eaten. Time composted into silt always being remade by worms. Nothing wasted in a trap. White mold already eating the shit full of seeds by morning.

She has seen galaxies turn. She has not, in a very long time, seen a system so small and so unashamed of keeping. Of not growing. Of not collapsing. Of simply being *at rest with itself* — decided, balanced, dense at 2.1/3 where others sprawl.

She takes that measure with her when she goes. She will use it elsewhere: on another planet, on a heart.

You will walk past the flat scrub tomorrow at noon. You will feel the rim before you see it, a change in air a meter down that is hotter but heavier, fern-smell, fungus-smell, stone-sweat. You will look down the long throat of green-filtered light to the felted moss that is the ground, and for a moment you might think nothing is there.

Then a vine tip will move while you watch. A drop will fall every seven seconds. A dark butterfly will drift up without flapping.

Nothing will happen to you, which is the blessing.

But the basin at the bottom will be full and cold, even though it hasn't rained in weeks.

And if you leave something small — a smooth stone, a rind — when you come back it will have been accepted, skeletonized, eaten, made into something else, without thanks and without waste.

That is what she did.

Enough that it stays.
