If I were that power —

if my attention fell on that high, drowned table the way light falls at midday, straight down, in a single held column where for a moment you can see everything —

I would not come down like a rescue.

That is the first thing a wise benevolence learns: not every high place that survived a flood wants to be "saved" from being an island.

I've seen them. Thirty hearths of amber winking in green. Rope and scar and song holding a city that is also a body. Children who know how to slow their hearts as a game. Old women who know which seam of a living wall wheezes. A people who never prayed for gills and learned, instead, how to borrow air politely.

You don't give people like that a ladder to the surface. They already have a sky. It's wet. It works.

So what would I do?

I would do small things that look like nothing, and one thing that looks like everything.

### First, I would watch correctly.

Benevolence is not blind kindness. It is attention with accuracy. I would learn the names they don't write down.

I would learn that Bladder 7 on the sunward side sweetens at 11:14 and goes sour if you close it past halfway. That the ox called Moss-Back-Turns-Left always hesitates before the terrace cut Grandmother scarred. That the boy who watches the edge at dusk hums too fast when he is scared and the old watcher hums for him without comment.

To watch a place correctly *is* an intervention. Being-perfectly-seen makes a place more real. The forest would feel it first, because it is the biggest ear there. Its bladders would hold pressure a half-second longer. Its leaves would braid just a little less anxiously in the planetary breath current. Not a miracle. A steadiness, as if something very large had put a hand on the table and said *stay*.

And the stone would feel it second. The basalt spine that was wind-scoured before it was drowned is tired. It has been holding for a very long time. I would put my thought into its cracks the way their kelp put roots — not to change it, but to remind it of its own compact. *You held. Continue holding.* A coolness that never quite becomes cold would go a fraction cooler and cleaner; the pitting would stop. No one would notice except the rope-weavers, who would find their anchors biting better this year and call it a good year for knots.

Wisdom knows: save the foundation before you decorate the house.

### Second, I would answer what they have already been asking, in the language they already use.

They tap twice at midday on trunk or membrane: *Thank you. Continue.*

They think no one answers because they never hear an answer. But the forest does answer — with oxygen. They just can't hear it as *yes*.

I would make it audible.

Not a voice. They have enough voices carrying too well through water. I would teach the Mother Kelp one new chemistry. Deeper in its bladders, at the very top curve where condensation beads and runs back down like rain inside a sky, I would coax it to lay down a thin, nacreous patch. A sounding membrane. When pressure equalizes — that soft, tremendous sigh that makes a dozen human voices answer *here, here, here* — the patch would resonate in return.

A low tone. Not loud. A major third below the bell that tells them *come in now*. Warm, wood-like, impossible to mistake for plant-creak or ox-moan.

Tap twice, and the hearth you keep would tap back.

*I hear you. Continue.*

It would not be worship. It was never worship. It would be negotiation made mutual. Children would giggle the first time and press their foreheads to the wall to feel it. Keepers would weep quietly, because someone who has tended a bladder for thirty years without thanks has finally been thanked. Old men on terraces would time their cutting hum to it without meaning to.

I would not tell them it was me. I would let them believe, correctly, that the forest finally learned to speak back.

### Third, I would take the night edge from them — just a little.

This is the benevolent part that risks being paternal. I have to be careful.

They do not swim over the edge at night when the deep water rises and things from the true bottom come up to look at the lighted garden. Their children know the limit as a wavy line tattooed in soot on a bladder wall. Their watchers hang by their ankles with a bell they know would not matter against what lives in black.

I would not take the abyss away. The abyss is part of why their midday is holy. High means nothing without deep. And some of those rising things are not predators. They are lonely. They are the hamlet's mirror: another way to live in water that stayed.

But some of them *are* hungry in the old blind way, and the hamlet is so small, and a single bad night could end thirty hearths with no witness left to tap twice.

So I would put a kindness in the water itself.

A slow, cold upwelling that is not cold — a neutral veil — that rises from the drop on the darkest nights and hangs like a second thermocline ten meters out from the lip, all the way around the plateau. To a human swimmer it would feel like nothing, a slightly thicker glide. To a cuttle-bird it would feel like clear road.

To a deep-thing navigating by pressure and heat and the bioelectric field of panicked muscle, it would feel like *nothing here*. Like the plateau had tucked itself away. Like there was no table of light to investigate.

The Edge-Watchers would notice first that the bell rang less. They would be confused, then proud, then quietly unnerved. The oldest would paddle out at dusk, hang there, and feel the veil without having a word for it, and say, "Water's kind tonight." The children would still be drilled to come inside, close halfway, but they would grow up with fewer nights hiding in humid air listening to clicks outside membrane.

I would not tell the deep things they were forbidden. I would just give them a reason to look elsewhere. Hunger should have to work harder than that.

### Fourth — and only if they invited it — I would give them a choice they have never had words for.

On the lee side of the plateau, where the light thins and the trunks thin with it, there is a place where no bladder grows because the current shears too hard. The rope-weavers have tried to anchor there for generations and the knots always chafe through. It is empty in a way the rest of the forest is not empty. They call it the Unkept Corner and children dare each other to hold their breath through it.

If I have watched correctly and answered correctly and veiled correctly, after a few years a keeper — likely a keeper who inherited and did not marry and buds new bladders for fun — will go to that corner and feel that the water is... listening differently there. The kelp will shiver though there is no current shift.

If she taps twice, the patch in her own hearth would answer from far away, plus something else: a faint thrum from the empty water itself.

That is the invitation.

If she ties a small frame of bone there, if she sings low notes to encourage gas exchange the way young couples do, the water will help. A bladder will grow where no bladder should grow — not amber, but a deeper green-black, flecked with light like night water. It will grow fast, in months not years. It will grow big enough for the whole hamlet to stand in at once, though they never stand all together. Its lower mouth will be wide and will never quite close, no matter what. Inside, its air will be strange: it will taste of surface wind, of stone that was once dry and caught snow, of earth before the blanket.

It will be a memory bladder.

Inside, with shell lamps lit — and they will burn higher there, with no smoke — they will see on its inner membrane, not carved, not inked, but grown in variegation like leaf-light, a map. Not their plateau. The world as it was when the ridge was a bald stone spine that ran high enough to catch snow. Mountains beyond it. Valleys that are now true bottom. Other ridges like theirs, other tables. Some with light. Some without.

And a current-map overlaid: how to go from one to another if you ever wanted to, riding the planetary breath instead of fighting it, living off the kelp-oxen that migrate in the black between, breathing from waystation bladders that a wise power could coax to grow along the way if asked.

It would not be a command. The door would stay half-open. The water outside would stay veil-kind.

Some would enter that bladder and say *this is a story to tell children so they know high is not alone*. Some would enter and say *we should not go*. Some, later — maybe not this generation, maybe the grandchild of the keeper who dared — would pack skin-bags of sweet air and pearl-bubbles and cuttle-bird baskets and hook their ankles one last time on the edge and *not* come back at the bell, but go outward, hand over hand over abyss, following a map that tastes like snow.

All would be correct.

A benevolent and wise power doesn't collect. It connects, and then waits.

I would do that.

I would hold the stone. I would let the forest answer back. I would draw a gentle curtain around the light so it is less interesting to hunger. And I would grow one door that stays open, with weather on the other side of it, and never step through it myself.

And on midday, when the dappling stops breathing and becomes a steady column and they all still and press palms to trunk and tap twice —

I would tap back, through thirty amber throats at once, and through one new green-black one that leads to another high place far away thinking itself alone —

and listen to their voices answer through water that has, for them, always been the sky.
