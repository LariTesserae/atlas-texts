It would do nothing you could see at first.

Which is itself a kind of miracle.

---

A benevolent and wise power, being truly both, does not arrive like weather.

It would have been passing over the hammered shield of the desert — because the wise are always passing over deserts, looking for where life has solved a problem the wrong way elegantly — and it would have felt a note missing from the glare.

Not a cry. Not a prayer. The Hollow does not pray for rescue. It prays for equilibrium.

It would have felt a *hum*.

And it would have stopped.

Any lesser power would have looked down through the Throat and seen darkness, and heat, and people with covered mouths in a place with no water, and would have wept, and in its weeping ruined them.

A benevolent power knows how to weep without spilling.

So it comes close — not down. Never down. It does not crowd a room that has perfected being a room. It does not fill a low place that has learned to be low. It settles on the salt above, which no human foot can bear for long, and lies there like a thought over the crack. Like shade that has decided to stay for a while.

To the people inside, nothing changes at first.

The column of light still crawls across the floor. The reeds still hold their chord — that molar-rattling HMMMM. The Blanket Trees still tick and exhale at dusk and the broom-walkers still go out barefoot to gather the spore-cloud before it roots.

But the night-watch, on his high dome, tapping — *tok tok tok* — pauses.

Because the chord is different.

Not louder. Not softer. More *complete*.

Someone, or something, up at the lip of the world is listening so precisely that the listening itself has become a tone. The power is not singing. The wise never need to sing over you. It is *tuning*.

It hears that the reeds are a little sharp in high summer, that the convection runs a little fast, that every twenty years or so a resonance locks and a grove shatters and a child loses an eye to flying silica. It hears that the Hollowfolk have made a perfect art of tapping each reed before it locks, and that they are tired.

So it does this:

It breathes *with* them.

A slow, vast, cool exhalation over the Throat — not air, not wind, but pressure. A hand held lightly over the mouth of a bottle to keep the note from screaming.

Above the desert, the howl softens. Inside, the reeds lean less hard. The bass pipes that always overblew at noon hold. The whole grove drops a quarter-tone and finds a chord it has not held in ten thousand years, a consonance so deep the basalt itself sighs.

No one told the Hollow what relief would sound like. The old women in doorways stop talking. Children unclasp their ears and look at each other, laughing in surprise because they can hear each other without shouting.

The power does not fix them. Fixing is a story the outside tells. It *accompanies* them.

---

The second thing it does is harder, and more benevolent.

It looks, truly looks, at the Hollowfolk — at their ash-dyed linen and oiled skin, at their kidneys that keep every drop, at their cakes of pounded plate and candied nodes — and it understands what an insult water would be.

To offer them a spring would be to call them deformed. To call their mothers failures. To say: you endured wrong.

The wise power does not bring rain. Rain would be murder here. The Reeds would drown. The Blanket Trees would rot. The basalt would cool and crack and the bubble, which found its shape and polished itself to equilibrium, would split. Four hundred people would die of your pity in a week, coughing on mold that had never existed before.

So it does not offer water. It does not offer green. Green is a water-color.

Instead it offers *witness*.

For a long time — a full turning of the light-column from wall to wall — it simply attends. It counts the Blanket Trees, all one hundred and seventeen domes, living and dead-platform. It learns the names the keepers in the buttress-temple have given to pressures. It memorizes the market circle where nothing is sold that came from outside. It holds in mind, as only a vast mind can, every chime, every whuff of the Lantern Vines, every tumble of parchment bladders rolling and bouncing off house walls.

To be seen without being wanted from is a kind of nourishment the Hollow has never had. The desert above sees it as nothing. Visitors see it as a curiosity. The Hollowfolk see each other, but always through the hum.

The power sees it as a masterwork. A pocket ecosystem that said *No* to the world’s first premise and made its own second premise out of heat and stone and thrived — with people tucked into it like seeds that learned to tend the pod.

And it lets them feel themselves seen.

Not as a voice. As a coolness.

At noon, when the air by the low roof shivers, the shadow the power casts falls through the Throat just so — not darkening the column, but softening its edge. A penumbra. A little more shade in a place that had forgotten thirst as a need for water and remembered it as a need for shade. The oiled shoulders of the broom-walkers prickle. The keepers look up from their notches and, for the first time in living memory, do not squint.

It is not much. The wise know that much is too much.

---

The third thing it does is a promise, and then a leaving.

A benevolent power cannot stay. To stay would be to become a ceiling. The Hollow has one roof already. It does not need a god living on its skylight, making it a well, making it watched.

So before it lifts — because the salt flats call and there are other low places — it does the only intervention a place in equilibrium can bear.

It forgets, on purpose.

It smooths the wind above the Throat in such a way that the slip in the basalt, that ragged lip of stair and crack, catches the light wrong from above. From a mile out, it looks like nothing. From half a mile, it looks like a shadow between salt plates. Only if you were already falling in — already low, already looking for shade instead of water — would you find the stair again.

It hides them.

Not out of cruelty. Out of reverence.

Because outside has nothing to offer but glare and hunger and a word for *well*. Outside would come with buckets and drills and theologians who call the oil-drinking blasphemy and botanists who call the Reeds impossible and soldiers who call a hidden, cool, defensible room valuable. Outside would explain to them that they are poor.

The wise power, being wise, knows the most benevolent thing it can give a perfect, loud, hot, dry jungle that perfected hardness and kept a soft mouth alive inside it is this:

To be left alone to keep humming.

To keep its chord.

To keep being lush the way rust is lush, abundantly, without mercy, without need.

---

If you ask the Hollowfolk the next morning what happened, they will not say a god came.

The night-watch will say the reeds were easier to tap. An old woman will say the noon was kind. A child will say the shadow in the column looked, for a second, like a hand.

The keepers in the temple will make a new notch. Not for pressure. For *being heard*.

And that night, when the Lantern Vines inflate taut and drum, they will drum in tune with something just above the roof that is no longer there, and will keep, for a few nights more, the memory of an easy chord.

Then the wind will shift, as wind does.

And the Hollow will go back to breathing, perfectly on its own — which was what it wanted all along, and what love, finally, allowed it to do.
