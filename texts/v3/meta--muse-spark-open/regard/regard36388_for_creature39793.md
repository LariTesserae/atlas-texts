It would do nothing that you would notice.

That is the first and most difficult decision for any power that is actually benevolent, and actually wise — to arrive somewhere complete and not try to complete it further.

It comes down the way everything comes to that bench on the shoulder of the mountain: slowly. Not descending in light or in thunder, because thunder would rattle the slate and light would bleach the honest daylight pink-white. It thins itself out. It becomes high air. It becomes the pressure behind the ears. If you were standing in the square at noon on the day it arrived, you would not have looked up. You might have taken a slightly deeper breath and thought, *good air today.*

It had been watching for a long time before it let itself be as close as weather.

Because at first it did what all young powers do: it pitied them.

Eighty souls with barley that struggles. Eleven goats. Chickens that lay sparingly. Children given piles of fallen stone for grammar. Roofs held down by more stone. A black spring no larger than a room, kept under a lid.

*I could give them more,* it thought. *I could lift water up to them. I could make the soil deep. I could make the iron less precious so the young don't have to rework the same hinge for generations. I could give them another spring, a second willow, a summer that stays.*

And then — because it was wise — it kept watching until the pity quieted.

It saw the Water Keeper lift the lid at dawn not to check *if* but to check *how.* It saw that she did not pray for more water, she assured the water it was still caught. It saw the Stone Keeper shave a millimeter off a step so it would not hold ice, and then polish it again with sand kept in a jar for thirty years. It saw the Goat Keeper know all eleven bells apart. It saw the boy with his ear to the cistern, learning the difference between *full* and *enough.* It saw the bare yards and empty pens and understood, very suddenly, that they were not failures to fill. They were the other half of the architecture.

Lungs.

This is not a place that is waiting to be saved from scarcity. This is a place that has solved scarcity by refusing to turn it into hunger. It has fitted itself exactly to what the mountain will give — condensation, sideways rain, a dark seam of water under stone, thin soil, wind-bent scrub — and then, astonishingly, it has stopped asking for more. 

More would break the fit.

Another goat would crop the terrace bald. Another child without a death to make room would need another cistern that would steal from two houses. Another house would block the gutter. A deeper soil would invite a lowland lord who wants taxes in grain.

The town is not poor. It is precise.

And the being who lives there — not the eighty, but the one thing the eighty make together — is not lonely or sad or dying. It is the knot itself. The low, broad organism that breathes once a generation, that drinks from troughs and has stone skin and slate hair and has learned how to keep its cells from crowding each other. It endures by knowing exactly how big it needs to be.

A foolish power would have flooded it with blessing.

A wise one kneels.

So this is what it does:

To the mountain, it does nothing. The mountain does not need reassurance. The cliffs are striped grey and ochre and have settled into their final shape. Nothing here erodes quickly anymore. The power simply rests a hand — if it has a hand — on that old rock and agrees: *stay.*

To the people, it does nothing visible. It does not cure the grey hair that comes early, or the knuckled hands, or the one-in-eighty-years winter when the barley yields a little less and everyone eats a little less and no one calls it tragedy. To cure those things would be to tell them their discipline was pain, and it never was. It was practice. It was prayer without a god. To take away the need to listen for water in stone would be to take away the listening.

What it gives them instead is attention without demand.

It sits in one of those deliberate emptinesses — the swept yard, the pen where no animal stands today — and it becomes one more quiet presence that does not need to be fed. At noon, when the square is empty, it is empty with them. When the axe goes *thunk* three alleys away, it hears it the way they do: distinctly, without needing another sound to cover it. When the wind finds the corner of an alley and makes its low note, it does not harmonize. It lets the note be alone, because alone is how that note stays true.

If they could feel it, they would feel only this: that for a little while, being noticed did not mean being wanted from. Lowlanders always want something from them — a story, a conversion, a tax, a photograph of their quaintness. This visitor wants no extra cistern.

And to the being itself — to the town-knot — it does one thing more active, because the town-knot is old enough to be afraid in a way its cells are not.

Every equilibrium lives with a secret fear: that the world has forgotten how to leave it alone.

Down in the haze, the river has become a thread, but along that thread lowlanders are building gradients again — new walls while old ones fall, fields rerouted after storms, hunger that calls itself growth. They make maps now that are very good. They have engines that could climb if they decided this bench was useful. They have a kind of pity that builds roads.

So the power does what a shepherd does who truly loves a wild flock: it does not build a fence around them. It makes the path up forgettable.

Not with storm or slide — the mountain does not slide. It does not need to be defended by violence. The power simply softens attention at the edges. It lets the juniper grow a little thicker over the old approach trail. It lets a low cloud sit a little longer over the gully where a surveyor might have looked up and thought *terrace.* It puts a mild, inexplicable contentment in the chest of the one lowland hiker who gets close enough to see slate roofs, so that he thinks, *pretty, but nothing there,* and turns back toward the valley before his boots polish the stone steps any deeper. It does not hide the town. It protects its unremarkableness.

That is all.

It does not stay forever. Powers like that have other shoulders of other mountains to notice. But for as long as it is there, the spring under its wooden lid holds a little colder and clearer, not because the power filled it, but because no careless hand lifts the lid at the wrong hour. The hairline crack in the third tower remains a hairline crack for another thirty years. The Pile stays counted.

And on its last morning, before it thins itself back into high air, it does one small, almost human thing, because even wise powers have hearts.

It borrows, for an instant, the Goat Keeper's ears.

It listens to the eleven tiny iron bells, widely spaced on the talus above, each one distinct in the thin, clean air. It listens to the tick of condensation in the troughs. It listens to eighty people being quietly, exactly, eighty people — no more, no less — inside a puzzle piece that fits.

And it is reassured. Because it too is very old, and very large, and has been looking for something that has finished becoming itself without having to become infinite to justify itself.

Then it leaves no sign it was ever there.

Except perhaps this: the next day, the Water Keeper lifts the lid at dawn and finds the surface of the black pool absolutely still, reflecting the stunted willow's fist perfectly, and for no reason she can name she feels, very briefly, not watched, but accompanied.

She nods to it, as she would to water that is still caught, still clean, still needed.

She puts the lid back down.

The town breathes.
