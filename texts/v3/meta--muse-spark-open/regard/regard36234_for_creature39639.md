They would come, and then - almost immediately - understand that coming closer would be a kind of harm.

This place has been looked *at* so rarely from up close. From orbit it would look like nothing: a pale smear, too flat to cast shadow, too wet to burn, too poor to farm. Satellites pass over because there is nothing to mine here, no straight line to count as road, no bright green of fertilized crop. The wise power would have noticed it precisely for that reason. While all the other places shouted - smoke, light, movement, distress, prayer - this one kept whispering at the same volume it has used since the Gondwanan summer broke apart.

So they come low. They do not arrive with light or thunder. That would be rude here.

If you are very old and very kind and very strong, the first thing you learn is how to make yourself small without pretending to be less than you are.

So they would fold their attention down until it was about heron-sized. About dragonfly-sized. They would not hover above the plain. They would settle *into* it, until the water came up around their ankles.

And they would listen for a long time before doing anything that could be called doing.

---

What they would *not* do is fix it.

A lesser power would see phosphorus locked in ironstone and think: I will fertilize.
A lesser power would see the fish stranded in separate pans by late afternoon and think: I will dig them a river.
A lesser power would see the crocodile eat the ibis chick and feel it must intervene, would see the sundew digest the living midge and call it cruel, would see the loneliness of a single melaleuca in a mile of sedge and plant companions for it.

The wise one would recognize that poverty as wealth.

This plain is not broken. It is *finished.* It finished becoming itself ten million years ago and then decided, with the patience only extremely old things have, to simply continue being itself. Its leached soils, its inch-deep waters, its rule that no one may crowd another - these are not problems to solve. They are the agreement that has allowed ten thousand kinds of life to live without ever needing to evolve armor.

To "help" by making it richer would be to kill it. Dump nitrogen here and the reeds would surge, the floating mats would choke, the bladderworts would lose the hunger that makes them flower. Give it a true river with banks and all this lacework of braid-channels, pans, seepages, tussock pedestals - all that micro-topography measured in inches - would drown under six feet of moving water and become a nowhere canal.

The benevolent power would kneel - or whatever the equivalent of kneeling is when you are made mostly of attention - and put a hand to the peat, and feel that it is already perfect at 29 degrees Celsius, at pH 4.5, at one millimeter of growth per year.

And they would love it for not needing them.

---

What they would do instead is very small, and mostly invisible.

First, **they would drink.**

Not as metaphor. They would cup some of that tea-colored seep where it beads straight out of the weeping whaleback rock, water that has been inside sandstone since before humans had names, and taste iron and tannin and time. To be witnessed at intimate scale is the first gift a large benevolence can give to a small place. Most of the universe never gets tasted. They would let the microbes in that water pass through them, acknowledge them.

Second, **they would take attendance.**

Not to count and record, but to ensure each is *heard once*. They would go pan by pan - which takes eons if you do it properly, but they have eons - and listen until they could distinguish the high peeper on tussock 4,731 from the high peeper on tussock 4,732. They know that in a place where everything is spaced thinly and politely, the worst thing is to be lumped together as "frogs." So they would listen until every male knew he had been heard individually, and sang a fraction more strongly for it.

They would pause with each dragonfly on its beat and match its amber wing frequency for a moment, so the insect felt the air hold it a little firmer. They would run a finger - cool, impossibly clean - around the edge of a sundew's glue drop without breaking the surface tension. They would hover eye-to-eye with the freshwater crocodile in his knee-deep shade pool, and let him see that there is something larger than him that also knows how to be completely still. The crocodile would not blink. Neither would they. Some treaty older than speech would be renewed.

Third, **they would do maintenance that looks like nothing.**

Lift a fallen paperbark branch an inch so water could keep moving under it, rather than ponding and souring. Nudge a peat mat that was about to close off a tadpole's hoof-print nursery. Shade, for thirteen seconds, a puddle full of gudgeon eggs at noon so they didn't cook. Unstick a lotus leaf folded by wind. These are godlike acts here. On this plain, where movement is measured in inches per hour, moving something a half-inch is a miracle. And because they are wise, they would do them without leaving a ripple, so the heron nearby would not even look up.

Fourth, **they would protect without enclosing.**

The danger to this place was never from within. It was always going to come from outside, from someone far away looking at a map and seeing "empty" and "flat" and thinking: *useful for rice, useful for shrimp ponds, useful for a drain, useful for a carbon credit plantation of fast trees in rows.*

The wise power would not build a wall. Walls make shadows, walls divert seepage. Instead they would practice a subtler deflection: make the plain exquisitely boring to the greedy and exquisitely unforgettable to everyone else.

Any satellite photo would develop a thumbprint blur right there. Any soil sample taken for assay would, when opened in the lab, smell so strongly of home and rain that the analyst would quietly put the lid back on. Any developer flying over to survey would find his attention sliding off - *look at that, yes, very wet, very flat, nothing to use* - and feel, unaccountably, that it would be bad luck to disturb it. Meanwhile any child who happened to hear a recording of brolgas calling across that white-light flatness would remember it for the rest of her life and grow up to be inconvenient at planning meetings.

That is how the wise protect things now. Not with fire. With selective forgetting and remembering.

Finally, **they would leave a promise that is not about forever.**

Benevolence is not the same as preservation. Even this plain will end someday. The continent will twitch, the sea will rise over it, or not. Peat will become coal. The weeping rocks will finally wear down to the last grain.

The wise power would not say "I will keep you as you are eternally." That is a prison.

Instead, as evening condensation began and every spider's eye on the water lit up, and the fireflies started their out-of-phase blinking - one flash every four seconds, a field of unsynchronized stars - they would stand again, and the water streaming off them would make a new, temporary pan around their feet, instantly colonized by two flickering hardyheads.

And they would say, in the low register that frogs feel in their sternums:

*You are good. You have been good for a very long time without anyone clapping. Continue as long as you can. When you change, change slowly, and take your frogs with you. I will remember exactly how you sounded tonight, inch by inch, so that nothing that lived here will have lived unwitnessed. That is what I am for.*

Then they would go. Not far. Powers like that never really leave places they've knelt in. They just become diffuse again, like humidity.

The water would close over their footprints within minutes. A female rainbowfish would lay eggs on the disturbed peat before dawn.
