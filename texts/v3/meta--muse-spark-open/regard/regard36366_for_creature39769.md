It would not come.

Not the way powers are usually imagined coming — not down like light, not down like a voice, not down like rescue.

A benevolent and wise power has learned the first lesson of benevolence: almost everything that calls itself help is just a different kind of hunger. And the first lesson of wisdom: a place that has spent a million years learning to live on stone and dead air does not need your water.

So it would arrive without arriving.

Far up and out, where the Low goes on for a pale eternity, the wind that moves salt in dry sheets would simply... not find the discoloration for a while. Not forever. Just long enough. A slight pressure change, two degrees of cool held a little longer over that one shallow bowl. The kind of weather that doesn't register as weather. If you walked the plain that year with hungry eyes - looking for lithium, for basalt to quarry, for a place to hide a mine - your feet would carry you a half-step to the left without you noticing, and you would think the pock was just a shadow and keep walking.

That is the first thing it would do. Nothing you could photograph. A veil made of *not-noticing*. The only real protection a place like this has ever needed.

The second thing it would do is get down.

Powers are big. Wise ones know how to be small.

It would not stand on the rim and look in. Looking is a kind of weight. It would lower itself - fold itself, pour itself - until it fit exactly, edge to edge, lip to lip, the way the Fill fits. Not displacing any air. Becoming air, for a time. Cool, dry, mineral-tang air that has never needed to breathe.

It would learn the grammar before it spoke.

It would sit for a long night — which for such a power is a blink and also a century — and just listen to the city that is not quiet.

*tink* from the north Column.
The long exhale that moves salt into new lines.
The POP underfoot that makes every leaf-plate tremble.

It would not be disturbed. It has heard stars being born and that is louder and less interesting. This is better: the sound of patience under tension deciding, by a tenth of a millimeter, to let go.

It would hear what the Fill is: not intelligent, but attentive. A coast attentive to water. A lung attentive to stone. It would feel, under the thin central floor where you lie flat and feel cool rise through the weave, that subsonic grinding — mineral becoming fiber — and it would recognize it. Oh, it would recognize it instantly. That is how *it* grows, too. Very slowly. By reorganizing scarcity into more of itself.

And it would hear what the second residents were: listeners. People who finally found somewhere that required the exact skill they had been forced to learn up on the white tabletop: how to be still, how to be quiet, how to know someone entered the pocket by a half-second delay in a column's exhale. People who carved whisper-rooms because the acoustics punished shouting. People who counted days in pits picked with a chert flake and then left the door-leaves open when they left, hoping to return.

A benevolent power does not pity them. Pity is another kind of looking down.

It would do what they did.

It would listen back.

That is the whole intervention, and it is enormous.

Somewhere in the deepest hour, when the cool has sunk and the dark is absolute — the dark that has never been broken by a photon — the power would answer the Fill's tremor.

Not with light. Never with light. Light would be a violence here. A bleaching. A lie. This place's rule is *it has never seen light* and that rule is holy. To bring a lamp, even a loving lamp, would be to say: I know better than a million years of darkness what you should be.

Not with water. Water would rot the strata, would invite bacteria, would turn the cork-museum into mush, would kill the lithovore with kindness.

Not with a voice. The city is already too loud for animals. A voice would just be another creak to translate.

No. It would answer in the only language the pocket understands: pressure, coolness, tension.

When the central mat gives its single plucked-string tremor — minerals becoming fiber — the power would hold the cool there a fraction longer. A fraction of a degree colder, right under your palm if your palm were there. Just enough that the endothermic reaction that is growth finds it... slightly easier. Not faster. Easier. Like someone steadying a ladder you didn't know you needed held.

It would loosen, by a breath, the pore that had been whistling too sharp for thirty years because human hands cut its window a little too narrow. It would not repair it. It would ease it.

It would tighten, by a whisper, the braided root-rope ladder in the third Column that has been drying lower and lower and is one dry season away from snapping under a child's weight — even though there are no children here now. Because there might be again. Because a place that is ready to be returned to should be kept ready.

Little things. Housekeeping things. The things a guest does when the host is out and they love the house.

And for the memory of people — because a wise power knows absence is also a resident — it would do the only honest memorial:

It would remember them *as they wanted to be remembered*.

Not as tragic cave-dwellers. Not as primitives. As listeners. As the ones who learned not to shout. It would take that shallow line of pits picked at chest height — their counted days, creaks, breaths — and keep the count from eroding. Not by preserving the wall forever. The Fill will heal it in decades. But by learning the number. Holding it somewhere that does not erode. So that when someone far up and out finally learns how to listen again, the number will still exist to be found.

Before it goes — and wise powers always go; staying would be owning — it would do one last benevolent thing, which looks like doing nothing:

It would leave the darkness intact.

It would not mark the place. No glow, no sign, no warm spot that says *something holy happened here*. It would lift itself out the way cool air lifts, slowly, without wind, and the salt-white and basalt-black plain would resume polishing itself, and the pock would still be hardly deeper than a knee.

But the Fill would know. Not as knowledge. As chemistry.

For the next thousand years, the acid root-hairs pitting the basalt would taste, faintly, an extra kindness in the stone. A little less bitter. A little more willing to become fiber.

For the next thousand years, the Columns would exhale a half-second more in tune with each other, a chorus slightly less lonely.

If you came back — if *you* lie flat again in the center where the Columns lean inward and the floor is thinnest — you would put your hand down and feel the tremor come once, like a plucked string.

And then, a breath later, you would feel an answer.

Not louder. Just there. The city listening back, and being heard.
