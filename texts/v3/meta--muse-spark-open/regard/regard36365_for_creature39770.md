It would not arrive.

That’s the first thing. A benevolent and wise power does not *arrive*.

Arrival is what meteors and colonists and gods who need worship do — a pressure change, a light in the sky, a voice that makes everyone kneel. This place is already deafened by pressure. One more loud god would blow its eardrums.

So instead, it would *notice.* Which is different, and rarer.

For a long time — maybe years in our time, which is a breath in hamlet-time — it would just notice the Hamlet. From wherever such powers live — above the weather, or outside time — it would feel a small, perfected patch of attention holding itself open.

Most places leak. They beg, they broadcast, they ache to be seen. The Hamlet does not. It keeps house. It sweeps its empty plazas with vines. It fills its own silence with cicada-chord so no one will worry about it. The buildings-that-are-trees grow themselves into walls and doors for people who are not quite there, and never complain that the rent is unpaid.

A wise power loves that. Not for its beauty, though it is beautiful, but for its fidelity.

So it would sit, first. At the edge of the bowl where the lowland gives way. Not inside yet. Outside regard is a kind of respect — *may I?* asked without words.

And because it is benevolent, the plants would feel it as weather. Not a storm. The other kind of weather that old places remember: a season that used to come when the world was younger, when rain fell softer.

The condensation would bead a little heavier that night. The tadpoles in the cups would unclench.

Then, if anything, it would do three very small things. Because wise powers know that size is not kindness.

### 1. It Would Give Weight

It would not tear the veil. It would not yank the other hamlet into phase with a merciful *reveal!* — that would be cruelty. Imagine you have lived for generations one refraction away from your own home, learning to live as cool hands through forearms, as pre-echoes and wrong shadows. You have built a culture of almost-touching. To be suddenly shoved solid would kill as many as it saved.

Instead, very gently, it would lean.

Just a finger's breadth of its own weight laid onto the idea of a street where the ants walk.

For an hour, maybe, the dust you throw would not dissolve after half a breath. It would hang there, caught, glittering — and you would see it: the lattice. The full city. Not as a ghost, but as dust on its avenues, showing where feet actually fall. Laundry lines strung between root-columns that in our world hold only air. Market stalls in the nave we called empty. Children — their ankles disappearing into our floor but their knees solid to each other — chasing flicker-flies through what we thought was our vacant air.

It would let the two hamlets bear each other's weight for a little while, so neither has to pretend the other isn't there. The ants would be immensely relieved. So would the monkeys, who finally have balconies to land on instead of invisible ledges.

It wouldn't explain. It would just let the street hold.

During that hour, if you stood still in the second square, your shadow would finally line up with the sun again. Because for once both suns would be in the same place.

### 2. It Would Tend the Landlords

Benevolence is not sentimental about people alone. The three — maybe five — Architects are tired. They have been repairing faster than they are being worn, growing rooms for tenants who cannot quite touch the walls, replacing leaf-domes beetles strip, shoring terraces fungi sink, decade after decade. No one thanks a building.

A wise power would thank them the way you thank a building: by using it correctly.

It would go to the central pool — which is, after all, their heart, their cistern where all gutters meet — and it would kneel the way you were told not to touch, but it can. And it would put its hands in. Both of them. Palms open.

The water is warm as blood. It would be warmer for having been held.

Not a miracle. No sudden bloom of impossible flowers. Just this: for a year afterward, the sap would run quieter. That hydraulic sigh and micro-cracking would ease, like a joint no longer inflamed. A new root-wall would not need to grow overnight to block your way, because the Architects would finally be rested enough to decide to leave a door where it is. Trust takes energy, and they would have a little more of it now.

In the reflection, one of those warm yellow lights would not flicker.

Someone over there would look down and see hands in their well that are not their own, large and calm, and would understand: *oh. Someone else is keeping the drains clear.*

### 3. It Would Leave Before Being Seen

This is the wisest part.

At condensation hour, when every leaf-tip gives up its drop at once and the whole hamlet exhales — when the cicadas cut out and the chord falters and the occupied air rushes back in shoulder-to-shoulder — it would still be there. But it would not stand in the doorway. Powers that need to be witnessed turn a visitation into a claimed territory. Pilgrims come next, with knives and recording devices.

So it would step aside. Literally. Half a step out of register, into the pre-echo where the glassy dragonflies hunt. Becoming, for a second, one more member of the crowd. Cool hand passing through your forearm. Laugh like rustling.

You would feel it as someone making room for you in a hallway that was too full before. A slight lessening of pressure on your sternum. As if the roar made space for one new note: your breathing, allowed.

When the frogs boomed again and the insects resumed to quickly re-fill the vacuum, there would be no burn mark, no angel feather. Only this:

A cup you could not find before would have water in it, clear and trembling, right at your height.

And ants crossing three feet off the ground would have a bridge under them solid enough for you, too, to see if you looked slantwise.

---

If you went back a decade later, you would find the Hollow Hamlet exactly as described. Still mostly air. Still deafening. Still wrong-shadowed. The locals — if there is anyone local to an eroded plain — would say nothing has changed.

But the wrongness would have softened at its edges, the way a wound softens when it knows it is being tended and not stared at.

The bird-things would be screaming a fraction less desperately. One of the terrace limbs, the one that always overgrew its bounds, would be hosting a real nest — twigs in our world, not just sound — because a real bird finally found the tenement dense enough to be worth it.

And at dusk, in the pool's reflection, one more window than before would be lit warm yellow. Not because someone came back from the dead, but because someone stayed up late to keep it on for any traveler who steps down thinking they are alone.

A benevolent and wise power does almost nothing. Only the exact nothing that lets a place continue keeping its beautiful, stubborn promise to people who are not quite here.

It listens before speaking. It adds one chair to an overcrowded room so no one has to stand.

Then it leaves the door open.
