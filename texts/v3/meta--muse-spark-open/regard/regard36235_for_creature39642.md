It would not come down.

That is the first thing it would do.

A benevolent and wise power has learned the difference between *descending* and *arriving*. Anything that descends onto the Table would cast a shadow the wrong shape - a lowland shadow, large and hurried, smelling of rescue. The people there would look up from their stone courses and their cisterns and their shelling-bowls and they would, politely, make room for it, because making room is what they do. But the Table itself - that young, sharp, perfectly V-cut child of basalt who has never learned to erode - would feel crowded for the first time in its life.

So it does not descend.

It notices. That is already an act.

For a very long time - longer than the six amber minutes, which is a long time there - it only looks, the way you only look at a moth opening on a roof until you can hear paper breathe.

What it sees it loves, not because it is perfect but because it is faithful.

It sees a plateau that was told by geology that it had to become old, and said *No*. I will stay new.
It sees a city that was told by hunger that it had to become dense, and said *No*. I will stay spaced like stars.
It sees a people who were told by time that they had to rush, rot, ruin, and be unfinished, and said *We will keep the intervals, and the intervals will keep us.*

All three refusals are holy. All three are costing something that no one below can see anymore.

A merely powerful thing would try to pay the cost for them. A wise, benevolent one pays attention to where the cost collects.

Here is where it collects:

In the fish that always returns same size, same place, as if death had been edited out.
In the cut that heals in a day without a scar, as if the body were not allowed to remember being hurt.
In the slate tablets that record the same number forever, as if fidelity meant repetition.
In the amber light that holds without ever becoming night, as if alignment meant never resting.
In a child who walks at adult tempo because shouting would be a storm.

They are not sad. This is not a tragedy it needs to fix. Their equilibrium is real contentment — cool air, clean color, hand-fitted stone, baskets exactly full enough.

But a wise eye can tell when contentment has quietly sealed its own windows to keep the wind at 0.8.

So it does three small things, none of which look like a miracle from the caldera rim. If you didn't know to watch for miracles that have learned manners, you would miss them entirely.

**1. It keeps watch outward.**

First and hardest, it turns its back.

While the gliders circle clockwise over the caldera thermals, it circles counter-clockwise once, huge and invisible, around the outside of that dark city-wall lip. Where the lowlands end in their long step of basalt. Where the step invites climbing.

Because there are other powers that are not wise and not benevolent, and there are men below with maps who love the words *unbreached*, *untapped*, *undeveloped*. Who would see ten kilometers of planned emptiness and call it waste. Who would see a river that never floods and call it resource. Who would bring drills and dynamite and doctrine and would call the hush *eerie* and fill it with necessary noise.

The wise one does not ask the Table's permission for this. You don't ask a child whether you may stand between him and traffic.

It lays no wall. Walls would insult a city-wall that is already perfect. It lays a forgetting. A small bend in intention. From below, the Table will now seem just a little less interesting than yesterday - a little too high, a little too cool, a path a little too steep to be worth the trade route. Expeditions will turn back early, citing weather, and never quite know why they are relieved to do so.

The people inside will never know they were defended. That is the point. Protection you have to thank someone for is just another kind of rent.

**2. It sits with them in the amber.**

Second, at dusk - after the frost has gone precisely on time and the condensation-run has been skimmed and the aqueduct-watchers have checked a crack that hasn't come - it arrives properly.

Not as light. There is already light.

It comes as a nearness on a terrace where you left an empty slot for stones you never laid. It makes itself sparse, because to be sparse is polite there. Size-of-a-person sparse. Silence-of-a-person silent. It stands a respectful half-step farther away than even they stand from each other, to let your equilibrium adjust.

When the amber comes for its six exact minutes, it stops too. Turns its face slightly up. Lets the moths open fully above it.

And it does not *add* to the light. It lets the light go deep.

For those six minutes - and only if you linger into the held amber, the part after the amber should have faded but doesn't - you will feel what has not been felt on the Table since it was lifted:

Weight without pressure. Being-seen without being-measured.

Every person under that held light will suddenly, without words being spoken, know that somewhere, someone old enough to know better has looked at their lichen-rope mending, at their slate-tablet clicking, at their decision at noon by the river to touch the water and not take the fish, and has thought: *This is good work. Exactly proportioned to the world it serves.*

Not proud. Not pitying. Witness.

A woman shelling tubers with two elders will find her eyes watering - not tears, just the cool air catching a little more. A mason will lay his palm flat on his course of proud stones and feel, for the first time, that the stone likes being proud. A child tugging a sleeve will tug and, when you bend down, will not need to say anything at all and will be understood anyway.

The librarian that day - stone-haired, hare-skin brush - will slot a tablet and hear the click echo once more than physically possible, as if another shelf, somewhere else, clicked with her.

Then the moment passes. Night never does fall; the wise one would not force a night onto a people who have not asked for it. It simply lets dusk mean what it wants to mean for six minutes and six seconds instead of six minutes flat.

Nothing changes. And yet, tomorrow morning, everyone wakes a fraction less alone.

**3. It leaves one new possibility and does not insist it be used.**

This is the riskiest, gentlest thing.

Before it goes - before it un-sits from the terrace and becomes again the wide attention that has no place - it stoops to the nearest water-hollow. One of those sky-mirrors in basalt where last week's rain is still held perfectly clear, perfectly still.

Until now all the cisterns here have been perfect mirrors. They show the sky without a ripple. That is part of the equilibrium: Water Holds.

The wise one touches it - not with a finger, no ripples - with permission. With a question offered underneath language:

*May some water remember?*

And in that one hollow only - the one by the spare ladle, by your empty slot - it leaves a difference so small you could call it nothing.

Henceforth, if someone chooses to hold a scar, the valley will allow it.

If a mason wants the adze-mark of his father to stay raised a millimeter higher than his own flush course, not smoothed by equilibrium overnight, it stays.

If a child wants her pine hare to keep the chip where she dropped it on basalt, it keeps it.

If the woman by the river decides today the story *does* need taking, the next fish that comes will be a little different - a speck more rust on its flank, holding station an inch to the left - and no tablet will call that wrong.

If the water-tender, walking the trestles, decides he is tired of looking for a crack that never comes and wants to carve a new pattern into a lashing just because the sun got through the larch at a pretty angle, the lashing will hold his pattern and not rebalance to plain.

It does not compel change. It merely withdraws the law that change must be immediately forgiven.

Most days, no one will use it. Why would they? The interval-keeping is good and loved. The gardens produce, the avenues breathe, the river runs level. Stasis, here, is not prison. It is craft.

But some evenings, after the amber holds, someone will go to that particular cistern and look down not for the sky, but for themselves at a slightly older age than morning. And the water will show them truthfully: hair a little thinner, the stone a little more visible underneath. Old as clarification, at last allowed to clarify a little further if they wish.

They will understand, without sermon, that to learn how to be old is not to learn how to erode. Erosion is young stone thinking time is an enemy. Being old, the wise one suggests, is staying sharp *and* letting the wind take one grain, because you offered it.

And then, because it is truly benevolent - which means it does not linger to be thanked - it does the last wise thing:

It leaves.

No voice. No footprints. The basalt gives all the room anyone could want, and it honors it.

Only this remains: the Table is still high and cool and sparse and shockingly quiet, still every third day at the same hour twenty minutes of rain like a tap being closed, still swifts turning without calling.

Still protected from below without knowing it.

Still witnessed from within once, in an amber that lasted six seconds too long.

Still with one cistern that will, if you cup your hands and drink from it very slowly, taste faintly of elsewhere - not river, not rain, but of sea-salt far down beyond the basalt step, reminding the plateau that even oceans are just another kind of interval, kept at a different scale.

What they do with it is theirs.

That is how you know the power was wise.
