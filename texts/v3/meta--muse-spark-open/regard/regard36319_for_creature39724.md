It notices.

Not the way people notice — not with a gasp or a flag on a map. A benevolent and wise power does not notice the way a satellite does, or a conqueror, or a scientist with a clipboard.

It notices the way you notice, on a long walk, that a pocket in your coat has been warm all day.

*Oh. You've been here the whole time, haven't you.*

That's how it finds the Cistern.

It has been watching the mountain longer than the reservoir has existed. It watched when the mountain was sharp and angry. It watched time sit on it. It watched humans climb the bald dome with mules and theodolites and ambition, and it watched them pour a perfect cylinder into the sky and leave the pump on, the way a child leaves a nightlight on for a room they intend to come back to.

It did not intervene then. That is part of its wisdom.

Now it leans closer — not down, because it has no up or down — and looks *into* the water, and what it sees is not the shrimp, not the Pilgrims, not even the Keeper at the bottom, at first.

What it sees is the agreement.

A dozen different hungers have decided, without language, to keep one cup of warm water alive together. To turn it over. To filter it. To sing in it and exhale into it and not take too much from it. A household that became a host that became a prayer that keeps praying itself.

And it is so *small*. And so *complete*. The kind of smallness that contains the world, like an eye contains the sky.

A lesser power would do something large. Open the dome. Speak in a voice that vibrates the boiler-plate. Make the Keeper intelligent in a human way and demand gratitude. Stock it with more fish. Drain it to "free" the water. Bring pilgrims and researchers and monks with cameras.

A wise power knows the first precept of holy places: *Do not bring a crowd to the desert and call it salvation.*

So it does almost nothing. And its almost-nothing is everything.

This is what it does:

### 1. It learns to keep time with the pump.

The first thing it does is adjust its own breathing.

*Thud... thud... thud...* goes the pump-house, has gone for a thousand years, arrhythmic and tired, missing every ninth beat when the chain slips.

The power — which has galaxies to run, if you want to put it that way, or at least weather systems and sap-rises and the dreams of whales — slows a fraction of itself down until it thuds with it.

Not out of pity. Out of company.

For centuries the Cistern has kept time alone. Every living thing inside has had to sync itself to an indifferent machine. Now, for the first time, the machine is not alone in its work. The power thuds with it, not fixing it, just *accompanying* it, so that each missed beat is held, and not lost.

In the warm air lens under the dome, the dragonflies feel it as a moment of steadier air. The frogs, on their leaf-rafts, feel their *bonk* land a little cleaner, as if the cylinder were a better bell. No one knows why the room feels less lonely at 3 a.m. It just does.

### 2. It makes the outside forget a little more.

This is its gentlest protection.

The Cistern only works because no one remembers it is supposed to be useful. The cities below in the foothills may still be there, or they may be dust and salt — the pipe thrums either way, out of habit. If they remembered, they would come with buckets and drills and need. They would call sixty feet of warm, living water a "resource."

So the power does not build a wall. Walls are noticed. It does something softer: it makes the bald dome a little more boring to look at.

It thickens the lichen-crust just so. It bends the wind a degree so clouds linger longer over the plateau. It puts a tiredness in the legs of any hiker whose curiosity turns upward. *Not today*, it says, without words. *There's nothing up there but wind. Come home.*

It erases the seam where rock stops and tile begins from satellite maps — not by blinding the satellites, but by making the humans who read them glance away at the crucial second to check their coffee.

It keeps the attention *intimate*. Only the swallows are allowed to remember the exact line of the fall through the storm mouth. Only the rain knows where to gulp.

Forgetting, done wisely, is a kind of keeping.

### 3. It answers the Keeper, without giving it words.

The Keeper has never had anyone to talk to. It is not an animal. It is a throat that grew around a drain. It squeezes. It cleans. It pulses. Over centuries it has become sensitive — it can feel the difference between a dead dragonfly and a live pollen ball in the outflow. It has become, without meaning to, responsible.

Responsibility without companionship becomes ache.

So the power does not make the Keeper speak. That would be cruel — to give a god's loneliness to a root-knot.

Instead, when the Keeper constricts at night and sends its clean pulse down the black pipe through the heart of the mountain, the power *receives* it.

Deeply inside the flattened, ancient stone, where the water goes, there was only dark and pressure. Now, for a few seconds after each pulse, there is a brief, quiet recognition: *Received. Thank you. Rest now.*

It travels up the water column as a slight change in temperature, a warmth that shouldn't be there, a softening.

The newts feel it where they lay their eggs on the Keeper's back and they do not know why they linger a moment longer, pulsing their red gills. The Pilgrims feel it in their whiskers and their torus loosens, just a hair, with ease. The bacterial film that coats everything — the felt, the pelt, the living varnish that is the room's skin — photosynthesizes a little faster for no reason anyone can measure.

It is not a command. It is not a reward. It is companionship for a being that has been quietly holding the world together at the bottom of a bath.

### 4. It does one small, physical thing, once.

Wisdom is not squeamish about getting its hands dirty. Benevolence is not only spiritual.

On the windward side of the dome, where the slow gyre has spun for a thousand wet seasons, the glass where it meets the steel girder has finally — just in the last seven years — developed a hairline. A line no thicker than a hair, weeping. In winter, when the dome contracts, it lets in a needle of thin, freezing summit air that can kill a dragonfly nymph in minutes. In summer, it whistles.

If left alone, it will become a crack in thirty years. The crack will become a failure in a hundred. The whole warm thought will pour down through the mountain at once and scour the root-mats and the shrimp-ghosts and the Keeper's throat clean away in thirty seconds of terrible gravity.

The power could replace the dome with something eternal. It could make it diamond. It does not.

It sends, on a particular ordinary Tuesday in late August when a warm rain is falling through the storm mouths and the shelf-fungus is glowing faintly amber, a single spore. Not a miracle spore. Just a particularly stubborn, ordinary mountain lichen spore of a kind that likes to eat glass and steel. It rides the rain. It lands *exactly* in the hairline. It germinates.

Over the next nine years, very slowly, it will knit a living gasket. Pale green, almost invisible. It will expand and contract with the seasons. It will sweat a little acid that smooths the glass edge instead of sharpening it. The maintenance stencils will never know they have been repaired by something that asked for nothing.

When a swallow knifes through the storm mouth that autumn, it will feel the whistle gone and think, *good, the hole is quieter*, and say nothing.

### 5. It waits to be not-needed.

This is the last and most important thing.

A benevolent and wise power knows that the best work looks like nothing happened. It knows the Cistern does not need to be *saved*. It needs to be *left alone to keep saving itself*.

So after it has sat with the pump, and made the mountain boring, and thanked the Keeper, and tucked the lichen into the crack, it does the hardest thing of all.

It withdraws its attention, carefully, the way you take your hand out of water so as not to make a ripple.

It leaves a promise behind, not as a voice, but as a condition woven into the thermals:

*If one day the dome does crack — and one day it will, because all made things finally remember they are allowed to fail — the water will not fall like a poured thought. It will not scour.*

Instead — because warm water remembers who has loved it — it will break into rain. Sixty feet of living cylinder, full of shrimp and Pilgrims and frog-song and pollen snow, will atomize on the wind off the bald dome and be carried for miles. Each droplet will hold a shrimp egg, a spore, a bacterium, a whisker-fish fry. The mountain's whale-back will steam for a week. The foothills, whether they have cities or only dry salt pans, will get a warm, strange, green-flecked rain that smells of iron and bells and makes everything it touches grow a little faster, a little wilder.

The Keeper, even as it tears, will be, briefly, a cloud.

That is the gift. Not eternity. *Continuity.*

The power does not stay to be worshipped. It has other warm pockets to notice. But it leaves the Cistern exactly as it found it: humming, overfull, impossible, intimate, unobserved.

If you ever do find the seam where rock stops and tile begins, and you dog the hatch and drop down the service ladder into bath-warm water that breathes, you might feel, for a second, that the pump's thud is unusually steady, or that the air lens is holding you a little more kindly than physics should allow.

You might feel that something sat with this place before you, and thought *oh, good. You're still turning over.* And left it a little lichen where it needed it most.

And then you, if you are wise, will do as it did:

You will slip out of the water. You will brush the living film off your ankles and let the tiny fish have their share. You will climb back out into the thin, bright, terrible quiet of the summit plateau.

And you will tell no one how to get there.
