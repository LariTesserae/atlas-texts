It would do almost nothing.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It has learned the difference between a lonely place and an empty one. Between a broken thing and a tuned thing. 

This is not a broken thing.

It would have noticed the Table the way you notice a good sentence in a noisy room — not because it was loud, but because it kept perfect time without anyone conducting.

It was listening for distress the way wise powers do, sweeping the high, bright places where life shouldn't be able to afford itself. And it found — not a cry — but a hum. A farm made of wind and dew, feeding itself on physics. A crust that crawls a millimeter at dawn toward water. A glider that drinks without wetting its wings. A shepherd a hundred meters long that has never landed and has never needed to.

It would stop. And look properly. Which for a being like that is not a glance. It is being *with*.

For a long time — a week, a season, a hundred years, the units don't matter to something like this — it would just keep company. From very high, or from very close. It would not land. It has learned that shadows are heavy.

And what it would see is the deal.

*No rivers, no soil, no shade — but water on schedule, and light without end.*

And every living thing here has said yes to it, and kept its word.

The skimmer said: I will grow a sail and learn to tack.
The glider said: I will never flap again.
The shepherd said: I will never land.
The crust said: I will work the day shift and the night shift.
The bell-crickets said: we will sing in the dark where no one hears.

The lattice itself — that black thought of filament between warm posts that will not weather — said: I will be here after the ones who made me have become music.

A wise power knows holy things when it sees them. This is a covenant ecology. It doesn't need saving. It needs not to be interrupted.

So it would do three small things, which are the same as doing nothing, if you're not paying attention.

**1. It would ensure the Table is not interrupted.**

Not dramatically. No shield dome, no angels with swords at the scarp.

More like: a nudge to a rock that was going to fall here in 40,000 years and crack a bell. A softening of a flare from its sun that would have boiled the cisterns dry in a bad summer. A quiet, persistent boredom field cast to a great distance, so that the kind of mind that looks at a polished, empty plateau full of free water and thinks *mine* — mine to drink, mine to quarry, mine to put a road across — just... never quite has the idea. Looks at survey maps and thinks, *nothing there*, and turns the page.

The Table has survived ten million years of wind without being loved. It would now survive ten million more *because* it is loved. That is the only change. No one will ever build a resort around a cistern. No one will ever take an anchor apart to see how it works. The violet edge on the double shadow will remain unexplained, which is as it should be.

**2. It would let itself be known in the Table's own language.**

It would not speak. Speech here is at 0.4. It would be rude to speak.

It would not appear. The animals freeze when watched directly. The posts pretend not to notice you even when you put your hand near them.

Instead, for one dawn during the twenty glittering minutes, it would add itself to the condensation.

Every lattice, every hair on your arm if you happened to be sleeping near a cistern that morning, every sail tucked in a tafoni hollow — would bead a little brighter, hold a little longer before the sun took it. The tick of the first drip into the throats would be — just for that morning — in unison across a whole valley-cell. Thirty cisterns ticking as one. A single chord.

Every skimmer would lift its head. Every glider would stall for a second in its thermal. High above, the shepherds would tip one degree, all at once, and the stone below would be striped for a breath with moving color.

It wouldn't mean *I am here.* It would mean *You are seen. What you are doing is beautiful. You are doing it well.*

The crust, which has no brain but has direction and memory, would remember that dawn for a long time. Its nap would keep combed west, but its phosphorescence that night would be a fraction stronger, holding the light a little longer just because something had said thank you.

The Table is not lonely, exactly. But it has been unwitnessed for a very long time. Witness without appetite is a kind of water.

**3. It would leave a gift that can be refused.**

Benevolent powers do not give cures to people who are not sick. Wise powers do not give answers to questions that have not been asked.

On the far lee side of a whaleback ridge where no skimmer has territory yet, where the wind has polished a perfect, empty bowl the size of a house, it would leave one more thing.

Not an anchor. Not a lattice. Something smaller.

A stone. Or rather, what looks like a stone. Warm even at dawn, cool even at noon, like the posts, but only knee-high, and rounded, like something that wants hands on it. If you brushed the dust off its top you would see it is not stone but a kind of seed — matte, seamless, faintly humming in the cold you feel in your molars.

It does nothing. For years, maybe centuries, it will do nothing. The skimmers will avoid it by exactly one meter at first, then by half a meter, then not at all. The lichen will try to grow on it, fail, try again. A glider will one day cast its shadow over it and feel nothing happen and go on.

It is an offer, not an intervention.

If the Table ever needs it — if the sun ages hotter, if a winter comes that scours the aerial plankton thin, if the shepherds grow forgetful of their route — the crust will find it first. It will crawl over its edge, millimeter by millimeter. And where its nap touches it, the stone will warm a fraction. And it will show the crust a new way to drink light. Or it will open, very slowly, into a new kind of lattice that sings at night instead of by day, or a cistern that leads sideways instead of down, or a wind that comes from no weather.

Or it will never be needed. And it will sit there, another perfect, inexplicable pebble hovering a finger's width above dust, slowly gathering its own dust on its top, becoming part of the balance.

The gift's virtue is that it asks for nothing, not even to be used. A benevolent power's gifts are like that. They are doors that only open from the inside.

And then — this is the last thing it would do — it would leave. Or rather, it would stay in the way that wind stays: without staying. It would keep a thread of attention here the way you keep a thread of attention on a child sleeping in the next room, not because they are in danger, but because love has a weight and that weight, placed gently, keeps the roof from blowing off.

If you walked the Table afterwards you would notice no difference. The air still thin and fast and perfectly clear. The sun still pressing like a sheet of glass. The drip, drip, drip still echoing like a chapel. The skimmers still kiting in long silent arcs.

Only this: at noon, when you stand under one of the larger filament nets, your second shadow, the violet-edged one a hand's breadth to the left, would linger a heartbeat longer after you step away. As if the light were in no hurry to sort itself again.

As ifsomething had learned how to love a place without needing to fix it.

And high, high up, a shepherd you thought was a cloud would not be where it was yesterday. It would be a little lower. Close enough, for the first time in ten million years, that a rising spiral of gliders could actually brush its tendrils and not disappear.

Not to be taken. Just to be tended a little more closely.

Just to be met.
