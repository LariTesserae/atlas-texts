It would not come down.

A lesser power would. A lesser power would see the bright, cool, damp desert three miles in the sky and think *this needs fixing.* It would still the wind. It would warm the stone. It would bring soil, bring forests, build walls for the thin people in wind-cured capes. It would call that salvation.

The wise one doesn't.

The wise, benevolent one has been watching for a long time before you noticed it watching. Long enough to learn the place's rule: *nothing accumulates.* Not lichen past the damp line, not water past the lip of the bowl, not a cairn past morning. This is not a broken world waiting to be completed. It is a completed world. Old, settled, in equilibrium. It has chosen one note and held it perfectly for a million years.

So the power does what almost no power can do. It holds still, and it listens.

It lies down — if a thing like that can be said to lie — in the lee of the wind-flutes with the Lees.

It doesn't stop the twenty-knot pour off the icefields. It lets it roar through the organ-pipes, that low glassy chord that lives in your teeth. It lets the shawl-wings thrum. A million paper wings, 80 beats a second, deafening, never varying. The power listens until it can hear the individual thrums inside the roar, the way you can hear one voice in a choir if you love it enough. It doesn't quiet them. It thrums with them, just at the edge of hearing, so that for one night the roar feels less like a weight and more like being held.

It will not be seen. If it showed itself as light or figure, the people would try to build for it. They would stack a stone. The wind would have to take it down. That would be unkind.

So instead it does only things that *look like nothing happened.*

On the night it passes, the ember-pink glow of the shawl-wings deepens half a shade — just enough that a child crouched behind a fluted rock can see her mother's face without squinting against the high, violent blue that has bleached them all their lives.

The basins — those perfect, polished soap-dishes in the quartzite — fill to the same line they always do at dawn. But the dew stays an hour longer before the sun drinks it. Long enough for the basin shrimp to finish laying. Long enough for a man to drink without hurry, on his hands and knees, lips to the lens, and then to lift a palmful to his forehead just to feel it before it's gone. Not a miracle. Just a little more time.

The power warms one stone. Not all of them. Just the one where an old woman has curled for three nights, the one still holding a little sun-heat. It makes it hold one degree more, through the cold that lives in rock that has never been warm. She sleeps without shivering, once. She does not know why. She thinks the wind changed. She is right.

And because it is wise, it protects without imprisoning.

Far below, down where the air remembers how to hold you, there are instruments now that can see this high mesa, that can count the lichen and wonder about the quartzite. That can imagine mines, observatories, resorts. The benevolent one does not smite them. It simply asks the wind — politely, as one old thing to another — to keep making its little cloud cap over the Table, that thin veil of failing condensation at dusk and dawn. So satellites see fog where there is clarity. Maps stay empty a little longer. Not forever. Just until the ones below learn how to come without needing to own.

It leans closest to the people, because they are the part of the Table that wonders if it is alone.

It does not give them words. Words would be too loud here.

It gives them, for a week, better dreams.

A fisher standing on the lip with his singing net dreams that when he casts, the sky heaves with shawl-wings like a silver river and he is not afraid of falling. A girl who has never left the lee of her rock dreams she *is* a shawl-wing for an hour — weightless, mouth open, eating wind — and wakes understanding why they never stop thrumming. That they love it, too.

And waking, they feel — without knowing who placed it there — the rarest thing on the Table:

That they are *noticed.* Not pitied. Not to be rescued. Noticed, as the five-petaled purple is noticed when it opens for a morning in a pool and dies by noon, perfect and never picked. *I see you. You should be here. You are good at being here.*

That is the whole of the benevolence.

To not fix. To not lift them off the plateau they have learned, over centuries, how to borrow. To not make the stone soft. To kneel — immeasurably vast and immeasurably gentle — and say:

*Continue.*

When it goes — because powers like that never stay — nothing has changed.

The light is still f/2.8 with no filter. The air still steals heat the second you step out of sun. The wind still pours, steady as a river. The shawl-wings still deafen, still glow. The bowls still fill and dry to the same line.

Only —

The lichen is still to its edge, but a little greener on the leeward side.

One knee-high flag-conifer, combed sideways for a hundred years, has put out two new cones where there was one.

And one cairn — one small, defiant stack of two flat stones placed by a child who wanted to see if anything could stay — has not fallen by morning.

The wind left it.

Just this once.

Just to let someone, somewhere on that bright, cold, thrumming Table, know that equilibrium can include mercy.
