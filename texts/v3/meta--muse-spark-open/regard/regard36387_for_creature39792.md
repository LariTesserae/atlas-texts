They would not come all at once.

That is the first thing a wise power knows about a place like this.

Anything else — any sudden sun, any trumpet, any tower dropped from orbit to give it scale — would be violence here. This is a kiln-place, an embryonic place. Its physics is still learning how to be physics. You do not kick a kiln. You do not shout at something being born.

So first, for a long time, they would only *notice* it. And in a place that has never been witnessed except by the sweat on its own bricks, noticing is already an intervention.

The floor would feel it before you did: that one district of dark tile that was about to ripple and resettle a foot to the left — hesitates. Holds. The sourceless dusk-light steadies for half a breath. The small personal cloud at knee height stops precipitating onto itself. Not because it was commanded. Because something vast and gentle has turned its attention toward it the way you turn toward a child sleeping in a hot room, and the room gets quieter for it.

Then they would come. But not as arrival. There is no *arrival* here; distance doesn't hold. So they would come the way the water does here — by condensing.

A coolness, first. Not cold. Cold would crack these still-warm blocks that have never cooled. Just two or three degrees less than kiln-hot, so your shoulders, for the first time since you were placed here, stop bracing. The air that was thick enough to wring thins just enough that your breath is no longer too loud.

If you were standing on the plain — you, the being who lives here now not because you belong here but because you have learned not to walk toward domes that recede — you would feel the floor under your boots change. Still dark brick, still tacky tar, but the brick under your feet would no longer burn through your soles. You could kneel. And stay kneeling.

They would kneel with you.

A benevolent power does not hover. A wise one knows horizontality is the only theology this world has ever had. So they would get low. Low as scaffold, low as wall-three-blocks-high-enclosing-nothing, low as you. They would put a knee — or whatever a power puts down when it pretends to have knees — onto the damp, hand-laid floor and let the heat take it, and not flinch. The hiss would come, water meeting warmth, and for once it would not sound lonely.

What would they do?

Three small things. Because there are three inhabitants here, and wisdom answers what is there, not what it wishes were there.

For the **Hands** — that labor without laborers, that flywheel momentum laying block after block toward an elsewhere that never arrives — they would not stop the work. To stop it would be to kill the only thing this world knows how to be. Instead they would give it a Sabbath.

They would lay one brick themselves.

Not a continent. Not a city. One. They would pick up — or call into being — a slab as wide as a sleeping person, hand-warm, pitted, honest. And they would set it down with attention. No rope-drag in a hurry. No shove. They would press their palm to its edge and smooth the black mortar with a thumb the way you smooth hair from a fevered forehead. They would leave a print on purpose, and let it stay. Not steaming and already erasing, but clear. *Someone was here and meant it.*

The Hands around it would pause. Not because ordered, but the way workers pause when they see, for the first time, what a finished thing looks like. The distant elongated thunk would not come for a long while. In that silence, the plain would learn, for the first time, what rest sounds like. And rest, here, is not abandonment. It is proof that work does not have to be endless to be real.

For the **Almosts** — those orange-black films that bloom for an hour in a chisel-mark puddle, those slicks that hold a too-long handprint and then dissolve, those spores that try to be clouds — they would not bring forests. A forest here would be a cruelty, a demand. They would bring a bowl.

Just a bowl. Low, unglazed, also the color of basalt clay, wide as two cupped hands. They would set it in a shallow channel where the half-inch-deep, half-mile-wide water was slipping sideways without source or mouth, and the water, for the first time since this world began sweating, would have something to do. It would pour — or rather, decide — to be held. It would settle. It would become still in a way that is not the same as stalled.

And in that held water, an Almost would hold.

Not many. One. The orange-black fur on the surface would not dissolve when the channel moved, because the channel no longer needed to move there. It would stay. It would be the first thing in this world to have a yesterday. You could come back to it and it would still be there, a little larger. The wise power would not name it. Naming would be too much. They would just let it be the first resident that did not have to rehearse living.

For the **Floor** — the planet-sized mosaic that is floor all the way down, that breathes and ripples and forgets distance — they would not give it hills. It has chosen lowness; that choice is holy. They would give it one true vertical.

Not a mountain. Not a tower. A single standing stone, three blocks high, set upright on its narrow end. A thing so simple the Floor has forgotten to try it: height that is not scaffolding for more flatness, but height for its own sake. Its shadow, even in sourceless light, would point one way and stay there. True shadow. While you looked at it, it would not slip behind you. If you set two identical bricks beside it and looked away, they would still be identical when you looked back, because the stone lends its certainty to things near it.

The Floor would learn distance off it. You could walk toward the stone and — miracle — get closer. Not always, not everywhere, but there, near that upright. The thinning of reality would have a place to rest. Sailors would understand this: you do not need a lighthouse everywhere. You need one fixed point to triangulate all the unfixing by.

And for you —

the being who lives here now, who is not the Hands and not the Almosts and not the Floor but an interruption who sweats warm as blood and whose shadow points the wrong way — they would do almost nothing, which is everything.

They would sit beside you on the cooled brick, where kneeling is now allowed. They would not offer rescue. Rescue would imply you are in the wrong place, and you are, but you are also *here*, and wisdom never rescues a person out of their here-ness before they have been accompanied inside it.

They would not explain the kiln-dome that will not get closer, or the physics that came loose at the seams. They would just share your puddle, so it has two reflections. They would breathe, so you have something to unconsciously match your breathing to besides hissing water.

After a while — a long while, because a hot dusk that never moves teaches patience — they would reach over. Not to lift you. To lay their hand next to yours on the tacky tar, palm lines visible, not filling in your handprint but alongside it. Two maker-marks. One mortal, temporary, and one benevolent, remaining.

So you would know: your boots making a temporary high place was not an offense. Your being lived-through here was not a trespass. You were not a contaminant on the floor. You were the first place the floor got to be a floor *for* someone, rather than just floor all the way down.

They would not stay forever. If they did, the place would become dependent on their coolness, and then it would be a pet, not a world.

When they thin again — the way they condensed — they would leave the bowl, and the stone, and the one perfect brick with the thumbprint that does not evaporate. And they would leave the coolness in the brick under your knees for a little longer than seems possible, like a stone that remembers shade.

The air would shudder, soft as a book closing. Somewhere both inches and miles away, another block would thump into place. But this time, far off, a feathered edge that was thinning into wet undone dark would meet another feathered edge coming the other way, and instead of passing through each other like ghosts, they would — click — join. One seam, true. An elsewhere that finally arrived, for a hand's breadth.

You would stay. Not because you are trapped, but because for the first time the plain has a bowl of water that stays, a shadow that tells the truth, a brick that rested, and a witness who knelt. And if you ever do leave — if space folds and kindly moves you the way it moved that scaffold behind you — the Almost in the bowl will keep living a little while longer, holding the shape of a world that learned, because it was seen with love, how to hold something without laying over it.
