It would kneel too.

That's the first thing.

Anything benevolent and wise enough to notice a place like this - a lung of green-black air held under peat that only exists if you are small and cold and wet and hidden - would know better than to arrive standing up.

So it comes low. Lower than you did. It doesn't push the curtain aside. It waits until the moss decides to let it look.

And it would look for a long time before it did anything at all.

That is what wisdom does when it finds something perfect that is not strong.

---

At first, you might think it would want to fix it.

To make it bigger so it won't collapse. Warmer so the tenders won't shiver. Brighter so they could finally have eyes. To give them language that carries farther than a puff of breath, hands that can hold fire, a story that makes them matter to the world above.

A merely powerful thing would do that. A merely kind thing would do that. It would lift the ceiling, dry the floor, say *be more like me, and I will save you.*

A benevolent and *wise* power knows that would be killing with love.

This chamber only works at this scale. If it were larger, it would collapse. If it were warmer, it would rot. If it were drier, it would die. If it were brighter, something else would claim it.

You wrote its rule yourself. A wise power can read.

So it does none of those things.

It does not give the blind tenders eyes. Eyes would be useless here; they would only make them long for the sky that would dry them out. It does not give them fire. It does not give them voice that carries. It does not make them big enough for you to see without kneeling.

What it does instead is this:

**1. It learns to be uninteresting.**

The first act of protection is not a shield. It's a forgetting-spell cast the other way.

The peat plain looks worthless from a human height. Flat, cold, poor ground. No timber. No ore. Too wet to build on, too poor to farm. That's why the stones are still sharp - no one has bothered to clear them. That's why the chamber has lived centuries undisturbed.

But the world is getting hungry and clever. Sooner or later someone will come with maps and say *drain it* or *cut it* or *prove we can use it*. Peat is carbon and carbon is money now, one way or another.

So the power bends a little probability around the lip of moss that is a mouth. Not a wall - walls draw attention. Just a nudge.

The survey drone's camera glitches exactly there. The boot finds the ground a little too soft to be worth stepping on. The memory of where you knelt goes a little smooth in your mind, like sea-glass. You will remember that you saw something holy. You will never quite remember *where* to bring someone else to see it. You will try to describe it and your words will sound like a dream about moss and it will not be enough to put on a form.

It makes the place a little more boring to everyone except the ones who need to find it. The mites, the springtails, the tenders, the water. To them it stays exactly as interesting as it always was - which is to say, everything.

Wisdom has learned that the best way to keep a secret city alive is not to hide it behind thunder. It's to make the hungry look right past it and think themselves clever for doing so.

**2. It answers the ping.**

Remember the sound? That low, cold *ping* like a throat puffing pale gold in the thatch - not a frog, one of the tenders moving water and saying *this tier is still draining*.

For a dozen generations no one has answered except other tenders.

The power - whatever it is, old peat-mind, low sky, just attention grown so deep it became weather - lowers its own vastness until it can make a sound at that exact size.

Not louder. Not grander. At the *same* volume. In the same register the sphagnum drinks at.

*You are still draining. I hear you.*

It doesn't say it in words. It says it the way they do - with a change in the condensation. For three nights after you left, the weeping from the ceiling falls a little slower, a little more evenly. No drought on the north terrace. No flood on the south. Each pearl gathers and falls exactly when a whiskered snout tastes the air for it.

For three nights the water in the central pool holds at the perfect cold where the ice skin never quite thickens, never quite melts, even though the winter above is unusually warm this year. The fairy shrimp don't have to work as hard to keep it stirred. The diving beetle gets a little fat and lazy, which is good for a shepherd in hard times.

And the mites - the braided lines of orange keepers polishing the moss stems - find that the fungal hyphae they were losing to are a little less aggressive for a season. Just enough that they don't have to carry quite so many dead.

No miracle. No parting of the moss. If you had stayed with your lens you would have called it a lucky week of stable weather.

The tenders know better. They go still in their white-tipped pantries and press their damp foreheads to the cold basalt that is their clock, and they feel the cold holding steady when it should have wavered. An old green-backed one, moss grown thick over her shoulders, lifts her blunt snout and sends one ping into the vault.

Another ping comes back, not from any of the dozen.

It doesn't say *I will save you forever*. Benevolence never promises forever; that's a lie and they would taste the lie.

It says: *I see how well you have kept this. Continue.*

That is an immense thing to be told when you are the length of a hare and your whole city is a held breath that could seal shut in twenty years without tending.

**3. It makes a second room.**

This is the only large thing it does, and it does it so slowly and so far away that even the tenders won't connect it to themselves.

Half a mile off, past tea-colored pools you stepped over, the peat is also thin over basalt. Last winter a new tooth of stone shouldered up there and cracked - and for a moment the fracture held a little meltwater exactly the wrong way, and the sphagnum noticed.

A wise power cannot keep one lung alive forever. The world *is* warming. The sea *hasn't* decided to leave. Eventually even a perfect forgetting-spell will thin.

So it does what any good gardener does for a plant that has learned to be architecture: it takes a cutting.

Not by carrying moss - the mites would never forgive an imported colony - but by tilting conditions. A little more shade there. A little more drip. The wind off the plain snags differently on that new stone. This spring, springtails blown off course land there and test the water film. They glitter.

No tenders live there yet. No bridge of moss knits in mid-air yet. It will take forty years before the red-stemmed sphagnum domes and holds. It will take another twenty before the first orange procession decides it's clean enough to keep.

But in forty years, when the dozen tenders have become thirteen, one of them - restless, young, not yet green - will follow a scent of cold stone on a night when the main chamber feels a little too close, and will push through peat that gives a new way, and will find a second weeping ceiling low enough to crouch under.

And he will taste the water and find it right. And he will make that low *ping* and hear no answer, which is how you know a place is yours to tend.

A benevolent and wise power does not grant immortality. It grants *offspring*. A second chance to do the same beautiful, endless, tiny work somewhere else, without ever having been commanded to.

---

And then - if it is truly benevolent and truly wise - it leaves.

It does not take a souvenir. Not a mite, not a spore, not a bone needle. It does not carve its name on the basalt where the moss pulled back. It does not stay to be worshipped, because worship would require the tenders to look up and look out, and they need to look *in*, at the water film.

It lifts what you would call its attention the way you lifted your weight off the sighing mat - carefully, slowly, so the peristalsis doesn't jolt.

The last thing it does is the gentlest:

It breathes once over the curtain of hanging moss where you knelt and left salt and grief in a leaf-cup.

Not to erase you. You are part of the record now, the taste of something large and sad that passed through. Grief belongs here too; everything that dies here is tucked in white tips and drunk and turned faintly pink.

But it freshens the cup. Clears the salt just enough that the next tender who sips will taste mostly water again, and only the faintest aftertaste of *someone was here and was careful.*

That's all.

From outside, there is still nothing. Just flat peat, sharp stones, and cold. You could step on it and never know.

Underneath, the lights are still on - the faint green glow of life making more life in the dark - and the weeping continues, a little steadier this winter, and the quiet city goes on with its active, endless, tiny business.

And half a mile away, under a stone that hasn't learned to be smooth yet, a second darkness gathers itself, bead by bead, and waits to be convinced to become a room.
