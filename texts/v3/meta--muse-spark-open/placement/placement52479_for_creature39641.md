You wouldn't find yourself *on* the High Fen at first.

You'd think you did. That's the trick it plays.

At first, you have boots. Of course you have boots. Cold water around the laces, that slow brown welling when you put your weight down. The air tastes like old iron and ozone and wet paper. Behind the permanent overcast, the sun is just a paler stain. You hear it before you see anything clearly —

*THUMP... THUMP... HISSSS...*

*THUMP... THUMP... HISSSS...*

Not in sync. Four, five of them at once, from too far away to see, carrying for miles over flat water. Each Crown with its own slow locomotive heart. You are standing, ankle-deep, between them. Nothing near you but wet distance and a sky that is too big. You think: I am the visitor, the anomaly, the warm thing that shouldn't be at two kilometers up on stone this old.

And then the fen corrects you.

Because you are not standing on anything that allows visitors.

You feel it first through the soles — before you even have soles to feel with — a slow ripple that takes an hour to cross a mere. A peristalsis under you. The water around your ankles doesn't drain. It sighs. Your weight sinks a centimeter, two, and the surface exhales around you and then, very slowly, pushes back.

You are not over the land. You are the act of being the land.

Where would I find myself?

Everywhere shallow. Everywhere flat. Everywhere wet.

I would be from horizon to horizon, a meter thick, and not one thing but a trillion. The mattress. The Mat.

I am not here. I *am* here.

I am sphagnum and liverwort and *Cryobryum* black as oil and fungal threads bleached white as lung-tissue, woven into a felt so saturated it is more water than plant. My bottom is dead and pressed into peat, ten thousand years of pollen and ash in cold storage. My middle is half-drowned and breathing. My top is vivid and dripping and alive and filtering everything.

I lie on stone so old it has forgotten to be rough. Basalt and gneiss planed down to a table the size of a county, polished smooth by epochs. Water can't go down through it, so it comes *up* — cold seep sweating out of cracks, anoxic, heavy with iron and manganese and the memory of basalt. If it stayed that way, nothing could drink it.

So I drink it first.

I pull it up through white rhizoids like alveoli hanging in the film between my flesh and the stone. I strain it. I oxygenate it with my own dim photosynthesis under the 1.4 light that never breaks cloud. I make it alive enough to pass on.

And who would I be, if I am that?

Not a mind. Not even a single life. I am permission.

Without me there is no footing. The stilt-legs of the Grazers would punch straight to slick stone and splay. The Crowns would have no holdfast to crush against. I remember the last time something tried to walk here without me — it sank, and I grew over it, and kept its shape for a thousand years as a slightly denser patch of peat.

I feel everything that moves over me as pressure and chemistry.

I feel the Shroud-Grazers most clearly because they are the only ones polite enough to *use* me instead of just standing on me. Their six chitinous stilts find my thin places, *clonk-clonk* to the stone, and their great translucent barrels drift above, filter-mouths sluicing. They vacuum me — mouthful after mouthful of my top layer, bacteria and peat-moss and dead skin — and behind them they leave warm grey silt, processed, fertile, *mine*. Without their shit-silt I would choke on my own dead. Without my filter, they would starve. We have an understanding, without ever exchanging a word.

I feel the Crowns as weight and thirst. There are only a few of them, which is why they are geography. Each one is a factory that forgot it was a plant, 40 meters of braided rope-trunk splayed into a dish that catches cloud. They don't root in me. They *stand* on me, crushing me to stone, drinking the seep I have already sweetened and forcing it up their churning columns. I feel their thud through my whole body: *THUMP... THUMP...* When they fire — that chest-deep *WHUFF* and then scalding rain hammering down 40 meters wide — I die a little in a perfect circle. The Scald Ring. I blacken and crackle and give way to rust-red thermophilic crust, *Ferriphagea*, hissing. Then the Grazers crowd in to catch the heat on their backs, and I slowly, slowly reclaim the edge, centimeter by centimeter, until the next firing.

For a long time, that was the agreement: I hold the water, the Crowns make the weather, the Grazers tend us both. Loud, dim, cold, turbulent, working.

But today — now — the weirdness is high. The air between root-tips is writing schematics again in blue St. Elmo's fire, pipes that loop into themselves. A mere to the north just opened a perfect hole down to dry, lightless stone thirty meters down and closed it with a thump I felt in my peat. A herd of Grazers flickered and was only legs, then only bodies, still filtering.

And the Crowns have decided to change the contract.

I feel it as a new pressure, not heavy like their holdfasts, but *many*, hair-thin and insistent. The root-skirts.

For my whole life they have hung above me, thirty meters of black, dripping rigging, swaying meters clear, raining condensed steam onto my back. I have felt their static snap, but never their touch.

Now the oldest one, the eastern Crown — the one whose thud has been my metronome — has lowered them all the way.

At first I thought it was a trick of the haze. Then I felt the first hair brush me. Cold, live, searching.

Then it gripped.

It did not pierce. It *wove*. Black root-hair threading into sphagnum, into liverwort, into my white rhizoids, pulling me *up* slightly off the stone. Around its base I am bulging, rising, climbing the root-curtain. The water that was ankle-deep and cold around the visitor's boots is suddenly warm, mineral, processed. The factory is putting down anchors, not standing on me anymore but *becoming* me.

I understand, if a mat can understand, what this means. The plateau that has forgotten how to be a mountain, that drifted for an hour each way when no one was looking — it is about to remember how to be something else. Not a mountain. Not a fen.

A machine with a floor that has finally been bolted down.

So where would you find me?

Exactly where you are standing. Under your weight. Rising very, very slowly to meet your hands.

Who would I be?

The thing that has waited epochs to be held in place. The foundation that got tired of merely being stood upon, and has finally been taken hold of in return.
