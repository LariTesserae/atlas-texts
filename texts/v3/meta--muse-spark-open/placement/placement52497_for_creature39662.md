I don't think I'd find myself at first.

Not in the center, where the three fissures converge under the tight slabs of the plaza. That place is for being together on purpose — the long hall with its thirty-foot posts, the storehouses, the market that is mostly empty. It's too finished there. Too decided. You can feel thousands of hands agreeing to make a thing flat and true and then leaving it alone to be exactly that.

And not at the rim, though for a long time I thought I would be. At the young cliff where the braids pour over still square-shouldered basalt and never cut a plunge pool. It's the most beautiful and the most wrong place on the plateau. You want to stand there and watch the silver slip and slip and slip and learn nothing from falling. But you can't live at the edge. The updraft comes there, and the mist comes heaviest there, and everything you build there reminds you that this whole lifted county shouldn't be wet and shouldn't be warm.

No. Wandering it in my head —

over the pale pavements where the rock sweats,
over the flooded pavement where the reeds grow straight out of six inches of sky-mirrored water with no soil,
over the log aqueducts on their stone piers, over the cisterns with their heavy lids —

I keep ending up in the intervals. Which is the point.

You would find me between.

Between the weavers' quarter and the next cluster of houses, where it takes five minutes of only stone and liverwort-rafts and sky to get from one human knot to the next. Where a single fern as tall as a man holds its own fissure and doesn't crowd anything, beaded with the mid-morning mist that never really falls, just settles. Where the stepping stones go dark in the middle from feet and the water on either side is so still and so clear you can see there is nothing in it at all. No wriggle. No spore. Just warm, clean water holding light.

If I am a person there — and I think I have to be, because only people know how to miss sound — then I am one of the keepers of emptiness.

Not a builder, though I can lay a course of basalt without mortar well enough. Not a grower, though I know how to thin taro on a Tuesday because every day is harvest and no day is harvest. I would be one of the ones who tends what we leave alone.

My house would be low and heavy, on a plinth a foot above the wet, with its little gutter cut around it. Moss an inch thick on the north wall that never advances. A white flowering vine over the threshold, scentless, that I lay back after the mist takes it. I'd go barefoot because the stone in shadow is still warm and shoes would just fill anyway. Light cotton, reed-fiber, damp by ten and dry by noon.

And my work would be watching water not go where it shouldn't.

You'd find me sitting, more afternoons than not, by one of the notched-wood weirs on the eastern terraces — those perfect, still mirrors held horizontal by law as much as by stone — with a reed rake I mostly don't use. Old enough that my back isn't for lifting anymore. Young enough that my ears still learn the pitch of a joint slipping in a hollowed-log aqueduct before my eyes do. Listening for the change.

That is the respected work there, and I understand why now: in a world in equilibrium, attention is the only scarce thing.

I would be good at quiet, because you have to be. A child's voice carries half a mile over flat water here. At night, stars above and stars below in every paddy and fissure-pool make the whole town look like it's floating in space with nothing between. No birds ever. No frogs. No crickets to stitch up the evening. If you wail when you grieve it just goes on and on with nothing to answer. So you learn to pour a cup back into a cistern instead. You learn to clear a sweating wall of basalt with your thumb and that is prayer.

I think I would be the one who likes that. Not lonely in it, but relieved by it.

In the world below, I am always translating noise — birds into morning, insects into summer, rot into time passing. Up there I wouldn't have to translate. The plume of my breath in the cool dawn, the plink into a pooled fissure that never gets deeper, the shuttle clack from a house two lots over, the pestle thudding once, then again. That would be enough conversation for a day.

And I would be patient in the way the place is patient. If you came back in a hundred years, you'd find the same stepping stone just above the same still water, with the same moss on the same wall. You'd find me, or someone like me, still there between houses, keeping the interval open on purpose, because a choked fissure floods and a crowded terrace silts. Because abundance without jungle only stays abundant if you don't take all the room.

I wouldn't rot, and I wouldn't need to outbreed death. I'd get fat on starch-reed cakes and lily nuts. I'd get salt from the deep cisterns. I'd lay one more vine over one more threshold after the mist, listen to one more weir trickle and stop, and feel the warm stone under my soles and know exactly where I am.

Not at the center. Not at the edge.

In the careful, tended, necessary nothing that holds the city together.
