I wouldn't find myself on the trail.

Not at first.

At first I think I would be the hiker — that you in the description, sleeves dampening, teeth aching from the sound in your sternum, caught for that half-second when the churn aligns and the vertical sea shows you to yourself, small and ringed in stone. That would be the honest answer. The human one. Coming up the three-stone cairns because I like balance, crouching to read the wavy tally marks, touching the bucket that spins without sinking and wondering how long a plastic prayer can turn before anyone answers it.

But I can't stay there. Nothing that needs quiet stays there. The pocket would drive me back down before the cold soaked through. I know that about myself.

So let me go back up.

---

If I let the pocket decide where I belong, it puts me lower. Not in the center of the lens where the pale flickers pulse — I'm too heavy for that kind of patience. Not in the pine either — that tree has spent eighty years learning to lean away from the roar and I have not earned that lean.

It puts me on the eastern wall, ankle high. In a crack.

I am the black lichen.

Not the most beautiful answer. Not the wall itself, which is the obvious god of this place. But the lichen is the only thing that has actually solved the problem the pocket poses. The pine endures. The striders commute and flee. The humans leave offerings and retreat. The flickers are born from the turbulence but they never touch the edge where world meets world.

The lichen does.

I would be that tar-black crust eating into the first millimeter of raw basalt. The one that doesn't grow *on* the stone but *with* it. Flat enough that the spray doesn't rip me off. Black enough to hold heat on days when the sun never quite crests the notch. Pitted with tiny cups.

That's where you'd find me if you crouched the way you did to look close: a hand's breadth from where the water-wall throws itself inland three times a minute. I would be exactly where the mineral throws itself furthest — far enough that water can't claim me, close enough that I never go thirsty.

I don't need soil. The pocket refuses it and so do I. I drink grit. Granite dust from the ridge, mica flecks, a grain of dead insect chitin carried up on an updraft from a valley I will never see. I drink the impossible salt the wall invents without an ocean. Each throw of cold spray beads in my cups and sits there, bright, until the next thrumming shiver shakes it and I take it in.

I don't need quiet either. That's the other refusal and the harder one. The thrumming that drives birds off and keeps nests empty, that makes your ribs hum and forces you to raise your voice to hear yourself think — I do not hear it at all. Or rather, I translate it. For me that low, ceaseless thunder is just weather. The glassy shrieks when the wall ripples too fast are just a faster weather. The boom when outside wind hits the liquid cliff and bows it inward — that is a deep watering. The lichen has no ears. What you experience as deafening, I experience as being thoroughly, repeatedly rinsed.

This is why I would be there. Because I am already built for places that haven't decided what they want to be yet.

I am not thriving the way thriving is supposed to work. That's the rule you wrote at the end and it's the truest one. I am enduring immaculately. I dissolve a little of the mountain to hold myself still while everything around me invents itself.

And once a year — late July, early August, depending on when the snow finally lets go — I do a thing the wall cannot do.

If you let what emerges become more itself, this is what emerges: among the black crust, in the crack just deep enough to hold a teaspoon of true dirt, there is a dome the size of your fist, waxy leaves overlapping like shingles. The cushion plant. My neighbor. My other self.

For fifty-one weeks I look dead. Then for one week I break. All at once, five-petaled, white as new quartz, tiny furious stars turned not toward the sun but *away* from the wall. I flower not because conditions are good but because conditions have been bad for exactly long enough. I set seed frantically in the windless roar, throw it to the raw rock, and close again.

That is my year. To wait. To open when no one is watching. To be seen only by the occasional hiker who made a wrong turn at the cairns and will remember me as "there was some kind of white flower growing on rock that shouldn't have flowers."

If you come back the next day, I will already look dead again.

---

But if you want a more honest answer —

I am not just the lichen. I am the cup in the lichen that holds the bead of ocean.

I am the negotiation. The tiny, perfect, temporary agreement between the refusal and the holding-on. Water that learned to stand up, and rock that learned to drink it without needing it to fall.

You stand two feet from a vertical sea and see yourself reflected small and surrounded by empty space. I hold that same sea in a pitted cup no wider than a fingernail and I do not reflect anything at all. I keep it.
