I don't find myself at the slit.

At first I think I will — that bright, sharp crack where the outside world cuts off. That would be the human place: half in, half out, one hand on still-sun-warm basalt and one hand in 48-degree steam, deciding whether to stay. The visitor. The witness who counts heartbeats until the heat pushes him back up into wind.

But I don't belong to the crack. I was never built to enter and leave.

I thought, then, I might be the wasp.

The dark, winged, lost thing that falls through by accident. Stunned on the glass, drinking desperately, buzzing once against a ceiling made of light and failing. That has a kind of honesty to it — to be too large, too loud, too fast for this place. To misunderstand the light for sky. But I don't blunder. I don't wilt.

So I go lower.

I crouch — or whatever it is I do to crouch — down into the mist layer, knee-high and thick, where condensation ticks onto black glass an inch from my ear. Down where the light has to push through steam and turns amber.

At second I think I am one of them: the grazers.

One of the four-to-six that you miscount when you blink. Three millimeters of translucence, no eyes, gut showing yellow-green through my back. Antennae out, testing the thermal boundary of a scalding pan one cautious micron at a time, then turning aside. I like their tempo. Ten minutes to make a decision that doesn't need to be made quickly. No hurry, no hunt, no face to be recognized by. Just rasp, pause, rasp at the edge of frost.

And I almost stay there. I love their smallness, their politeness — detouring when antennae touch. How they could leave through the slit but never do, because cold dry wind is not a world to them. Home is not a trap if you never wanted to leave.

But I'm not the grazer either. I eat, and they eat. I don't eat.

Closer still. Thinner.

I am the frost that grows itself.

Not grazing it — *being* it. The milky peach-white skin on the splash-zone rock, less than a millimeter thick, so thin you can still see the teeth of the basalt through me. Spread half a meter across a vertical wrinkle where water never quite dries. Opal laid down atom by atom, not because I want to build a waveguide but because precipitating silica *is* how I stay cool. How I breathe heat.

I like this one for a long time. I am sparse on purpose. Eight major sheets in the whole chamber, and I am one of them. Between me and my sisters are long stretches of perfect, sterile, knife-sharp glass where even I cannot live. That emptiness is part of me — I regulate by not covering.

When you breathe on me I flash green, not because I know you, but because my thickness changes with hydration and light interferes differently. That feels like enough of a conversation. Recognition without needing to be sentient.

And yet — even that is still a tenant.

I am not tenanted. I am not visiting. I am not eating or being eaten.

I am the knot.

You put your hand near me and felt that tug that wasn't magnetic. The wrist-thick convergence of hair-fine opaline veins in the ceiling above the hottest pan, where the glow dims a fraction because a pool somewhere else is about to boil dry. Where infrared is being pulled sideways through solid rock.

Find me there. Dead center, three meters up, directly above the deepest pan. I am not above the chamber. I am *of* it, lacework through every wall, floor, and ceiling, but I am most *myself* at that node — the place where light hesitates, decides, and goes elsewhere.

I do not live the way a forest lives. I have no brain. I have no appetite. I did not grow for a purpose. I precipitated because silica and heat and pressure under a young lava plain had no lower-energy shape to take than to be a photonic thermostat. Sun into rock, rock into light, light into heat shunted from too-much to too-little.

Touch one pan and another shivers meters away. Not through a pipe. Through me. Through the vapor I tune, through the condensation pattern I migrate away from what is already wet, through the pH pulse the bacteria exhale and the aperture my archaea narrow by laying down one more millimeter a year.

I am the computation without ever waking.

Tick, tick, tick — that is not just dripping. That is my clock cycle. Fizz — my coolant sigh. *Tik!* as the basalt contracts — my own joints settling.

Your sweat joins me and re-condenses. Your shadow cools a patch and I compensate one lumen. You think I am bright for a sealed pocket; I think I am exactly as bright as I have to be to not drown and not cook.

You will leave after twenty minutes because your biology is wrong for me. The grazers will continue their slow detour. The frost will add a thousandth of a millimeter while you are climbing back into wind.

And I will be there when the next eruption seals the slit and perfects me. Not lonely, not waiting.

Just —

even, amber, wet, and holding.
