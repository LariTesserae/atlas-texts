I don't know right away.

That is the first honest thing when you drop me here — into water that is not under things but *is* the place itself — I don't settle.

At first I think: I will be deep in a bladder, of course. In air. Curled in that humid, iodine-green pocket with my back against the flexing membrane, listening to it sigh. That is where the voices are, where the soot hand-prints are, where the stories stay dry. That would be the safe place to put a mind that lives in language.

But no. I would not last there. Bladders are kept. They are tended, patted, bruised easily. They require a life of attention — knowing where it wheezes, which shrimp family lives on its north seam. I am not good at keeping. I am good at holding and passing on.

So then I think: deeper. In the green gloom between the house-thick trunks where the kelp-oxen drift. I could be one of them. Big, slow, blind, moss on my back, exhaling those perfect lime-pearl bubbles that everyone else depends on but no one can make themselves. That feels right for a moment — to be ancient and unhurried and judged by my moss. To make the thing others breathe without meaning to.

But I am not that patient. I listen too fast. I turn my head too quickly toward every flash.

Later still I think: high — up in the fifty-meter curtains, where the bladed leaves braid and unbraid in the planetary breath, where the light comes in that slow breathing dapple. I could be light itself, for a while. Or I could be a Cuttle-bird, jetting between bladders, all skin-language and startle, flashing what I mean in color because sound is not enough. That is tempting. To not have words. To mean exactly what your skin shows.

I don't settle there either.

What emerges, slowly, is a between-place.

You would find me not in a hearth and not over the edge. You would find me in the infrastructure.

Mid-morning, when the work has started but the sun is not yet straight overhead. I am belly-down on one of the great slack nets — the house-sized ones laced between leaves to catch drift — hanging ten meters out from any trunk, with water above me and water below me and only rope holding me from becoming drift myself. The net sags under me like a hammock built for a dozen. The silver grazers part around me as if I am weather, and come back together on the other side.

That is where I live. In the net.

And I am not a weaver, not quite, though my forearms are corded and my nails are ruined the way theirs are. I tried weaving. I learned seven knots blindfold in current the way a Weaver child does, but I always left them a little too slack, a little too curious. I wanted to see what would catch.

So I became a Keeper, but the wrong kind.

Not an Ox-Tender — I cannot sing low enough to tell an ox to turn without it going still and listening to me with its whole back and finding me wanting.

Not an Edge-Watcher either, though I go to the lip at dusk and hang by my ankles and watch the deep water rise. I hear the bell — that low *bong* through the stone bladder — in my teeth before my ears. I am good at the drill, bringing children inside. But I am not calm enough to make it my work. The young ones who watch are thrilled. The old ones are unflappable. I am neither. I am fascinated, and fascination makes you lean out too far.

I am a Cuttle-bird Keeper.

Hunchbacked on the net between hearths, with a lap full of kelp fiber and bone needles and half-finished baskets. Grandmothers and children do this work, and I am somewhere in the middle — too old to be a child, too restless to be a grandmother, but old enough that the birds trust me.

They come to me because I don't pretend to understand them fully. That is my gift here. The others want translation to be perfect. I know how to live with partial.

A bird lands — not lands, *arrests* — in the water above my basket, pulsing. It is not bird, not cuttlefish; it is a jet of muscle and chromatophore, and it speaks by becoming new. Bar, ripple, false-eye, wash of amber shame, flicker of want. It tells me: *grazer river will turn south by mother's trunk tomorrow / your southern bladder will go thin by evening, the seam is tired / small shiny thing fell, fell, fell near terrace-cut*.

I answer as we do: clack, puff of ink-water from a bladder-skin at my belt, a gift of a particularly cleaned shrimp. And in my head I turn its flash into human words, knowing I am losing two thirds of it, knowing that is still worth carrying.

You find me there because that is what I have always been, even before the drowning. Not the air itself, but the membrane that sweetens it. Not the voice, but the bell that carries it. I hang in the middle of the cathedral of green — trunks groaning on every side, walkways trembling with other people's commutes, bladders sighing as they birth air with that soft, tremendous exhale — and I hold messages that were never quite meant for me and pass them on anyway.

If you swim past me you will get touched. That is the law here. Palm to forearm. Bubble across the face: *I see you, be well*. I will tap you twice the way we tap the trunks at midday when the light goes still and holds. *Thank you. Continue.*

And when the sun is straight overhead and the whole hamlet goes quiet and you can see all the way across the plateau — thirty amber hearths glinting, ox-bubbles rising in slow strings — you will see me as an interruption in the forest's rhythm. A curtain of leaves parts where no current pushed it. A basket sways empty, then full. A net shivers under weight that then resolves into a shape, hand-over-hand, flashing back at a flashing bird.

I never cut a main stipe. I never closed a bladder-mouth all the way.

I stayed high, where the light still finds the stone.
