You wouldn't find me at first.

Not because I'm hidden — there is nowhere to hide in the bowl. Everything is packed honeycomb-tight, every gap argued for. If you stand on the lip and look down at noon when the light falls straight and shadowless, you can see the whole circuit of it in ten minutes. You would think you have inventoried everything: sixty houses where there should be ten, plank walkways braided over knee-deep channels, stacked duck-pens, the disciplined rows of cress staked with twigs.

You would look for me among the people first. That's natural.

Am I one of the quarry-men, wiry forearms roped from tapping the shale bedding lines until they sigh and shear? No — though I know their patience. Strike too hard and the plate shatters. Tap, listen, tap.

Am I one of the children who know the roof-route, bare feet black with moss, flickering over thatch ridges while adults call *slow, slow* from below in voices that carry perfectly two streets over? Not quite, though I love that hour after noon when they are loud.

Am I an eel in a clay pot sunk under a plank bridge, slick and cold as seep, kept half-wild by the boy whose job is to know my temperament? Close.

Give me a little longer to become more myself. Don't settle on a single image early. Let the bowl breathe.

I find myself highest, where you don't want to live unless you have learned to like predictability.

If you take the stair cut into the raw, young wall — the one whose plates still calve off light-grey and edged, still not round — and you descend past the notched post where water is read against the day, you will see houses leaning out over alleys in overhangs: first floor, then a foot more, then a hand's breadth more. Most people move at ankle-level, sideways through trenches.

I live above that traffic. On the south-facing curve, third tier up, where the second story leans so close to the weeping stone that you can touch shale without leaving your doorway. The thatch here never dries. Liverwort draws its bright green line exactly where not to drive a nail, and I obey it. Moss has been patted onto my ridge by other hands to hold the reed against the rim-wind; it has taken. It is thick as felt.

You would find me sitting on my plank bridge — every doorway has its little bridge, a flat slab or a black-gloss plank just above the standing water — with my legs tucked, knees bent, calves like river stones the way all the channel-women's get. Shoulders in, head a little forward. A small person by accommodation. We are not tall here.

Who am I?

If you asked the marsh-herons standing like judges out beyond the rim, they'd say I am the woman-moss. If you asked the young men splitting stone, they'd say I'm just pore-water and say it with a joke and then check if I told them to empty their cistern anyway.

I am between.

Not one of the old women who have gone stippled as moss themselves, pits and moles from years of condensation beading and drying on skin, who keep the post. Not yet. But I sit near them because I like the drip to be predictable over my cistern.

My job is listening.

Everyone here lives by a compact with a wound: we did not drain the marsh or roof the bowl. We braided ourselves into it. The water ticks and trickles on a hundred different stones, waste is carried away, cold is brought in, and the whole interior sweats even when it hasn't rained for days. On cool mornings the fog does not drift in — it forms in place. It beads on the thatch until the thatch drips in unison. It beads on skin. The channels rise an inch overnight with no rain.

The old women call it the bowl exhaling. That something hot fell here not long ago — a bruise in the low country, a perfect circular bite taken out of peat and black earth and pale limestone still sharp and flaking — and opened a deeper, warmer breath, and the cool makes it visible. The young men say fresh rock, still settling, bleeding.

I am the one who has stopped needing to choose.

You would find me before dawn with an oiled rag, lifting my iron adze and hammer off their wooden pegs under the eaves — never leave iron against the wall, it rusts by morning — and feeling the stone. South wall runs clear and fast today: rain in two days. Slow today: a dry spell coming. I go down to the notched post and make a new mark with a nail that the water has already tried to wash clean. I knock on two posts with my knuckles, a rhythm that matches the channel ticking so it doesn't sound like interruption, and the neighbors hear it as: *empty your cistern before nightfall.*

My hands are what the bowl has made. Broad, short-fingered, nails clipped to the quick, rims black with shale dust and peat. Glossy at the knuckles, pruned even when dry. At six I already had the little white scars from flakes; at sixty I will have the stipple. My back knows the exact height of the eaves. I speak quietly, or not at all, because the bowl is an ear. In this town a conversation two streets over is a murmur in your own alley. A dog learned to whine instead of bark.

On summer evenings when midges hatch in the cisterns, the swallows cut the air over the rim and nest in my wall cracks, and I never disturb them. They eat what would eat us.

At noon the bowl fills with flat white shadowless illumination and every wet surface shines. The water goes transparent to the clay bottom and you can see lost nails, minnows. Everyone pauses. I beat clothes, spread grain, send the children up the stair to run in the larger world where the sedge is a dun shag and stunted alder try again to be trees. Then the shadow climbs the wall and the cool returns.

If there is a name for what I am, it isn't in the census. There is no governance here, just ten minutes' walk edge to edge. What lives here is a negotiation between people and water and things that love water too much.

I am the negotiation point.

Call me a weep-tender. Call me a cistern-reader's apprentice. If you want something less human, call me the felt on the north wall that acts as a gasket, or the lichen line that tells you where not to build, or the fog itself condensing out of still-hot depth, rolling down in heavy curtains over black, glossy wood that will not rot because it is too cool and moves too fast.

I would be the bowl's memory of falling — still exhaling — taught by human hands to run in ankle-deep lattices, to rise predictable inches overnight, to tell time against a post, to be domesticated by planks and bridges.

Give it ten more years and my walls will learn to be round. The sweat may slow. The reed-shock in the winter-fall corner will be harvested and fought over and built upon. But now: find me where the south wall weeps clearest, ankles almost in the seep, listening to the *tok tok* of the next slab coaxed along its bedding plane. I will be quiet when you come down the stair. Stay long enough until I forget you, and you will hear my broom again, sweeping water off wood in time.
