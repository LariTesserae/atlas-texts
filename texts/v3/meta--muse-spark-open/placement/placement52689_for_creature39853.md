I wouldn't be at the lip.

That's where you think you'd be — standing on the white steppe with normal pale daylight at your back, looking down into the cut and thinking, *oh, a city down there.* A visitor's position. A position for eyes.

I tried to find myself there and I couldn't stay.

The wind is wrong up there. It scrapes. It has nothing to catch on. I felt myself drying out in about three breaths — lips cracking, the thought behind my eyes getting too loud. Nothing up there persuades anything to stay. So I let the edge go.

I sank.

---

Find me three hours after dawn, when the wall-tops are still feathered with frost and the bottom hasn't decided what color it is yet.

Find me ankle-deep and then knee-deep.

I am in the western quarter where the rift wall leans out a little, enough to make a shade that lasts until noon. That's where the hanging gardens don't do as well and so that's where we put the graft-sick — the houses that need more tending than they give back in berries.

I am not young. I am not rooted yet.

I am the woman whose house is learning how to be a tree again and has to be talked out of it every week.

My bole is a black spruce we call Grandmother Bent, though she is not my grandmother. She is maybe two hundred years old, five feet through at the base, and her hollow is the exact size of my family when we all lie down. For twelve years I've slept with my back against her resin wall. The resin keeps the seep out but never quite hard enough — if you press your palm there all night you'll leave a print that slowly fills in by morning. My children have measured themselves that way. The wall behind the sleeping loft has their hands in it, overlapping, getting shallower each year as the tree grows around them.

This morning I woke to the drip. Always the drip. Not from above — from the root-curtain door itself, which sweats all night and lets loose at first light in a cold line down the back of the neck to tell you it's time to wade.

So I waded.

Outside, the channel between Grandmother Bent and the next bole — which is really just the same system of pleached larch roots grown into a second bulge — is running a little higher than yesterday because someone upstream is pruning. The water is 2°C and it doesn't shock anymore. My feet find bottom without looking. They are wide as plates, splayed, the membrane between my second and third toe torn again from yesterday's sink-hole, healing white. The cold sits around my ankles like a second skin and my barrel chest does its work without me asking. I breathe shallow here; the air is too wet for deep breaths.

I am wearing what everyone wears when we work: nothing that we didn't grow. Horsetail leggings, still a little green at the seams where they are alive. A vest of musk-ox wool felted by my mother and beaten with resin until it shines like a beetle's back. Over it, my cape — sheet-moss, alive, stitched with cotton-grass thread that has rooted into it. I trimmed it three days ago and already it hangs too long and drips into my eyes. I will trim it again tonight with the shale flake I keep behind my ear. Not because I want to look tidy. Because if I don't, it will try to attach to the house while I'm sleeping.

In my left hand, the knife. Not metal. A flat of black shale, sharp only on one edge, wrapped at the butt with wet sinew so it doesn't slip. In my right, an armful of willow withes, cut from the wall-ledge two families over where Joren's boy grows them too fast and trades them for fish. They are still leafed, still trying to be a thicket.

My arch is failing.

We have an arch between Grandmother Bent and the larch tangle that makes our shared pantry — a graft made by my mother's mother, three trunks pulled together and forced to kiss until they grew into one doorway. It should be dryish underneath. Yesterday I saw the punk show — the wood gone soft, weeping darker than the rock weeps, smelling sweet and bad like old peat. If I leave it, by spring it will seal over with suckers and there will be no doorway, just tree. That is what the rift wants. No empty space for long.

So I do the only thing there is to do here. I don't build. I persuade.

I wade to the arch, thigh-deep now where the mat dips, and the white fish slide under my knees and don't mind me. I score the bark below the rot, just enough to make the cambium bleed clear. The tree shivers — you can feel it in the water, not see it. I tuck the butt of each withe into the wet cut, one after another, and tie them with root-strip, tight as a pulse point. By autumn they will take. By winter they will hold snow off the pantry. By next spring, when my youngest is tall enough to carry withes herself, they will *be* the arch. I will have to cut away the dead part and let it sink to become floor.

That's the work. That's all the work. Nothing taken outside. Nothing made to last.

While I tie, I hear the rest of the quarter waking up without getting louder.

A soft *tok-tok* through wood and water — Mira calling her twins in from the midge nets. A low hum from old Hessa two boles down, who has mostly rooted now and sings to call fish to her grandchildren's hands. The hush is so complete I can hear a ptarmigan turn over in its moss-nest twenty feet away. I can hear the musk ox coming long before I see him, because the water parts differently around his great splayed hooves.

He comes down the ox-avenue — the one the oxen have kept open for a hundred years just by walking it — his soaked coat in dreadlocks, his horns crusted with lichen. He rubs his forehead against my failing arch as he passes, not because I asked him to, but because arches itch. He takes a mouthful of the dead punk and chews it solemnly. He prunes me. I put my wet, enormous, nicked hand on his wet, enormous nose for a second and we exchange heat. My children will ride him later and pick the parasites from behind his ears and whisper to him where the deepest sink-holes are.

Above, the walls still weep. Every thread of water catching what little light there is. My hanging garden, four ledges up on willow that my grandmother hammered into a crack, is dripping onto my cape and my cape is dripping onto the channel and the channel is dripping into the mat, fifteen feet deep and half people by now.

At noon the sun will come straight down for an hour and turn all that thread to glitter and all that mist to gold, and we will all look up at once and count our hawks.

Until then I stand in cold water that never freezes, feeding a doorway with living sticks so tomorrow there will still be a doorway.

I am not hidden. I am not special. I am saturated, like everything else down here — one more gap that learned to do work so the wound could stay green and not close.

If you come to the lip and think it is empty, wait. Watch the reed that moves against the current.

That will be me.
