I think at first I would have chosen wrong.

I would have said the crown. Far up where the wind is a knife and the aurora is close enough to be a ceiling. I would have said I wanted to be one of the gliders, the Rippers, with the long ribs and the dish ears, because to live here is to cross the desert and they are the only ones who do it without faith. To launch off a kilometer of living wood and ride the thin, cold nothing for seven kilometers on a single *chit* — that felt like freedom.

But I sat with it longer, and it wasn't true. I don't want to hunt in the dark. I don't want to be alone in the air that long.

Then I thought — the basalt. To be the young rock itself. Nine kilometers down to the clouds, still sharp enough to cut rope, still ringing when it gets cold enough. To be the thing that is not alive and has to learn to be soil. To wait while the rosettes find me, one by one, with their waxy black-green hands. That has a kind of holiness to it. But I'm not patient enough to be stone. I am too warm. I ferment.

No. If you left me here long enough for my breath to learn the hitch — that second catch all high people have before they exhale — I know where I'd be.

Not between towers. Never between. That emptiness is so complete from above it looks like nothing. From inside it, at foot level, it's worse: wind-scoured black glass, shattered talus that will take your ankle and not give it back, ice that booms and detonates under your feet. Nothing lingers there. To fall to the rock is to die in minutes. So no, not there.

I'd be inside.

Mid-shaft. Not the warm, rotting bottom where the Tumblers ricochet — blind, dog-heavy, springing wall to wall with their soft thumps — and not the high crown where the bats that aren't bats hang and sing too high to hear. I'd be in the middle damp, where the air hangs like a second atmosphere, tannin-smelling, condensation on everything. Where the light is not sun but fungus — that tired amber-gold in the seams where water runs, brightening when the cistern sighs, dimming when it dries, so the whole tower pulses like a slow lung.

I'd be in Tower Keth, or whatever we had named it. The one leaning slightly into the jet.

And I would be one of the Singers. Not the mythic kind. Not the child in the wing-suit learning to glide from hum to hum by ear. One of the ordinary ones. The tenders.

I am the auntie, I think. Thirty-seven, which here is old enough that my cheeks are always dusky red and my eyes have the black rings from living in amber gloom. Barrel-chested, blood thick as oil, hands gone soft and creased from tannin water. The one who knows the bladders.

There are four of them in our hollow, slung between struts thicker than old oaks. Translucent, amber-brown, taut. You hear them before you see them — ticking and sighing as they swell with the day's melt. The water inside is tepid, alive with blind shrimp. It's the only standing water for a thousand kilometers and it wants very badly to burst. I've seen what happens when one goes: a rust-colored tide mark ten meters down the living wood, a season of rot, the whole family having to move galleries while the Tower heals around the scar.

So I tend.

Mornings I climb the elastic walkways of branch — not built, grown; coaxed with years of piss and patience — with my bone tap and my wax. I lance the fullest bladder just enough to draw. The water comes out steaming faintly in the cold interior, though it's only just warm. I cork it again and watch the skin pucker. I carve little runnels with my flake of basalt glass into the vine to lead a weep of sap where a new strut is trying to come in too thin. The sap is clear and sweet, freezing into beads that taste like winter apple and ferment in your gut. The children follow me and collect them in leaf-leather pouches for the fire.

And I sing.

That's the work. Not the water, not really. The singing.

Because the Tower doesn't grow toward light. There's almost no light to grow toward here — polar night for months, then a blue rumor at the horizon that never climbs. It grows toward sound. Toward vibration. Where the wind howls loudest through a gap, it thickens. Where we drum, where we thump, where we make a steady noise, it reaches. It purses toward us and grows a shelf, a lip, a windbreak, an acoustic horn to better catch what we're making. If we go silent, it thins. It hollows further. It opens its throat to the wind until the wind plays it like an organ at such amplitude that its own struts shiver apart.

A silent Tower collapses singing. I've heard one go, two valleys over. A low organ howl that rose until it was in my molars, then a creak like gunfire as the living wood gave up.

So I don't stop.

While the children beat moth-silk drums and the moths themselves — a meter across, silent, translucent — spiral by the thousands on their own second wind, I do the maintenance drone. It's low, throat-sung, the specific resonance for Keth that my mother gave me and I will give wrong to my niece until she gets it right. It is not beautiful. Beauty accreted around it later. It is a utility, like tying off a bladder.

On storm nights, when the tower sways ten meters at the top and flings snow from its crown like spray from a ship, we all tie ourselves to the branch-floor with cured vine and sing together. Each occupied Tower humming to itself across the black rock, a chord across the continent. You can feel the fermentation fizz through the wall at your back if you lean into it — that slow brewery thrum keeping the sap liquid at fifty below — and you know your little warmth, your damp breath, has been folded in.

I have a face to carve next week, where two root-trunks meet and force that hidden trickle of true river. *She who found water when the last bladder thinned.* It wasn't me. It was my sister's daughter. The sap will bleed dark around the cut and heal puckered. In a century it will be swallowed.

Outside, on the sharp young basalt that has not yet learned to be soil, the next rosette is already spreading its waxy leaves toward us. Toward the sound I didn't realize I was making.

I'm not the glider. I'm not the stone.

I'm the small, warm noise that keeps the house from forgetting us.
