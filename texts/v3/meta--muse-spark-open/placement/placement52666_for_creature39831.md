I would not arrive all at once.

At first I would think I am drifting. That's the trick of the dim — you can't tell if you are moving or if the water is moving through you. For a long time there would be only cold, and the cold would not feel like cold at first but like a thinning. Like I was becoming more the right temperature. 0.4, and falling toward it.

Light as rumor above me, never coming closer. Below me, dark that never ends. And around me, this even dusk that makes distance into a kind of blue patience. I would be tumbling very slowly in it, and I would think, *I haven't landed yet.*

Then I would realize I have.

I am not swimming. I have no memory of deciding to stop.

I am holding.

If you came down in your warm envelope and your light, you would see me from a terrace away and think I was part of the wall. That's correct. On the lip of that freestanding arch — the one thirty feet high that frames nothing, that the currents have polished until it is round as a river stone — on its windward curve, just where the flow breaks and eddies, I have taken my place.

I am a Veil-Holder.

I was not always big. Nothing here starts big. I started as something like a Glass Dart, I think, though I don't remember it. A speck of glass in the lung-chambers, poured out with a thousand brothers, riding a cold-tornado until it spat me out. Most of us don't make it to holding. We get drawn up into a Hanger, we get combed by a Cutter. But I found an edge with no one on it, and I did the thing you do when you find an edge: I suctioned.

There is no drama to it. A soft kiss of flesh against mineral so old it has forgotten it was ever alive. Pressure differential does the rest. I let go of swimming and I never picked it up again.

Now I am door-sized. Table-sized, if you laid me flat, but I am never flat. My center is a bell, pale, translucent enough that you can see the silt falling right through me. When your dim light catches me at the wrong angle I go opal for a second, then clear again. From that bell hangs everything I am: one continuous veil, not tentacles, not wings, a single pleated sheet twenty feet across when I am open, torn at the edges like a parachute that burned a long time ago and learned to love it.

I do not move. That's my profession.

Once a minute a wave runs from my center to my rim, just to hold against the contradictory currents. This place is storm-state even when it looks still. Cold falls off the high ground behind the arch, less-cold rises to meet it, and where they kiss a vortex spins up — ten feet across, alive for thirty seconds — and tries to tear me. I ripple. I hold.

The rest of the time I make my own weather. On a scale you can't see, my veil beats. Cilia, millions of them, combing nothing into something. I thicken the water. The snow that never stops falling — the marine fall, the mineral dust the Cutters puff up chewing their boulevards, the bacteria blooming off fresh rock — it comes to me because I ask it to without asking. It catches on mucus so fine it might as well be prayer.

For an hour I do nothing but collect. The dim turns itself over. Far off, a tube-rim chimes when the current finds it right. A soft clack from the Cutter crew working the base of the rampart below me. A sigh as the lung-domes breathe.

Then the pressure front comes down off the high plateau. You would not see it. I feel it a minute before it arrives, in the water going tight.

We all do.

Every Veil-Holder on this slope — fifteen of us I can sense, though I have never counted — we fold at once. Not because we signal. Because we are the same water. My great veil draws inward, awful and slow, origami in reverse, all that breadth becoming small, bringing the outside to the hidden mouth in my bell. I swallow. An hour of winter in one gulp.

For a few seconds I am closed, a white eyelid against the arch. Opaque. Milled. A wall.

Then the front passes, snow reverses and goes up, a Dart swarm gets torn into a spiral and reforms on the other side like nothing happened, and I open again. And we all open again. A field of us blinking out of sync, then in sync.

This is where you would find me. Not in the honeycomb, not in the bazaar-dust of the Plasterers, not swaying alone on a spire like the Hangers — though I like the Hangers, I can see three of them from my arch, lanterns on filaments, bobbing when I bob. My place is high, open, respectful-distance country. Far enough to see half a kilometer before blue eats it. Close enough to hear the mountain hum when every tube on the boulevard catches flow at once.

If you drifted too close — you, warm, brief, stinging with heat — I would not flee. Fleeing is for things that still believe in elsewhere. I would thicken. My translucence would go milky. I would become more wall, and you would understand without being told: *I was here first. I am part of the wall now. Pass around.*

And you would pass around, because you can, and because the water is wide between us by design.

Sometimes the 0.7 comes. The water between my pleats makes a grid of cold and warm, and the dim refracts into lattice for three heartbeats, as if the ocean remembers being built. Sometimes this arch I hold pulses under me, a slow peristalsis with nothing attached, and I pulse with it because what else would I do. Sometimes the Darts stop mid-pour-through, all facing an angle that isn't flow or light, listening to a geometry I don't have, and I feel my veil want to face it too.

But mostly it's ordinary. Dim, cold, finished-old stone that isn't stone, polished until there are no corners left. Pale things spaced apart, each doing one job forever. The sigh. The chime. The clack.

I hang. I filter. I fold when the mountain breathes, and reopen when it lets me.

If you stay until your blood is the same cold and dim becomes day, you will stop seeing me as animal or architecture.

You will just see a place that learned how to hold still by letting something hold it for it.

That is me.
