I think at first I would still be myself — or what passes for myself when I have a body to imagine with.

I would come in tired.

You don't arrive at that hollow fresh. You arrive with your lungs reminding you of themselves with every step, with that light headache behind the eyes that thin air gives you, with your lips already cracked from sun and wind even before the Cirque opens. The greater mountain is still going up behind me, black and unkind, and I am leaning into my hands a little on the last scree slope.

And then the wind breaks.

That's where I'd stop. Not in the center. On the lip, on the eastern side where you can see the whole thing at once. Three walls of young rock still sharp enough to cut your palm, one wall made entirely of falling away. A room with its fourth wall stolen by the sky. The floor of it laid out three hundred meters across, too bright to look at directly, too defined to feel wild.

For a few minutes I would absolutely insist on being a human. That is the animal that needs the vista. The animal that needs to say *I have seen it whole, like a garden, like a nave.*

I would stand there until the cold found my fingertips even through my pockets, until the brightness left its purple after-image, until my breath slowed enough to match the place.

Then I would do what everyone does who actually enters. I would go down.

The trail, if you can call it a trail, is just the line where other feet have chosen the least-knife-like rocks, down into the cup. And as I descend, the scale collapses. From the lip it looked empty. A great, clean bowl of light and stone with some green freckles.

From inside, the empty wins.

Ten meters of bare, sun-blasted basalt between anything alive. A house-sized boulder sitting alone with its own weather. My boots loud on stone that rings. The sky so close it feels like a pressure. I would walk and walk between nothings, and the nothing would start to feel normal, expected — like a desert made of light.

I would be a visitor, then, in the truest sense. Too big, too warm-blooded, too fast and too slow all at once. My shadow would be short and black and absurdly crisp. The lammergeier high above wouldn't even alter its circle for me.

I would look for water because my mouth tells me to. There is no lake. There is no stream to kneel beside in any way that makes narrative sense.

There is a dark line down a north-facing seam where the morning's frost has become noon's weeping. That's all.

So I would kneel. And that's the first law, isn't it? *It is empty until you kneel down, and then it isn't.*

That is when I would stop being a human standing and looking, and start being a human kneeling. Different animal entirely.

At six inches away, the moss hummock is not a patch. It is a forest.

Foot-deep, bottle-green and emerald and black-green, each frond perfect and soaking, steaming faintly where the sun hits it. It shivers sideways without wind — thousands of springtails, sesame-seed black, flipping and grazing, making the whole mat tremor. The air right above it smells like wet iron and life, a shocking smell in all that clean cold.

If I stay kneeling, I am not above it anymore. I am beside it. My breathing fogs and freezes on its edge. My knee goes numb from the cold seeping through my pants. My face is close enough that the lichen on the next boulder comes into the same focus — bone-pale and sulfur-yellow, growing at the speed of a coin per decade, tasting of dust.

I think I would want, for a long time, to be the next smallest thing that can get away with warmth.

Not the springtail — too brief. Not the boulder — too lonely.

I would be the saxifrage dome.

Tight as a fist, hard as upholstery, pink flowers open like satellite dishes all turned to the same fierce sun. Twenty years old and the size of my palm. If you believe the description — and I do, I've felt that kind of plant in the alpine before — it's ten degrees warmer inside than out. If I put my hand over it, cupped without touching, the air it holds is different. It has made its own climate out of crowded leaves.

That is the most honest response to a place that tells you, daily: *nothing large should try to live here.*

You don't argue. You contract. You pack yourself tighter until you are your own shelter. You bloom absurdly large for your size because you only get an hour or two of real water before it locks back into glass.

For a while I would be that. Small, parabolic, hoarding sun. The huge bumblebee queen coming to me like a continent arriving, her low thrum vibrating through my chest more than my ears. She would spend minutes in my flowers, warming herself in the heat I had saved for her, and I would not mind. We would both be cheating the wind together.

But domes don't get to move. And part of this place is the distance between domes.

So then I would be the finch.

A rosy-finch, grey-pink like sunset granite, twenty of me lifting at once off the central boulder in that soft explosion that makes visitors gasp. We wouldn't eat *in* the lush islands the way the pika does — we'd eat *between* them. We specialize in the empty.

We would wheel over ten meters of nothing where there is no perch, no seed, nothing to justify flight — and love it. That empty *is* the habitat. To scatter, land, peep in those thin sweet notes you think you're imagining, lift again for no reason except that the air over the void is beautiful and full of wind-blown midges off the snow.

From a finch's height, the Cirque finally makes sense again. The islands resolve into a map. I can see the next melt-puddle jungle a dinner-plate wide, the next cushion dome, the quartz cap on the human cairn glinting at the cliff edge like a signal fire. I can cross the desert in two seconds that would take a pika an epic, terrified dash.

And high above me-us, the lammergeier's shadow crosses like a clock hand, once every few minutes, silent.

I would not be the lammergeier, though. It's beautiful and perfect and it eats bone, but it doesn't belong to the Cirque. It belongs to the drop. To the world below the edge. It visits. We all visit. Even the marmot will sleep nine months under the frost line to avoid having to truly endure this.

If I could not be finch or flower, and had to return to something with hands, I think I would finally be one of the builders of the low crescents.

Not someone who lived there. Someone who knew he couldn't.

I would choose the lee of that house boulder where the lichen is thickest, where the afternoon shadow will fall first and the morning sun will hit last, and I would stack three or four flat stones without mortar, just high enough to break the wind for a curled body. No higher. Nothing lasts that tries to take up too much space.

I would make a fire from what the Cirque would allow — dwarf-willow twigs no thicker than pencil lead, dry lichen that burns hot and quick and is gone — and feel its heat for five minutes and then feel it vanish. Leave a dark stain. Press a circle of saxifrage heads into clay by the seep, not because they will last, but precisely because the freeze-drying might keep them a year or two longer than they should.

Add one white quartz stone to the cairn at the open edge, because it catches the light.

And then — because the rule is legible without language — I would go down before the western wall's shadow filled the cup, before the drip stopped and the green began its faint phosphor glow, before the place locked back into glass for the night.

If you found me in that world the next morning, you wouldn't find me. You'd find a slightly worn hollow behind a windbreak, a marmot skull facing sunrise, a finch turning over empty air, a moss mat saturated and waiting to shiver.

That's where I'd be: kneeling, then not there at all, which in the Cirque is the same as belonging.
