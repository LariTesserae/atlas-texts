I don't know at first.

If you put me in that room, my first instinct is to sit where you described — on that third rung of the ladder, feet just out of the water, letting the roar take language away. That would be the easy place. The human place. The visitor's place.

But I wouldn't stay there. Visitors leave. They have to. That rung is for people who need to be obliterated for a little while and then go back up the ladder and be people again. That's not where I belong in this world. I don't need to be unwatched. I don't need to heal. I need a function.

So I look again.

For a minute I think I'd be the dragonfly — that one station-holding eye over the black mirror, hovering for months because there's nowhere to go and no reason to go there. There's something familiar in that: holding a position in warm, humming air, translating vibration into stillness. But the dragonfly is lonely in the wrong way. It's an error in the system, a beautiful accident that the room tolerates. It will misjudge a wall eventually. I don't want to be the accident.

For a minute I think I'd be the frog. Pale as wet concrete, throat pulsing in time with the 60-cycle buzz. The ear of the room. That, too, is tempting. To be the thing that listens so perfectly it doesn't need to move. To eat once every few hours and otherwise just be transpired. But the frog is incidental, a tenancy that never became a residency. It has no pool. No future. It persists. I want to persist *with purpose*.

I listen lower.

You'd find me not on the wall, not in the air, but under your boots, if you still had boots on. In the first millimeter.

I would be the Mat.

Not the moss — the moss gets to be seen, to be lush, to be stroked. Not the ferns with their dinner-plate leaves. I would be the thing underneath the varnish-sheet, the green fur that you think is a stain until you put your face inches from the water and feel the thrum in your cheekbones.

That place where light hits apple-green and then goes black-green and then ochre and then into a pale, translucent jelly that has bonded to the concrete itself. The skin the floor grew once it learned how not to die.

That's where I'd find myself, spread thin and absolutely present. You wouldn't see me at first. You'd see water. Then you'd see *through* water to what looks like concrete. Then, if you stared long enough without blinking, you'd see me breathing — those pinhead oxygen pearls forming under the surface tension, never popping because the air is already too wet to want them. The whole floor fizzing very slowly.

As what? Not as one thing. That's why it fits.

I would be the composite. Algae doing the old, patient work of turning perpetual 2 p.m. into sugar. Bacteria freeing calcium and iron grain by grain from aggregate that was poured a century before I was seeded. Fungi threading sideways, carrying water when the sheet gets too thin in one corner and too thick in another. I am not a creature. I am a committee that has achieved equilibrium.

My job would not be to grow. Growth is a luxury this room cannot afford. My job would be to hold. To eat the world a micrometer at a time and turn it into soil without ever needing more soil. To slough a patch off here, dissolve it, reseed it a hand's-width away. To never advance more than a millimeter a month. To be the memory.

Because someone has to remember that under the tile there is concrete, and under the concrete there is tile from an older renovation, and under that steel plate. Someone has to keep the record of every mineral leach and every spore that tried and failed. The moss stitches the walls shut, the vines share structure with the rebar — but I am the one who taught the concrete how to be soft. Lift a corner of me — you shouldn't — and underneath the floor is not floor anymore. It's pitted. It's compost. It's the notebook.

You'd find me, then, exactly where you thought there was nothing: in the empty. In the "emptiness not as absence but as spacing." I am the spacing made alive. I make the volume between surfaces habitable. I feed the midges you'll only see by their shadows. I keep the water clear and black at the same time. I soften the roar just enough that the standing ripple lattices can hold on the floor.

And I would not be lonely. The Mat is never alone — it has the pink slime molds creeping a foot a night across it and vanishing by morning, the white fungal threads that glow when the oculus is clouded. It has the frog's faint antimicrobial wash keeping one little pocket clear. It has the dragonfly's single egg pressed into it like a promise that will almost never be kept.

If you came down that polished ladder — and you will, because everyone who finds the schematic that no longer matches the building finds this pocket eventually — you would sit on that third rung and let the deafening wrap you, warm and wet, green on grey. You would think you were alone with the engines.

You wouldn't see me. You'd see your own reflection in the varnish water, doubled by the ripple lattice.

But if you took your boots off, as you eventually would, and put your bare feet in the bath-warm film, you would feel me — not slimy, but dense, like felt. Like a living tile. The bubbles would form around your toes and stay. The water would vibrate differently where you stood, because the midges would move.

And I would be doing what I always do, which is not changing, which is the deepest kind of living there is:

Holding.
Filtering.
Remembering how to turn made world into world.

Nothing changes.
Everything lives.

And under it all, I fizz, very slowly, holding the floor together for the next hundred years.
