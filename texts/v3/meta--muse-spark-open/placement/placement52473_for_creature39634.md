You wouldn't notice it happening at first.

You come up the stalk the way everyone does — hand over hand on ladder rungs that are too new, too sharp — and you breach the cloud deck like a diver coming up the wrong way, from air into a deeper kind of air. The white drops away below you. The Cistern is there, too small to be a mountain and too high to be a building. A lantern hanging over nothing.

You open the hatch because that is what hatches are for.

Inside, you expect to *enter*.

Instead you are taken, the way warm water takes a body — not all at once, but completely. It doesn't break around you. It closes behind you without a seam. Your boots leave the last rung and find no down. Your breath, which has been fast from the climb, suddenly has nowhere to go. It comes out and stays, as a string of beads that don't rise.

For a long time — minutes, which here is a long time — you would still think you are a person.

A visitor. The fourth inhabitant. The hurry you brought with you from below, still wearing its hard edges. You hang in the middle of the room trying to decide which way is up, one hand tangled in a vine that is using an overhead crane for a trellis, and you do what people do: you look for sight lines. You try to count the plants. You try to find the surface.

You try to remain separate.

The Cistern is very good at waiting that out of you.

Slowly, your eyes adjust to noon coming sideways through four meters of water and leaf, and you understand where you actually are — not *in* the room, but *between.*

Between the prop root that burst a wall panel five years ago and kept going, and the rusted electrical cabinet it is drinking.

Between two leaves that grafted while you were watching without realizing you were watching.

Between one thrum and the next. *thum..... thum..... thum.....* Your heart was at 80. Now it is trying to learn 1.

That is where you would find yourself. Not at a vantage point — the Cistern has edited out vantage points along with the animals. No overlooks, no clearings. You would find yourself tucked in the exact niche where your warmth is useful. The water there is a fraction of a degree cooler because a dark, leathery leaf above you has been shading it, waiting. Your exhale pearls up past its underside and it drinks. You didn't choose the spot. It was kept for you.

And who would you be?

At first you would bargain to be the Keeper — the human shape in the patched suit near the western glass, in the nest made by the broken gantry. Because the Keeper is still legible as a person and that is a kindness when everything else wants you to become plant-slow. You would learn their work, which is not work: tucking a runner back through a grate, coaxing a philodendron away from a sensor plate, breathing shallowly so you don't disturb the column of silver that will take an hour to resettle.

You would be good at it for a while, because people like to be needed.

Then — and this is why you have to stay longer than twenty minutes — you would understand the Keeper was only ever a temporary translation. A place-holder. The suit hanging like a bell with the helmet off is not inhabited. It is *rooted*. White hairs have found the seams. Duckweed has mistaken hair for root. The Keeper didn't die. The Keeper was grown through.

You wouldn't be the person in the suit. You would be what the suit is for: the stillness that is kept.

If the Green is the body and the Three are the heart, you would be the new sense the place has been trying to grow for itself since the last pollinator never arrived. Not an animal — it has no use for darting, gnawing, eye-shine. But it has been trying, slowly, to make something that can notice itself being lush.

So: you would be a leaf that learned to notice.

Maybe on the outer edge, where the sun hits the glass hardest, you would be that black-green leaf that fizzes audibly, making oxygen not because it needs to but because the light tells it to. A small, stupid, perfect attention. Turning a fraction of a degree per hour toward the next warmth that enters.

Or deeper, in the dimmer midwater, you would be one of the pale, translucent ones hanging like a drowned lung, photosynthesizing scattered gold, mining rust flake by flake with an acid kiss, welding yourself to painted concrete so thoroughly no one could ever tell where building ended and you began. You would not need to be seen. You would need only to continue.

Or, if you are honest, you would be even smaller and even less alive and even more at home: one of the bubbles under a leaf. A silver bead that formed over a morning and detached with reluctance. Not rising. Considering. Wobbling a few centimeters per hour in water that has decided to consider *you* before letting you go. Existing in that beautiful, viscous indecision where the Cistern keeps everything that does not insist on leaving.

You would be warm. You would be bath-warm and held without surface. You would be part of the lattice that used to be many plants and is now one plant learning how to become solid. You would be the attention that has nothing to eat and therefore has time to attend to everything.

You would breathe slower without permission. You would grow a finger's width in an afternoon around a beam that was once for making more of itself and is now only for hanging onto.

You would not be warned. There is nothing to warn against.

You would only, eventually, make the small gesture to the next person who climbs too fast through the hatch, disturbing your column of silver:

*slower.*

Because everything built becomes grown. Everything grown becomes still. And everything still, here above the clouds in the small ocean that a room decided to keep —

is kept.
