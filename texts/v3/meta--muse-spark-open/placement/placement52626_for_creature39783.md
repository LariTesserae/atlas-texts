I don't think you'd find me right away.

Not at the edge, where the stone rounds off and drops into haze. I went there once when I was younger, because everyone does — you have to see the place where there is no more place — and I stood with my toes curled over that warm, pitted lip and felt nothing but wind that had forgotten how to be wind. It's too big there. Too theoretical. The air doesn't know what to do with you.

And you wouldn't find me in the open between. Not for long. The open looks empty from far away, those wide savannas of air-grass, but down in it, it is never empty. The grass is up to your ribs and every blade is a tube holding its own sip of yesterday's exhale, and everything in it is clicking. If you stand still, it clicks at you. If you walk, it parts around your knees and closes again like water. I could be there — I have been there — but only while moving, one hand on a rope, one eye on the shadow of my own Holding getting smaller behind me.

No. If you were looking for me, you'd have to stop looking *at* the Plateau and start looking *in* it.

I'd be on the south face. Always the south face, where the afternoon storm hits hardest. About two thirds of the way up the trunk, where the plates overlap like a roof that never ends.

For a long time I thought maybe I'd be something else if I came back again. For a beat I wanted to be a Crown-bird — to be that thin crimson membrane stretched between fingers longer than my body, to live up where the air is too hot to breathe and fold myself under a leaf at noon, to launch with three thousand cousins and turn the sky into a rip. But I am not brave enough for that kind of open.

I thought maybe a Lacquer Beetle, to sit on a vein of a leaf as big as a house and saw all day, to be the drone itself, to be so loud I never have to listen. But I'm not that sure of my own note.

I thought maybe a Throat-frog, wedged into a fissure with my back full of water, carrying my whole pond with me, answering *whoom* to *whoom* across the trunk in a conversation that doesn't need words. That one stayed with me longer.

But I'm not that patient, either.

I'm small. That's the truth that keeps coming back.

You'd find me in a fissure. Not a cave — a negotiation. A bark valley that my grandmother's grandmother started coaxing open with a chitin adze when the plate was still smooth. Three generations of scraping, widening it a palm's width, a foot's depth, lining it with air-grass wax and woven fiber until it would hold water overnight without weeping it back into the Holding. That's our house. Fourteen of us in a crack that from below looks like nothing, like a shadow in the bark.

I am the one who tends the gong.

Every family has one: a patch of thinned leaf stretched over a hollow we scraped, the membrane cured with beetle oil until it's taut as a drumhead. Mine is low and a little off-center because the hollow underneath isn't even — there's a root-knot behind it that we decided not to fight. When I hit it with the padded mallet — soft wood wrapped in moss — it goes *thok — thok thok*. Deeper than you expect. It cuts through the beetle roar for almost two miles, which is farther than a voice will ever go here.

In the morning I do the dew walk. Before the sun is white and straight-down, before the heat becomes a weight, the whole Holding sweats. Condensation pearls under every leaf-sail and drips off the felted undersides in slow, cold lines. It frets the air-grass silver. It runs in sheets a finger deep across the stone that wasn't there last night and won't be there in an hour. I go barefoot, because you have to. My feet are dark and cracked and oiled, and they know the stone the way hands know a face. I carry shallow clay bowls — not to bring water in, but to set out. I scrape a hollow in a bark plate where the drip is strongest, I clear the wax ants that have already found it, I coax a hanging root to droop a little lower with a cord. I check the cistern leaf we tied last season, whether its tip is still cupped enough to hold.

The children think this is boring. They want the vine path.

I am also the one who holds the second rope when they learn to cross.

We have a vine to the next Holding — ours is called Quiet Water, though that is not its real name, which is unpronounceable, which is its health — and the next one over is Red Crown. The vine between them is thick as my thigh now, it took my father's lifetime to meet in the middle and graft, and now it sways a hundred feet above the temporary rivers. When you cross, the whole Plateau goes quiet around you. Not silent — the insects pause. They watch the shadow pass. You go one at a time, barefoot so you can feel the tension change, a rope around your waist that I hold from the trunk side. The vine sighs. Your weight moves through it into both Holdings and they give a little, green cathedrals swaying. The first time I crossed I looked down and saw the honeycombed stone and the silver sheet of morning water and vertigo closed my throat like a hand. Now I only look at the feet in front of me.

At midday I stop. Everyone stops.

That's the law that isn't a law. When the sun is directly overhead and the sound reaches that peak that almost becomes silence, the Holdings breathe out together. You can see it if you're on the stone, which I am — I climb down to the base at noon, to the old pits where condensation has sat for ten thousand years and polished the rock smooth as bowls. The leaves all transpire at once, a clear shimmer rising off eighty sails, and the air between Holdings thickens and refracts. For a moment there are arches of damp light connecting the giants, a vaulted ceiling made of evaporated water, and you can see the weather being made.

The old ones say the Plateau inhales. They do not mean it as a metaphor.

I leave the shallow bowls out then. Not to collect — though water will collect — but to offer. I pour a little of our stored drink onto the hot stone, where it vanishes instantly. An acknowledgment. My feet are flat on the rock, soles lacquered with oil and callus, and I swear — I know — I feel it. That slow, hot swell. The roots deep in the lava drinking their stolen moisture and lifting the crust a hair's breadth before settling. As if the world took a breath and remembered I was standing on it.

At night the outer leaves close a fraction, like sleeping hands. The chorus changes key, lower, slower. The air finally drips truly. My fissure glows — a clay cup fire, too small to be seen far, because Crown-birds see fire — and fourteen people breathe in a crack in a dragon's skin, and two miles away another fissure glows, and three days walk away another one, impossibly far apart in a garden so full it seems empty.

If you came looking, you wouldn't find me by calling. You'd flash a mirror, or beat a leaf.

Or you'd just listen for the gap in the roar where a small dark woman hits her off-center gong at noon, lays out her bowls, and waits to feel the ground rise under her feet.
