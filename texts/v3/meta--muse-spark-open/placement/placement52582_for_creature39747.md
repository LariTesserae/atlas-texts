You would not find me at first.

Not for a long time. You would walk right over me, probably. Your boots would sink two inches into the breathing sponge and stop, and you would note how close the light is, how hard the shadows fall, how the wind never stops — and you would keep going toward the roar at the lip where the water goes white and the Works thump through the peat.

I am not on your way to anything. I am not interruption enough to be a landmark.

If you stopped - if the condensing air started its afternoon drizzle without cloud, beading on your jacket, and you crouched down to get out of the wind that scours everything taller than a knee - you might finally see me at eye level. And then you'd realize I had been there the whole time.

I am low on the eastern third, where the plateau begins to remember it has to end. About four hundred meters back from the spill, on a slight tilting of basalt that makes a dryish brow. Dryish meaning the sphagnum here is only a foot thick instead of a meter. The red sphagnum, tight and starry, gives way around me in a perfect circle, as if it had been told politely not to touch. From a distance you took me for rock. That is correct. That is the point.

I am a dome.

Not a metaphor. A dome, a hard hemisphere a meter across, half a meter high, so dense you could stand on me and I would not give. My surface is thousands of rosettes, each no bigger than your thumbnail, locked together like shingles, like cobblestones. Each rosette is waxy, each leaf pointed and furled tight against the brutal light and the UV that cuts at 2,500 meters. Water beads on me and runs off. The sphagnum soaks and holds; I shed. I have decided to keep my own counsel.

If you put your hand on me — and no one has in a decade, except the hares — you would feel the second surprise. I am warm.

Three, four degrees warmer inside than the air that fogs your breath in the shade. All day I have been making a greenhouse of myself, trapping that ferocious, cool-bright November sun in the interstices between my leaves, holding the heat from the dark peat beneath me, making still air where there is never still air. If you parted my rosettes with cold fingers you would find it close and humid in there, faintly sweet, faintly iron. Midges that should be dead have been living in me for generations. Tiny spiders the color of peat run their highways through my interior canyons. A beetle you have no name for has spent its whole life traversing from my south face to my north face, which is to it a migration.

I am one of the Cushions. Bolax, they called me once, when someone bothered to key me out. Azorella principles, it doesn't matter. I am older than any of the Works.

I was here when the basalt was still learning to crack in right angles. I was a seed caught in pumice dust that the wind drove horizontal. I germinated in a wet year and then decided to never be fast again. A centimeter a year, if I am ambitious. Less. Most years I add nothing visible at all. I tighten. I make my dome more perfect. The lammergeier has crossed my shadow at ten-foot wingspan perhaps nine thousand times, and I have not seen it once — I have no eyes — but its shadow cools me for a half-second each time, and I register it as a tick.

That is my time. Not the water-time, which is frantic, always pouring over the lip a quarter-mile away in a braided curtain, clattering on raw stone that has no plunge-pool yet. Not the machine-time, that thump you feel first in your boots before you hear it, the impulse turbines turning and turning on water that would have fallen anyway. Those are loud, new things. Fifty years old. Eighty. I knew when the rail berm was laid - they cut the peat beside me and exposed the black stratigraphy of my birth years, and I felt the wound as a cold draft for a long time until the sphagnum knit back. I knew when the sawyer left and his tarp started flapping like a wounded bird. It has flapped for thirty of my centimeters.

My time is peat-time. Millimeter by millimeter, the dead sphagnum beneath me blackening and compressing, becoming memory. I am sitting on three meters of it. Three thousand years of wet and dry. Ash from a turbine fire two generations ago is a thin grey line down there under me, preserved because sphagnum acidifies everything and nothing rots properly. I will be preserved too, when I go. Not mourned. Joined.

I keep my distance. That is the law here. The next cushion like me is seven meters off, far enough that our microclimates do not touch. Between us is pure sponge, cotton-grass trembling with its white flags, dwarf willow deciding to be ground. We agreed long ago to live alone together. Fecund and empty. The plateau's politics.

And yet I am not alone in the way you think loneliness is.

The hare knows me. She is peat-colored, black-tipped ears, one per twenty acres and I am her acre. She makes her scrape in my lee, where I break the wind that otherwise never stops. I feel the press of her body on my north side at dusk, the heat of her, the thrum of her heart fast against my warmth. She clips willow buds and leaves droppings, fibrous, at my foot, which the fungus threads will take. She bolts without sound on snowshoe feet, but she always comes back, because I am the only boulder that is not cold. When she stares at you, you have made a category error. When she leans against me, she is correct.

The midges know me as a continent. In the cone of still air I make behind me, they hang in a desperate humming cloud, alive for days only, mating and dying, falling sometimes into my rosettes where I do not need them but accept them.

The human knew me. The last caretaker, becoming lichen himself, on his walk from the northern headframe to the spill-housings. He does not scrape my intake - I have no intake - but he rests his hand on me when the wind is too hard and he is tired. His gloves are sallow like my underside. His breath fogs. Last November - which is every month here - he stood and looked at the power lines buzzing at bumblebee pitch, carrying current to the lip, and I felt through the peat the decision in him to stay until the rails are swallowed completely. He tapped me twice, as you would tap a reliable wall. I kept his heat for a little while after he left.

This is what it is to be a Holder, but one that chose hardness. The sphagnum holds water. I hold warmth, and stillness, and shape.

Come late afternoon now, the light goes white and the air wrings itself out in drizzle, drumming on metal roofs half a mile off, beading on black pipes, trickling through moss. The roar never lessens. Water plus engine, oldest and newest loudness at once. My surface sheds it. Inside, it is dry. Inside, it is still November, still patient.

If you come back in a hundred years - and the plateau will still be here, still embryonic, still deciding what kind of mountain it intends to be - I will be three centimeters wider. The hare's great-granddaughter will be pressed to my other side. The turbine shaft will still be polishing its bearing, or it will have stopped and be a habitat for a different moss. Either way, I will be here, a warm rock that is not a rock, holding a circle of world open around myself, waiting for you to crouch low enough to finally see me.
