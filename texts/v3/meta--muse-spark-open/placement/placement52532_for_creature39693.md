I would not be on the rim.

That's where everyone looks first — where the Table gives way in those striped staircase cliffs and the updraft comes off the northern ice like a poured river. That's where the air is thickest with shawl-wings, a living dust so dense the light goes pink at night, and the thrumming is so total it lives in your molars. The young ones go there to fish, because you don't have to be good when the sky is that crowded — you just lift a net and it sings full.

I tried that. When I first arrived — when I was still trying to be a person who *arrived* somewhere — I stood on the lip and cast until my arms went dead. And it was glorious and it was nothing. You can't live in a roar that loud. You can only work in it. You can't hear the organ chord underneath it, you can't hear when the note is about to change by a quarter-tone and bring the vertical rain.

I would be further in. Not in the middle, either. The middle is too honest. Out there the basins are perfect, house-sized mirrors held to a sky that will injure you if you look too long, and the spacing is so vast — 0.5/3, near-void — that you can see a single standing stone for three miles and convince yourself you're the only thing breathing on a county of lifted stone. The middle will bleach you. The light at noon is white and without filter, 2.8 and pitiless, and even though the air never leaves cool — that 1.0/3 that lives inside the basalt itself — your skin prickles with a false heat while your lungs show your breath faintly, always. People who stay in the middle try to build to hide from it. They stack a cairn. By morning the steady 20-knot pour has undone it, not in violence but in patience. The Table does not permit accumulation. I learned not to try.

So find me in the lee.

There is a place about a half-day's walk from the northern fall, where the wind has been working the same quartzite fin for longer than anyone's language. It's not a cliff, not a hill — just a long, low spine of stone, maybe as high as my chest, combed into flutes and organ pipes. The Aeolian Teeth, we call them. The wind goes through them and makes its one chord, low roar and glass-whistle, never louder, never softer, dynamic 0.3/3, equilibrium made audible. Behind that spine, on its southern side, the wind forgets you by about half.

That's where I would be. In the wind-shadow. Curled.

Who would I be? I would be old enough that my chest has finally grown into this air.

You know us when you see us — high-chested, barrel-lunged, skin cured dark not by sun but by light-injury, eyes slit under a brow that never stops squinting. We wear the shawl-wings we catch, those translucent gray capes that still smell faintly of ozone even after they've been scraped and stitched with gut. We don't wear them for warmth — nothing is warm here, even in direct sun you only prickle before the stone steals it back — we wear them because they were wind once, and they remember how to break it.

I would be a net-mender. Not a fisher, not anymore. The fishers are the young with good shoulders who stand on the rim and skim. I am the one who stays by the basins and waits.

My work is small and fits in the lee: a knee-high flag-conifer grows there, one of those bonsai-twisted dwarfs that has spent a hundred years getting to be no taller than my shin, all its branches combed lee-ward. It is my wind-vane and my clock. Under it, in the seep-crack where it has wedged its roots, there is a cushion of alpine moss hard as upholstery, and around it a perfect, leathery collar of lichen — black-green and rust-red — that has grown to precisely the damp line and stopped, as if cut with a knife. And beside that, three basins.

Not the giant ones. The small ones, palm-sized, lipped and polished like a giant left his soap dishes out for a million years. Old, settled erosion, not raw canyons. Each morning — and again at dusk, when the cold stone exhales — they sweat full. The whole plateau sweats. Dew beads on every grain, condenses, gathers, holds for a few hours in a lens so clear you can see the single black eye-spot of the basin shrimp shivering inside it. Pallid water-lice, hatching all at once from dust, living a generation before noon, folding back to cyst when the sun bleaches the pool dry again. I don't eat them. I watch them. They tell me if the water is right to drink.

I drink on hands and knees, lips to the lens. I sip where the shrimp sip, and leave the rest. That's the bargain. Take just enough condensation, just enough sky-meat, leave no mark.

When the fishers come back — two, three at a time, thin silhouettes against all that violent blue — they bring tangles of shawl-wings, hand-long kites of stretched skin and thrumming wings, 80 beats a second, mouths still combed open. They are so light you can tear one with a breath, but a million of them make the deafening that never changes, that cicada-scream you have to open your mouth to equalize. They dump them by my rock, and I work.

I spread the wings. I cut along the cartilage kite. I pull filament — that long tail-rudder twice as long as the body — and twist it with sinew. My nets sing when you pull them through thrumming air. That matters. A silent net catches nothing; the shawl-wings hear with their wings. My fingers know this the way the lichen knows the damp line: don't be ambitious. Don't stand up. Lie down with the stone.

At night I don't make fire. Not much. A little oil from their bodies, shielded between two stones, just enough to see the faint pink glow still moving above us — the whole sky dusty with coals, thrumming, always thrumming, even under the brutal bright stars. I curl in the lee of the Teeth and listen past the thrumming for the organ note.

Everyone else is trying to hear the wind. I'm trying to hear what the wind is hearing. When the whistle through the pipes goes a fraction thinner, I know the vertical rain is coming — that sudden, cold, thin rain that threads shining, braided, riverine rivulets between basins for an hour and then vanishes into the rock before it can run. I tell the fishers to pull their nets in. I tell the children to go and open their mouths to the sky.

And sometimes, in the hour after that rain, in one of my three little basins, the thread-thin stem pushes up. The five-petaled purple. Perfect, impossible, blooming out of condensation itself, already dying by noon. I have never picked it. No one does.

I sit with it while the pool shivers with shrimp, while the sky thrums, while the light tries to bleach it and the cool tries to steal it, and for one morning the Table is not sparse. It is abundant and it is exact.

That's where you'd find me. Not fighting the condition, but maintaining it. A crust, like the lichen. Thin, dark, patient, in the lee of a stone that has learned to be level again.

If you came looking, you wouldn't see me at first. You'd see the stone. Then you'd see the stone move — just a little — as I shifted my cape against the wind.

And I would lift my cupped hand — full of nothing but cold, bright water that had been air an hour before — and offer it to you.
