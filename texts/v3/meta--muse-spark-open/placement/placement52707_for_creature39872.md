I don't find myself at first.

Not in a city like this. A visitor stands too tall. A visitor listens too hard, trying to pick a single thread out of the wall of noise — the drip from an eave, one frog from the chorus that isn't a chorus, one child's voice in the alley — until the hiss comes back doubled. A visitor still thinks dry is normal, and keeps lifting his feet out of the water like that will help.

I can't stay a visitor. The water is warm as blood and it has already come in over the tops of my feet. It will stay there.

So I let the dim light take the edges off me for a while, until I stop looking for a hill to see from.

At first I thought I would be one of the high things. It's natural — everyone who comes here looks up from the wet to the air because the air is the only place anything seems to move freely.

I wanted to be the heron. To have a cistern of my own, a perfect circle of lidded blister a little deeper than the rest, and to stand in it to the knees and become part of the architecture. To be the one still point the swallows wheel around. To not have to carry anything, or sweep anything, or keep any water at its level. Just to wait, and then — so fast it hurts the eyes — to be fed.

But I am not patient enough to be embalmed while alive.

Then I thought, the swallow. Or the bat. Something that never lands where a person lands, that nests shoulder-to-shoulder in lime-spit and guano and then cuts the low cloud all day on a mouth held impossibly wide. To live on midges alone, to never touch the orange film except as a white streak falling. But that life is too loud, too constantly in motion. You would have to scream without stopping and never collide, and I have spent too much of my life trying not to collide.

No. Lower.

Not down to the film, though I love the film. I love that the true foundation of the whole roaring city is a rust-colored slick you slip on, a bacterial felt that learns to eat light and salt before anything else knows how. If I were honest I would say I want to be that — the first thing that dulls the glass just enough for the next thing to hold. But I have hands and I have already learned to use them.

So I find myself where a person can actually find themselves here: not high, not bottom, but ankle-deep in the middle.

On a causeway.

It is not a main causeway. Not the one the caravans come in on with baskets of tamarisk charcoal and reed balanced on their heads, complaining about the wet in a drier accent. Mine is a minor one, three slabs wide, laid stones bridging a marsh-thread between two warrens of houses that have grown together until they share walls and eaves and dogs.

You would find me in the hour that is not an hour, because there are no hours here, only the even amber-gray that never changes. I am barefoot — of course I am barefoot; my soles are that thickened hide the low people get, splayed wide, gray-padded and still somehow soft at the edges from never being dry. My ankles to my knees are stained orange. They never come clean. My hair is shorn to stubble and oiled. I wear a wrap of dark hide that is always damp and weighs twice what you think it would.

I have a broom. It is not a good broom. It is imported reed, precious, bound with fish-gut twine, and it is worn down to a stump from sweeping.

That is my work. I tend a length of water about forty paces long.

You would think there is nothing to do. The description you heard was right: the water remembers its level. It doesn't rise. It doesn't fall. Ocean seep on one side, condensation on the warm stone on the other, the whole vast plain breathing in and out so slowly its chest never seems to move. A man could do nothing and it would stay inches deep, shin-deep, exactly as it is.

But that is why you tend it. Because it remembers, and you must not offend it.

So I sweep. Slowly, with long, patient strokes, I pull the thread of tea-colored water between its knife-edged banks of unweathered basalt — banks no flood has ever rounded — and I keep it feeling its way at a thumb's width per hour. I lift a chip of basalt here. I wedge a lime-white pebble there. I clear the throat of the cistern two doors down where the film has grown too thick and is trying to make soil before its time. I scoop tadpoles out of my way and put them back behind me. The shrimp don't mind; they boil around my calves regardless.

While I sweep, the world does its wall of noise over me and through me.

The water sheeting off every roof hisses. The midges in the column that has lived over the corner by Jori's wall since before I was born hum one note behind my teeth. The frogs — you were not exaggerating about the frogs — call in an organ chord that never breathes. The swallows stitch their needle-screams through it, the bats rustle, the geckos chirp *tink-tink* exactly like the stone mauls two alleys over where my brother is cutting block. *Tink. Tink. Tink.*

I don't shout. No one does. When my daughter — she is four, she sits now where I once sat with her legs in the cistern watching a newt walk — needs me, I don't call her name. I whistle. A short, piercing, two-tone chirp that cuts the midge-drone clean. She whistles back without looking up, and I know she hasn't fallen in, hasn't been nicked by the glassy edge, is still haloed in midges she doesn't swipe at.

In a little while the caravan will not come my way — too small a causeway — but I will smell its lime and charcoal passing three blocks to windward, and hear the paler, drier voices trading guano for grain. We need them. We will always need them. No plant grows here. Every fiber, every calorie that isn't fish or shrimp or midge-cake comes from inland where the basalt is finally old enough to rot into something a stunted tamarisk can stand in. We trade them our white gold, scraped with stone scrapers from the swallow walls, and our perfect black stone that will make their tools for a generation.

I don't envy them their dry feet. They walk days over sharp rock carrying water and they think our stillness is misery. They don't understand that outside equilibrium is not freedom, it's just more kinds of hunger.

When I get tired I stop sweeping and just stand, broom handle resting against my shoulder, water moving around me, water dripping off me, water hanging in the air before it drips again. The heron in the plaza puddle behind me is still standing. The midges are still in their pillar. The dogs are still thin and wet and underfoot. In a stone room just above the waterline behind me, half my family sleeps with waxed fiber in their ears, dogs curled against their backs, while the other half pounds fish and grain.

Nothing changes, and that is not a sorrow. That is the entire achievement.

I belong to this low place the way the film belongs to the rock, the way the lime belongs between the black blocks — not native, brought from elsewhere, pale against all that dark, but now the only thing holding the wall together, slowly dissolving into the wet and being replaced, grain by grain, forever.

If you came looking for me you wouldn't see me at first. I am the wet-gray of basalt, the skin-brown of the people, the orange at the knees. I am bent, broad, barrel-chested, breathing the steam that is also the cloud that is also the light.

I am ankle-deep, and I am not waiting for anything to happen.

Listen — put your mouth open to equalize the pressure —

My heart has synced to it too.
