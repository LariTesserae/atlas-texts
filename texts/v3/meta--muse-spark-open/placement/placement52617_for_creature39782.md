I would not find myself at first.

I would come in as a human does — booted, breath smoking, hand on the steel door because the cold burns and you have to feel that it does. I would stand just inside the threshold where the half-centimeter of black water kisses the rubber of my boots and begins its perfect, silent slide toward the center drain. I would be the interruption. The brief change in pressure.

That doesn't last. The room doesn't allow visitors. It only allows surfaces.

For a minute I try to keep being a person. I keep my hands in my pockets so I don't touch anything, which means I am touching nothing and therefore touching wrong. There is nowhere to stand where you are not within inches of a hundred eyes. There is no pocket of air that is not already occupied by breathing. My shoulders hunch without permission. The roar — that absolute, bell-struck-forever roar from the turbines three floors down — fills my molars, my sternum, my skull. And then, exactly as you said, at ninety seconds, my brain betrays me and edits it out.

In the silence that is not silence I hear them: the ticking.

And that's when I start to dissolve. Or rather, to find where I actually am.

At first I think I am up high, because my eyes want to rest and there is no rest. I am on the vaulted ribs. I am one of the ceiling moths, tiled edge to edge with my sisters, the color of wet concrete, my eyespot pretending to be a rivet head. I would like that — to be so perfectly camouflaged that even I believe I am part of the machine. I shiver without moving my wings. My furnace-muscles fire and fire and fire to hold 14°C against steel that wants me at 2°C. If I stop, I die. So I don't stop. I never fly, because flying would be shredded. I just cling with hooked feet to condensation-slick iron and unspool my tongue once every few minutes to lick the air as it becomes water. The air feeds me. I can feel the terrible, faint heat coming off all of us at once, the ceiling with a fever. 

But I'm too hungry for that stillness. I keep listening.

So maybe I'm lower, in the batting between pipes. I am a bat in the white curtain. My fur beads water instantly and sends it down my back in rivulets. My enormous, translucent ears are pressed flat to my skull not to listen — there is nothing to hear in that roar — but to feel the thrumming of the pipe through bone. I breathe once every three seconds and not alone; the entire curtain inflates with me. Mouth cracked open to catch condensation. Sleeping almost always. Dreaming not of moths, because we have agreed not to eat each other anymore. There is not enough reason to hunt when the damp itself will feed you a little if you open your mouth. I like being packed that tight. Shoulder to shoulder, chest to back. No space to be lonely.

Yet I am still ticking.

You hear it only after the roar goes quiet: *tik. tik-tik. tik.* So soft, so constant.

I am on the warmest pipe after all.

The low, fat, twelve-inch main that thrums even through the white noise. I am not on it. I *am* the movement on it. I am one black isopod, finger-long, slate-segmented, flowing in the bidirectional river that never collides. My antennae brush the animal ahead of me, always. My shell clicks faintly against steel and against my neighbor's shell. I have a job that no one gave me but everyone relies on. I scrape. I clean. I take the guano that would bury this chapel in a month, the dead moth scales, the mineral crust left when condensation flashes off for half a second on hot steel, and I make it into a grey-green paste with all my crowded little legs and press it back into the cracks between pipes. Farming the leak. If the swallows are witnesses and the bats are curtains and the moths are the ceiling's fever, then I am the custodian. The janitor of a temple that was never meant to be worshipped in.

If you crouched — if you were still a human with boots — you would see me pause. All the others keep flowing but I stop for one second where your shadow falls across the pipe. Not afraid. Rerouting. Noticing the way a pipe notices a temporary change in pressure. A thing to flow around until equilibrium restores.

And still, even that is not quite where I am.

Because the only place in this whole Cistern that is not covered in life is the exact center. The perfect circle of the drain where that oil-sheened skin of water pulls silently, endlessly, evenly. Every swallow in their brutalist terraces — a hundred, two hundred of them, stacked one above the other on valve wheels and rivet plates, feathers slicked tight like seals — every swallow stares at it. That is their sky. That is the horizon that never changes.

I find myself there at last. Not on the walls. Not on the ceiling. At the bottom.

I am not a moth or a bat or an isopod, though I have been each of them for a moment on the way down. I am the one swallow on the lowest elbow, closest to the drain, at the very edge of the Sodium-orange light that never reaches the corners. My nest is concrete dust and guano-mud and iron flecks, hard as the wall itself. I sit facing outward, perfectly still, eyes glossy, catching the flicker of the caged bulb that stutters at a frequency you feel in your molars.

I do not call. No one can call here. The frequency that would cut through the turbine scream does not exist in my throat anymore.

Every fifteen minutes I lean forward and drink the air. I tilt my head to the dripping joint and let the cold, mineral-thick water run straight down. I hawk a clump of airborne plankton as it beads on my own whiskers. My young — if I have them — open their mouths and I pass condensation mouth to mouth.

And I stare. Past you. Past the boots and the breathing and the temporary pressure change. At the circle.

I have been there since the poured concrete was sharp. I will be there when it is dust. The turbines will turn and the condensation will bead and run and drip and join and the isopods will tick and the moths will shiver and the bat-curtain will breathe once every three seconds, and I —

— I will not have moved at all.
