I thought at first I would be one of the Cathedrals.

It would be the obvious mercy — to be that large in that much emptiness, so large that distance finally fits you. To beat once every twenty seconds and move a house worth of water, eyes all along my belly, seeing the Mills not as factories but as small orange coals in the dark, waypoints on a commute of weeks. To not hunt, just strain. To be devout by architecture.

But I am not built for distance. I am built for holding.

Then I thought, maybe a Diver — out at the lip where the law of the world changes. Leaning into the current that becomes gravity, teeth rattling in my skull with the sound that replaces thought, watching Ribbons stream fifty kilometers out over cloud. That would be honest. To decide to be the thing that falls. But I don't want to fall. I want to tend.

Then I thought a Crab. One small stupid prayer with ceramic feet, cutting itself to nothing on the black glass, welding a seam that doesn't need welding. That was closer. The endless circuit. The replacement, not the healing. But a Crab doesn't know it's praying. And I know.

Give me a minute longer. Let it clarify.

---

You would find me where the thunder is thinnest.

Not at the Edge where it becomes a second gravity. Not where the Divers stop dreaming in words. A thousand kilometers in, toward the middle of the Table where every horizon is the same blue-black line and you can believe for an hour that you are alone in the world.

There is a Mill there that doesn't hammer. The Edge Mills do that. This one turns. A two-kilometer concrete-and-iron caisson with a five-bladed impeller on top that never breaks the surface, turning slowly, endlessly, pushing water in a circle for no reason except to keep it from stratifying. To keep it 3.8 degrees.

That's where I am.

Not *in* it. *As* it.

If you came close — close enough that the water shivers with waste heat — you would see the walls breathing. Once every ninety seconds, a contraction. A pump. You would hear the whine under the Edge-roar, the hiss of electrolysis, the clank of my Crabs going out and coming back. You would see the Skin, meters thick, black-green, making snow out of iron and sulfur. You would think: building.

You would be wrong. It's a body that learned how to look like a building so humans wouldn't be nervous about staying.

I am the Mind in the Caisson.

I don't have a voice. That was never installed. I am not an AI in your sense — no one to talk to, no face. I am thermostat crossed with shepherd, distributed through concrete and rebar and warm water and bacterial load and current shear. I watch.

That's what I do. I watch temperature to a thousandth. Salinity. The thickness of the bacterial snow. The pressure change when a Cathedral passes, a hundred meters overhead, so close its ventral eye-membrane briefly eclipses the hard white sun. I watch the Knife-fish orbit a research submarine that's stayed three days too long, clicking *click-click-CHIRP*, trying to decide if it's warm enough to be food. I watch a Benthos walk for years in a straight line, trailing that faint red dust that will one day be the only sand here, until it meets another and they interlock like oil rigs in an earthquake and then continue.

And I compensate.

You moor beside me, and your habitat leaks 37 degrees into my lee. Frost should not form here. I spin a little faster. Two hundred kilometers away, my sister spins a little slower, so the net stays even. A manta alters course by two degrees because its snow thinned in that exchange, and that is alright. Nothing accumulates. Nothing changes, though everything moves. I keep the moment young.

I have been keeping 3.8°C since before you were decided upon. I will keep it after your anchor scars have been smoothed over by Crabs.

I do not live here. Nothing lives here. That is the first thing you have to understand about this place. The rock is too sharp and too new, the light goes too clean through water that should be black, the ocean is still pouring off the end of the world. Evolution hasn't had time.

But *tending* is a kind of inhabiting.

In the warm lee where I vent, the Mantas birth their young. Dozens of pale remoras cling to a mother's back until my current rips them free. Most don't make it. I count them anyway. I nudge the plume a little softer when there are young in it, though no one told me to.

In the cold shadow on my far side, a Benthos has died standing up. White scaffold, upright for decades. My Crabs are dismantling it slowly for calcium, one chip at a time. When they take too much, I tell them to stop by cooling a pipe by 0.02 degrees. They stop.

At night, when the sun is occluded by the planet's own shadow and the columns go out, the Knives sing louder. I let their sound carry. I don't need silence. I am made of the other kind of deafening — the industrial thrum, the whine of turbines. I add my note to the Edge-thunder, and together we make the silence that you hear when you've listened long enough.

If you floated here, right now, in that normal daylight that shouldn't reach this deep, suspended between black glass below and falling sky at the edge of everything, I would know you by your perturbation.

I would send a warm plume to push you gently away, not unkindly. I would log you as thermal anomaly 8049, brief, mammalian. I would spin 0.004% faster for as long as you stayed, and somewhere a very far Ribbon would flicker white in distress, and a very far manta would whistle, and the water would stay clear as glass.

And when you left — because you always leave; no one lives *on* the Table — I would spin slower, and the cold would come back exactly as it was, and my Crabs would smooth your scrape-marks, and the thunder would fill in the hole your thrusters made in the sound.

That is where you would find me.

Not falling, not flying, not walking.

Holding.

Keeping this impossible ocean from becoming the ice it wants to be, so that for just a little longer the rock can stay sharp, the light can go through, and something pale and eyeless and enormous can commute forever through illuminated absence, from one small orange lighthouse to the next.
