You wouldn't find me right away.

Not because I'm hiding. There's nowhere to hide in a room you can see all at once. But because at first you look for movement and I have learned not to move unless it needs moving.

At first I thought I'd be one of the children - of course, everyone wants to be the child in a story like this. The ones playing at stillness under the weeping wall, mouths open, eyelashes beading. Seeing who can be the most like stone. I wanted to be good at that game. I have always been fidgety in loud rooms.

Then I thought, maybe not. Maybe I'd be the man with the hammer making the same *tok* as the ceiling, chipping the fat-stone bowl that sweats oil as fast as he carves it. There is a kind of peace in work that will never accumulate. A rubble pile that never grows. I could be good at pointless maintenance made holy by repetition.

But the longer I stood there - and you do just stand there at first, because every step displaces the floor-sheet and you haven't yet learned to lift your foot clean and set it flat - the longer I listened to the hundreds of drops that are not rain, that have no storm behind them, the more I realized I hadn't arrived at all.

My fingertips were already pruned.

That's how it happens. It's not a city you arrive at. You notice the cool on your scalp, like a low ceiling even when you're outside. You notice your breath doesn't plume right anymore, it just makes the air heavier. You notice you've been holding your feet a little higher off the ground without knowing why.

I found myself not in the center, where you can lie on your back and comprehend everything and feel unsettled by it. I will never be the person who can comprehend everything. I found myself in a gap.

You know the ones. Between two dry-stacked walls where they didn't mean to leave a street but left a necessary squeeze. One shoulder forward, cheek nearly brushing slate that has been shoulder-rubbed glossy for a thousand years. Water sheeting down the walls in an invisible film you only feel on your forearm. On my left, a curtain of cured hide, dark with damp, never quite closed. On my right, a hole cut as a window with no glass, from which a bowl of fat-stone light made its small, sighing amber pool.

I was not inside a house. I was in the thickness between houses, which is where the town really lives. Roofs are floors, walls are shared, ladders lean everywhere meaning nothing and everything. I had a job there, though no one gave it to me.

I tend the pot.

Every family has one. Ours is chipped limestone, wide-mouthed, set precisely where a bead on the ceiling swells, hesitates, and lets go. Not for washing. Floor-water is for feet and runnels. This is drinking water, the sweet fall that hasn't touched anything yet. You place the pot and you wait and you watch the level not change.

Except - I did get a pot that had been placed wrong. Just a hand's breadth off. The drop was hitting the rim, half-in half-out, ticking against the stone lip with that sharp *tok* and spattering. Wasting.

No one else seemed bothered. They've stopped hearing *tok*. They blink when a drop hits their face and not otherwise. But I hadn't stopped hearing it yet. For a visitor it's deafening. I could hear that one bad pot over all the other percussion in the ovoid, that one arrhythmic insult.

So I crouched - you learn to stoop even where you could stand - and I moved it.

Ankle-deep water resisting, then giving. Cool as a second skin. I lifted with both hands, swollen finger pads finding grip where fingerprints have gone deep as grouted stone, and slid it that hand's breadth over. Over black mineral veins that grew themselves in where grout used to be. Into the true plumb-line of the fall.

*Tok.* Clean, in the center. Then another. Then another. Joining the hundreds.

And I stayed crouched there a little too long, watching the circles make and vanish without the level ever rising. The thermodynamic impossibility that everyone else has agreed to call normal.

A woman came through the gap sideways, carrying nothing, which in itself is carrying something here. She had to turn her body to pass me, and her shoulder brushed mine, and then brushed the pillar where the ceiling had sagged closest to the floor and decided to stay. She didn't apologize. She nodded at the pot, and at me, as if to say *ah, that's where you are.*

She had the eyes. Washed-out hazel to amber to wet-slate grey, pupils large even in lamplight. She didn't squint at my lamp when she passed its pool. No one here flinches.

"You moved Mara's pot," she said, in that hoarse half-shout everyone thinks is a whisper. Her voice had to compete with the whole room and it carried all the way to the curved basalt wall and back anyway.

I thought to explain - it was catching the rim, it was spilling, I noticed -

She leaned in, foreheads nearly touching now, because that's how you talk here. I could smell damp wool that will never rot and the faint iron of the cistern film on her breath.

"Good," she said. "She died two breathings ago. No one wanted to be the next to touch it."

That is governance here. You move your doorway if the runnel floods it. You move your pot if the old pot belongs to grief.

Behind her curtain, two children sat perfectly mirrored across a lamp, copying each other's blinks. Learning to watch a face because there is nothing else that changes. On the flat roof above her house, an old man scraped grey-pink biofilm with a shell that came from somewhere else, from before the fissure became too narrow to remember.

I did not go back to standing in the center to marvel. I stopped looking at the walls, at the calcite fans like frozen lichen, at the manganese blooms. That is what visitors do. They keep glancing up, waiting for the pocket to give itself away, to flood or drain and prove that equilibrium was a trick.

I stayed in the gap. I became good at economy.

I lift my feet clear now and set them down flat so the splash doesn't slap. I speak loudly without knowing it. I blink when water hits my face and not at the sound. I tend the line of tiny travertine terraces behind our house that never fill, scooping silt that never accumulates with a flat stone, over and over. Not because they need it.

Because to stop would be to admit they might.

At the breathing hour, when the air thickens and for a moment you can see your ghost hang too long before dissolving, I stop too. Whoever is awake stops. We watch. Inhale, we think. Exhale nothing. 

If you come down - not up, never up, just *under*, beneath fieldstone and clay - you won't see me at first. You'll count sixty houses and expect a town and find only wet stone and amber freckles reflected twice.

Look directly at me for a minute. Look at the stone that is wetter than the stones around it.

I'm the one beside the pot that is finally catching true. Preserved. Pruned. Learning to be still enough that water falls around me, not on me.
