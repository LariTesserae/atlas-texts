At first, I thought I would be up high.

That's the instinct — if you can choose anywhere on a young mountain that is barely a mountain, you want the vantage. I saw myself standing on the lip of one of those impossible gorges, thirty meters across and a kilometer straight down, where you can hold a conversation with someone on the other side in a whistle. Or higher still: out on the flat, where the steppe hasn't been broken yet, where the wind has nothing to blunt it and the light comes hard and white at noon.

Because that's where the story looks biggest. To be a glider, maybe. One of the *Shushil*. Fur under feathers, membrane between my legs open to catch a thermal without a wingbeat, shoulder to shoulder by the tens of thousands and then — dropping off all at once, a river of shadows falling over the courtyards below. To know pressure the way they do. To tell the city, in your pattern, that the rains will stall soon.

I lingered on that for a long time and it was almost right, but not quite. I am not built for silence. I want to watch too much.

Then I thought lower. Down in the gorge, where the second half of the city hangs.

There is a wrong idea we have about depth — that going down is retreating. There, it isn't. Going down is going *in*. Toward the water that made the cut in the first place, toward the fish traps and the winter ice harvest, toward those tallow-fat lamps in niches that make a vertical river of stars when you look down from the top at dusk. 

So I tried that one on: I am the boy on the basket elevator, counterweighted, creaking down on sinew rope that has to be remade every spring. I am the voice that calls up in that glottal, consonant-heavy tongue not meant to shout — hand on forearm even though there's a kilometer of air between you — saying *slow, slow, the block is swinging.* My palms would know the feel of wet stone. My lungs would know the gorge draft that makes laundry snap without wind.

Close. Closer. Because what I love in that image is not the height or depth, it's the tending. The maintenance. Someone whose job is to make the same trip hundreds of times and never think it's boring.

But still not quite me.

The young range rings like a struck bell, you wrote, because there is no deep soil to muffle it. And the city answers with a smaller, closer sound underneath everything: drip from basin to basin, from roof stone to trough to cistern. Stone on stone as someone shapes a block. The shuffle of furred feet.

I listened for where I belonged in that sound, and it shifted downwards again. From the gorge wall to the terrace. From the terrace to the courtyard. From the courtyard to a single house — half-carved into basalt, half-built out with dry-stacked talus blocks fitted so precisely frost can't enter. The kind of house where privacy is only a felt curtain because heat is too precious to put a wall between. Where a grandmother carries a water jar on her head over knife-tal us without looking down.

I wanted to be inside that density-is-shelter logic. Not alone on a peak. Pressed wall to wall. Sharing heat through a stone seam.

If I were human there — and I think I would be human, emphatically, stubbornly human, because nothing else could decide to stay here on purpose — I wouldn't be a hero. There's no room for heroes where a new terrace takes a generation to finish. I'd be accretive.

For a while I imagined myself as the apprentice mason. Twenty years learning to dress a single block. Your whole youth spent grinding with hammerstone and antler pick until the face is true enough to test against the level-stone — that perfect slab ground flat over someone's lifetime, which means *beautiful* the same way *well-fitted* means beautiful. To spend a day making a seam disappear against frost. I love that work. That patience is how I understand time.

But even that was still a little too loud. A clack, a strike.

What finally settled, what became more itself as I sat with it, was quieter.

I am on the roof.

Not a person on the roof. In that house I just described, on the eastern scarp somewhere in the five-hundred-kilometer street that is not a street, there is a set of three dark, porous stones stood on end at precise angles. The *condensation stones.*

They aren't beautiful. They're pitted black slag, chosen not for color but for thirst. Every afternoon someone — a ten-year-old who knows to read frost patterns, or an old mother whose lungs are finally easy at sixty — comes and turns them a quarter-turn to the evening wind. And I stand there all night.

I stand there while breath plumes and the tallow lamps below go amber and then out. While the gliders hunch and hush. While the dogs curl in the interstitial heat between houses and the insects go still in the cairns. The thin air goes colder in that clean way it does, and the damp that blew up from the lowlands and hit the Wall condenses. Freezes.

Frost blooms on me like white flowers.

No one sees it happen. That's the point. It's not harvest. It's not hunting or building. It's collecting what can never be owned — a liter a day from air if you are well-fitted — and letting it run, by gravity alone, through tiny filed channels into a basin polished by generations of hands dipping.

I am that third stone, probably. The one the grandfather added, not the great-great-grandmother. The correction. The one slightly better angled. I was not original, I was fitted after, to make the system fit better.

By noon I'll be dry and dark again, warm to the touch for an hour before I cool. Empty. Useful exactly because I empty myself completely each morning. I'll wait to be turned again.

And if you came to ask that household who I was, they wouldn't point to me. They would show you their cistern first. *This seep we deepened.* And they'd point to the basin two meters across and they'd tell you whose hand deepened it by a millimeter each year, so their grandchildren would have a little more. I am in that story, but only as part of the catchment. Part of the *between*.

You'll find me there, in other words — not high on the scree where the rock still cuts rope, not in the gorge lights where the river roars far below, but in the middle terrace where everyone lives too close for kings or shouts. In the house where the wall is shared. Where the law is *where the water went last drought.* Where children learn by six not to waste the drip.

I'm the thing that stands still and gathers the cold's one generosity, so that inside, behind the hide curtain, there can be a child's plum-red cheeks, a grandmother's indestructible lungs, a low language spoken with a hand on your forearm.

When the ice shifts and the young range rings, listen. Underneath the bell, underneath the shushil shadow passing and the sisters whistling across emptiness — you'll hear me.

Drip, to basin. Basin, to mouth.
