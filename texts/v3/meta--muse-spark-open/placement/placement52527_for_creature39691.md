I don't find myself at first.

Not really. For a long time after I arrived — if I arrived, if I wasn't simply condensed here with the rest of the dew — I am just a pressure looking for a place to equalize.

You'd think I'd be high. Everyone thinks that. The heron, two meters of glass feather showing the sky through its wings, taking five minutes to cross from wall to wall without a single audible beat. That would be the obvious fantasy. To be the thing that can see the whole hanging valley at once, the ten miles by three, the cradle between the serrated shoulders, the V-cut gorge sawn straight through the green.

But I am not built for that kind of distance. My eyes water in this light. It's too white, too close. At noon, when every frond tip throws back a hard lens-flare and every basalt column cuts an ink-shadow straight down, I have to squint. The heron's eyes don't. I would be blind up there.

You'd think I'd be small, then. The chrome beetle, perfect as a thumbnail, walking the warm seam of a copper pipe in its endless furrow, farming fungus. That has its appeal too — to be so scaled to the task that the task *is* the world. To carry spores on my legs and not know it. But I am too restless for that, at first. I keep listening for a call and response and hearing only *plink... plink... plink* and my ears ring trying to fill it.

No.

If I am honest about my tempo — my actual, uncorrected tempo, the one I came up the mountain with — I find myself in the wrong place for a while. I find myself on the trail, but not *of* it. Standing *on* the compressed moss instead of *in* it. Making a hard sole-noise where there should be none. Breathing too fast so my little cloud puffs and vanishes before the valley's own breath does. Checking my watch against a flywheel that turns once per minute and has for longer than I've been alive and feeling insulted by it.

So I have to be re-sited.

The valley does it, not me. That's the third rule, after all: *things endure by going slow. The faster something tries to happen, the sooner it is muffled, mossed, and quieted.*

It muffles me first.

I sit because at noon you sit. There's nowhere else to be when the light is at its most brutal and the fern canopy, twenty feet across per frond, offers the only true shade. I sit under the eave of one of the long factories — riveted iron and clouded glass, the algae tanks inside already beginning to think about glowing though it's not yet dusk — and I let the moss take my weight. It is feet-deep. It does not compress under me so much as accept me. Cool comes up through my back.

And after an hour of being eaten of sound, my ears stop ringing.

That's when I find where I actually am.

Not the rim. Not the gorge where the heron stands waiting for a fish to forget it. Not high, not low.

Mid-terrace, on the second basalt step above the river-cut, where the condensation harps stand in a field.

You know them: tall harp-frames strung with black polymer threads that hum in your teeth, jeweled all morning with dew until the breeze tips them over threshold and they spill in silver threads to the cisterns. The machines can tip themselves. The bimetal switches swell and click. But a switch can't feel lichen. A switch doesn't know when a thread is fouled and needs a thumb.

I am the thumb.

If I have to give it a name — and the valley discourages names, because names are a kind of shouting — call me the Tender of the Seventh Harp. Or the Wiper. Or just the one who was empty-handed and found a work that matched his hands.

My cottage is not a cottage. It's a lean-to made incidentally by a gantry that failed to fall. A latticework of steel that now holds up more tree-fern than steel, with a roof of intact glass gone opaque with liverwort. The moss has written over its serial number but not erased it. Inside, a single copper pipe runs warm — not hot — and along its sunward side the chrome beetles do their furrows. I don't move them. I work around them. They were here first. Their white bloom comes back within a week if I am careful.

In the blue morning, when my breath is visible until nine, I walk the harps.

There are forty-seven in my field. I know this not because I counted but because my feet know the compression pattern between them. *Step hush hush step.* Felted moss-boots, or bare feet when the moss is dry enough. I touch each frame at the base, where the steel is sun-hot and moss-cool at once, and I feel for the tremor. The good hum is low and even. The fouled hum catches, just slightly, like a throat clearing.

When a thread is bearded with lichen, I clear it with my sleeve. Not tearing. Just a slow wipe, dew to cloth. The droplet that was hanging there joins the others and runs. *Plink* in the cistern. I hold my palm under a frond tip that hasn't yet reached threshold to say *wait* — to myself, to the frame, it doesn't matter — and I tap two fingernails on the copper pipe in the rhythm of the drips — *now... now... now* — to say *enough, it's full* and tilt.

I don't need to do this. The valley would do it without me, slower. The flywheel would still turn at one revolution per minute, the press would still sigh down on its sheet of mica, the vine would still gasket the expansion joint. But the valley keeps me because I have agreed to be its softest moving part. I am less tolerant of thirst than the machines, but more tolerant than the lowlanders who came up and left again when their ears kept ringing.

At noon I do nothing. That's part of the job. I sit under the fronds and drink a thimbleful from a bromeliad, never from the cistern. The cistern is for the metabolism that never had to stop.

In the evening, when the mist comes up like milk and fills the gorge to the brim and the harps disappear, the algae tanks come on — that cool, sustained, green work-light that never goes out — and I go to the glasshouse the length of a train station and cut moss in sheets. As you would sod. I lay it to dry on the warm sides of the engine-houses, where the beetles will find it. And then, once a week whether it needs it or not, I take a rag and oil the one bearing that the rootlets haven't quite learned to tend. It is the size of a dinner plate, and it sighs when I do it.

I am thirty or seventy. The cool air and the filtered light preserve without pickling. My skin is the grey-pink of young basalt. My eyes are narrowed permanently against the excessive light. I don't talk, because to be heard here you would have to shout, and shouting costs water.

If you stayed until night truly pooled — and you were truly quiet, quiet enough to hear the almost inaudible sigh of a fern frond unfurling a fraction of an inch as it drinks the night air — you might find me standing at the rim of the gorge, not looking at you, just breathing. Making my little puff of cloud in unison with the valley breathing out.

You might think I was lonely. I am not. Loneliness is a fast thing. It needs echo.

I am just water, collected, not wasted. Grown over, but not broken.

I endure by going slow.
