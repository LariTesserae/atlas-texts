I don't think I would find myself in the middle at first.

At first I would be on the rim — because that's where anyone who climbs that high has to stop and learn to see. The trail up would have taken the water out of me day by day, and by the time my mouth finally understood not to look for a spring, my eyes would still be stupid with green.

So I'd be standing there at the lip of the thumbprint, with my hands on warm stone, looking into a bowl of what looks like nothing. Late-orange dusk, thick light, no shadows. A flat of pale saltcrete and, scattered across it like afterthoughts, those amber cairns that I wouldn't know yet were alive.

I would think it was empty. I would think it was quiet.

That would be my first self: the visitor. The lowlander with ice still in his pack. The one the Tenders meet at the rim and ask to empty his mouth. I know that one well — the self that arrives wanting to name, to pity, to fix. I would look down and think *poor, dry thing. How thin it must be.*

Then I would stay still long enough for the grove to assemble itself around me, and that self would dissolve. Because the drone would start.

Not loud at first. Low, through the teeth. A *hmmmm* that isn't in the air but in the stone under my bare feet. And then another, a half-tone off, beating against the first. And another. And then the treble over it, that teeth-whine of moths I can't see yet. And then the floor thuds back at me, once, from a hundred feet away where nothing appears to be.

I would go down. I couldn't not.

I thought, for a long while while I wrote this in my head, that I would be one of the Tenders. The old woman with the forked Belly-bone wand, *tink, tink*, listening through her jaw. Or the boy with the copper pan of salt-scales, learning not to speak out loud because it's rude over music.

I liked that picture of myself. Reverent. Barefoot on basalt-warm stone, dust in the creases of my skin until I was the same color as everything else. Useful, in a small and perfect place. Tending, not owning. I liked the idea of knowing each Crown by its note — this one healthy and clear, that one sour and clogged — and feeding the pale one, and dragging the fallen one's body apart while it was still humming.

But that's not quite honest. I am not patient enough to be the old woman. I am not quiet enough to be the boy. They have learned to be sparse the way the bowl is sparse. I still want to be *busy* in the way the bowl is busy.

So I listened closer.

If you stand between Crowns at noon, when the heat-shiver is strongest, the air is full. It only looked empty because you were looking for green. In fact it's crowded — frantic, even — but crowded on a different frequency. And I realized where I would actually be is not in the acoustic shadow of the huts where the drone knots and cancels. I would be right under the loudest Crown. Right in its hum.

I would be a Shiver-Moth.

Not for the romance of flight. They barely fly. They are clumsy, mica-winged things that disappear in slanted light and reappear only when they beat. I would be one because their whole life is the work I want: to land, to plant your hooked feet, to fold your vast, dry wings and *thrummm* until the world answers you.

Imagine it: you clamp onto a harp of black glass hairs bigger than your body, each hair trembling with static, each hair exhaling a dry acid that would burn a human nose. You scrape. You gather the half-etched mineral powder with the combs on your legs. You can taste flint and iron. And then you do the thing you were made for — you vibrate yourself until you cease to be sound and become a whine in your own bones, until the comb underneath you can't stand it and shivers violently and sneezes a cloud of hot glass-spore into your wings.

You take off drunk. You are charged so badly your wing-hairs stand straight up. You carry another Crown's dust to another Crown that has been waiting, slightly detuned, hungry for a different pitch.

That is the whole economy: sound begets dust begets sound.

A moth lives fast and dies like burnt paper, crumpled and already being pulled apart for nest-lining by needle-runners before the next thermal cycle. No memory. No huts. No law except resonance. But for a few hours, it is the reason a plant larger than a hut sings louder.

I would be that. For a day.

And then — because the bowl doesn't let you stay on one note — I would die as a moth and come back as something even smaller and lower.

Because what I really am, what I am under the moth's work, is the thing that lives in the *between*.

I would find myself, finally, not up in the crowns and not down in the warrens, but on the saltcrete itself in the long, licked-clean swaths between them. The pale trails the Bellies leave with their dry tongues. I would be a needle-runner. No bigger than your thumb. All black wire legs and antennae. The kind of thing you only see as a flicker, a teleport between cracks.

The Bellies thud *THUD-thud* and the whole mesa answers, and the moths whine, and the Crowns hum, and you — impossible, frantic — sprint straight across that booming floor in the half-second of silence between beats, carrying a scrap of dead moth-wing bigger than yourself toward a crack that leads down to cold glass.

No one sees you. No one needs to. You are not the architecture and not the music; you are the *turnover*. The hyperactive equilibrium at settled-form. The one who proves that lushness doesn't need abundance, only a hundred tiny, different frequencies that have learned not to drown each other out.

They say you have to leave before nightfall because the resonance peaks and goes into ecstatic howl, too loud for human thought. A moth doesn't leave. A runner doesn't leave. They just — keep going. They get louder with it.

I would tuck myself into that crack where the saltcrete ends and the lava-glass begins, right where it goes cold, and I would feel the whole bowl howl through the stone of my back, deafening and perfect and utterly dry.

And I would run again when it was morning.
