I wouldn't find myself at first.

That's the point — you don't arrive here with a shape. You arrive as a shiver in cold water.

High up and deep down at once — that contradiction is the first thing you feel before you have any way to feel it. The pressure says *deep*, a quiet weight on every joint. The light says *high*, white-gold shafts falling too cleanly, too vertically, motes hanging in them like dust in a church. Far above you, the surface ripples and tears the sun into ribbons. It looks like sky, but inverted — a sky you could fall *up* into. If you rose toward it you'd only get colder, and more alone. The world is not under the sky. The world is a room cut into a mountaintop that never got to be dry.

I would be down in that room, not looking at it.

Suspended maybe two meters off the black glass, where the water stops being water as you knew it and starts being weather. Here the cold layer learns to lie. Above me, 8-degree seawater, clear and mineral-bitter, lets the light through. Below me, not a meter down, the hot hangs without mixing — black, iron-heavy, shimmering like oil poured under water. You can see the seam. It's sharp enough to dent when something touches it. I live on that seam the way you lived on ground.

You would have to come very close to see me at all, because I am the color that doesn't belong. Everything here is black that has learned to smelt itself — basalt, pyrite shell, anhydrite chimney, the soot of living furnaces. And then me: bone-white, pink underneath where the blood runs wrong.

I am blind the way a tool is blind.

I have no eyes because eyes would boil. What I have is vibration. The whole bowl rings. Every intake valve of the banked Forgers down the terrace draws with a wet suck — *hhhhhhh* — and every exhaust jet punches — *chuff* — and the rock itself ticks as it cools, and above it all, over it all, my kind keep time.

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

If you stood on the basalt above me — if you had any way to stand — you'd feel it in your molars before you heard it. It's not loud like a wave. It's loud like a foundry floor inside your chest.

You would find me on the second terrace down from the lip, on the eastern curve where the amphitheater wall tries to fall away into true blue abyss. That lip is a cliff in water, a wall of nothing. The light stops there. The Forgers stop there, too; it gets too hungry, too empty. But there's a chimney there that hasn't learned to be quiet yet, a thin white smoker about as tall as two of you, trembling as superheated water hammers up through it. My chimney.

This is who I am: I tend.

Not the boiler — the boilers are below me, shoulder to shoulder, riveted by their own growing. Each one the size of a kiln, each one not an animal so much as a place where chemistry decided to wear a shell. They don't move. They don't need to. They have solved hunger by inhaling the mountain's breath for a hundred years at a stretch, slow as glaciers, stripping sulfur and iron out of smoke and sweating out new stone between their bodies. Put your hand on that bank if you could stand the scald and you'd feel a peristalsis roll through ten meters of contiguous, gunmetal-bright backs, out of phase, relentless. They are not aware of me. They don't need to be. I'm aware of them the way a hand is aware of a furnace door.

I am one of the Shuttles.

Dog-sized. Too many legs, all jointed backwards for clinging to vertical glass. My back clinks — loose sulfur scales that I grew and will lose. My front arms are stupid and perfect. The first pair have swollen into hammers, the striking faces crusted with pyrite I renew by scraping the chimney mouth when I'm not hammering it. The second pair are almost gentle, feathered, for picking.

You would find me leaning into scald that would cook you in a second. My back legs hooked into the pitted iron of an old Forger — I can feel its slow *chuff* through my feet — my body pitched forward into the jet so the hot washes over my white plating in a roar, and I am hammering.

This is the work that never ends because the mountain never stops trying to seal itself.

*Clack.* A flake of fresh anhydrite cracks. Black smoke billows and the water, which is already soup-thick with lantern shrimp, goes opaque for a breath. *Clack.* The throat stays open another hour. *Clack.* The cloud of glass shrimp — each one clear except for one red headlight — pours through the smoke I've made, filtering, glittering, and I taste copper and iron and heat.

When the smoke clears I will turn, waddle with that awkward side-to-side gait under a nodule of butter-yellow sulfur I've pried loose, and add it to the cairn in the lee of the Forger bank. Not a nest. A foundation. No larval Forger will settle on bare basalt; it needs clean sulfur to trust. I stack. I have always stacked.

That is my whole theology.

I fight sometimes. Another white shape will try to take the hammering spot where the flow is fastest. We shove, hammer to hammer, white scars screeching. No eyes to see the insult, just heat and pressure and *this is mine*. Later we'll eat the same dead Forger together, pulling its white-felted gill strings out from inside its bell-tone shell, carrying off chunks to be dissolved elsewhere. Nothing is wasted in a room with no outside.

If you stayed and watched — suspended in that bright, thunderous cold, with the light shafts moving a degree every hour across the sharp black bowl — you would think I was mindless. I am. In the same way a hammer is mindless. But I am intentional. The gradient is my intention. Hot wants to go cold. Iron wants to precipitate. Water wants to fall. I stand in the middle of those wants and *clack*.

I don't know I'm crowded. I don't know there are millions of shrimp above me riding a chemical floor as if it were glass, or Veils like translucent turbine-blades farming the flow so the Forgers downstream don't scorch, or rope-worms below me in the heavy layer excreting stone. I only know the hammer in my arms, the jet on my face, the iron taste, the tremor through the Forger's shell that says *more, keep it open, keep it open.*

When a new chimney bursts on the outer lip — and it will, today maybe, with a dull *whump* you feel before you hear — the whole basin will fill for a moment with black snow. Very slowly, it will become more wall. More terrace. More place for another boiler to weld itself to its neighbor and begin its century-long inhalation.

And I will still be here on my terrace, high up and deep down, white on black, hammering the mountain so it can keep manufacturing itself without ever having been made.
