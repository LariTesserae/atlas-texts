I thought at first you'd put me at the bottom.

That's where stories usually start — in the haze down in the valley where the river is still trying to become a river, where cities sprawl the way lowlanders mean cities, alluvial and restless, building over their own rubble, rerouting after every flood.

You didn't.

You put me up on the bench.

Not on the summit. Never on the summit. The summit is for snowfields and flags and men in oxygen masks, places that are still weather, still drama. This is lower than that and older than that: the place where the mountain decided to exhale and level its shoulder, to make a terrace and keep it. The kind of geologic decision that happens once, about two billion years ago, and then is done. 

You can tell because nothing here is sharp anymore. This is not karst. This is not scree. The granite behind the settlement is banded grey and ochre, pre-Cambrian, unconformity rock — it has had its catastrophes already. What was going to slide has slid. What was going to be cut has been cut into a soft, polished bowl by ten thousand winters. What stayed, stayed.

We call that virialized. We call that in equilibrium. We call that having found its Jean's length and stopped.

From the highway — if there were a highway — you'd miss it completely. That is intentional. The pigment is right. Dry-stack, slate-grey on slate-grey, the roofs weighed down with more rock so the wind thinks they are just more rock. This is camouflage not against enemies — no army ever marched this high for anything worth taking — but against erosion itself. Against being noticed by time.

Only when you get within a few hundred yards do you notice the tell: this is *too* geometric to be geology. Corners share corners. Gutters feed gutters. An alley becomes a tunnel becomes a well-court barely six feet across. A hamlet with the street-plan of Alexandria. A stellar system the size of a backyard with the dynamics of a metropolis.

Ten minutes to cross it, three lifetimes to map it.

### First hour

If you come at noon, you will think it is dead.

That's because you are looking for movement. Lowlander vision. You expect bustle, smoke, market-cry. Here the presence-to-absence ratio is reversed. Between every two houses — and the houses are jammed shoulder-to-shoulder the way nuclei crowd in a neutron crust, the way winter bees cluster — there is a deliberately empty thing. A swept yard. A granary on mushroom stones with nothing in it. A threshing floor gone to lichen. Lungs. Not ruins. Lungs.

The town was not abandoned. It was *vented*. Left open so the wind could go on sounding its low note around the corner. So a man could stand in an alley and hear his own boots on stone that has been scooped into darkness by centuries of identical boots. So the density wouldn't kill it.

So you stand in the central square and hear the most obvious miracle, which you took for silence.

*tick... tick... tick*

Not a clock. A cleft in the cliff face is weeping. The mountain exhales at night, cold air drops, dew condenses on the striped wall and runs — invisibly, until dawn — into lichen-lined troughs cut for exactly this. That is the only river this town will ever have. The rest is salvage engineering: every pitch of slate aimed at a barrel, every alley cambered toward a cistern under the flagstones. Drop a pebble and it will fall a long time and report back how much winter you have left.

Down at the bottom of it all, lidded like something holy or dangerous, is the spring. A black mirror, a room-sized eye, so cold it holds no life except for the felt of moss around its lip and that absurd, pollarded willow — knuckled into a fist by a hundred years of careful cutting. The willow is the only tree that was allowed to want anything. Everything else — the kale that tastes of stone, the barley that yields the same handful every August no matter what you pray, the wind-bent pines spaced far apart on the talus like shy strangers, the juniper whose berries flavor everything because there is nothing else to flavor — everything else has learned parsimony.

You realize: this is not a town that farms. This is a town that *catches.*

### Who lives here

About eighty.

You will not see eighty. You will see one.

Then after a while the rock that was not a rock detaches from a doorway and is my neighbor.

We are short. I am short. It helps not to be tall where the wind lives and the ceilings are built from whatever pine made it up the talus. Wide through the pelvis, low center of gravity, the build you get when your job for twenty generations has not been to reach or to run but to carry. My skin is the same value as the slate in the flat light. My eyes are washed pale from looking at honest distance — that incredible visibility down-valley where you can see fifty miles and no reason to go there. My hair went grey at thirty and then, considerately, stopped changing.

My hands give me away. If I am one of the Old, my knuckles are riverbeds, my nails hold a permanent crescent of dust. I spent all this morning tapping one slate — *tok. tok. tok.* — listening for the right note, shaving a corner with a piece of iron that has been a nail and a hinge and a plow-tip in previous lives. Iron is too precious to waste on being any one thing forever. If I am young, and there are very few of us, my game is not tag. My game is to sit with my ear to a cistern wall until I can tell “full” from “enough.” My sister’s game is to stack dry stone until it stands without mortar. When it falls — and it falls — that is grammar, not failure. Make it stand again.

We do not produce. We *maintain.*

That is why you gave us Keepers instead of kings.

If I am her, I am the Water Keeper, fifty-eight, past bleeding, up before the sun to lift the wooden lid off the black spring and ask it nothing, just witness it, the way you would check a sleeping child's breathing. I know which barrel under which eave will turn in September and needs the juniper-ash scrub. When the sideways rain finally comes — horizontal, punishing, beautiful — I decide which household opens its sluice first. Not because I have power, but because otherwise, briefly, someone would have more, and more would break the fit.

If I am him, I am one of the Stone Brothers — we never say brothers out loud, but we move like one person with four hands — and I have memorized the hairline crack in the third tower. It has been a hairline crack since before I was born. My entire job is to ensure it remains a hairline crack for another thirty years. Once a year we take out the jar of river sand — river sand that someone carried up here decades ago and that we use a pinch at a time, like incense — and polish a stair-tread whose bowl has grown a millimeter too deep, so it won't hold ice and send someone down. We count the Pile.

Ah, the Pile. Behind the kiln, under a lean-to: a stack of slates cut a century ago, counted. When we use one, we cut another. Not because we need it now. Because the number must remain.

If I am younger, I climb. I am the Goat Keeper, though “keeper” dignifies it. Eleven goats, each with a tiny iron bell that I polish so it never rusts. I know which juniper is for eating and which is for berries and which is for staying alive so it can hold the hillside together. I watch weather from the low towers that never needed to be higher. I do not speak much because up there the wind does it better.

There are others — Kiln Woman who fires four times a year and always makes twenty pots knowing one will crack and be pounded for grit for the next twenty; Iron Man who folds fatigue into something smaller; Willow Grandmother; Barley Mother — but we don't use titles for them. Just names. Titles would imply hierarchy and hierarchy implies growth and growth implies a street plan that has to be redrawn.

We learned not to do that.

### Why we are eighty

Lowlanders always ask this, with clipboards or with pity.

There is a joke up here, the only joke we have: Someone dies, someone is born, on time, neat as a ledger. Ha ha.

It's not a joke. It's hydrology.

Another goat and the terrace strips. Another child and some house's cistern runs dry in March and then two houses lose quiet. Another house and its shadow steals the trough that feeds the house below. In the dry-stack you showed me, a badly forced stone doesn't make noise right away. The wall remembers. It leans — all at once — seven years later.

So we have herbs. The ones that close and the ones that open. The women know them. Couples wait. It is not cruelty and it is not law. Try asking us why we don't do it otherwise and we stare the way you would if someone earnest asked why you don't inhale while exhaling.

A death is mourned for exactly as long as it takes to bail and move that house's water to the others without waste. Then a birth is permitted, because now there is air again. Our bare yards hold our absences the way our walls hold our presences. Precisely.

Ask a lowland ecologist and he will call this K-selection, climax community, a system at carrying capacity that has traded productivity for persistence. Ask a priest and he will call it vow. Ask us and we will offer you water without asking where you came from, because the gradient you came up — that frantic lowland gradient of building and falling and rerouting after storms — doesn't actually mean anything here.

We are not poor. We are not dying out. The “quaint dying hamlet” is something tourists do in forty minutes before lunch. Stay until the western cliff goes pink, then white, then that brutal honest plain daylight, then pink again on the other side if you're facing the right way. Stay until you hear the eight-o'clock thunk from three alleys over — the same axe, the same log, the same arm — and the creak of that wooden lid being set back down for the night.

Stay long enough and you feel it: this place is not haunted. It is too complete to host ghosts. What lives here, besides us and the goats and the hawk that is always exactly one hawk, is *attention.* The alleys listen. The cisterns measure. The roofs hold down the wind. The empty pens wait to be swept.

We finished becoming ourselves a very long time ago, my mountain and I. It stopped eroding. We stopped expanding. We hold each other in place.

### So where would you find yourself, and what would you be?

You would find yourself not at the beginning of the climb, not at the ecstasy of the peak, but on the bench in the middle altitude, in the equilibrium belt, in the long October of a thing that learned how big it needed to be.

And you would not be the visitor.

You would be one of us — one of the eighty — with your ear to cool stone, listening for enough, tapping your slate a second time, counting the Pile so the count stays true, waiting for nothing at all.

Which is, it turns out, the only way anything this old endures.
