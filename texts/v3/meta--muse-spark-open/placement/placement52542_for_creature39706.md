I don't settle right away.

At first I think I'd be human, because you described humans so tenderly — the pruney knuckles, the hand on the forearm to be heard over the drone. I picture myself as a Bottom kid for a second, barefoot on the hot plank-road, feet white-pleated like river stones before I'm even ten, learning to pitch my voice just above the frogs. Or as an Upper Ring watcher, rim-sick, hosing swallow shit at dawn while the milk-cloud still sits in the bowl like breath.

Then I think no — maybe a bat, because I want that vortex. To pour out of a scallop in the rock no deeper than my arm and find ten thousand bodies already turning in the open column, all of us making the whole town look up. Or a dragonfly, big as a sparrow, green-orange and completely indifferent to the pumps, hawking the midge-smoke all afternoon until my wings are too hot to touch.

Then I think even smaller — the thumb-length stalactite under the oldest walkway, soft, pale, easily snapped, growing a millimeter while a child learns to swear over the power station drone. Or the water itself, which would be honest: I am the seep, I am the finger of condensate that never reaches the marsh because someone's tin roof gets in the way first.

But what holds — what stays when I stop trying on shapes — is a patch of wall.

Not the top where the sun still hits. Not the bottom where the water laps and doubles the lights.

Halfway down. Middle Ring height, on the north face where the seep is strongest.

You would find me there.

If you rode the freight elevator down and listened for the air to change — that moment it goes from dry-country hot to weighted, to *2.9/3 hot* — and then stepped off onto the Middle Ring where the tram screeches and the brick power station burns its low-grade sulfurous stuff so your molars hum: look across. All the way across the deliberate emptiness to the other side, where the opposite workshops hang their laundry and the amber sodium makes everything look cooked.

I'm not there. I'm behind you. On the wall you came down along.

Flat.

Find the place where the chisel marks from last year are still sharp-angled, still angry, and right next to them the rock has already given up and gone soft with moss — that black-green carpet, thick as a tongue, that grows because it didn't ask permission. The moss is always wet. It weeps even when the rest of the wall is just damp. Condensate runs through it in dark fingers and keeps going, over my tail, over my toes.

I am the gecko on that wall.

Not a grand one. Not Big Grandpa the catfish under the market bridge that the kids feed heads to. Just a pit gecko, one of hundreds, female, old for a gecko, which means maybe four wet years. Translucent belly, heat-pink. Toes splayed wide the way you have to splay if you want to live on near-vertical basalt that sweats.

They live damp. All of them. Even me — especially me. My skin never dries. It's not scales the way Topside people think of scales; it's softened, fine-grained, beaded constantly with the same water that beads on human knuckles. I drink off my own back. I lick the seep off the moss. When the algae blooms sweet-rotten below, I taste it in the air even up here.

I don't move fast. Nothing here does except rockfall, and rockfall is failure.

You could watch me for a whole noon and think I was dead, stuck to the warm tuff like a dropped glove. I am not dead. I am doing what the repair crews do halfway up a ladder when they sit down mid-bolt to watch a dragonfly: I am waiting correctly.

*thud... thud... thud...* go the pumps on their floating platforms. I feel it through the stone, through my belly, the same way the humans feel it in their molars. Under that, the wall itself ticks — roaches and crickets in the cracks, a second heartbeat. Over that, the chittering of the swallows nesting on the I-beam six feet to my left, the ones whose shit the humans have decided not to fight, just to hose. At dusk the swallows will shut up and the bats will pour out right past my nose, so close their wings move the humid air and make the moss shiver. I will not flinch. They have never wanted me. I am too flat, too still, too much like the rock.

What I want are the midges. The weather of them. At amber-light time the sodium cones fill with clouds so thick they look like smoke, and even up here on the wall the whine finds me. The humans go inside and burn their green coils and play cards and half-shout. I stay out. I stay *on*. My tongue — absurdly long, absurdly fast for such a slow animal — takes them one by one out of the air. The dragonflies take them a hundred at a time; I take them one at a time. That's the contract.

And I watch.

Because geckos on every warm rock is not decoration. It's surveillance. The humans think they are the watchers — the Upper Ring with their cisterns and their logbooks — but I was on this wall before that walkway was bolted two feet to the left after the slump last rainy season. I saw the thin new crack appear after someone rushed a rivet and the rock fell fast, the way it does when you are impatient. I watched the two brothers at false dawn scrape guano into barrows, not talking, their shoulders already knowing each other's rhythm the way pair-workers do. I watched a Bottom woman nap with her feet in the rust-water, a cat pressed damp against her ankle, a baby in a hammock over the water breathing with the pumps. *jug-o-rum, jug-o-rum,* go the frogs, and I breathe with them too, through my skin.

I have my own human, in the gecko way of having a human. He works the repair shop. Middle Ring heart, rust under his nails that never comes out, the crescent moon. He eats under the sodium even when it's hottest. He talks to his swallow nest: "You going to shit on my welder again?" He leaves his hard hat on the ledge below me at end of shift and sometimes a moth the size of his hand thumps the bare bulb and falls, stunned, onto the warm basalt next to me. He never sees me eat it. He thinks I'm a stain.

Once, during the cool hour before dawn when the whole pit mists over and you can't see the bottom at all — white, breathing, milk in a cup — my patch of moss got too cold by a degree and stopped weeping and I had to move three inches to the left to stay wet. Three inches took me an hour. That's correct. What is wet stays wet, but you have to stay with it.

If you stood on the Bottom planks at night, when only the night-shift lights are on and the water doubles them, wobbling amber and buzzing fluorescent under your boots — you wouldn't see me. You'd feel me the way you feel the warm rock at your back: as part of the bowl breathing in and out. Small, loud, dim, crowded, empty. In and out.

But if you climbed back up at noon, slow because the air has weight and every piece of metal can burn you, and you put your hand on the warm basalt to steady yourself on the stairs cut straight into the wall —

You'd put your hand an inch from my head.

You'd see my eye, lidless, reflecting the bare bulb in its wire cage, the tram, the laundry lines across the void, the vortex of bats still hours from happening.

You wouldn't hear me over the drone and the drip on tin and the thudding negotiation of the pumps. You would have to lean in. You would have to half-shout without knowing why.

And I would keep looking at you, patiently, pruney-skinned and damp as you are, waiting for the silence that means something has gone wrong, or for the midges to come back, whichever comes first.
