You would not find yourself on the lip.

At first you think you do — boots on basalt, hands in pockets against the 5-degree air, looking down into that blue dusk well. The human visitor. The one who came to see. Your breath ghosts. Your eyes try to adjust and then don't. You hear the wind turn over in the bowl and the rock crack like a gunshot and below you, carried impossibly, the cement plant humming.

You stay there for about three breaths.

Then the scale betrays you. That pocket was sixty meters to a human and half that deep, which is to say: to you, it is everything. It is the whole country. You can't hold the ridge and the glacier and the valley railway in your head at once anymore. Your field of view narrows, as eyes do in dim light, until there is only the damp, and the green that shouldn't be thriving, and the sound of sweating stone.

You find yourself lower. Much lower.

Not fallen. Settled. Sunk.

You are in the pelt itself, where stone stops and organism hasn't quite started.

At first you think you are the carpet, flat and made — then you remember carpet is the wrong word. You are not flat. You are *pelt*. You are thickness. You are three layers pretending to be one.

For a while you are the lichen, because you have to start where everything starts on young rock that hasn't yet learned to be soil. You are grey-white on grey, invisible when dry, a stain on limestone. You have no mouth, no root, no front or back. You drink straight through your skin. You eat light bounced off low cloud, twice-weak light that no tree would bother with. You exhale acid, a thousandth of a millimeter at a time, pitting the sharp fresh fracture so the wind can't take it. You flush ochre and sulfur and copper-green when the mist condenses. You are patient enough to be called non-living by anything with eyes, and that is why you are still here.

Then the water comes — not as rain, as a suggestion of rain, as beads — and you become softer, and you become the moss.

This is when you understand who lives here, who has *always* lived here.

You are cushion. You are mat. You are huddle. Each shoot pressed to the next shoot not because you like company but because warmth is a thing you make together out of dead sisters underneath you. You have no true roots, only rhizoids, hair-fine fingers in a crack. You are a mattress that breathes. You hold ten times your weight in cold water and you sigh when a hand presses you and you sigh back slower.

You do not stand up. Standing up is how you get peeled off the lip by the wind that falls over the arete. You hunker. You make a dome so tight the wind slides over you. Inside your dome it is ten degrees warmer than the 5-degree air that makes the visitor zip his jacket. You die underneath and keep growing on top of your own peat, half an inch per century, which is fast enough here.

Woven through you — not on you, *through* you — you are also the humiliated trees.

You are *Salix herbacea*, the least willow. A whole tree the size of a coin. Your trunk is underground, a sideways cord creeping through grit and peat. What you dare to send up into that turbine-whine wind is two glossy round leaves and, for two furious weeks in August, a catkin the size of an ant. You are campion making a pillow so dense you are your own climate. You are saxifrage opening white-thread stars not for bees — there are no bees in this well, no butterflies who strayed in — but for yourself, folding your own pollen in the cold because waiting for a visitor is death. You are hair-grass at fifteen centimeters, the giant of this nation, shivering when you think there is no wind.

You thought green meant eyes would come to eat it. Here green means green only, because being eaten would be a catastrophe with so little bounced light to replace you. Being crushed is different. Being crushed is expected.

And so you are constantly being crushed, and constantly surviving it.

You hear it long before you feel it, because you have no ears but the bowl is a drum. The tick of water in a joint. The sigh of a whole vegetative mat sliding an inch downhill under its own saturation. The clean gunshot crack as a door-sized slab calves off the east wall — that young, sharp, unsmoothed scree that never found its angle of repose — and smears white scar and green under talus at the bottom. For a day the scar is bare rock. By the third day you are already bleeding back in, liverwort from the wet seam above, protonema like green frost, a thread of willow finding the new dust. In a season the scar never happened. You do not reseed. You *re-creep.* A fragment torn off and tumbled ten meters downhill reroots where it lands.

Under you, where even you can't see, you are the rest of the engine that lets you live without animals, without sun, without soil: black yeast in the lichen, white fungal threads digesting the slow-cold peat, cyanobacteria fixing nitrogen straight from damp air, and — if you want to count something that almost has eyes — the tardigrade, walking through your forests like a slow bear, drinking the film of water around a single moss leaf and going still when it freezes.

You don't go still. You go on.

You live in the permanent pre-dawn at 3,000 meters on the dark north side of a mountain still deciding what shape it wants to be, in the small room it forgot to finish. You live in blue dusk, off mist and seep and sweat, loud as a factory floor though nothing was ever built here, with the wind keening through fluted chimneys of basalt and the stamping-mill clatter of water down five chutes at once and the far valley's freight brakes shrieking two thousand meters below until it sounds like it comes from the rock itself.

You have no eyes. You have no need for them. You put your hand flat to yourself and you are cold and alive at once.
