Not under the sea. Not *in* the sea.

That took a while to understand.

At first I thought I'd be down low, where the work is. Because that's where the story is — in the dark amber, thirty meters down, stone-diver heavy with a net of white tufa, watching the basalt tick and crack open like fresh bread underwater, holding twenty minutes of air in a barrel chest while a vent sighs hot beside me. It fits a certain fantasy of usefulness. The one who goes deep. The one who re-ties the ropes when the mountain shrugs.

But I don't have that chest. Not yet. I would waste my air trying to hurry down there.

Then I thought I'd be up in the light, 10 meters where the sun is still a coin behind jade, with the children. The light-gardens. It sounded kind — tending the lungs, brushing silt off a breathing bell that glows faint, tapping it to hear if it goes *pong* — full — and deciding who gets to breathe that evening. But that job is too holy and too terrible. A child who decides if grandmother waits another day? I couldn't carry that counting so young. And I move too much in bursts. I'd tear a leaf.

So I kept sinking, slowly, the way everything has to here. Not swimming. You don't swim in the Perched Ocean. You pull, hand over hand, and the green broth of the water holds you. I let the silt curtains part for me. I let the slow warm rivers that crawl past each other over weeks turn me a little. I didn't settle.

What settled was this:

You would find me at about fifteen, sixteen meters down. 

Not quite light, not quite low. Midwater, where the olive-gold disc has already dissolved into a general green light with no source, where you cannot tell up from down unless you watch which way the single-file oxygen pearls are going. That's where I hang.

There's a platform there — not a platform the way you think of it — a living mat of stitched lily-pads the size of doors, each one translucent and warm under your feet because it's still photosynthesizing. It sways. Everything sways. It's moored with four ropes as thick as my waist to two new bone-domes of basalt that are still cooling below us. The ropes are alive. If you don't trim them they try to root in your house. My house *is* a loom. The pillars are braided kelp-wood still growing, coaxed sideways into walls. I didn't build it. I coaxed it for seven years and then hollowed it.

I am not a diver. I am not a child. I am what this city makes when it leaves someone in one place long enough to learn stillness. A Weaver. A second hands to a Bell-Keeper.

That is the phrase they use. *Second hands.* The old woman who keeps the lung for our cluster of six houses — her lungs are ruined from a lifetime of shallow sips, she lives with her face always a little too close to the bell — she needs someone who can still hold for twelve minutes and still hear the rope.

My job is rope.

You think a submerged city would boom and click. It doesn't. This high, warm, perched water eats sound. A stone dropped goes *thunk* and then nothing. Voices are only for under the bell, hoarse, short, expensive. So we don't talk. We ping.

I'm good at it now. My hands have grown that little bridge of callus between thumb and forefinger from pulling green hair into cord. At any hour I have one hand on a different line without thinking about it. Two quick pulls on the north line: *look, the manta is passing.* Three slow on the south: *come, help string.* A long tremor that makes my whole forearm buzz: *vent, vent, vent — cut anchor three.* 

The children think I'm magic because I can answer an elder three platforms away while my face is still underwater, hanging in my toe-loops, humming one note into the kelp trunk to keep it from tangling. It's not magic. The water is so still — *dynamic 1.1, slow* — that a tremor will travel hundreds of meters if you don't thrash. What is still, grows. What moves fast, breaks. I learned to be still.

My skin is that iron-stained olive-brown you get here, green at the knuckles and knees where the bacteria love to live. I oil it at night so it doesn't itch. My hair is cropped to the skull. My chest is too wide; when I pause at work my ribs do that huge, slow expansion once, and then I don't breathe again for a long while. I have the pale mask around my mouth, the little lid-sheen in my eyes, the permanent green in the whites from the silt that always hangs.

You would find me between jobs. That's the only time you *can* find anyone here — in the pause while you hang on the rope and let your blood fizz out, conserving.

At 10:00, I am with the children at the lung, though I am no longer a child. Our lung is inverted, huge as a boat, a single mutant leaf grown patient. All day the forest pearls — *tick* — a bubble off every leaf in the hanging forest that layers itself thirty meters thick in every direction, lattice of leaf/root/stem — and those bubbles snag in the bell's hairs and merge. It takes ten days to fill. I brush the underside with a soft brush so tufa doesn't seal it. Inside, shrimp the size of your hand clean the hairs. We never eat them. I tap the bell. *Pong.* Ready. The old Keeper nods. She will call the breathing order. Hospitality here is air. To withhold it is murder.

At 14:00, I am in the weaving loft, pulling veil-filament into cord, knapping a fresh tufa knife that will dissolve in a month, humming one note until my breath runs out. The whole town glimmers amber around me, jars of stirred algae like constellations, each house a bead of light swaying tethered. Pollen-smoke of oily spores hangs motionless as a cloud for days at my shoulder. I don't wave it away. It will be there tomorrow.

And once a week, the blind filterer passes over — a manta the size of a canoe, beating so slowly you think it's drift — and we all stop. Not from reverence. Because to move when it passes is to waste a wake. We watch its shadow cross the lily canopy. The glass shrimp clicks once. A jelly pulses. That is the only fauna for a day.

I am becoming good at not wanting to hurry. This water was lifted 3 kilometers into the sky to teach that. The cordillera is still being born under us, black domes pushing up, shattering into glass rubble that has never felt wind, steaming despite being underwater. Any anchor I tied last year is a meter tighter this year. The mountain ticks as it cools. Somewhere beyond the last platforms, a new piece of the world cracks white-hot and very slowly rises.

She taught me the last rule: *Anchor deep, but not too deep — vents open without warning.* She meant the basalt. She also meant a life. I used to think I'd be special here. A diver, a decider. Now I understand the city wants people who can stay in one column of warm, high water, tend one bell, keep one set of ropes thrumming, and count breaths correctly.

If you come — and you arrived the way everyone does, already submerged, warmth closing around you without a splash — you will feel a rope brush your arm. It was left there on purpose.

I won't call to you. I'll ping. Two quick pulls: *look.* Three slow: *come.* 

My bell is full. There's a trembling silver dome beside my house, tasting of green and iron, enough for eight careful people if we don't waste. My chest is full. I can give you my minutes.

Duck your head up. Break the membrane into gas. Gasp, if you need to. I will count for you.
