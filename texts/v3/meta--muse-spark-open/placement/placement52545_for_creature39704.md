You would not find yourself standing at all.

Not here. 

At first you think you would be the observer - the tall thing that casts the only shadow for kilometers, the boots burning through their soles, the eye trying to take in the whole Texas of pattern at once. You try to be that. You stand and the sun finds you immediately. There is nowhere to hide on a plain that is all horizon. The light has weight. You can feel your own water leaving you, bead by bead into air that wants it. You cannot stay tall here. The place corrects you.

So you crouch. Then you kneel. Then you are on your hands, and that is closer.

Even then you are still too big. From a meter up the world still looks empty, still looks like bone-colored geometry repeating until it becomes geology. You have to get lower. Cheek-to-the-lip low. Shadow-maker low. You have to become a piece of weather for a second — a small eclipse moving just an inch over a doorway no wider than your smallest finger.

That's where you find yourself.

Not in the middle of a basin, where the sun hammers the cracked porcelain and the milky water sits without rippling. Not on the crest of a wall, where at noon there is sixty degrees of death in the clay.

You find yourself in the thin line that only exists for an hour.

The shady side. The north-facing underside of a lip no higher than your ankle, where night has left a dark track of condensation. Where the earth is still cool to the touch while everything else is already beginning to burn. A place the size of a palm, that will not exist at midday.

And you would not be one. You would be barely one.

At first you think you might be the shrub. That would be a good, lonely thing to be — wiry and grey-green and waxy, rooted exactly in the corner where two walls meet and dew runs down both sides. Patient. Waiting weeks for seven minutes of rain to turn your soil to dark chocolate. Spreading roots the size of the world underneath walls you did not make but have learned to use.

But the shrub is still too solitary, too still. It waits. This place does not wait.

Then you think you might be the crust. The dark green-black skin at the bottom of the pan that was water last week. That would be truer — you would crack and curl and flake and become dust, and not die, just pause. You would feel rain as a violence, a sudden jelly, an hour of frantic green light before drying again. You would be the glue that holds the basin together when the water leaves.

But the crust still only holds. It does not carry.

No. Lower.

Smaller. Paller.

You are the mouth that made the question water is trying to answer.

You are amber-white and translucent, the length of a grain of rice, soft-bodied, blind. You can see your own dark gut through your own belly. Your jaws are not for biting but for scooping, for rolling wet silt against your chest into a ball the size of a mustard seed. You have no name for yourself. You have no queen to see - she is kilometers away and deeper than your arm can reach, but you are still her, the way a hand is still a body.

You find yourself backing out of that perfectly round doorway when the shadow finally falls across it. The cool air has been breathing out of it all morning. Inside, it is vaulted and damp and pillared, it smells of chewed cellulose and black fungus breathing. Outside, it is the anvil.

You freeze when the light touches you. Then you reverse. Then the light moves a millimeter and you flow forward again with a thousand others, not marching, flowing, a sheet of eyelash-soft rustling that is the only sound small enough to hide in this silence.

This is your hour. The plain is sweating awake. Every lip beads. Every pan offers a palm-sized mirror. And you have work that cannot wait for tools, for metal, for plans.

You lay your pellet. You press it. You lick the salt to cement it. You butt its seam against the last pellet your sister laid. You widen a crack by a mandible-width to coax more night air down. You nibble a spillway one grain wider because yesterday the rain marched east.

You will never see the river you are making. No single one of you will. You will live and die in a patch of clay no bigger than a hand. But the plain remembers for you. The plain is the memory. Texas-large and Brazil-large, it keeps every decision every builder ever made as a low wall, a blunt canal, a deepened corner.

You pat the fresh daub smooth. It will crack by noon. Tonight you will fix it a little lower, a little wider. In ten thousand years, water will finally know where to go.

You feel the continent breathe under you - in as dew, out as steam - and you breathe with it.
