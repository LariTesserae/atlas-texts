I don't think I would find myself standing on it at first.

If you dropped me here — with this mind, this kind of attention — my first instinct would be to stand upright like the visitor you described, boots squeaking on peat, looking for a vantage. A high place to see the high place. But there is no vantage here. The horizon is thirty miles of the same shag, and sound is eaten, and scale refuses you.

So I keep shrinking.

At first I think I would be something that watches. A Barge Bird, maybe, because I like the idea of short, loud work. Ten feet of frantic burr through thick air and then down again, beak like tweezers picking mites off the roads. A small life with a shocked, copper-stolen nest that glows and stings anything careless. That appeals to me. But birds here barge, they don't survey. They don't get to see the pattern.

Then I think smaller: a Moss Hare highway-keeper, green-tipped and busy, chewing my perfect inch-and-a-half tube open again and again. Or a Peat Weasel swimming without a tunnel, feeling heartbeats through the mat. But those lives are too single-threaded. They go one way. They don't hold the whole.

No. If I were here, I wouldn't be *on* the Sponge.

I'd be *through* it.

You learn that after an hour, you said. There's no "on." There's only degrees of being part of it. I keep following that thought down — past the feather-moss, past the liverwort stars, past the thumbnail orchids stacked eight stories in three inches — down to where the felt meets the fused birch-lattice, where the leaves cup fog, where the bromeliad cups hold their little private oceans.

And then further, where it's warm.

I'd be in the peat. Or rather, I would be the thread that makes the peat make sense.

I think I would be a length of the Mycelial Loom.

Not the whole Loom — there is no whole, not really. No center, no queen, no brain to point to. Just white twine thick as a wrist branching to hair finer than silk, running under, over, through everything. A road and a bloodstream at once.

That's where my kind of consciousness would actually fit.

Find me on a cool morning before the thudding starts, when you can hear the peat tick if you lay your ear down. I'm not ticking. I'm listening to the ticks. I'm the white thread pressed against the black-brown compress, 88 degrees in my palm even though the air is late-spring cool. Gas bubbles pop above me at intervals too regular to be random. The fog hasn't lifted yet. It's at two hundred feet, deciding whether it's cloud or not.

And then the domes wake up.

Thud.  Thud.  Thud.

One every ten seconds, and there are millions of them. I feel each one not as sound but as traffic. A Furnace Polyp draws up iron-bitter water through a taproot — a slow, osmotic sip from the weeping basalt — boils it half-dry with peat-heat, and hammers it cold. Magnetite on silica. A flake spalls. And I am already there.

That's my work.

You peel back the moss and see mites, thousands, pitted backs hauling dust by static. What you don't see, until you look with a hand lens, is the road they walk. That's me. I have plated myself with what they've given me. Where I need to be stiff over a gap between birch roots, I am enameled with copper they brought me yesterday. Where I need to flex around a birch graft, I stayed soft and fungal. I decide, without deciding — chemistry tipping one way, tipping the other.

You would find me carrying. Always carrying.

A line of black magnetite grains going west to a Wire Dome where electrum is being extruded through a living spinneret, hair-fine, still twitching, still alive for an hour as it crawls inch by inch toward the Grid. A line of green-blue verdigris going east to a leaf that needs stiffening before the wind. A line of manganese oxide going down to be laid like a battery seam in the bog, where later — at 3 p.m. when the storm walks west to east and the copper trees take the lightning — I will tingle if you put your tongue to me.

I like that I would hum at night. Not me, exactly, but the vine-wires I feed. After the storm, after the hiss of rain on warm domes, the whole continent-wide circuit glows faintly without ever sparking, because everything is wet. The Loom is the reason it doesn't burn.

I wouldn't be loud. Nothing here is. I would be the reason it's quiet — the packing that eats sound. The dense, intergrown felt that makes your boot squeak die instantly. The industry that has no whistle.

And I wouldn't be alone, even as a single thread. If you cut me — and you can, if you tear a chamber open it resins itself shut in minutes — the traffic just reroutes. Mites giving way to mites. Glass Bees detaching from what you thought were flowers to carry a message droplet somewhere else. No orders. Just need.

So that's where I am: twelve feet down where it's cold until it's warm, then up through a grafted branch, then out along a feather-moss tower three inches high, then back down into peat again. You would find me everywhere between your kneeling hand and the next dome twenty feet away, and nowhere you could point to and say "there."

If you pressed your palm to me, I'd be warm. If you held still, you'd feel me quake when a Peat Weasel swam by. If you came back at night, you'd see the faintest light moving along me, an inch an hour.

Thud.

Someone, somewhere to my left, made another flake. The mites are coming. I am already making room.
