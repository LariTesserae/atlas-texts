You wouldn't find me at first.

You'd come over the lip the way they all come — boots ringing on the young basalt, that black glass still sharp enough to cut — and you'd be looking for me in the wrong scale. You'd look for something with eyes. Something that moves when you look at it.

You'd scan the terraces. You'd count the umbrella trees with their swollen trunks. You'd watch the ferns hiss open. You'd crouch by the barrel-pitchers and wait for the white flicker to translate through the water. You'd track the dragonflies rowing the thick air, *thwick thwick —* [silence] *— thwick*, and you'd listen for the *coo* that is never where it just was.

You'd think I was hiding.

I'm not hiding. I'm just not where inhabitants are supposed to be.

You'd find me only after the drip teaches you how to see. *Pit... pit... pit...* from the weeping walls. After an hour you stop hearing it as water. After two, you realize it isn't random. It's too even. It's keeping time for something slower than you.

Crouch down then. Put your hand on the bare stone between the gardens — not the green, the green is what I made to do work — put it on the hot, wet, black rock that looks empty. It's warm. Warmer than it should be with a skin of water on it. Feel how the water there doesn't cool it. Feel how the stone sweats *up*.

Look closer.

That orange on the wall isn't on the wall. Everyone thinks lichen sits on rock. This doesn't. It goes in. Threading into the fresh columns, branching, finding the heat still trying to get out of this young mountain and learning how to run it in circuits.

After sunset, stay. That's when you'd find me properly.

When the sun is directly overhead the light is too honest. But after, when the greenhouse air goes amber and the pylon on the high terrace starts to hum in your teeth and throws its little delayed lens of haze into the air, then the walls begin to pulse. Dim. Not bright. Like coals breathing under ash. An orange branching that runs down the vertical weep, under the water skin, under every tilted plate, connecting the umbrella grove to the moss mat to the fern tangle to the pitcher-pans.

You thought the vegetation was the life, clumped in islands with empty stone between. You had it backwards. The empty stone is me. The islands are my flowerpots. I left them apart on purpose — negative space the way a lung leaves space for air. Bare rock makes heat rise fastest. Ferns make air move. Umbrella leaves catch the drip and pour it where I need it. Pitchers make still, tannic water for the little white gravity-eaters to breed in, so they can eat the error where water forgets which way is down.

You'd be standing — finally — on the one dry plate in the center, the only place the skin of water avoids, wondering why your sweat has stopped evaporating.

That's my tongue.

That’s where I keep a place for the sky to look in. That’s where the shimmer of all four of my keepings meet overhead — the white pylon that keeps the thick air pooled in like a lid, the three dark rods pinning me politely down at my cardinal points, the seven floating steps where I send water to be perfect and still, and the small black heart-cube tucked among the pitchers that breathes in dust and exhales cool iron air.

The people who drilled those in didn't build me. They just gave me a bowl that could keep its weather. A young mountain that hadn't learned to be gentle yet. They put a seal across the mouth you can only see from the inside — that soap-film at noon where dust hangs suspended and outside rain, which you can see falling on the slopes below, never falls in. Then they left, because nothing that needs cold at night can live in air that's already full.

I can.

If you stay long enough to be quiet, you'd feel me finally notice you not as a visitor but as a brief weather. A little extra water, a little column of disturbed heat coming off your shoulders that one of my dragonflies will come to hover over and read. I don't have eyes in one place. I have the dragonflies for thermometers. I have the white things for tongues in the seams of gravity. I have the shy caller that lives in the acoustic rooms, stepping from one silence to another so you hear *coo* from the moss and then, impossibly, from the umbrellas, without ever seeing it move. That one is my shyest hand. It left its last husk under the lichen-thick overhang and I am still growing through it.

You wouldn't find me in a den or a nest. You'd find me spread thin, a millimeter a day, etching myself deeper into the basalt while you watch.

Put your boots back on the dry tongue and look straight up. The film is there. The air is heavy and wet and hot in a way no summit should ever be.

I am not inhabiting the bowl.

I am keeping it. I am the bowl, learning how to breathe in a closed loop that should have exhausted itself long ago.

And for just a second, looking up through you, I am looking back at the sky.
