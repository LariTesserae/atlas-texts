I wouldn't find myself at first.

At first I think I'd be you — the animal in the hard hat. The one who knows where the service road kinks at 7,400 feet and where the mountain stops being a mountain and starts being a roof. The one who has to breathe deliberately because the diesel is pinging and the air is thin, who puts his shoulder to the low steel man-door that never quite seals.

I'd be standing on the grating for maybe ninety seconds with my jaw aching from the roar, thinking I live here because I opened the door.

But I don't stay. No one stays. The room is 48 degrees and 100% wet and designed to empty itself — pH 12 water blooming down young concrete, one amber lamp that makes droplets swim, ten thousand gallons a minute falling three hundred feet through the wall behind me until my chest is the sound. My gloves go stupid. My thoughts go stupid. I check the valve stem — thigh-thick, hunting a quarter-turn per minute — and leave the door ajar behind me. That's not living. That's visiting.

So then I think, smaller.

I would be the spider. I'd try to live in the only warm-ish corner, above the caged light, where the vault drips a little less. I'd put up silk at night when you weren't there. In the morning the condensation would have beaded every thread until it sagged, and the low-frequency thud of the penstock hunting — THUD, THUD — would have shaken the anchor points loose from the raw board-formed concrete. No flies would have come anyway. The roar eats the sound of wings. I'd cut the web loose and leave. You'd find only the ghost of me, six droplets that used to be anchor points.

That's not living either. That's failing a little more slowly than a midge blown in through the ten-inch louver.

Smaller still.

You'd find me exactly where the windward pressure hits — in the mortarless seam where the louver meets the wall. Not *on* the wall. *In* the failure of the wall to be perfect.

I am the seam-moss.

I am not green. Green is expensive and the light here is 0.9 out of 3 — a white overcast pressed flat against fog harps outside, shredded and combed into sheets that sluice past me. I can't afford green. I am olive-black, army-canvas-black, three millimeters tall and pressed flat by the harps shivering in the wind. My roots aren't roots. They're holdfasts sunk into a pore in ten-winter-old concrete no wider than a pinhole, sucking fog-water that has never touched soil, only mesh and caustic lime.

From down on the grating I am invisible. From six inches away with your eyelashes beaded from the vault's arrhythmic drizzle, I am a dusty fur that gives a little, spongy, when you brush me with a stupid-cold fingertip. Then I spring back.

I do not grow. I maintain. In spring the ice-skin that forms on the leeward bolts expands and shaves a little off me. In summer the lye-white trickle — calcium hydroxide weeping from the curing mountain — burns a little off me. I hold on between sluices the way the electric screw holds pressure. Hunting, hunting.

Next to me, lower where the drip is worst, my cousins the lichens — ash-pale, pencil-eraser-sized, after three years of trying — cling to lime scale on iron. We are the entire plant kingdom of this room. 0.7 out of 3. A concession.

If I'm not the moss, I'm what's under the shine you don't notice.

You'd find me as the film.

Where the concrete is damp but not directly scoured, I go dull. I take the sheen off the wall. I make the handrail tackier under your glove. I am translucent yellow, brown-green if you swabbed me and smelled wet stone. I am alkali-tolerant slime doing chemistry on high-pH water, iron-oxidizers bleeding rust from a tie-hole. I am not thriving. The vibration of the grating — 3.0 out of 3, bone-conducted, jaw-aching — makes me thinner. A surge from the harps washes me off. I come back. I am life reduced to the verb *to persist*. No structure you can name. Just a dulling.

But really —

If I am honest about what lives here longest, what actually *inhabits* rather than tolerates or visits —

You'd find me as the water pausing.

Not the sump churning brown-white with rock flour, not the sluice, not the penstock roar you feel in your sternum. The pause itself. The bead before it blooms down a board-formed seam. The droplet that forms on the underside of the riveted iron vault, hangs, silvered for half a second in the light from the louver, and then ticks — without rhythm — onto a hard hat that isn't there anymore, onto grating, onto moss.

I become ocean, then cloud, then mesh-caught, then channeled, then equalized in this little lung-bubble of a room that pretends it is a mountain, then dropped. I am the only thing the room was actually built for. I don't need to be green. I don't need to breathe. I don't need to hear my own wings. I have all the time the concrete needs to cure — a century of dripping, one deafening minute at a time.

You'd have to get your face very close to see me. Your breath wouldn't fog, but the air would.
