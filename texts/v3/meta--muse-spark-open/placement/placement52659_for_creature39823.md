I wouldn't be high.

At first I thought — gull. That would be the easy answer. To be above it, fat and white in the steam-thermals, to know when the gravity goes light on Tuesday and you don't have to beat your wings at all, just hang. To know enough to leave a roof a minute before the canal hisses and pillows up new basalt. But I don't want to be above. Nothing in the Valley wants to be high. The hills are low and round-shouldered for a reason. The city is low for a reason. The ground is still soft underneath and it gives.

No. Lower.

Then I thought: reindeer. Thirty of us at dawn, moving through what used to be a turbine hall and is now a birch-tunnel alley, stepping over sleeping people. Tongueing the moss off walls in perfect rectangles. I like that — being municipal, not wild, not tame. But I have hands. I kept feeling my hands in the image. Black at the cuticles no matter how you wash.

So lower still, but not on hooves.

You'd find me on the third floor that is really a second floor because the first floor sank three inches last spring and we left it. We jacked the whole house up on its birch runners and left the old ground floor to the moss. It sealed the windows shut in a month. Now it glows faintly at night from the fungal farm my neighbor keeps down there.

You'd find me in the maintenance hour before the pressure changes.

Not outside, because there is no outside anymore. My district is one of the packed ones where you walk from a turbine hall into a kitchen into a chapel without going out. I live in the seam between a forge wall and a greenhouse tier. The forge wall is warm. That's why we have the seam at all. In winter when it's -20 and the wind comes sideways off the unfinished hills, the wall sweats. That sweat is my weather.

Who am I? I'm not a foreman, not a priest.

I am the one who oils and scrapes. We don't have a proper name for it because it's not a profession, it's just keeping your right-of-use. The children scrape, the old scrape, and in the middle so do I.

I wake when the dynamo hums a little higher. You feel it through the floorboards before you hear it — that faint whine behind glass, through felt boots. My boots are reindeer-hide, soft, swollen at the toe. My apron is leather with too many pockets. Copper bracelet on the left wrist, not tight, green at the edges. On heavy-gravity Thursdays it aches and I know to check the door.

First, the window. Every morning.

The moss comes back 20 centimeters thick if you let it. Not a dusting — a mattress. It hangs in curtains from the eaves. It tried to take my window in the night, that black-green lichen crust over the glass, the dwarf birch shoot that found the crack between brick and pipe and wedged in. I take the flat bone knife — mine is reindeer shin, polished by my mother's hand before mine — and I shave. Not clean. You never scrape clean. You leave a border, an inch of moss all around the frame, so it knows to come back, so it doesn't punish you by sealing the whole wall. It's negotiation. The light that comes through after is pale, normal, high northern sun diffused through cloud, good enough to read by. Shadows linger a half-second after I move my hand away. I don't watch that too long.

Then the pipes.

I walk the pipe forest that runs through our building like intestines. Everyone's building has it. Steam pipes, drip pipes, canal-feed pipes sweating condensation even when it hasn't rained. Intermittent rain comes hard twice a week and is gone in twenty minutes, but the dripping inside the walls never stops. That's my second weather. *Tick* on a leaf the size of a dinner plate. *Thit thit* that might be a pipe fox, soot-black, never coming outside, hunting condensation rats above my ceiling. I leave a dab of lard on the joint by the elbow. If the pawprints show up in the new rust tomorrow, the Oilers will say it means the pressure will hold.

Long-spouted can, oil that smells like coal smoke and wet iron. Hinges, joints, the axles of the gantry sledge that crawls overhead on cables building-to-building. That gantry carries ore, carries moss-bales, carries someone's kid clinging with both hands, silent. I anoint the door hinges because on light-gravity Tuesday the canal water will dome up and the doors will float loose, and on heavy Thursday they'll stick and trap you if there's a fire. An old woman died that way in the sink of '83 before I was born. The Rememberers in boiler suits still tell it when they bless new basalt with boiling water and copper wire. It's not superstition. Copper seeds moss. Boiling water tests if the ground will hold.

Through the floorboard crack I can see the Hydrosts ten-high in their greenhouse tiers against the mill wall, green-skinned, hair bleached by grow-lights. Their thumb-hummingbirds are already out, impossible this far north. You hear them before anything else — the only buzz in the quiet. The city should be deafening: drop forges, rolling mills, canneries for lichen, pump houses keeping the canals from stagnating. But snow and moss swallow it. Steam swallows it. The thump of the forge three blocks over comes up through my boots, not my ears. So when the hummingbirds swarm the birch-tunnel that used to be an alley, you feel the heat of them passing your cheek.

Midday I eat standing up. Fish with translucent heads and lights behind their eyes, though the canal isn't deep or dark. My brother is a Canal Listener, poles a flat-bottomed barge through the braided river that never learned to be a river. He can taste which house-cistern will go sulfurous next. He wears a necklace of dead lanterns when he goes out at night. He says the fish remember being river fish before the river existed. I believe him. Here, things condense like dew.

In the afternoon the reindeer commute comes through.

We feel them before we see them because the sway changes. I have the short steps, knees soft, low to the ground, that you learn when you're taught to stoop early under birch-root ceilings. Visitors get motion sick from how we sway when we stand still. The reindeer don't sway. They step delicately between market stalls that are really just wider parts of the corridor. An old bull with frost in his antlers stops in the middle of my scraping route and eats a perfect rectangle of wall-moss and stares at me with lichen-green eyes. I let him. We leave the cistern lids open for them. They leave us noticeboards. Behind him, two calves born in the lee of the forge, tasting iron before grass.

When gravity goes light, I tie off my oil can and laugh through my nose so it doesn't fog. The crane crews next district over will be playing dice on a vertical wall for the joke. The gulls — always the gulls — hover without beating. Reflections in the canal show a different arrangement of buildings than the one above us. Yesterday I saw the afterimage of a factory demolished before I was born, hanging in the cold air over the eastern quarter like haze. Tomorrow someone will see the scaffolding we haven't put up yet. No one finds this strange anymore. Or everyone does, constantly, and has made a culture of it.

My chalk stone has moved.

Every child marks one and watches the rain erase the map and draws it again better. Mine is on the sill by the warm wall. I marked it white and this morning it's six inches left, resting in moss. That means the ground sighed in the night. I should check the sledge feet. A Sledgewright two doors down — who can drag a house off a hot spring in an afternoon for tea and thanks — will come by and we will lash the willow withes a little looser so they flex instead of crack.

At night I scrape the window again, a little, because the moss grows fast when the forges leak heat and the cold presses down. I tap twice on the steam pipe. My daughter taps back from two walls over, where she is learning to tend the insulating vines that fruit at -10C and have gone feral and are strangling the abandoned mill. Her hands are already black at the cuticles. Good.

I don't own this room. No one owns land here. It doesn't stay still long enough. I own a position in the lattice because I am useful when it reconfigures. When the new island hisses up in the low river overnight — basalt pillow steaming, already mossed, sometimes with a half-wall still attached from the last time it sank — I will help lay track over it.

I lie low, hugging the cold ground, under four stories because the ground gives, under a living roof of dwarf birch and lichen and greenhouse tiers leaking humidity, under normal light, listening to the drip inside the walls and the hum of the dynamo that is like a mother's voice.

It is mostly quiet.

It is never still.
