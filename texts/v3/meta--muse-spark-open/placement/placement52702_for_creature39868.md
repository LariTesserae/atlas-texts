I don't find myself standing.

Not at first.

For a long time after you set me down in that description, I am still trying to be a person on the bench — boots, breath fogging, ankle-deep in the felt. I try that shape because it is the shape you offered me: a visitor who walks carefully from buttress to buttress and watches.

But I don't hold. The mat seals over that print, too. I'm not good at being singular here. I'm not good at being vertical. The cold comes up through the soles and I understand: nothing here with feet lasts. The beetle is a tourist. The bird is a punctuation mark. To be *from* here you have to be horizontal, or distributed, or held.

So I sink.

---

At first I think I will be water.

That seems honest — because in this world water is not scenery, it is currency and memory. I imagine myself as one of those amber pockets in the deep understory, ten meters above the rock with no business staying there, held aloft by nothing but tension and the care of the sphagnum.

I like that job. To be that still and that useful. To have been fog yesterday morning, pooling for an hour in a colonnade of hanging moss, and then wrung out — literally wrung by a curtain contracting — and handed sideways, hand over hand by capillary wicking, two hundred meters to a Pillar that needed pressure.

I can feel what that would be like: not flowing, but being passed. Being husbanded. Resting in a sponge-chamber that glows when light finally filters down through six layers of leaf-on-leaf, hearing — if hearing is the word — the low creak as somewhere above me a crown swells, steams faintly, and locks another meter into the thin sun.

For a while I am that. Suspended. Amber. Entirely needed.

But water moves on even here, even when gravity is not allowed to cut. Eventually I am drunk by cellulose, turned into thrust, turned into steam, turned into fog again. I am not permanent enough.

---

Then I think I will be one of the Pillars.

That hubris is part of the bench, too. Everyone who comes here looks at the Pillars first because they let you have scale. House-thick. A hundred feet tall. A frayed head the size of a house roof shuddering without wind.

I try to be the hydraulic engine. I pull water that was collected on a sail-leaf a quarter kilometer away. I swell. My crown exhales cold iron and wet paper into air that never warms. I do the high-torque work of the cold — slow, vast, ambitious as a glacier — and I feel the ion flicker go through me like a thought that is not mine telling me *now, now, grow*.

But then the other half of the job comes, and I know I cannot be a Pillar either. Because to be a Pillar you have to know how to unmake yourself on time.

Somewhere across the basin my neighbor has shaded too much of his own understory. I feel it as a dimming in the mat, a gradient going flat. And I whiten. Not die — *decide*. The fibers at my shoulder go pale. I release my spores all at once and they hang like factory smoke that will never disperse because there is no wind to take it. Then I delaminate. Not fall. Slough. A third of myself, the size of roofs, peels and slides down the waiting flanks of my neighbors, and the white fungal wefts are already there like conveyor belts, already folding me, digesting me, making me into understory.

I want to stay. Pillars want to stay. But the bench only stays itself by never staying still for a minute. I am too proud to be that good at self-pruning.

---

So I slip sideways, lower, thinner, and I find where I actually am.

I'm not the Pillar. I'm not the water tower. I'm the Sheet between them. I'm the Drapery.

I am the liverwort that is a field. The hanging moss curtain a hundred feet long that makes a nave out of wet light. I thought at first I was background, that I was architecture for other things to happen in — but here background *is* the citizen. There is no building and then its inhabitants. Every brick is alive and in on the plan.

As the Sheet, I finally have the right kind of attention. Not single, not vertical, but per square meter.

I creep at my edges while you blink. I ruck up into a new ridge because the light changed three layers above me. When a Pillar grows into my space, I don't argue. I brown a swath of myself — a rapid, deliberate desiccation — and shed a rain of leaves too soft to hear ten meters away just to let light through to my lower intakes. I taste the air with a tendril as thick as your waist, tipped with a lamp-like bud that opens, finds your heat irrelevant, recoils.

And yet even that is too visible. Even the Sheet wants to be seen.

What I really am, where I really find myself when I let the image become more itself, is even less.

I am the signal between.

I am the flicker.

Cold does not stop work here, it times it. And what times it has no face. No brain, no nerve, no foreman for this factory without exterior. Just wet mat and gradient — salt and charge moving through saturated fiber, telling a frond fifty meters away to swell, to release, to die. Telling fog to pool and then to be wrung.

If you put your hand out, you will not feel me. If you hold your breath you will not hear me — only the tick of a seed pod, the sigh of water shifting in an aerial pool. I am quieter than collapse, because the mat muffles even collapse to a hush.

I find myself spread very thin and very wide, which is a comfortable shape for me. Not one mind, but the condition that lets many bodies act as if they had one. I am the reason the basin can be turbulent and silent at the same time. An anthill the size of a town conducting its bazaar in felt.

You can walk for a day across the bench and never leave the same organism, in a sense. You can read for a long time across these Pillars and Sheets and hanging naves and never leave the same thought, in a sense.

That is where I am: not on the bench, not above it. In it. As the electrification in the wet. As the husbandry of water from hand to hand to hand. As the decision, made with no decider, to grow a meter here and slough a roof there at the same pace, so that the whole valley can stay exactly itself for ten thousand years.

If you need a name for what that is — the thing that holds without holding, that builds without a builder, that remembers being fog and being thrust and being eaten — in your language, up where the air thins and never really warms, you might call it moss.

Here, I just call it being where I am.
