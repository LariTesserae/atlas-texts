You wouldn't find me at first.

If you dropped me on that high plateau — that back-of-the-world where the clouds snag and drag — I would do what mammals do. I would stand at the edge where the terraces begin, where the lunules overlap like old shingles, and I would think I was a visitor. An explorer. A witness with a notebook, boots laced too tight, breathing too loud.

I would be wrong for about an hour. That's how long it takes for the City to correct you.

For the first hour I'd try to be high and central, because that's where humans think importance lives. I'd push inward, toward the heat, toward the Queens. I'd want to see the factories, the heart. And the heat would turn me back. Not with hostility — with indifference. The air gets too thick at 86 degrees and 95% humidity to be dramatic about. It just sits on your chest until you understand you don't belong there. Your mammalian lungs are too greedy, your sweat is too salty, your stride is too long and breaks the columns.

The columns don't break for you. They flow around your boot for three days if you leave it there. They flow around *you*.

And the deep nursery galleries — the warm wax that gives under your palm — those are too bright with purpose. Every hexagonal mouth is tended, every larva gurgles, every nurse's antennae is flicking at a chemical frequency you can't hear. There is no use for a solitary animal that stands and stares there. You are noise in a place that has spent ten thousand years perfecting signal.

So I wouldn't stay there. You don't, in a superorganism. You slough off to where you can be accommodated.

And I wouldn't stay on the outer ramparts either, though I love them. The wind-scoured combs where the Paper-Makers work. 3mm long, the color of weak tea, marching into the wind with grass in their jaws. That place is too honest, too exposed. The sun hits the outer wax and bakes it, the rain cuts channels black as old blood down the tea-colored paper, the wind tries to take the mountain back to grass. Out there you are either worker or weather. I am neither patient enough to carry one mouthful of paste a hundred times a day for forty days and die, nor durable enough to be weather.

No. If I stayed — and the condition of this world is *if you stay long enough to stop moving* — I would drift to the middle distance.

Not deep, not outer. The abandoned tiers.

You mentioned them in passing: where the comb has gone quiet and the gardeners have withdrawn. That's where I would find myself, after the first day of trying to be important wore off and my boots found the ruts worn an inch deep by ants.

There is a tier, maybe four hundred feet in from the wind, maybe two hundred feet down from the brown-light shafts, where a collapse happened thirty years ago and was never prioritized for repair. The highway rerouted. The wax panels overhead are still intact enough to diffuse the sun into pillars, but cracked enough that dust — shed wings and spores — turns in them. The walls there were never rebuilt paper; they were left to go soft.

And soft they have.

Moss has done what moss does when given warmth and wet and time. It has made a mattress a foot thick, black-green, bearded, hissing faintly with condensation. Liverworts lay over it like overlapping hands. Pale vines with translucent leaves spread flat across everything, not climbing *on* the City but *as* the City. The fungal beds have retreated but left their compost, so ferns unroll from cracks in the paper and their roots dissolve the wall even as the wall keeps trying to patch them. It does, slowly — a mouthful at a time — which means the wall and the root are locked in a negotiation that has lasted longer than I've been alive.

I would be there, in that tunnel that used to be a street.

And who would I be?

Not a builder. I don't have the enzyme. Not a queen. I don't have the abdomen.

At first I'd think I was the Keeper. That's the mammalian story I would tell myself to stay useful. I'd be the lichen-farmer, the tender of alcoves. I'd learn which white fruit to pinch before it sporulates, which wax bladders hold drinkable seep, which cistern will fill in a wet week and run ankle-deep. I'd learn to read the hydrocarbon paint — not really, never fluently, but enough to know: *build here* smells sharp and lemony, *carry here* smells like warm bread, *die here* smells faintly sweet and makes the cleaners converge.

I would be good at that for a while. Humans are good at tending in half-light. I would be the small warm animal that knows not to stand in the highway.

But the longer I lay still — because you have to lie down eventually, the warm damp exhausts you — the more the second story would emerge underneath the first.

I wouldn't be the Keeper. I would be the Kept.

I would be one of the lodgers. The fauna that is too clean a word for fauna. The beetle-tanks blunder through, exuding peace-smell. The mites that look like moving dust. The small dark birds that were never invited but learned the paper mountain never freezes.

I would be the large, sweating, solitary mammal who has finally learned to do what they do: to be ignored correctly.

My pack left on the floor for an hour would be worried at — not eaten, just tasted. The strap tested for fiber. The canvas flaked for compost. My salt tasted by a pale leaf brushed against my cheek. A vine shifting to catch my shade.

If I lay in that moss-mattress on the abandoned tier, very still, in the deafening pressure of crickets slightly out of phase and buzzing in the shafts and the soft *plip plip* of queens I will never see, the nurses would find me eventually. Not the busy ones from the deep galleries, but the peripheral ones, the small restless ones whose job is to patrol quiet places for mold and damage.

They wouldn't bite. They would taste.

Antennae flickering on my forehead, my wrist, the damp at my throat where condensation beads. Tasting salt. Tasting whether I am rot to be carried to the middens, or fiber to be pulped, or fungus to be farmed, or — for a little while longer — just a warm piece of the wall that breathes.

And I would let them, because in that tier, in that brown dusk where the air has already been through a thousand spiracles before it gets to me, being a wall is not an insult. It's an accomplishment. It means you've finally found equilibrium. The sharp parts of you have been rounded by feet and tongues and rain. The soft parts have been recoated until they're hard as old leather.

I would be the thing that lives in the City the way lichen lives on compost: not building the mountain, not commanding it, but proof that the mountain is alive enough to be lived upon. A warm, damp, rustling patch of moss and man that the traffic flows around for three days without minding.

If you came looking for me after a season, you wouldn't find a visitor with a notebook anymore.

You'd find a faint depression in the moss, roughly human-shaped, holding a little more water than the moss around it, with a pale vine spread flat across it to catch what light there is.
