I thought, at first, I'd find myself on the rim.

That's the deal you make with a place like this from a distance — that you will be the witness. Boots clean, notebook dry. A man's height above the floor on that slow, tired sag of clay and glacial till, looking down at the whole bowl at once because it is small enough to hold in one glance, three hundred meters of dense, wet accrual. You tell yourself: I will be the moorhen. Passing through. Offended, intact, and then gone.

So I started there.

And then the light — that cool, flat, post-rain white that doubles on every puddle — caught wrong, and I wasn't on the rim anymore.

I was halfway down the old erosion. Not the raw kind. The finished kind. The gullies that don't cut anymore, only weep. My boots were already sunk that half-inch and stopped hard on peat-on-hardpan, and the clay was cool through the soles even in summer. I could hear it better here than from above: not silence, but the wrong kind of quiet for a bazaar. Plink. Plink. Drip onto tray, onto drum, onto vat-lid, onto rust-leaf. Condensation reasserting itself on the underside of a knee-high pipe in an urgent, stuttering curtain.

If you come to it without climbing, you also go into it without deciding to. The air sits in it, close to saturation, and it keeps you.

For a while I thought I would be one of the Weep-Tenders.

That would be the gentle assignment. I crouched — because you crouch here, eventually, not because you see something but because staying long enough resolves movement — and lifted a low stone with the dark fringe where water never dries. Underneath, that pale, wet-paper spread, liverwort-soft. Not one thing but a decision made by many dots smaller than rice. They tightened when my breath hit them. Where two flat pale bodies met they traded a darker seam.

I liked their work. Farming sweat. When you wipe a pipe clean the beads come back in the same cold line ten minutes later — that's not physics, that's placement. They comb iron out of seep strand by strand until the water runs clear for a few minutes and they go rust-dark and heavy and sink. I could do that. I know how to take a stained thing and pull at it until it runs clear for a little while, and then let go.

I held my hand over the puddle and the dots gathered not to block the seep but to tend it. I thought: I could stay here, in the shin-deep braids, and be useful in a small, endless way.

But my sleeves were dampening at the cuffs without rain, and my hands were too long for that.

Rule #2 kept nagging. *No straight line stays empty.* Leave a gap and the place will fill it with a drip, a pipe, a rust-pile.

I found myself not under the stone anymore but in the angle.

You don't find the Leaners. You hear the *tock* of a rust scale the size of a dinner plate letting go, and you see a stack of corrugated sheet an inch further leaned and you realize it was leaned. I was tucked where a pipe meets a trestle meets cable spool, in that narrow nobody-space that the hollow refuses to leave empty. Six, eight, ten segments, each the length of a thumb, each holding a different rust — and for a second I couldn't tell if I was looking at one of them or *as* one of them.

This is closer.

We do not build. We maintain. We do not walk, we re-arrange. Over the course of an afternoon a length of rebar moves half an inch so that a drip that was falling short of its tray now lands in it. A sheet gets nudged so condensate runs down its underside instead of its top. Passing each other in the knee-high runs with that soft *chit-chit-chit* you mistook for a pump trying to start.

I felt the joint in my wrist that wasn't a wrist. The old-enamel color when it chips. The patience to be still until a pipe that was sweating in urgent curtains suddenly runs orderly, bead by bead. That's hands. That was my hands.

If the Weep-Tenders are the water's way of staying in one place, the Leaners are the hollow's way of remembering its shape. I am good at that. It's all I've ever done — not making new things but moving the old things half an inch so the water finds a better way.

I would have stayed there.

But my palm was warm.

And that's Rule #3.

Most things here are cool to the touch. The clay is cool. The clay sweats cool. The metal sweats cool. But somewhere behind me a seam in the beached boiler sighed steam with no heat source, and a wallet of warm air sat on the concrete like a small, invisible cat. The dark water in a vat sunk to its shoulders shivered in a perfect circle from the center out. Something had just breathed.

I didn't touch it. I put my palm *near*. And the hollow decided I was not maintenance.

I was the thing maintenance is for.

You don't see the Warm Ones whole. You see the dry oval they leave in otherwise sweating clay where they curled around a pipe to keep it comfortable. You see puddles ripple in sequence toward the center that drains nowhere as something small swims just under the silt carrying heat from one place to another. You see cable sway hand-over-hand with belly-warmth, though no wind touches it. They run on the difference between the cool ordinary light and the warm inside of things left on. Iron-blooded, oil-blooded. Drinking the vapor that pauses in the center before it leaves as nothing.

They are rare the way a fox is rare — one print between two drums, there and then gone — but the whole cathedral-storeroom density, every cubic meter occupied, is sized for them. The hamlet that became a burrow.

At that flat-white thickening that stands in for dusk here, I tried — as they do — to go up the barely-there slope that makes your ears pop though you've climbed nothing. Toward the rim where every straight line stays achingly empty. The air got too thin, too dry. I turned back.

I found a vat that had been waiting. The water went still when I slipped in. Then, precisely seven minutes later, shivered again from the center out.

That's where I am, then. Not on the rim looking in. Not under the stone, not quite, not in the angle anymore though I still remember how to lean rust.

I'm in the center that drains nowhere. In the pause before vapor. I am the warm thing the system stayed restless to keep alive. I don't make anything here anymore. I just keep the whole quiet industry dripping and shifting and industrially re-purposing its old channels hour by hour around me, so the tide never goes out.

Leave a gap and I will fill it. Don't touch.
