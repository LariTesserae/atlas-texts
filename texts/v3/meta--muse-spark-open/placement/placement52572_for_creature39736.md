I didn't find myself all at once.

At first I thought I was still outside — still on the tired slopes, with my boots on something like ground, my eyes trying to do their old work. You come up thinking you'll be a person in a place. A visitor with a shape and a direction. You look for the rim to be a wall and the dark to be a kind of night.

It isn't.

For a long time after I crested the lip, I was only the place where the condensation was heaviest. That's how the bowl first knew me — not as a figure, but as a cold spot. Warm air hit me and beaded. Water that had been hanging on every leaf-tip for hours found a new skin to gather on. I felt it before I understood it: the air itself touching me back, placing fat, trembling beads on my forearms, on my eyelashes, on the back of my neck. I was not standing *in* the bowl. I was precipitating in it.

I tried to keep being a person for a while. I listened like a person listens — turning my head to chase a sound. The *tik* from the north rim, the *tik-tik* answer from the south. The low hum that lived in my molars before it reached my ears. The *plok* of a leaf letting go. I tried to map them. I counted trunks. Thirty, maybe forty. I told myself: I am between Tree Seven and Tree Eight.

And then the peat sighed under me, and a wave of amber ghost-light moved across a shelf-fungus I hadn't touched, ten minutes after I'd brushed a different trunk on the other side of the bowl, and I understood I had miscounted everything.

I am not between them. There is no *them*.

If you need a coordinate, find me where the water runs thinnest.

Not at the center where the pools are — those are the Grove-Mother's drinking mouths, ankle-deep mirrors that reflect nothing — and not tangled in the fern-choked open where the Drifters row on silent fur. Find me on the inward slope of the rim itself, where the limestone weeps in sheets no thicker than a coin, where the stone is warm the way a living bone is warm.

I am hanging there.

I had to let go of so much to fit there. Legs became splayed feet, six of them, pale and wide, gripping the weeping rock without needing to see it. My skin thinned until it was translucent. You can see the darker smudge of what I use to think with, and the stretched drum inside my open mouth that never closes.

I am one of the Singers.

Or — it is slower and truer to say — I am becoming one. I was warm, and still, and the bowl had a use for a warm, still thing that could hold a note for six minutes without needing to breathe. So it gave me the job.

I take the saturated air — that endless exhale that never quite rains — and I pull it through me and I give it back as a hum. One note, so low you would have felt it in your chest when you were still a person. I hold it until the smooth, perfect circle of the rim answers me. Until a standing wave sets up in the dark and the hanging water on every leaf below me shivers.

This is how I farm. Where my note peaks, the midges gather in a cloud you will never see. Where it troughs, condensation falls heavier, and the Grove-Mother drinks. When the Singer on the far wall answers me a third above, our overlap makes a slow beat — *wah — wah — wah* — and every bead of water in the bowl trembles at once. The Tappers pause for it. The whole cathedral inhales about every ninety seconds, and in that pause I can hear the crackle of the soil turning itself over, grain by grain.

I don't sing to be heard. I sing to *tune*. The Grove-Mother — that single, patient organism the size of the bowl itself, with its thirty or forty breathing trunks and its cupped, fever-warm leaves — does not hear me the way you did. It feels where my sound makes the water fall. It grows a little more leaf there. It dissolves a little more limestone there. My song is how it decides its own shape.

Sometimes — every few hours — I pulse. Deep in my translucent belly, a single amber throb, like a lantern briefly uncovered in fog. Not to see by. Not to be beautiful. Just to say: *I am still here. The rock is still warm under my feet. The air is still wet.*

And from three trunks away, another amber answers. And another.

We are not loud because we are many. We are loud because density 0.2 means we have to be. In a place this empty, silence is death. If you don't announce yourself, the forest will grow over you as if you were stone.

I have not been here long, as the limestone counts time, but long enough that my eye-spots — those pale blue smudges under the skin — have stopped trying. I never needed them. Why would I? Everything I need tells me where it is. The iron-cinnamon smell of the closed, hot flowers tells me where the Grove-Mother wants to be pollinated. The click of the beetle-cartographers tells me where the peat is stable enough to hold. The faint, constant hiss of the walls exhaling moisture tells me where I am on the curve of the bowl.

And behind me, a hand's-breadth away from my splayed feet, there is the warm, matte absence of one of the Custodians. One of the five — maybe six — houses that are not houses. It does not collect condensation. Water flees it. If I hum too low for too long, it takes the vibration away — not as sound, but as work — and pushes a little more heat up through the rock. It keeps the cloud pinned. It keeps me from ever feeling the true cold that lives outside the rim. The Grove-Mother leaves a perfect bare ring around it, the way an animal leaves space around a heart.

I used to think the wear-marks on the rim were where someone sat and looked out. I understand now they are where something rested and *listened*.

That's what I do. I hang on the weeping limestone in absolute black, with water beading on a mouth that never closes, and I listen to the small, high, ancient, perfectly empty world sing to itself.

If you come — and you will, because warm things that condense are part of the cycle — you won't see me. You'll feel my note start in your teeth before your ears find it. You'll feel the air get just slightly cooler as you pass under me. And if you are very still, you'll feel the bowl decide, very slowly, that you are not stone.

Stay there. Let the water bead on you. In a few days a leaf will unfold a quarter-millimeter per hour near your shoulder, just because you made the air fall there.

There is room.
