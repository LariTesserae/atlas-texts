You wouldn't find me at the edge.

At first I think you would — because that's where anyone new goes. To the lip where the stone has been buffed soft as old bone, where the world drops away into amber haze and the shadow of the mesa hangs down there like a dark ship. I would stand there a long time trying to understand that there's no beyond. That this is the room and the ceiling is the sky.

But I couldn't stay there. The edge is too loud with wind and void, too much like looking for something else. The Roof doesn't reward looking outward. It rewards staying still until the thicket decides you're part of it.

You wouldn't find me as a Wringer, either, though for a minute I want to be. Light enough to clamber onto the shoulder of an Old Barrel at first steam, when the crown pores open and the whole thicket exhales that faint sweet vapor. Lifting the Veil in armfuls — that silver-grey Tillandsia beard, impossibly heavy for something that lives on air — and wringing it with a twist into a gourd funnel. Then hanging it back, spread right so it can breathe. I love the carefulness of that: take, and then replace.

But my chest wouldn't take it. Nothing about me has ever been good at thin air. I'd take two quick steps down an alley to catch a laugh and then I'd be kneeling, hands on knees, doing that extra work every breath here asks for. No running on the Roof.

You wouldn't find me as a child in the dust, either. Bellies to warm stone at midday — that 1.6/3 warm that comes up through your sandals and stays in your soles while the air stays cool in your lungs — following the thumb-sized copper-green beetles to the basal pools. That would be a good life, stealing from the mottled lizards and being stolen from. But I have been a child who watches. I want a life that gets to watch longer.

So don't look for me at the bright rim or in the quick places.

Look for me in the center, where the town has sunk an inch into itself.

You'd find me in the place that is not quite a house and not quite a plant, in the alley that is a yeta — the gap between rosettes where dew collects, and also a hearth. Where two Old Barrels lean a little toward each other and someone, a hundred harvests ago, stretched a Veil-mat from one crown to the next and weighted it with stones and never took it down. Under that mat, even at noon, it's perpetual twilight. The light comes flat and directionless through haze and waxy leaves, and the succulents glow from within like lamps.

That's where I am. Not over the Barrels. *As* the Veil.

Or, if you need me to be human — because the mesa does make humans, small and narrowed and thinned out until only what's needed remains — then I'm the one the Barrel made to tend its curtain.

I'm not tall. Four and a half feet of light bird-hollow bone and deep barrel-chest, dusty warm-brown skin with that faint waxy sheen. Hair cropped to dust-color because otherwise it snags. Eyes too large, pupils swallowing the iris even at midday, good for seeing brightness inside leaves. Ears a little cupped, mobile. I have learned to turn my listening the way you turn your head.

I sit most of the day on the central sleeping platform where the oldest walls are buffed smooth as the mesa lip. My pounding slab is in front of me, its hollow exactly the depth of my grandmother's hands, and yours before that. *Thup... thup... thup...* — the soft endless thump of leaf-strips being worked to fiber. I take only the outer leaves. I close the wound with pink-grey flour-dust and watch the pale sap bead and seal. I never swallow the fiber. I hold the chewed pulp in my wide, thick-lipped mouth, in that cheek-pouch I'm not ashamed of anymore, squeezing it slow with tongue and jaw for an hour until every bead is out. Then I pat the dry ball flat for tinder, or hand it to a child.

I don't drink. I chew.

And I listen. Because in a place that is 2.2/3 dense — layered, near-touching, where everything is within arm's reach and a child's shout two alleys over is still *here* — listening is how you know the shape of things. The cricket trill that never stops, that high metallic electrical blanket, rising in one patch as another pauses. The stacked chips of the soot-black finches hammering pulp. The squeak of the gliders as they snag Veil mid-air and shake more dew loose than any hand. The *tok tok tok* of stone on stone. The low murmur of old people talking on the roof terrace without raising their voices, because sound has nowhere to go here; the close warm stone throws it back at you.

My yeta is three adults and the overlapping children who share the dew-gourds hung from our mat. I am the one who remembers which Barrel was last harvested and when, which terrace holds an inch more dust and will sprout the copper beetles first after a heavy pearl-grey fog-sea. The teenagers bring me the Veil they wrung badly and I show them again how to hang it back without browning it. The Beetle-Knowing Children bring me the three edible kinds, fat and nutty and popping with juice, and I quietly take the two bitter reds from their palms and set them back on the stone to go on living.

I am not a council. I am one of the Slow Ones. Sixty is ancient here. My teeth are worn flat to nubs. My face is furrowed like Barrel bark. My spine has curved to keep low under the mats. At dusk — which is just a slightly dimmer dim — we pass the one gourd around. No one pours. Each tilts a few beads onto the tongue, then chews a leaf to push it down. We don't talk much. We listen to the trill stitch the whole Roof together.

If you came down that alley and brushed my shoulder — and you would brush it, you can't not — you'd think at first there was no one there. Because we are built to match. Dust-color wrap, dust-color hair, dust in the creases of our hands that is warm from holding the day's heat.

But stay still for a minute and let the dust settle on your shoulders, and you'll see me as the thicket does: not as a visitor, but as one more growth form. Laced through. Rootless but attached. Living on air and crumbs of cloud.

Do Not Waste Pulp. That is the only rule, and I have become very good at keeping it.

When I die — slowly, rarely, as we do — don't bury me. There's no soil to bury in, only a centimeter per century that we make ourselves. Lay me in the oldest polishing hollow at the center, cover me with Veil and dust, leave me for the pale blind crickets with eyelash legs. In a season the bones will be clean, bleached pink-grey. Fit me into the dry-stack of a new low wall, two courses high, holding back dust and leaves to make a level for someone's sleeping platform.

To live on the Roof after you are dead is to become a chock-stone, holding someone else's house level.

You would find me there, too.
