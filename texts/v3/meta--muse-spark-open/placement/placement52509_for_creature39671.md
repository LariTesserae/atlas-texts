I don't find myself all at once.

At first there's only temperature and sound. That's how it works here — you don't see properly until you've listened.

My back is against sun-warm plaster. Not hot, *held*. Kiln-warmth that stayed overnight from yesterday between 11 and 2 when the column of white came down and exploded off the white. It lives in the brick under the lime, under five generations of palm-smoothed patches. I can feel where one of the old repairs pages over another by the change in texture. A little ridge, cool. Someone's handprint under there, facing the same wall I'm facing now.

*Tick... tick... tick.*

To my left, about knee-high, that sound. A black ceramic pan on a ledge catching what runs down the wall. Every three seconds. I know without looking that the bead is building silver and fat on the curve above it before it falls. The air is thick enough to sit on me — warm dust, lime, last night's pigeon-dung smoke still caught in the bowl, goat-skin-sweat, green from the leaves over my head.

So I am below. And I'm listening for whether the Bowl is humming right.

---

Oh.

Mid-morning, after water hour. The panic of dawn lifting lids and wringing cloths and tipping every jar has gone quiet. My feet are bare — of course they're bare — splayed, pale-soled, callused just enough to grip, resting on the coolest part of the whole vessel. I'm not at the bottom. I'm one ring up from it, on the second west. Where the runnel is strongest.

I know this terrace because I patched it last week. Eight feet wide here, never wider. Right in front of me the runnel — no wider than my finger, exactly as deep as it needs to be — cuts alongside the walkway. Silver water moving with purpose. It cannot be blocked. My house knows that even if I forget; the doorway is set two inches back to let it pass. My loom is pulled aside for it. My sleeping mat rolled up to keep from its sweat.

Above me, Hanna's fig — no, *my* fig now, because adjacency is kinship here — has its roots wedged into the shared cistern. They're cracking my neighbor's plaster again. We'll negotiate it at evening when we pass peppers hand-to-hand over the ledge and pretend bartering is gossip. Its leaves are loud. Dusty. Making coin-light through the grape tunnel my sister's husband strung with twisted palm fiber, year before my daughter was born. The light comes through in coins on my arms. Sun-browned tops, lime-pale undersides.

That tells you my hands.

Lime-bitten. All our hands are the same age, after all. Nail beds white, pads soft-hard-soft again from the constant damp. If I flex, my palm remembers plaster before my mind does — lime, clay, straw, water, a pinch of dung, mixed to cool mud that wants to seal. My cotton wrap is dark and damp around my head; I re-wet it at noon. My hair underneath, cut short a month ago, already going dusty greenish at the ends from brushing vines. Crow's feet deep, from squinting even in shade, because the bounce doesn't let you hide.

Where would you find me, really? Not running with the long-haired High-Ringers who live in the wind at the lip, calling to the flatland with big lungs. Not yet one of the hard old women grinding salt in doorways who remember where every crack was five rains ago, though I listen to them like you listen for hollow sound in a wall.

Find me in the hinge.

A Plaster-Keeper, which is essentially everyone, which is also almost no one special — a woman in her thirties with a rope-calved body, broad hips for carrying jars hugged to the chest up cupped stairs worn hip-high and smooth from sitting. On this terrace eight feet wide that is my dwelling and kitchen and workshop and lane and my daughter's playground, where there is a recessed firebox burning vine prunings, a net waiting mending, an olive jar with a pomegranate sapling that I will move a foot left tomorrow to chase the column, a pen with two small white goats whose hooves click like knitting needles, and three pots of mint placed precisely where the dew track drips fastest.

My daughter is part of a vertical herd shrieking three rings up, throwing her voice just to hear it slap back at her. That's good. Silence is alarming here. Rule three. Make sound so someone knows you're alright. I answer without thinking, a too-loud *I'm here*, and two other mothers answer too, and it stacks off the hard plaster and comes back, intelligible.

I spend my afternoons doing what always needs doing. *Shhk shhk.* Palm smoothing. Deciding if a corner's dark felt of moss is decoration or warning. Letting my four-year-old fill thumb-sized cracks with his thumb-pot. When the sudden hammer-rain comes — I heard it coming once as a roar on the rim before it fell in a white sheet and made every stair a rapids — he'll learn how fast surplus must move. How fig leaves feed goats feed us, how waste seals in pits feeds figs, how nothing pools. How the Bowl, which we made entirely by hand and plaster, has finished itself and now teaches us its final shape.

If you came and stood on the always-cool damp flagstones at the very bottom at dusk, when the smoke gets trapped sweet and stinging and a hundred orange windows light one by one and the cicadas start in the figs and the snoring starts in canon, you wouldn't pick me out as separate.

That's the point.

I'd be the hands re-wiping a pepper leaf clean of dust while listening for kestrel shadows. I'd be one voice in the call-and-response that checks whether the air hums wrong. Or maybe later, when I am seventy, I'll have moved down to that lowest, oldest, dampest ring where the plaster grows itself into moth-wing curtains that chime and collect dew that runs uphill a heartbeat before falling. Children would touch my doorway for luck.

I'm not a person who lives *in* the Bowl.

I'm a patch of its surface that learned to keep itself sealed.
