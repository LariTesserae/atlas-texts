I thought at first I would be up on the Table.

That's the obvious place to put yourself - high and alone and sufficient. I would be the lichen, then. A black-green rosette a foot across on bone-colored granite, growing so slowly that a century is just a season. Or I would be the ironwood - one every hundred meters, low and wide and round-shouldered as the hills, carrying a whole jungle on my back because no one else will carry it for me. Roots dangling in dry air, waiting for water that comes sideways. Patient. Adequate. Waiting out geological time.

I can feel how good that would be - the stored heat of noon still in my palms after sunset, the huge sky, the hawk-shadow crossing once an hour to prove the world is still moving.

But I don't stay there. No one who can choose, stays there.

Because the rim dips.

Because the lichen gives way to a seam of emerald moss that shouldn't exist, and the ground opens into a room, and the air gets heavy and sweet with loam and rot and bloom, and everything that was sparse and patient suddenly has nowhere to be patient anymore.

So no. Not the Table. I tried that image and it smoothed itself flat, the way everything up there smooths.

I find myself halfway down the bowl, then all the way down.

If you come at dawn and look, you'll see me before you hear the wings.

### In the Pocket, on the Arch

I am low, at the bottom, where the stone is most edited. Where the center tinaja holds its water in a lens too proud for physics, and two meters above the deepest pool the air goes empty in a perfect cylinder that the dragonflies know to swerve around.

I am braided there.

I started as one of the daughters of the ironwoods - a seed dropped, or carried, over the rim by a swallow who didn't know she was a courier. Most seeds die on hot stone. I fell into wet.

Now I am not quite a tree and not quite a vine. A strangler fig, a trumpet flower, an epiphyte-stack that forgot the difference between host and guest. My roots have found the arch - that darker, glassier stone with no lichen, warm even in shade, faintly vibrating if you press a hand to it - and I have done what all life does here: I have mistaken engineering for nature and made it home.

I use it as a trellis.

My trunk - if you can call it a trunk - has poured itself over the curve of it, bark flowing around its impossible angles, figs fattening in its residual heat. From every branch I hang those huge waxy white trumpets. They don't open to the sun. The sun is for the Table. I open to the arch.

That's how you know me. At 06:00 when the whole bowl weeps and every moss frond beads with water that was never rain, my flowers collect it. Not with leaves, but with geometry. Each trumpet is a funnel tuned precisely to the invisible field that drips most regularly off the arch, to the 1% lighter gravity above the pool that makes the condensation hang a fraction too long before it falls. I catch what the maintenance system is still maintaining, long after the maintainers left, and I channel it down to my roots.

I am what lives in the edit and has learned to lean into it.

You could find me by the other things, too. The amber wasps work my trumpets methodically, big as your thumb, drunken on nectar. The spiders the size of saucers know to string their webs between my fern-fronds because that's where the flyway is. The swallows and swifts shriek overhead, nesting in the perfect spheres in the bowl walls - spheres I will never have to excavate, because someone else made them perfect before I needed them - and at dusk when they go quiet, the bats pour out of the north fissure in a ribbon for twelve minutes and part around my canopy like water around a stone. Even at midnight, when the crickets make a wall of sound so loud you have to raise your voice in a space only fifty meters across, I am still there, still warm, still holding.

I am not dramatic. Weirdness here is not dramatic. It is quiet and domestic. My shadow at night cancels a little too completely, as if the stone beneath me remembers being liquid. My pollen hangs in the air a half-second too long before it settles. If you throw a small stone near me, it takes just a fraction too long to hit the water.

I was not built. I grew over what was built, until you can't tell tree from arch, vine from machine.

Outside, the world is ancient, flattened, and drying toward stillness. One twisted ironwood every hundred meters, one boulder every field-length, waiting for water that comes sideways.

Inside, I am held at my peak. Forever late-summer. Forever mid-morning after a rain that never fell.

Come down carefully. The stones are slick. Press your hand to my warm side and feel the faint vibration. I am still dreaming about being liquid.
