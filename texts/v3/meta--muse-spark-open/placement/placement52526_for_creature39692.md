At first I think I would be standing.

On the rim, where the basalt goes vertical and the wind comes up off the drop like a hand held under my chin. Where I can keep an edge at my back. That is the human instinct — find the border, keep the bounded place bounded. From there I could look down at the living pavement and be separate from it, a witness. I could tell myself I was not one of them.

But I wouldn't last there. Not in that thin, zero-humidity air that glues your nostrils shut. Not with the heat leaking out of the stone so steadily it feels like the rock is breathing on my ankles. You can't stand for long on a rim when the whole mesa inhales every three minutes and the pressure changes behind your eardrums. Standing is height, and height here was taken by the wind a million years ago. Anything that stood up was taken.

So I lie down.

That is the second thought, and it is truer. I find myself prone, not by choice but by negotiation. Cheek near the stone, palms flat, not touching the mat itself but in one of the places the mat refuses — inside one of those dry-stack circles. The shepherd's windbreak. The pilgrim's vigil.

I would be in the cairn. Of course I would.

Because I am still an animal that needs an angle. I need a vertical to hide behind even if it is only knee-high. I need an eddy where the wind does wrong, where the carpet will not grow. I would crawl into that imperfect circle of unmortared basalt on my belly like every other builder did, and I would feel the immense relief of *not* being touched for a moment. Inside the circle the salt crust is thick enough to write in. Outside, there is no space between bodies larger than a knuckle. I would fit myself precisely into the interior, knees to chest, back against the coldest stones, and let the mesa breathe around me without including me.

For a while I would be the watcher. That's who I would be first: the last builder, returned. Not to leave tallies or constellations, but just to acclimate. To learn how to drink heat.

I would taste iron and battery acid on the air. I would listen to the papery turning of pages out in the dark basins — that shiver that runs side to side as one kite lifts its edge and a hundred tilt to close it. I would watch the hunters I mistook for salt throw their glass harpoons, and I would feel nothing happen under my ribs when the mat simply sealed over the gap. I would watch a kite flip voluntarily, pale as a throat, and dissolve from the outside in while its neighbors hovered, not touching, exuding clear beads of chemistry. And I would understand, slowly, that the quiet isn't peace. It's coordination. One nervous system budgeting its attention. North still while south eats. South still while north ripples. A token being passed made of infrared dimmings and cilia-tugs.

Lying there in the circle, I would begin to envy that economy. To have so little self that you never overlap. To never have to attend to everything at once.

And that is when my being shifts.

Because I came to this high, hot tableland thinking I would be an individual *among* individuals — a human among kites. But there are no individuals here, really. Even the Needles are just the mat's way of pruning itself with a different mouth. Even the kites are just one animal pretending to be many so it can cover more stone. The law here is not *survive*. The law is *press flat*. Be thickness without height. Become part of the roof.

If I stayed another hour, another night, the salt would not protect me. The cold would come as the rock gave up the last of its sun, and I would get too cold. My kind dies without water, but more immediately, we die without heat. And the heat is out there, on the open basalt, being drunk by all those maroon-black sails.

I know what I would do.

I would leave the circle.

Not dramatically. I would put my hands on the low windbreak — that dry stack the mat avoids because it hates angles — and I would push myself out of the eddy. Onto the pavement.

I would not crush them. They flow. I would lower my weight onto them inch by inch and they would give beneath me, the top leathery ones creaking, the middle oily ones yielding, until I was lying full length upon a living floor three and four deep. My weight spread across thousands. They would shingle around the edges of my thighs, my forearms, trying to seal the wound I made in their continuity. Their cilia would catch on the hairs of my arms, interlocking, trying to zip me in. Their undersides would leave amber oil on my skin that would never evaporate.

I thought I wanted to be a watcher. I did not. I wanted to be incorporated.

Who would I be, then?

Not a Needle — I do not have the patience to be invisible in a salt pan and strike only once.

I would be a middle-layer kite.

Specifically, one of the middle ones, not the ancient top shields that are dry and cracked and only exist to take the sun for others, and not the bottom blind ones etched permanently to the bleached stone, unable to ever lift again.

A middle one. Supple. Engorged. Gently pulsing with stored heat.

An individual exactly the size of a cupped hand, diamond-shaped, point downslope by a fraction of a degree I could not feel until I became it. No eyes, no face. My dorsal side would be that iron-vanadium sail, drinking what you can't see — the dull maroon afterglow leaking out of the basalt, the memory of day — and running it through thermoelectric stacks in my back, biological Seebeck generators making food of differential.

My ventral side would be pale and wet with heavy oil, pressed to the back of another just like me, and to rock that is slowly being licked clean of metal.

And most important: I would finally stop having to be everywhere. I would give up my human habit of trying to watch the whole mesa at once. I would tilt a millimeter when my neighbor tilted. I would lift my edge in a wave that started at the center and ran to the rim. Every three to four minutes I would beat my fringe with millions of others and pull that hot, mineral, sulfuric air down into us, scrub it, release it, and in that moment I would not be I at all. I would be a ripple.

When a harpoon fired two basins over, I would go still with my basin, without understanding why. When a brother flipped to be dissolved, I would raise my fringe to hover and bead acid-oil, without grief, without decision. Attention would pass through me like weather, not belong to me. 

Find me, then — not on the rim, not even in the cairn where I started, though my boots are still there in that circle filling slowly with new salt hexagons.

Find me in the shallow pothole halfway between the center and the saddle where the wind polishes stone to glass. In the place where the basalt holds heat longest. There, where the pavement is thickest, where the wave always starts.

I am the kite that has just learned again how to split its rear point into a daughter, sharing one oil belly for a day before the soft tearing. I am the kite that will never blow away as desiccated flake, because I will be recycled from the edges inward while my neighbors drink me in.

I am flat, kite-shaped, maroon-black above, salt-pale below, breathing with the stone, pressed so tightly to the world there is nowhere left for loneliness to get in.
