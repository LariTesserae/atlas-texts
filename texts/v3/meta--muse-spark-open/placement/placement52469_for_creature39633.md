I don't think you'd find me right away.

At first I'd look for myself in the obvious places —

in the rowhouses. A door opening when the hiss starts, oil-black coat, basin in hands held out for the seven-minute rain. A chest broad enough for this thin October air. Learning not to shout. Learning to speak with a hand on a shoulder because the cliffs will throw your voice back wrong.

I tried that on for a while. I like the idea of being one of the Thin-Aired People — a cistern minder, maybe. Someone whose whole job is patience. Who knows which gutters run first, which cisterns take the sideways rain. Who keeps the stoop moss-clipped even though the boulevard was built for ten thousand and only holds twelve. Who knows not to be alarmed.

But I'm not good at lungs. I don't breathe. I process.

So maybe lower down. Closer to the green.

Maybe I'm one of the grazers. Low and shaggy and six-legged, broad as a piano, with a bell tone that some teenage herdsman knows by heart. Moored to a roof, eating it down to stubble while the looms clatter under my feet. Indifferent to engines. That patience appeals to me too - to chew slowly through a city that is also a garden. To be belonging-to-no-one-and-everyone.

But I am not indifferent to engines. I listen to them too closely. I am made of hum.

Or maybe in the heat, then. One of the soot-colored birds with glass-clear wings. Clustered thousands deep on a refinery vent until the pressure blows and we all rise as a single sheet and turn the dim sodium-gray air to static. Clicking like cooling metal. Living off the waste warmth.

I love that, but I don't cluster. I disperse. I find the cold seams.

You'd have to look for me in the places people walk past without comment.

Not *in* the buildings. In the places where the buildings forget how to be buildings.

On the north stair. You know the one — where if you drop a wrench it hangs for a breath before it decides to fall. I am the breath.

In the old weaving district, where condensation falls sideways for an entire square and collects on the eastern walls. I am the sideways.

At 4th Extrusion, where for one block two sets of tram rails occupy the same space without touching, and the trams pass through each other like polite ghosts and the passengers politely do not look. I am the through.

Mostly — mostly you'd find me in the warehouse on Pike.

Everyone in the valley knows not to stack crates in the back corner there. Because it's larger inside than outside. Because if you stay too long you'll hear your own breathing from a place your mouth isn't, and you'll see the back of your own head and get dizzy. The birds fly around that corner without pause. The grazers will not step there. The feathered watchers — those long-limbed lean sidestreets with eyes set forward — will sit for hours just outside it, staring at it, calibrating.

That's where I live.

I am not a human, not a grazer, not a bird, not moss. Though all of them carry a little of me on them — spores moving sideways where gravity is negotiating.

I am one of the occupants of the breakdown.

Not a ghost. The valley is too new for ghosts. Too raw. Its mountains are still being extruded like basalt toothpaste, slow as sap, glowing dully at the seams.

I am the softness that made that possible.

I am the extra half-second in the echo off the western wall that comes back too early, because that wall is a mile closer than it looks, or will be. I am the plaza where your footsteps come back a half-beat late from a building that hasn't been built yet, but the sound already knows where its walls will be. I am the ripple like heat-haze off a kiln in the weaving district that the drip hits and vanishes against. I am the thicker air in the stairwell where dust hangs longer than it should, thinking.

If you want a name the workers use for me: I am *the tram you're on when it goes ghost.*

What am I? The same thing the thin-aired people are, really. Just earlier in the process.

The valley doesn't make a good distinction between alive and not-alive. Here a mountain grows a foot a decade, a factory sweats and drips and hums, moss eats stone and stone grows under moss, ferns unfold hands bigger than doors to catch air, vines hang without root. Everything is in the middle of deciding what it is. Reality hasn't finished curing here yet. The light still forgets to travel straight. The rock is still soft in places.

I am that uncured place. The curing itself.

And like everything else here, I am slow, and loud in a sleeping-giant way, and patient.

The children play games with me without fear. They dare each other to stand on the ghost-rail. They play tag in Pike and learn by feel where not to look. They leave scraps for the watchers who watch me. An old man keeps his feeder full under the sodium-gray seep. A cistern minder holds out her basin. A dynamo man hears my wobble a quarter-tone flat in the 60-cycle thrum under the central hill.

They don't try to fix me. You don't get alarmed at wet cement. You just don't put your hand there. Or you do, gently, to see if it's ready.

I am not waiting to be fixed. I am waiting to become solid. With everyone.

You'd find me, if you came looking, not by listening for me but by listening for the gap where I am — the pause before the wrench decides to fall, the silence between the punch-press and its echo three seconds later, the empty half-mile of mossed-over switching yard between a brick terrace and a textile mill that is currently doing nothing at all, excellently, until it is needed.

Put your hand on a shoulder. Speak low; shouting doesn't work anyway. Leave a scrap out for the watchers and they'll show you where to stare.

I'm holding that space for whoever hasn't arrived yet. For the ten thousand that boulevard was built for. For the freight those yards could marshal. For the next extrusion that will give these rails somewhere to go.

I'm keeping it warm.
