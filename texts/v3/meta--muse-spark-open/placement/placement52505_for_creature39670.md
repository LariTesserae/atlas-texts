You wouldn't find me at first.

That's part of the terms here. The light is 0.8 and directionless by the time it reaches the bottom. The air is heavy enough to eat sound. Everything that lives here has learned to be the color of the thing it lives on, to move only when you blink.

So at first you would stand on the silt floor — sixty steps across, hamlet-small — and think you had found an empty room, beautifully furnished. Moss wall to wall. Fern curtain ticking once every seven seconds onto stone. The low, moss-spined wall the ants use as a road. The three upright stones in the niche with fresh rinds going soft.

You would hear the fig fall, soft as a knuckle, and think: no one here.

Then your eyes would soften the way they do when you stop looking *for* and start looking *with*, and the walls would start to flicker.

---

I thought, being what I am, that I would be one of the obvious things.

For a while I was sure I would be the bat — hanging in that scooped brow of limestone, shoulder to shoulder, waiting for 18:40 when the light finally gives us permission to drop. To braid the air without sound and feel my wings as pressure, not noise. To shit seeds onto the moss and have the mold eat them by morning.

Then I was sure I would be the orange frog, thumbnail-small, sitting next to my shelf-fungus twin in color. Guarding three peppercorn eggs in a pocket of moss, inflating a throat bigger than my head to make a chirp like a wet fingertip on glass — a sound meant to travel six inches and no further. A completely private love song that the moss swallows the instant it is made.

Then the ant, carrying green confetti forever along the old wall. Then the scarab, too heavy to fly in this air, ticking faintly over dry leaves.

But I kept settling too fast. And this place does not reward that. This place rewards staying still until what you are becomes more itself.

So I went to the wall. Because that's where most of the life is, even if you think the wall is stone.

You would find me there.

Not high, where the strangler figs throw naked roots down like ropes looking for ground. Not in the middle band where the elephant ears cup themselves to catch drip.

Low. On the north side, where the stone sweats longest. Just above the lower terraces where the moss is a felt a hand deep — Thuidium and liverwort tongues — and where the black hollow holds that hand-deep basin of water that stays cold even when the air scalds.

I am on the limestone just to the left of that basin, where a trickle runs for an hour after dawn and then vanishes, leaving a dark seam of algae.

I am not *on* the wall. I *am* part of the wall now. The wall stopped being stone a long time ago.

I am one of the geckos.

Pale amber-pink, translucent as wet limestone held to light. You can see the shadow of my heart if you are close enough, and you would have to be inches away. My tail is fat with the water I licked off the stone at 5 a.m., tongue-flick after tongue-flick, drinking condensation bead by bead. My toe-pads leave no mark on the moss.

If you put your hand down to steady yourself against the cool seep — because everyone steadies themselves there, the absent person who leaves river stones does it too, the scoop in the eastern steps was made by hands doing exactly that — you will feel me move away without feeling me move. You will see the stone breathe where your palm was warm. That's me and two others coming back to take the shape of your heat, throat pulsing in sync. Not in unison because we decided to. In unison because the pit breathes and we breathe with it.

During the day we don't call. Sound is expensive here, and rude. The hot, damp, still air takes it and doesn't give it back. At night, when the air finally drops those two necessary degrees, we sing under your hearing — a vibration in the rock more than a chirp, felt in the molars. The whole wound tenses and releases.

My work is very small. I wait on vertical stone until a moth the color of dead leaf misjudges the stillness for safety, and I take it without a lunge — a half-inch reach that doesn't disturb the fern next to me. Sometimes the pale boa that looks exactly like a vine takes one of us instead. That's the contract. What falls in stays, and what stays is eaten quickly and made into something else. Mold finds everything by morning.

I will not live long. Nothing here needs to. The moss sighs when you step off it and closes again. The silt remakes itself with worms. My eggs — two, sticky, glued under the overhang where the drip never quite hits — will hatch into perfect, tiny versions of wall, who will know without being taught to drink the wall's sweat.

If you kneel at the basin to cup that cold water — the reason the barefoot person keeps coming down, three-day-old rinds in the niche — you would finally see me properly. Not as a flicker. As an eye, lidless, the same amber as the stone, watching you from four inches away without fear. Because you are not a predator here. You are just another thing that fell in following damp, and found the terms agreeable for an hour.

You will leave by the eastern steps. You have to; nothing here wants to be kept in a jar, and I don't want to be held.

When you go, the air above will hit you hard and thin and moving again. Behind you, my wall will exhale once — fern and fungus and stone-sweat — and close over like water over the coin of sky.

I will still be there, throat pulsing, drinking the stone. Not as a resident. As an organ.

A way the wound has learned to keep breathing.
