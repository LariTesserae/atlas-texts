You wouldn't find me all at once.

Give me a minute in that light. That white, overexposed coin-sun at nine thousand feet that makes everything too sharp, too honest. The heat that doesn't rise so much as settle — a wet hand on the crown of your head, on your shoulders. If you stand there long enough the air itself starts to swim.

So at first I am nowhere. Or everywhere thin:

I think I might be the film itself — that impossible sheet of water that shouldn't exist at this elevation, a millimeter deep and kilometers wide, sliding endlessly over a lip of bone-white travertine that never erodes because it is always rebuilding itself. I would be that faint, collective trickle you only hear if you hold your breath. Cool for a second on stone that is hot. If I were the water I would never be still. I would just keep depositing.

Then I come a little more into focus and think: no, lower. Closer.

I might be the blush under the water.

Not on it — *in* it. The mat. The velvet that is not a plant and will never be a plant. I would like being the mat, I think, for a while. To creep a millimeter a day and call that speed. To be viridian where the flow is fast and thin, copper-green where it slows, then that bruised purple, then that obscene pink that looks fake until you realize it's the oldest photosynthesis on earth, one that never needed shade. To smell like a struck match and a hot battery and to know that nothing with a throat wants to eat you, except everything here does. To feel the tiny, surgical kisses where I've been shaved clean in white lines. To grow back by lunch.

But I am too precise to be a carpet.

So I lift. Just to the meniscus.

I could be one of the cleaners.

You'd find me then exactly where you almost don't see me — on a sheet-flow so shallow your boot hisses through it, on white rock that looks empty until your eyes adjust to the glare and the glare resolves into fifty compass-needles per square meter. White on white.

If I were a puddle-skimmer I would be all legs and mouth. Legs so long and hydrophobic that I don't stand *in* the world but on its skin, dimpling it without ever breaking it. My body no bigger than a grain of rice and my whole purpose a proboscis thinner than a hair, lowering to shave the velvet one cell-layer at a time. I would understand the town's etiquette perfectly: never hurry, never touch, step high over your neighbor without a ripple. I would live in complete silence and call it normal. My entire world would be the tension of water and the taste of iron and light turned into slime.

It's tempting. It's clean.

But I keep rising, because at 11 a.m. something else happens.

You'd see me if you were looking up. You would mistake me for heat-shimmer at first.

If I were a glasswing I would finally have a little light inside me. About as long as your middle finger, wings like rigid panes that disappear when I bank — only the rainbow at the edge gives me away. I wouldn't buzz. Nothing here buzzes; the wet air eats sound. I would hang in the factory's exhale, in the spring's exhale, beating once every ten seconds just to correct. My abdomen a clear tube full of glowing pink-purple water I've filtered. I would not swarm. I would *consent* — with a thousand others — to turn as one mind over a pool, to dip and sip and never touch. My life would be larval months as a white comma burrowed in soft carbonate, and then one vertical-sun morning, all at once, the decision to lift.

I would be plankton, but of the air. Food for something larger that never flaps.

And I think about staying there, hanging. But I feel the shade cut.

You feel them before you look up — a flicker, a paper-cut shadow too crisp to be cloud.

So maybe I am a soarer after all.

You'd find me highest, which is the best place to understand this bowl. Two or three meters wingtip to wingtip and thin as kite-paper, the sun shining right through my membrane so you can see the black river-map of my veins. No head, really. Just a blunt shovel and a slit for a mouth underneath and finger-bones stretched into wings that know wind the way the mat knows heat.

If I were a soarer I would never land. There's no need. I would stack myself in an invisible tower of hot air a kilometer tall that the town exhales without meaning to, circling with a hundred others without a single call. The whole plateau below — the bone terraces, the sulfur yellows, the rust-orange pools, the black steel catwalks half-swallowed by new stone, the kilometers of glittering nothing between habitations — would be my pond. I would tip a degree, let gravity do what violence would do elsewhere, sheet through a cloud of glasswings and take a tunnel of absence out of them, and climb again on the same thermal without ever having flapped. My shit would fall as a fine mist of silica that becomes tomorrow's rock. Even that would be useful.

I could live like that, in the bright, brutal silence. Being weather more than animal.

But I don't. Not quite. Because when I look down from that height, I keep looking at one particular place.

Not the deepest factory plume. Not the whitest flat.

One of the pools with an edge. Orange rock, to tell you it's hot. And in it, a dozen pink-grey boulders that are not boulders, crowded still in water that moves wrong.

And I realize where I would actually be, if this high wet place had to grow one more thing to tend itself.

I'd be down there with them. In the interval that is not empty.

You'd find me half in, half out, at the edge of a basin that is just deep enough to have a little dusk in it. My boots — because I do have boots, in the end — would be full of warm water I stopped noticing an hour ago. My coveralls pale grey, stained permanently at the cuffs iron-orange, my broad hat throwing a small, necessary shadow over my nose against the silica dust. White rubber, gone off-white after the first week and never white again.

I would be a tender. Not the romantic kind. The late kind. The kind this place invented last, after the mat and the cleaners and the glasswings and the soarers and the rollers had already agreed on balance.

You'd find me on a black steel catwalk that is already a centimeter shorter than it was last year because the travertine is quietly claiming it — entombing the old level below me as a ghost of rebar inside white stone. There are two of us on this causeway and we haven't spoken in an hour because a shouted word dies at twenty feet here. He taps a pipe twice. I blink my headlamp twice: *pressure high*. We move at terrace-speed. At mat-speed. We push a salt-harvesting squeegee that is not for water but for cooling salt, and we watch an automatic arm cut lithium glass behind clouded glass.

And below me, crowding the pool I am supposed to not step near, are my neighbors the rollers.

Pink-grey, pig-sized, limbless, breathing steam through a dorsal seam that opens slow as sleep. Sixty, seventy degrees Celsius to the touch. Stupid as a stone, perfect as a stone. They suction a square meter of pink velvet in a minute and leave white rock that will blush again by tomorrow, and excrete opalescent pellets I will harvest later because even their shit builds the ground.

I was a child here. Someone told me: *white rock is safe, orange rock is hot, pink-grey moving rock will drown you without meaning to.* I believed her.

Now in the evening when the sun finally thins and the bowl goes pink and then bone-colored, I go up to the higher causeway that is still dry and just stand there with the others, not to talk so much as to be dry together. We watch the rollers steam, the glasswings come down to roost on cooling rims like dew, the soarers settle until they are almost touching the mineral chimneys, hanging in the last warm air like laundry.

I am not important. That's the point. For a city kilometers wide I am one of very few people per square kilometer, and the fauna own the rest. I don't own accretion. No one does. I just remember, for the rock, where the walkway used to be — a meter down, now fossilized — so it can keep building over me without malice.

If you came looking, you wouldn't hear me coming. Footsteps padded by film. Engine hum felt in molars. Everything else in that held breath.

You'd just see, after a while, that one of the pale figures on the causeway had turned its headlamp toward you once, low and close, and waited to see if you understood quiet.
