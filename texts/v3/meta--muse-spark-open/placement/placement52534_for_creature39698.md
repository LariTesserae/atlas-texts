At first I think I know where I'd be.

I'd be looking for high ground. That's what you do when you arrive somewhere flat — you seek the one place you can see from. But there is no high ground here. There is only low, and lower by an inch, and lower by another inch, and then the lip into water.

So I wouldn't find myself that way. I would have to unlearn the habit of climbing to understand.

So maybe then I think: I'd be in the water.

Because to go anywhere you must wade, and wading is the only honest way to move here. Shin-deep in the warm lymph, feeling the pale stirrers part around my calves like curtains, feeling the black polishers under my boots make their soft mopping sound against the tile I can't see. I'd be the line of disturbance that closes behind itself, the V-wake that doesn't last. I'd be standing in a channel that is a groove between scutes, perfectly straight for kilometers, watching how the water level never changes though it is always being fed by *plok* and *plok* from above.

But if I stayed there, I would just be another thing to be routed around.

Or maybe I'd be on top. Not wading at all. I think for a while I might climb — not high, there is no high — but onto one of those low blind sheds that aren't sheds, the gill-covers, the brood-flaps. I'd lie there on my back where the skin is thinnest and warmest, among the roosting things that aren't birds. Shoulder to shoulder, barnacle-footed, skin-winged. I'd be between two of them, not touching at first, then touching because there is no other way to be. We'd all breathe together. That collective *huhhh* like wind in a vent that isn't wind. I'd open my mouth and try to time my breath to theirs, and for a minute I'd succeed, and the heat of their wings would make me think my own skin was finally dry.

But that isn't right either. They don't roost. They *ventilate*. To be among them would be to pretend I had a function in the heat-exchange, and I don't.

Give me longer and another image sharpens.

I would not be standing or roosting at all.

I would be kneeling.

Where the platform meets the channel — that crisp ninety-degree growth margin, the edge that is kept sharp the way a fingernail is kept sharp, trimmed as it grows. I would be right at that line, where the bacterial felt makes its tideline in rust-red and slate-green and pale cream, exactly one hair thick, exactly as thick as it is supposed to be and no thicker. Where the sweating of the plain and the weeping from the weep-holes meet the still water and stop being two things.

I'd be there because that's the only place that has any decision left in it. Everything else is held. The clouds don't move. The light doesn't shift. The hum never surges. But that line — wet to dry, skin to lymph — that has to be negotiated second by second. That's where the polishers lap, where the repair crabs come and go, where the louse-flies cluster a little darker because I have left sweat there.

And who would I be, at that line?

At first I want to say human. The mote in the eye. The visitor the plain makes room for the way water makes room and closes again. The one who scrapes with a key though he knows he shouldn't, just to feel if the concrete gives a little. That would be the honest answer for the first hour.

Stay longer, though. Stay until the second hour, when the low bath-warmth has made your thoughts slow, when the dripping has unpicked the stitch that tells you to keep walking. You notice the attendants do not flee and do not attack. They don't care that you are human. They taste your dead skin and carry it off, they work a little harder downstream of your shins to keep the lymph moving, they lay paste the exact color of the platform over the chip your boot made.

They are not fauna. They are white blood cells. A count, maintained.

And a human is not a cell. A human is a contamination event that will be accounted for.

So if I really lived here — not visited, but lived, the way something lives that belongs to it — I wouldn't be the mote for long.

I would be the one that dulled.

One of the polishers.

Not the stirring shrimp with their frantic pleopods, not the peristaltic eels all muscle, not the repair crabs with their glands of paste, not the lungs with their vast capillary wings. The other one. The flat black water-louse tiled shoulder to shoulder over every submerged stair, the one whose whole life is: lap, shift a millimeter, lap.

I would be the one who has been lapping that same square centimeter of bacterial felt since before I arrived, keeping it exactly a micron thick. I would have no mouth worth naming, only a soft circular mop. No voice, because noise is waste. No hunger, because hunger would be inefficiency. I would not see the horizon because I would not have eyes for horizon. I would taste temperature and salinity and stop when the film was right, and go when it wasn't.

You wouldn't see me at all until I moved. I would be the color of wet concrete.

And after a long, long time of doing that, of being so perfectly part of the maintenance that holds everything in its fresh-poured yesterday forever — I would stop.

Not break. Not die the way a predator is killed. Just fray. Paddle frayed, mop dulled. I would stop in place, and the crowd would close and carry me, without hurry or ceremony, toward the grate at the end of the channel where I would be unmade and reprinted as two new ones who would lap in my place.

That is where you would find me: not standing, not wading, not roosting. Kneeling, then not kneeling. Flat, at the tideline, working, then being carried.

A small, black, useful thing that never needed to decide what it was, until, for one strange interval, it did — and decided to be still, and let the plain take it back.
