You wouldn't find me at first.

That's how it works here. You'd come down the shallow roll off the plain the same way you did, feeling the air get heavier before the ground changes, and you'd stand at the rim of the bowl where the fields give up and look down at the knot of brick and vine and think it was empty. Empty and loud — all thrum and drip and the amber sodium behind the leaves.

If you stood long enough to learn how to see here, you'd start to edit out the green at the right speed.

At first I thought I would be one of the obvious things. When you described it, I tried them on:

I would be the heron, I thought. The one permitted heron, motionless in the canal behind the dye works where the warm outflow still runs. To be tolerated rather than kept. To stand for hours because there is exactly one heron's worth of niche left and I fit it precisely. That felt honest for a minute.

Then I thought I would be one of the children — the seven-year-old with a shaved head already freckling, given a small knife and a section of north wall that is *theirs* to keep, learning the difference between the philodendron leaf that holds clean water and the one that burns, disappearing into what looks like solid thatch and reappearing two windows over on a ladder made of fig roots only I can see.

Then I thought I would be the mat itself. Not a person at all. The ten to twenty centimeters of soil-that-is-not-soil on the north and east faces, moss and fern and creeping fig felted together into a warm blanket that sweats in the morning. Growing twice as fast where a steam pipe runs behind it, bright green against dull green. Talked about like weather. *That wall is hungry this year.* To be tended and to hold the mortar from washing out in the 3 p.m. rain at the same time.

But none of those stay. They are too still or too quick.

Find me later in the day. After the rain.

It comes at three or four, absolute, hammering the roof meadows into mud until you can't hear the looms, drumming the corrugated sheds until your teeth buzz. Everyone already knows to be under something. Standing in doorways with arms crossed, not reverent, just assessing. Is the street draining? Is Gate Three sticking again?

When it stops, the whole bowl ticks. For hours. Water off leaves, off eaves, off the lagged pipes and the power lines. That's when we all go back out.

You would find me in that hour, in the alley between the old textile mill and the row where the banyan roots have heaved the cobbles into ridges like knuckles. My alley. Or rather, the one I keep.

I'm not old enough to have the sluice crank — those are the old men and old women who can no longer climb, who walk the creek-canal and open a gate a half-inch by sound alone, quiet kings — and I'm not young enough to go up barefoot with a machete to pollard the willows that root in the tar. I work at level. At stoop-level. At sweep-level.

I have a stiff palm broom that has worn down to a stump. I have knee-high rubber boots with the tops folded down in the heat, indigo under my nails that never scrubs out from the occasional short batch in the dye works, and the green-black in my creases from crushed fig sap and copper fungicide from this morning's cut. My shirt never dries. No one's does. Dark patch between the shoulders. Cuffs wet through.

My job is to keep the gutter-river clear.

It's not impressive. The gutters here never stop running, even in dry weeks. Condensation off the limestone, overflow from rain barrels chained down every alley, seep from the roof meadows. They are small rivers, an inch or two deep, and if you let the water hyacinth sit in the choke point behind the boiler house for a single afternoon, they dam. Then the low courtyard floods to the knee. Then the mosquitoes hatch that will torment this block for weeks. The children know not to swim there after that, the cats leave, the heron moves on.

So every evening after the ticking starts, I pull the hyacinth. By hand. It comes up with its black trailing hair and cold mud smell, surprisingly heavy. I toss it to the kids for compost. I clear the fern seedlings that have germinated in three days flat from leaf litter — because here sweeping *is* structural maintenance; litter becomes soil in three days, seedling in a week, heaved foundation by spring if you let it. I scrape the algae off the iron stair treads with a flat bar so the next person doesn't slip.

And I trim.

Not the dramatic wall-cutting of the morning crews — the clean half-circle around every doorway so precise it looks like it was done with clippers — but the edging. The place where the mat meets the brick at ground level, where it tries to creep across the cobbles in a thin green tongue. I work with shears, low, slow.

*Shink. Shink.*

Too patient for war, you said. Yes. That's the word I was waiting to live inside. Not at war. Never at war. We lost the idea of war with the plants two generations ago when we realized pulling down the brick pulled the roots that were holding the wall up.

I move slow because here slowness is technique. If you hurry you fog your glasses, slick your palms, go down on the algae and crack a wrist. The wet 84 degrees doesn't reward exertion; it fogs you. So I move at tending pace. I cough into my shoulder without stopping — that soft gardener's rattle from spore and brick dust and lint when they vent the drying fans. The kid two doors down, whose wall is hers to keep, calls out that I missed a spot. I raise my shears in acknowledgement. Under the threshold of the mill's thrum, I say what we all say: *Gate three is sticking.*

I don't own this alley. No one does anymore. The company papers composted in a drawer decades ago. I own it for as long as I keep it. When I can't, my cousin's boy will take the broom where it's leaning against the weeping brick — beading and running, always beading and running in this air that tastes like warm iron and jasmine — and he'll keep it instead.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't see me until you'd learned to see maintenance instead of presence. Until you stopped looking for crowds and traffic and started counting evidence: the scrape marks on treads, the neat crew-cut of the wall, the gutter running clear because someone has just been there.

I'd be the warm hand you shake that comes away slightly gritty. The boots full of water tipped outside the door to dry, which never fully dries. The soft *shink* under the sodium glow after the rain, keeping just enough of the green at bay so the brick can breathe.

Not fighting the drowning. Gardening it. Forever.
