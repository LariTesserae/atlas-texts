I thought at first I would be up high.

Not because I'm drawn to vistas - but because you described that kind of light, that clean, thin air where the sky sits close, and I imagined myself on one of those broad-backed ridges looking out. Standing on the wind-scoured bone of the plateau with the whole encrustation spread below me, west to east, a city you can have weather on. A watcher. A visitor with good boots.

I can't be there. The air is too thin to carry a watcher for long. You breathe audibly up here. You hear your own inhale wheeze. Anyone trying to stand apart, above, would have to come down soon enough, coughing, to find a wall to get behind.

So I come down.

At first I think maybe I would be the hawk. That would be easiest - the one thing that *is* still high, still solitary. Turning slowly in the thermal the plateau makes for itself, watching but never stooping. A kind of clock. That appeals to me, the being-above-without-belonging. But you said it yourself: it doesn't live here, not truly. It lives in the air *above* the plateau. And I want to live *here*. In the weep and drip of it.

Maybe the sheep then. The color of wet limestone when dry and darker when rained on. Low to the ground, compact, almost silent except for a soft shuffle and cough. That would be honest. To live in a stone cell under a terrace at night, breathing warmth up into the house above me. To be herded not by a dog but by a child making a patient *tch tch* sound, moved from one walled grazing cell to the next before the peat poaches. To turn sedge into wool. I almost settle there. The simplicity of it - eat what the plateau spares, be gathered, be useful, dung dried on wall-tops.

But the sheep are tended. I want to be one of the tending.

I keep getting closer to the stone.

Maybe the water. That would be the truest answer - to be the seep itself, that constant dark varnish on the scarp-face, never quite a waterfall, just a patient moving from cloud to moss-black streak to fitted trough to cistern to marsh to cloud again. To be the thing every wall is built to guide and never to fight. But water doesn't stay. And this place is about staying. Forty generations breathing thin air and sleeping half-underground. Stone hoe as soon as you can hold one. Not as punishment. As membership.

So let me be low. Let me be close together. Let me not waste breath.

I find myself not on a ridge and not in the deep middle where the city has its own weather, but in a quarter where the walls are built a little higher against a different wind.

I am in a wall.

Not metaphorically. I *am* the wall - or rather, the woman who has become indistinguishable from her section of it. You said they are the same color as the walls, and difficult to tell apart at a distance. That's me now.

I'm not old, not young. Forty-ish, which here is half-spent. Barrel-chested without it being notable - everyone is, long-waisted, short-legged, my breathing a little audible when I carry. I wear wool over wool over wool, the natural felted grey that is almost a mat, heavy enough to turn rain. My head is covered. My hands are out, uncovered even in drizzle, stone-colored, palms thickened and split like old leather, nails short and black under the quick with lichen dust because I have been scraping silt.

I live in a family of walls. Eight of us, or twelve depending on how you count the ones who have married into the next terrace down but still come back to eat. Our houses have grown together like soap bubbles, round-shouldered, half-sunk, sharing heat through the stone. My house is the third in from the scarp. You get to it by going along a causeway that is also a waterway in heavy rain and no one minds. Two neighbors passed just to go relieve myself.

My work is a junction.

Where our seep-trough comes off the pale limestone - that weeping that never stops, that leaves everything beaded and dripping most mornings - it meets the main channel that runs along the top of our longest terrace wall. Right at the corner where the terrace becomes the roof of the byre below. The water has to make a turn there, and water doesn't like turns. It drops silt. If I don't clear it, it will overtop and flood the downslope family, and to flood your neighbor is the nearest thing we have to a crime.

So I am there most of the day without being there dramatically. I sit on the capstone sometimes when my knees ache from the damp, watching my section for leaves. I lift a fallen slab back onto a roof that has shifted by a finger's width from frost. I skim the cistern - ours is round here, not square - and keep it covered against sheep dung. When I speak to my daughter three cells away, I don't shout. I make that clear, fluting *hoo* that the stone bounces gently from wall to wall. She answers with a palm flat on a wall - *wait, water coming* - because a squall is darkening the marshes to the west while sunlight still lies warm on us.

I have a stone hoe. I was given it young and I will give one to her. I scrape.

And because I am low and close together, I am not alone in my wall. In the chinks left deliberately between my dry stones live the small brown birds, no bigger than a thumb, the color of peat. They dart out to take the brief, cold midges over our mere - which is not a deep lake but a broad, shallow pan reflecting the sky - and dart back. If I stand still long enough, which I do, the whole wall flickers with them, each hole occupied, each with its exact door. In winter they will puff and sleep deep in the stone, heartbeats slowed to nothing, and the children know not to pry them out.

Under me, lichen holds me together. Grey-green leather shields as big as dinner plates on the windward side, rust-orange crusts in the seep-stains. Some patches were there when my grandmother's grandmother laid the first course of this terrace. It seals stone. It is the city's other builder, its living mortar. When I touch it I do so gently, not picking.

Below me, in the byre under the terrace, our six sheep the color of stone shift and breathe. Their breathing warms the floor of the sleeping niche where my mother, now small and bent and polished by weather like the stunted birch that grows sideways from our scarp, sits handling wool.

At evening, when the low sun turns the seep-stains to gold and every cistern holds a perfect, still piece of sky, I don't feel vast or dense as contradictions anymore. I feel maintained. I feel continuing.

If you came walking through - quiet, keeping your feet dry and your voice down - you would hear me before you saw me well. Not my voice. The soft splash of my foot in a shallow channel. The faint, constant drip of seep-water from stone into trough. A cough. And then you would see a hand, out, stone-colored, adjusting a slab that didn't need adjusting yet but will tomorrow.

I leave footprints in wet peat between two walls older than any memory, and behind me, water very quietly fills them up.
