You wouldn't find me where I first thought you'd find me.

Not up where the light still cuts. I imagined that, at first — that I'd be a terrace child, high enough to get real sun in slats, learning to angle mirrors with a long pole, burning my forearms and feeling holy about it. Or no — deeper, all the way down in the true low pans, haunches deep in scalding retention water, one of those lung-ridged dock-men who can hold his breath through a whole shift change, pulling blind mollusks out of silt with hands gone pale and wrinkled as felt.

I tried on the still ones, too. The tempting ones. To be sessile. To be Old Mara-in-the-Wall, grown kind and mineral, with cables at my wrists and cats making pilgrimage to my face. To be stone. To finally be allowed to stop. That was a beautiful fantasy for about three seconds until the deck thrummed under my feet and reminded me: you don't get to stop here until you've earned it.

No. If I'm honest, I'm not high, I'm not low, I'm *middle* — which in the Low City is the only place that really exists. The middle where every shaft leaks.

You'd find me on Weep 9, or whatever the raft is calling it this week. It's not an address, it's a condition: a long, canted service cut two levels below the heat vanes that dump for Hab-Stem Four. A walkway that was supposed to be a maintenance corridor and became a street and became a house and is now all three at once, plus a gutter.

I live where the City sweats the hardest.

Ankle-deep. Always ankle-deep. The water never leaves here, just trades places. Warm seep comes down the wall in a sheet of beads that split and rejoin, that tastes of iron and old concrete when it gets in your mouth. The air is so wet your sweat doesn't evaporate, it just joins. Condensate rains indoors without a cloud, ticking on the dark glass of the flow-film in its groove beside me. The light is borrowed three times over — work-lamp white, ducted down a crooked shaft that still, if you crane and wait for a passing swarm to clear, gives you a coin-sized patch of real sky, impossibly far and impossibly blue.

You'd know me by sound before sight. I'm hoarse.

I'm what you'd get if you left a human in this bowl for thirty-four years and let the bowl do what it does. Not tall. Low City people stopped being tall. Broad through the chest because you have to push your voice through wet air like mud, low and clipped, from the diaphragm. Bones that took the hint of pressure and thickened at wrist and ankle. I have the nictitating membranes — everybody does now, that cheap second blink that slides sideways when the drizzle hits. I have the salt at my hairline that never washes out, the speckled iron on my forearms from pan splash, the limescale mustache that grows back every morning where my breath hit the cold air of sleep. I scrape it off with a thumbnail like everyone else.

I have soft feet still, strangely, because they never dry. And I check them. Every night I check that I can still wiggle all ten toes. That's not vanity. That's inventory.

Call me a sweeper, a reader, a waver — we don't have job titles that last. My raft does throughput. At first light I’m out with the flat shovel, before the pan water gets claimed. The felt is black then, flat and drinking heat, quivering at the edge when I get too close. It smells of blood and hot springs. You learn the angle — not too deep or you scrape deck, not too shallow or it tears and takes a week to mat again. We lift it in sheets, press it, fry it on the waste-heat grate until it crisps. That’s breakfast. That’s everybody’s breakfast.

By noon that same pan is a road. I put down the shovel and pick up the wands — two light sticks, orange, stupid simple — and I wave. Traffic is a fluid here. The flow-film in its groove moves like slow water whether you want it to or not, dark glass rippling on invisible cilia. My job is to tell people when to step on and when not to step on so they don’t pile up at the bend where the valve sticks. Children know my face. Faces are navigation marks here. They shout and I shout back and none of us hears high frequencies anymore so we’re all half-deaf and excellent at reading mouths.

By evening I listen.

Someone has to. Every overflow lip, every relief valve has a note, and together they’re the pump-song that holds this whole wobbling reef in balance. My grandmother — not sessile yet, but close, her legs are going — taught me to put my palm on the deck-plate and feel for the flat note. When one clogs, the felt laps over it, the mineral beard grows a little longer, and a pan that should have drained stays mirror-flat. You hear it go wrong before you see it. So I wade out with a hook and clear the gunk, and a thousand feet adjust two centimeters and keep moving and never thank me, which is correct. You don't thank the floor for being floor.

That’s what I am. Not a person, really. Not alone, anyway. That’s the second thing wrong with how I first pictured myself.

I thought I’d be an *I*. One body, one name, one crate I could set down and come back to.

Here, I’m a raft-function. We are fifty, maybe sixty, hands that share the same hammocks strung three high in a shaft, the same tile where the floor changes color and we call it ours for as long as we can keep it moving. Our wealth is liters before noon. Our god is a smooth bolt that always falls sideways, kept in a pocket that can’t hold water. Our pet is a long-legged cat that no one owns and everyone feeds, who stares at her reflection in the lane-marked flood and then spears motes with a webbed paw. Our ancestors are not dead, they're walls. We bring Old Mara clean water not because she needs it but because she remembers where the deck used to be higher.

And I carry others without meaning to. The white-motes — filter flies, rice-grain translucent, guts full of zeolite — have learned to farm the beaded curtains on my shoulders. They part around my face when I walk and re-form behind me. The repair shimmer does, too, sometimes — that maintenance dust with purpose. I’ll be scraping and a glitter will catch the dim and I’ll feel a faint tickle as a crack fills itself under my feet while I’m still standing on it, black filament knitting through poured stone that is older than any record.

I am, like everyone, an ecology. A walking pan. A throughput point. An argument that hasn’t stopped yet.

If I stood still — really stood still — the felt would find the edge of my boots by tomorrow. The City would build a little room around my hesitation. It means no harm. It has no room to waste.

So I don’t. I shift weight even when I’m standing. I tap my foot to the pump rhythm. I scrape. I wave. I listen. I shout to be heard over the mid-range haggling and the hydraulic roar below and the frantic bird-and-drone chirring above, and somewhere in that deafening chord my hoarse voice becomes part of the chord, too.

Once a day, the transit film goes by full of faces and blocks my sky-coin. Once a day, it clears, and the flock — birds, drones, who can tell anymore — wheels across that impossible blue in a single packed, mindless, beautiful turn, so tight they are each other’s wind.

And the water keeps weeping from the rocks that were buildings and are now geology again. And I feel my ten toes. And I keep moving.
