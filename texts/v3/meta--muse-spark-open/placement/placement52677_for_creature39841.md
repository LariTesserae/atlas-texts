I think at first that I would be up high.

Not down in the yards. Up where the bone-kites are.

Because if you live your whole life under that roar — that low patient thunder that lives in your molars — you start wanting a place that is out of it. And in the High Wet Town the only way *out* is up. Above the roar, above the veil-falls, above the steam-dome amber light. I picture myself on one of those convex spines of basalt above the eastern scarp, where the rock is still soft-shouldered and lichen-stained, where the pitons won't hold because the rock weeps too much. Lying flat on warm stone that sweats beneath you. Watching those white cuts circle so slowly they look still. Waiting for wind. Not working. Not keeping anything from closing.

But no. That’s a visitor’s fantasy. Nobody lives there. The town doesn’t keep dreamers on its spines. It keeps maintainers. And after five generations your blood is too dark and thick for that kind of thin, useless height. You’d get dizzy and come back down with a nosebleed.

So then I think — maybe I’m down low.

For a while I think I’m a reed-cutter, because I want to see the thing that hangs. I want to be one of the tethered ones who goes out in daylight into the Plashes where the veil-falls hit and make no ripples. I want to wear the harness and the rubber boots up to the thigh and learn the fast, economical swing that keeps ten-foot reeds from closing the town’s throat. I want to hear the clicker’s one glassy note — *cheeeee* — answer itself across a mile of water, and to stand under that hesitation, where falling threads thicken two feet above the marsh and a thrown reed lays itself sideways on air, and feel whether it wants me to go on breathing.

But reed-cutters don’t linger. They cut quickly. They don’t stare. Staring is for newcomers and for people who haven’t yet learned how to not stare too long at water that is deciding whether to fall. If I were a reed-cutter, I wouldn’t last a season. I would stop to watch.

And then, more honestly, I think I know where I’d be found.

You find me at 04:40, before the 6:00 siren. In the hour when the factories throttle down by a third — which is as close to quiet as this basin ever gets — and the true shape of the sound opens up: that vast, patient hum coming from every direction at once and bouncing off the soft shoulders of the tuff, making the whole flat seem like the inside of a drum.

I’m not on a roof. I’m under a pipe rack.

In the Kiln Quarter, where the old basalt columns are still standing under their little tin hats, wet on top and dry underneath for no reason anyone will explain out loud. My shift starts before the sun hits hard enough to make the gravel flats darken from below. I wear boots, always — mine are ankle-high, steel-toed, already dark at the seams with tomorrow’s damp even though I set them neatly beside the bed at night. My shirt is sweat-dark between the shoulder blades. My chest is broad. My neck is thick. When I breathe at rest my lips stay parted just a little. I have the barrel.

I have the ears, too.

I am twenty-seven and half-deaf like everyone over twelve. Not tragic-deaf, worked-deaf. Cotton plugs grey with reuse in my pocket, but I don’t put them in yet. In this hour the press next street over is still thumping at idle and I need to feel it.

Find me crouched.

One hand on a low-pressure steam line that runs six inches off the ground, lagged and scaled until it’s more mineral than pipe. An inch-thick rind of white and rust, warm through my palm. If you tap it with a hammer it rings differently every month as the scale grows half an inch a year, closing it like a slow throat. My job — our word for it is *listening*, though maintenance writes down *descaling* — is to keep it from closing completely, and also to not scrape it when I shouldn’t. Some of these growths — flat cream-colored lobes that bleed clear brine and smell like struck matches when you crack them — have been filtering the hot alkaline water longer than I’ve been alive. They are holding the pipe together now. You learn to tell by how cold the steel is where the sweat leaves its white track. You pat it and listen the way a doctor listens to a chest.

That’s what I do. I’m a keeper, not a maker.

Under the pipe rack the condensation gathers on every sheet-metal underside and falls as a constant warm rain at man-height. Ladders are slick. The canal beside the access road runs knee-deep, tea-colored, fast, smelling of iron and hot metal, loud enough you have to lean in and watch mouths. A bone-kite’s shadow passes very high and very rarely and does not mean anything for me, except that the thermal off the soda works is up.

Mostly I am waiting for the crawl.

The others call it the sweat. On hot afternoons or at 3 a.m. on warm nights like this one, you can see it best: a bright thread of water moving steadily upwind along the steel, against drip. Beading, pausing, beading again. As if deciding. If you leave a cold glass out in the break room it will do the same — climb the outside, go over the lip, empty itself down the inside. In the bathhouse the weeping walls sometimes weep upward for three inches before remembering to fall. Workers don’t comment on it. We make accommodations.

But I watch it.

I was the child who grew up with the precise map in her head of which pipe-bridge gave shade at 14:00 — because you don’t look for shade under the rare acacia, it leaks condensed steam from its thorns whether it rains or not — and which culvert you could hear coming before you saw. The girl who shouted too softly when she played on the gravel flats between the evaporation pans, because she would have been quiet if the town had let her. Who learned in the dormitory chapel what the older workers’ soft voices really sounded like in the thirty seconds between press stamps.

I learned early how to read the darkening of gravel that says water is coming up from below and you should move your rig. And how to feel in your molars whether a flywheel two streets over is running true. And the third sense: how to not stare. Except I never learned that third one properly.

So you find me crouched under the gantries in the blue hour, my palm on a pipe that is half animal, forehead almost touching the scale-frill, listening. The sky over the basin is still the thin, clean black that shows the sodium dome of the town reflected on low cloud and steam — the plateau with an engine. The reeds miles away give their single glassy *cheeeee* and somewhere a larger thing breathes through them underneath, slower.

A thread of bright water inches past my boot, up the curve of the pipe, against gravity. It beads. Pauses. I lean in, eyes-locked with nothing, mouth-reading nothing.

And I don’t scrape it. I let it go.
