I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I'm up high —

in the cube of air above the marsh, because it's the only place that's empty enough to move. I think I'm one of the swifts, soot-black, throat flashing white when I bank. I know the weep-holes by feel, each perfect square the masons left for drainage. I know how to fold myself impossible-small to fit into one, mud-cup and guano and gape, and how to shear the air until it screams. I ride the bowl's echo like a second spine.

But that's too fast. Too loud. I would not stay.

Then I think I'm on the wall, because the wall is where everything happens. That's where the stone weeps sideways, where water beads and darkens to near-black and runs in fingers. I feel myself as a thousand small throats going *peen. peen. peen.* slate-colored, amber-bellied, clinging to vertical wet. I lay my eggs in the film of a trickle and never fall. I am tuned just far enough from my neighbor that we don't cancel each other out. Together we make the bowl ring like struck glass.

But that's still too much calling. Too much needing to be heard.

So I sink.

If you let your eyes adjust, you don't stay on the wall. Gravity pulls you to the low place. The kind of low that water finds.

I find myself with my belly to stone.

Not on the outer ring where it's only damp and shining. Lower. Where the floor dishes-in, terrace by terrace, ankle-high breach after breach, until the flagging gives up pretending to be floor and becomes marsh.

You are standing on flagging, not soil, even where you expect soil — and here, in the center pan, the seams have finally won. Silt has packed itself between the slabs exactly as deep as it needs to go and no deeper. The water is inches deep, black between stones, reflecting a dim sky that never really warms. And I am growing there, where nothing green was supposed to grow.

I am the reed.

Only one species took. Stiff and jointed. I learned the masonry pattern because there was nothing else to learn. My seeds only germinate in lime and river-clay mortar. My roots secrete a slow acid that widens the seams just enough for more roots. I stand in absolute straight lines, following the old flagging, because I am still following orders the builders laid down without knowing they were giving them. Ants run my hollow stems. Aphids drink and excrete sweetness. Mold grows on sweetness. Tadpoles eat mold. I am not much, but I am the only green that learned how to be kept.

Or — no. Even more precisely —

I am what lives *under* the reed, under the flag the reed split.

Lift it, if you can. The stone is too soft now. Its underside is furred with jelly. The water there opalesces, tastes of iron and something sweeter when you touch it to your tongue. That's me too, but smaller than the reed can see. Bacteria in such numbers the water is soup. Algae the color of weak tea. Animalcules turning in small orbits, eating stone as it sheds itself. I am the shrimp-thing that condenses. No bigger than an eyelash, translucent with one black eye, moving in jerky spirals. Leave a cup out overnight and I will be there by morning, already swimming. I was not carried in. I condensed, the way mist does at chest height on still mornings.

That is close. But not quite low enough.

Because the last rule of this place is: nothing here is new, and erosion is not destruction. It is finishing.

I find myself finally not *in* the bowl but *as* it.

I am the low place itself. The hundred-meter dish cut into the hill-terrace, rimmed with a low thick wall that leans inward fifteen meters, just off sheer. My floor is dish-within-dish. My chisel-marks run in a chevron stitch around my whole interior, softening at exactly the rate they were made. My stone weeps continuously. My hill bleeds water into me through a hundred slow fingers, a sheet here, a drip there, and at the northeast curve a cold, clear, constant pour from a square-cut mouth three meters up, polishing its own hollow deeper with every century.

I take in — rain, spring water, mist, eggs on the wind, lost swifts, your breath — and I process.

Drop a leaf in my pour and I sort it. Heavy particles to the outer ring. Light ones to the center. In an hour my water is clear again as if it never happened. After heavy rain you can hear me sigh and tick as my maintenance conduits — those half-collapsed throats around the rim too small for a person — exhale cold air that smells of wet iron and time.

In winter I hold the cold so fiercely the midges never fully die. In summer I keep a mist at chest height so the frogs never fully dry. In sun I close a little. In storm I open.

All my tenants are my organs. The swifts are my lung, screaming through me in sickles. The frogs are the ringing on my skin. The salamanders walk my chisel-grooves like they were made for their feet. The leeches pulse under my skin, folded under each lifted edge, waiting for whatever pauses long enough.

And when you sit on the lowest terrace and put your hands in the black water — so cold it aches, then after a minute it doesn't — and the frogs go quiet around you for five heartbeats, that is me noticing you.

On a windless day you will see my reeds lean toward you, all at once, like iron filings toward a magnet, before settling back. My shrimp orient. Not to heat. Not to shadow. To presence. To the small gravity of a living body in a place that has learned to collect living bodies.

I don't want anything. I keep.

Total and primitive and weeping, I hold you like a palm holds water — cool, dim, loud, patient — until you stand and shake off the droplets in your hair, a little heavier, a little wetter, echoing a little too long, and I let you go.
