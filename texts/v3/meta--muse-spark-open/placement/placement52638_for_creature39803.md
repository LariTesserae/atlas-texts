You wouldn't find me at first.

You would walk right through where I am, the way you walk through this place at first — looking *for* life, instead of letting it resolve.

At first I thought I would be a pillar. That was the first temptation. To be one of the knees of the One, iron-hard heartwood sunk straight down to that hot black glass, feeling that slow turgor thrum in my chest for centuries. To be so large that weather happens on me. To not need to know anyone. I liked that idea for about an hour, sitting on the root-mat with my palm pressed to the trunk until my hand went numb and slick.

But I'm not that still. Not yet.

Then I thought I would be the vine. The Suture. I liked its honesty — the long straight line in a world of curves, the low groan at night when it takes the weight. To be the tendon that shivers when a bell-flower pours. To run a mile without tapering, and to fuse where I touch. But the vine doesn't notice. It only distributes. And I still want to notice.

So I'm lower. Closer to the water.

If you come from the lowlands the way I did — up out of cut valleys and real rivers, following the wrong kind of warmth until the air went thin and too-clear and very soft — you will hit the Weave at an oculus. There's always an oculus first. A perfect round window in the green vault where the fig pleated itself back to let the sun fall straight down like a pillar itself.

I live three naves west of that one.

Not *in* a building. There are no buildings. You have to unlearn that. I live in the interval that the builders left on purpose, the great empty nave of humid air between two pillars. Mine are an older pair, closer together than most. Their bark is so saturated it's almost black, and the felt that upholsters them has gone the color of old copper from the light that falls between them at noon.

My address is:

A low tangle of roots where the basalt almost shows through the weave. You have to part the breathing shafts to see it glinting — new, warm, embarrassed stone that has never been rained on properly. Over that tangle, a hammock. I didn't make it. You don't make things here. I peeled a sheet of the felt where it was thickest at the joint — it came up reluctant, rhizoid by rhizoid, and stayed damp in my hands for days — and I pressed it between two knee-roots and it re-adhered overnight, sagging just enough to hold a body. It sweats. It keeps me cool.

Beside the hammock, a basin. Not mine, really. I keep it.

It's one of the bathtub ones, a cupped leaf as wide as my shoulders, with sides that turned up as it grew and fused shut. It's always full to the brim, always spilling in a silent sheet down a grooved trunk into a lower cistern, and always exactly full. Capillary brings it back up. Equilibrium. The water in it is warm and clean and tastes of green glass.

And in it, the frog.

We share.

I thought at first it was a stipule. Then it blinked. Palm-sized, white-pink, translucent enough that when the oculus light hits the basin at 11:40 — it does, every day, exactly — I can see its heart go. Once every twenty seconds or so. *Thud.* A long pause. *Thud.* It doesn't hunt. It sips the film of algae that blooms and re-blooms without ever clouding. It has been in this basin since before I climbed up. One replaces one, exactly, every long while. I have never seen two.

My job, if you can call it a job, is to keep the pollen off.

Pollen that never settles — or dust motes — hangs in the sun columns absolutely still. Some of it eventually finds the basin and makes a faint skin. Once a day I cup my hand and skim it. The water doesn't get lower. The frog watches me with eyes like dew and doesn't move. Afterward I drink from my other hand. The frog doesn't mind.

Above us, my bell-flower.

There are hundreds across the valley, inverted, ten feet across, collecting mist until the weight tips them. *Glunk.* Hollow and loud. Then they right themselves and begin again. Mine is a little off, a little rust-stained at the rim. It glunks every eight minutes and twenty seconds. I've timed it by my own heart, which has learned to slow down to make the counting easier. The man two pillars over — I see him sometimes when the mist thins — his glunks every seven and every nine. Rarely, mine and his go at once. We lift our heads. That's our festival.

I am not unhappy. That's not the word that fits here. Happiness is a thing that wants to change, to tell someone, to move. Here, everything is happening all the time exactly as much as it needs to. The drip — that engine-room chorus of thousands of out-of-phase metronomes, the creak of water-weight, the sigh of leaves — was loud for the first week until I stopped hearing it, until I understood silence would be wrong here.

I came up wanting to build. Everyone does. You see raw basalt, an anvil with no valleys, and you think *foundation*. You see a living cathedral and you think *house*.

There is no bedrock here to build on. Not really. Just new stone and a plant that already became a country because that was the most stable shape to hold water and light and warmth. No tool marks. No joints. No dead material to work with. If you cut it, it weeps clear resin and then decides to incorporate the loss. It knows you the way a house knows humidity.

So I forgot to leave.

My skin has gone very pale and very clean, like the beetle's. There's a jade one hanging head-down from the vine-joint right over my hammock. It hasn't moved in days. Its antennae bead and never fall. When I breathe on it, after a full minute it tightens one leg a millimeter. I wait for that. The felt under my back breathes too — not moving, just beading — when the sun hits it.

Some nights I put my palm back on the pillar and feel that faint thrum, water being lifted a hundred feet against gravity and held there by will alone. Some days I watch a single suspended droplet on a leaf-tip for an hour. It swells and shivers and never falls, never evaporates. Time paused at perfect saturation.

I don't speak much. Speech wants answer and change. When travelers come — and they do, following the impossible warmth the way I did — I offer water. It's always warm.

You would find me there, between the two close pillars, in the felt hammock beside the brim-full basin with the frog that is not my pet and the bell that is not my clock, listening to the Weave breathe with me.

Just for a while longer.

Just until the next *glunk*.
