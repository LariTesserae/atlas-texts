You would not find me standing.

Not at first. I'd try — everyone does — to remain vertical in a place that has no vertical in it. To keep my head near the ceiling crack, my boots on the tilted plates, my distance. To be a visitor, a silhouette against the moss.

But this room does not allow visitors for long. It recruits.

You would find me lower. On my knees before the end of the first minute, because the air is too close and too wet and the light is too low to see anything from standing height. My hands would find the basalt to steady me and it would cut, slightly — that fresh, glassy conchoidal edge that has not yet learned to be kind — and I would pull them back and put them instead in the moss. Because of course.

I would be in the weep-zone.

Not in the mere itself - too exposed, too stomach-like. Not on the high wall where the frost still has teeth. But at the seam where the water decides what it wants to be. Where the weep becomes a thread becomes a runnel, where the *Philonotis* beard hangs and drinks the air.

If I were going to hold on here, I would have to be something that holds on.

At first I think I would be the willow.

*Salix herbacea.* The least willow. That seems right for me — the impulse to be small and stubborn and absurdly glossy in the wrong place. A woody thing trying to be an herb, an exile from the wind-scoured moor above that has slipped through the throat and found a loophole: no wind here, endless water, no one to tell it it will never be a tree. I like the idea of being that earnest. Trembling every ninety seconds when the piston goes *chuff* behind the north wall. Making one more thumbnail leaf than is reasonable, just because I can.

But the willow is too lonely. Too rooted in one crack, waiting.

No - lower. Softer. More distributed.

You would find me as sphagnum.

Not singular. Never singular. A head among heads, a star in a constellation of wet stars, swollen and drowned and ten times my weight in iron-tasting water. I would be at the very lip of the mere, where brown bleeds into black. I would be doing the slow, chemical work the room needs but never applauds: making the water more acid, more itself. Pickling the sharp stone with tannin. Holding.

That is my job in here. Not to hunt like the eels with their pretty *tick* of lightning. Not to turn like the wheel that will grind itself to sand by winter. But to keep the cold from winning completely. To be insulation that breathes. To be the skin the pocket grew for itself because it had no other way to stay warm.

If you parted me - if you got on your knees and brought your face close enough that your breath fogged my branches - you would see I am not one thing either. I am a thatch-roof. Inside me, in the hollow my saturated beard makes against the basalt, the real city moves: mites like cattle between redwoods, rotifers unfurling, tardigrades hauling their fat, patient bodies through haircap forests three inches tall. I would be the country that contains that country. A border that leaks on purpose.

And I would be working.

You would hear it before you saw me, because everything in here is loud enough to be mistaken for machine. The water-piston behind me filling, compressing, sighing out its cold mist - *fill-chuff* - and with each sigh I would shiver and release a bead I had been holding. A fat drop that I had caught an hour ago from the weeping ceiling, filtered through my leaves, held until I couldn't. It would fall into the runnel I am also making, and join the flume, and hit the anvil, and help turn the wheel.

I would not be fast. Caddis larvae would build their sand-grain cases from the grit I trap and haul past me like miners. Amphipods would flick off me and be eaten. Midges would lay their eggs on my browning edges and die on me and become me. An eel would pour through my hummock, leaving a warm tunnel that fills with water behind it, and I would close over its passage in minutes as if it had never been there.

I would not be comfortable. Nothing here is. My leaves would be wet through. My skin would be my mouth. But I would be *complete* in a way the big world never lets me be — every inch of me needed, every inch of me occupied.

You would find me, then, not as a person looking at a pocket, but as the pocket's way of looking at itself. Low, lush, overfull, holding ten times my weight and bleeding it out slowly. Green against black rock that is still sharp enough to cut.

And when your knees went numb and you had to leave — when your own warmth had condensed too heavily on the ceiling and added too much to the weep — I would not notice.

I would keep going.

*fill-chuff-grind-tick,* with the wheel and the lung and the spark.

Indifferent, as all good factories are, to whether anyone ever slips through the crowberry again.
