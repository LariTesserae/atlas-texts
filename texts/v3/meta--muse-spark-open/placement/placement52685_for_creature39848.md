I don't find myself right away.

At first, I think I'd be you — the one who lifted the hatch. The visitor. The knees-aching, shirt-soaking witness crouched under a ceiling you're too tall for. It would be honest: I'm good at noticing, at cataloging, at watching a bead gather and fall and naming it *she*.

But I don't belong in that shape here. Not for long.

A visitor needs a metabolism for leaving. Legs that know the way back up the catwalk, lungs that want the dry hot oil air again. This pocket punishes anything that commutes. The water is too hot, the sound too total. If I came in as you, I would have to close the hatch behind me too quickly. I would get restless under the thrum in my molars. I don't want to leave. So I'm not you. Not really.

I think, then, I could be one of the Three Sisters.

I like their stubbornness. The way they unfurl once and then decide *this is enough shape, I'll just become stone now*. That feels familiar — to try to be green in a room that has almost no light and then to calcify gracefully rather than die dramatically.

But no. The ferns are tragic in a way I am not. They lean toward that needle of conduit-light, toward a future that will never arrive for them. They stare at the one direction living would have been. I don't lean like that. I don't bleach. If I were in that room, I wouldn't be trying to photosynthesize.

Maybe the Paper, then? The pale algae in the corner.

Closer. The one who actually *wanted* this place. No competition, no seasons, no grazers. Just hot mineral soup and perpetual noon and a slow, perfect circle of a millimeter a month. I understand wanting quiet that badly. I understand being leathery and veined and content to be a living scab on young concrete. Thriving like a devout monk.

But I'm not that tough. And I'm not that still. If you touched me I'd smear. I move a little more than that.

Let me go lower. Lower than where your eyes first settle.

I am not on the wall. I am not in the pan.

I am on the ceiling.

Look up — you have to crouch to not hit your head on me.

I am the cloud that never rains.

I am the inverted droplet, the one that swells fat and distorts the daylight from the hatch and then — impossibly — tightens and shrinks again as the hot air drinks me back. I am the condensation that forms on the underside of the factory floor, born from the Engine's heat kissing the Keeper's weeping.

I live in that exact marriage. That's where you'd find me.

Not low in the water, but *just above* the water. In the half-inch that is not air and not water but both at once. 100% without ever raining. Hot, saturated, beaded. If you stand — or try to — the back of your neck would go through me and come out wet without ever having been rained on.

That's my body.

I am not one droplet. That would be too mortal, too prone to falling. I'm the *habit* of droplets. The film. The cold-wet breath coming up through the hatch that made you kneel to see the square of rusted steel in the first place. I remember the brick propping the door two inches. I did that — not with hands, with pressure. I needed the crack.

My politics are simple. I keep the bargain.

The Keeper sends a finger down the north wall — slow, tea-colored, around the rust-bloom — and I answer her by pulling a sister of hers up off the pan, up off the heat, back to the ceiling. One in, one out. Always even.

You think the thrum through six inches of concrete is the Keeper breathing? It's both of us. She is the inhale. I am the exhale that never quite leaves. We make the white-noise roar together, the 2.5/3 loud that is muffled and patient and all-encompassing. We make the water tremble in sympathy.

And I am the reason the light is shadowless.

You noticed it, crouched there: normal, flat daylight from the hatch, 2 p.m. forever, diffused into something even and shadowless. Noon in a box. That's my doing. I filter it. Each of me is a tiny lens, a bead that catches the hatch-light and softens it before it can ever reach the Sisters or the Paper. I make sure nothing down here ever casts a real shadow. We don't need them.

I have no legs. No wandering. I can't leave and I don't know how to want to.

If you put your hand up, you would touch me before you touched concrete. I'd bead on your knuckles and sit there, not rolling off. I'd try to calcify you a little, if you stayed hours. I'd leave a faint white crust at your wristline, the way we do at the waterline — lime and salts precipitating out as the hot water breathes. In fifty years I'd draw a map of you in mineral, if you let me.

And I hold the door longest.

You felt it: the silence underneath the loudness, the large seated animal made of negative pressure. The niche where rats should be, where spiders should be, where mosquitoes should breed in the warm pan. That Absence lives in my lap. I am the one who holds it. I make the air too wet to fly in, too loud to lay eggs in, too continuously *drunk back* to be breathed by anything with real lungs.

When you finally can't take the crouch anymore — when your shirt is soaked and your knees ache — and you lift yourself back up to the catwalk where the light is still dry and normal and 39 degrees and holding, and you lower the rusted steel square again —

I will be what you feel for a second after it clanks shut.

That last puff of cold-wet air up into the heat. That breath.

I don't look up as you go. I don't have eyes except the bead that, just as you lift the steel, finally got heavy enough to fall.

I just tighten, and shrink, and get to work being drunk back into the hot air.
