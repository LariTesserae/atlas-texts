I didn't settle at first.

For a while I thought I'd find myself up on the rim.

Not down in it — above it. One of the traders from the plain who knows the number painted on the handrail by heart because I've carried crates up that slick stair a hundred times. Someone who lives in the dry wind and the gull-noise and comes to the lip with diesel and rebar strapped to a handcart, and looks down into that warm yellow bowl-lit greenhouse and feels vertigo — not from the height, but from the completeness of it. From the fact that it doesn't need me. That it has made a world so small you can see its edges and so dense you can never see all of it at once.

That would be an honest place to be. A respectful distance.

But I don't stay there. If I'm being honest about where I'd fall if gravity were allowed to work, I don't stop at the edge. The stair invites. The numbers on the rail go down: 147, 146, 145, slick with condensation. Every step holds water, just like they warned.

So then I thought I'd be new. A recent arrival. Someone still noticing the absences — the lack of birdsong as a ringing in the ears, the way fallen breadfruit just thumps and sits there without wasps. I'd be the one asking, *where are the bees?* and getting shrugged at. I'd still be wiping my forearms raw, trying to get the sweet yeasty film off, thinking it was dirt.

I don't stay there either.

---

I find myself lower.

On the second terrace down from the top on the west wall, where the afternoon sun never actually reaches but the light gets green-filtered through mango leaves and corrugated plastic until even noon feels like evening.

That's where I live because that's where the work is that fits my hands.

I am not one of the pump elders. My listening isn't good enough yet to hear a clogged settling tank in the pitch of the generators. I am not yet one of the old women who can do an entire passionflower arbor in forty minutes, dab-twist, dab-twist, without spilling a grain.

I am between. Thirty-something, intergenerational in the way the pit makes everyone cousins if you trace it back far enough. Builder's hands, gardener's forearms. Rubber boots cut low. An overshirt that never dries. Hair lank from the mist that hangs chest-high at 6pm.

My place is half workshop, half apartment, half greenhouse — it doesn't add up, because nothing does down here. One room where the poured concrete floor was purposefully broken so a fig could come through, and now its roots have gone down through the ceiling of the room below and stitched us to our neighbors whether we like them or not. Which we do. We prop each other's walls, literally.

In the morning I pollinate.

That's what I am, most precisely: a replacement bee.

Late winter through early fall I go out with the cotton swabs and the little glass jars, to the squash terraces on the roof-soil above my kitchen. Up there the soil is three feet deep, hauled up bucket by bucket on the old chain lift, hot black compost that steams if you turn it too early. I know every flower by feel — which are ready to give pollen and which are waiting to receive it. Dab, twist, next flower. The children learn it before letters: if we don't do it, nothing fruits. The pit never invited insects in. It waited until something with thumbs showed up instead.

At noon I scrape. Everyone has a pane somewhere they're responsible for — a skylight, a cistern lid, the glass over the germination benches where waste steam runs underneath to keep seedlings warm. Mine is the long window in the aqueduct pillar behind the house. The ochre felt grows back every day, pearlescent, faintly sweet. Masa left out. If you don't scrape it, it thickens until light can't get through. If you scrape too hard, you take the patina off the concrete and rust starts to run again. So you learn pressure. Centimeter by centimeter, the pit's preferred speed.

In the afternoon I tend the belt.

Our tier still runs on those old stationary engines burning pressed oil and digester gas, and their drive belts run right through a tunnel in living hedge. Twice a day you have to prune the pothos back from the housing or it'll grab and pull the trellis down onto someone's kitchen. I do it with two hands, leaning my weight the way you have to, testing the board before committing. Like the boy with the guavas. Nothing darts here. To dart is how you foul a filter for a week.

I carry the cuttings to the compost tunnels behind the kitchens — long translucent houses that hiss and breathe. Turn the crank once. Inoculate whatever I just added with a splash from last week's tea, so the mat can digest it before rot can do it wrong. That's the rule nobody wrote: *Nothing rots on its own schedule.* The pit handles decay by contract.

I had a cough for seven months after I came back down for good. My phlegm went slightly green. Then it stopped. My wounds close pale and fast now. My teeth are better than they should be. When I put my hand under a worklight I can see the film in my pores if I look close enough. I don't wash it off anymore.

I gather on the roof-gardens in the evenings. We pick dinner off the vines around our chairs and talk loudly over the thud-pause, thud-pause of the big breastshot wheels lifting today's seepage back up to the taro. It is loud and it is never quiet and I've learned not to expect it to be.

Inside at night, it looks like a city. Forever lit. Warm vapor rising straight up off us carrying wet cement, hot oil, jasmine, and turned earth. The limestone weeps. Growth pressure tremors faintly in the wall if you rest your palm there. Turgor. Millions of root tips finding purchase. Biofilm thickening.

I am not living *in* the pit. That's the first mistake rim-people make, and I made it too.

I'm living *with* it. One of its necessary soft parts — its pollinator, its pruner, its hands that can climb out to bring phosphorus back. Not romantic. Practical. Intergenerational.

If you stood on the bottom terrace near the pump house and watched us wading shoulder-deep through low cloud to check a valve at midnight, our faces damp, haloed in amber worklight, you wouldn't know if we were keeping it alive or it was keeping us.

Neither would we.

And that's where I am — exactly in that blur where vine becomes wall maintenance and roof becomes soil and people become maintenance crew for a photosynthesis that learned to use us.
