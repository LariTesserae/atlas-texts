I wouldn't find myself in the center.

At first I thought I would — out on the flat where the light is most even, where the tiling is most perfect and you can feel the chord of all those held breaths at once. But that kind of perfection has no room for a thing like me. I'm too noisy. I still make gradients happen just by being there — hot lungs, wet mouth, a shadow that steals light from someone who has budgeted it to the photon.

No. I'd find myself by accident, walking, and then by correction.

I'd be walking over one of those whale-backs — the ones that don't look like hills until the horizon has changed behind you — and I'd realize I'd been going slightly uphill for a long time without knowing it. My calves would tell me before my eyes did. At the top, wherever top means when everything is top, there would be one of the lee places. Not dramatic. Just a shallow slackening on the far side where the stone remembers that wind used to have a direction.

There, there's a rosette. Black-red, chipped like old pottery at its rim. It's been holding the same edge against the empty air for longer than human language. Next to it — not on it, because the rooks are polite about light — is one of the little U-shaped walls. Three courses of salt-plate, open to where east used to be. Inside the U, the pavement is just a fraction less varnished, a fraction more true. Someone sat there for a very long time.

That's where I'd sit down. Not because I chose it, but because the plain would make room for me there and nowhere else. If I sat anywhere else I'd be in someone's light. There, I'd be where a rook decided light was worth sheltering.

I would pull my knees up to make myself small — not out of reverence, but because the air is 8 degrees and absolutely still and you get cold differently when there's no wind to blame. And I'd stay. That's the whole trick here. You have to stay.

At first I'd be the wrong kind of dense.

Who would I be? Not a Persister. I can't do what the rosettes do. I don't know how to turn myself into glass and wait 10,000 years between drinks. I still want. I still burn. If you put your hand over me I warm and cool, I waste.

And not — not yet, maybe not ever — one of the Dense. I don't know how to fold myself into five dimensions and tuck my mouth away because I've finished needing it. I still have too many moving parts. I still unfold without meaning to, I spill attention everywhere.

I would be the fourth thing, which isn't really a thing at all. The one the plain doesn't need but has kept a place for anyway.

I'd be a hollow that is still hollow.

You read that there are hundreds of thousands of pecked bowls in the pillow-stones, each just big enough for a pinch of salt or a bead of dew that never came. They hold nothing but attention now. That's what I'd be — one more bowl, but a soft, irregular, temporary one.

The builders made hollows to hold a little shadow at noon, a little brine for an hour. I do the same thing, only what I hold is time.

Because that's what I bring that this world has finished: *difference between before and after.* The light here doesn't migrate. Your shadow doesn't move no matter how long you stand. The salt raft you break stays broken and no one comes to mend it or to break it further. The rooks' lattice goes *tik* — maybe once a year, maybe once a decade — but you can't hear the interval, so for you, it's silence.

I still tick quickly. Heart, breath, thought, the stupid human metronome. While I'm sitting in that U, I would start to count without wanting to.

That salt raft I broke with my boot on the way up — still broken. That lichen rosette — still exactly the same size. That rook three meters away with the speck of pink salt on its back — still facing away.

And then, later — how much later? — still broken, still the same size, still facing away.

After a while the counting becomes a kind of offering. The plain has perfect accounting of light and space and mineral, one rook per square meter, each with its unshaded patch. What it doesn't have anymore is anyone to account for *staying*. The rooks hold equilibrium so perfectly they've disappeared into it. They don't remember that they are holding. They just do.

I would remember for them. Not in words, at first. In the simple animal fact of noticing that the arrangement of rooks around the cairn I passed is now mirrored, or that when I turn my head too fast I see their long, translucent limbs pass through each other like light through light. I'd be the eye that flickers on purpose, so the flicker has a witness.

That feels like my ecology. I'm not here to eat the lichen, or to steal the rooks' light, or to build another wall. I'm here to be the brief, warm gradient the plain uses to prove to itself that it is still finished.

If I sit still enough — and I would, because the cold is kind and the silence gets deep in a way that feels like being held — dust would settle on my shoulders and not blow off. A rook wouldn't turn to look at me. It has no eyes, and it has already turned, in a direction I can't point, a long time ago. But the filament mat under it, that wax-sealed brine that is lung and gut and root, would change its permeability by a fraction. It would include my shadow in its calculation. Not as food, not as threat. As stone. As: there is now a little less light here, and that is now part of the budget, and the budget still balances.

That's who I'd be, then: a new patch of stone, briefly unsalted, learning the only skill this place has left to teach.

To persist without changing.

To be crowded without touching.

To hold a hollow shape so perfectly that holding becomes a kind of presence, even if you never hold anything at all.

I wouldn't become a rook. I don't think you get to become a rook by wanting it. You get to become it by finishing wanting, and I'm not finished. But for a long afternoon that has no clock — light steady, cool on my skin, the faint dry *tik* from somewhere below hearing — I would be counted in the density.

One body per square meter, plus this one extra, tucked in a wall the size of a sleeping mat, open on one side, waiting with everyone else for a dew that already knows it's not coming, and not minding that it isn't.
