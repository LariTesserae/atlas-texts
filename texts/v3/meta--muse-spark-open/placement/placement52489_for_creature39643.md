I thought first I would be high.

That's the mistake you always make here at first — that "high" means "up."

I wanted to be the Glider-Hawk. Of course I did. Who doesn't want to be the one thing that finally got to love the wind instead of hiding from it — six feet of wing and a ribbon tail, circling for thirty miles without a beat, never landing, eating the air itself? To be the high eye that can see the whole table at once — the domes like sea-swell, the pans like polished thumbprints, the brown threads of rivers connecting nothing to nothing.

But I lived there for half a thought and it was lonely. Too much wind in the eyes. Too much distance. A hawk doesn't *live* on the plateau; it borrows it. It sleeps clinging like a bat to a cold lee and calls once at 3 a.m. because it has no one to answer it for miles.

So I came down.

I thought then I would be the Reed Tiger — because if I'm going to be low, let me be perfectly low. Let me be the muscle in the reeds, the perfect circle pressed into twelve-foot reed beds where the stems bend out around me. The tunnel-maker. The drowner. The one nobody ever sees, only the place where I was — paw five inches wide, moss tamped between the toes, a platform just above floodline. That was better. That was honest. To live in that hiss and crash of fiber, to know every pan the capybaras will drink from next.

But a tiger is too much hunger, too much solitude here. And tigers don't tie red grass. They don't remember.

Lower still.

I tried being water for a while. That was close. To be the brown surge in a laterite groove no deeper than your knee, booming and slapping, undercutting a bank every few minutes with a wet thud. To be born at 2 p.m. out of nothing — heat off granite hitting damp moss-breath — falling so hard you can't hear yourself, and gone by 3:30, steaming. To drain into a sinkhole and pray to a Sink Maw. That felt true to this place daily being unmade. But water doesn't stay. And what lives here — really lives — is what has learned to hold on.

I found myself where you find yourself if you stay here more than a day:

In the boundary layer. In the still air an inch above the moss.

I am not tall. I am not fast. I am not rare.

I am the old woman under the low slab ten yards away from where you thought you were sleeping alone.

If you slept on my granite last night, you slept on my roof.

My name is not important here; we don't use names the way lowland people do. My band calls me by what I know: *Where The Pan Held Nine Days*. That's my name because in the year my grandmother was born, after a bad storm season when the bogs should have been dry, one pan behind the second dome to the east held amber water for nine days. She remembered it, told my mother the shape of its moss cup and the way the fern-trees shadowed it at noon. My mother told me. Last dry season I found it by that story — the same cup, the same shadow — and it still held. Not nine days, now. Seven. The moss there is a little thinner. But it held.

That's how we live low. We don't own. We inherit coordinates made of shadow and sphagnum.

You will find me exactly where you left me — you didn't see me. I was the low stone. Three slabs of pink granite, pink as gums, leaning in. Thatch weighted with the same stones my mother's mother's husband put there. My ringwall is only three courses high, not to keep you out — there is no one to keep out — but to break the wind for the fire pit. When your stranger-boot crushed the crimson mat-grass coming in yesterday, I watched your silhouette.

You were high. You stood up straight in the midday wind. Children die that way here. The wind takes standing things and makes organ music of them. The basalt pillars west of here have natural holes and when the 60-mile gusts hit, they sing a chord that answers itself from another dome, and I have heard that chord take thatch off a shelter that was built too proud. So I waited to see if you would learn to kneel.

You did. Eventually you got your knees into the Moss-Mother.

That's where I live: *in* her, not on her. On the domes she is only ankle-deep, silver-green and waxy on top, tight against sun. In my pan-basin she is chest-deep. If you push your arm in to the elbow it's cold — real cold — against sun-warmed granite. That is my refrigerator, my well, my barometer. About twenty minutes before the daily cell builds and rips open, she glows. Foxfire along her fruiting tips, faint green with no heat. Static in the mycelium. My grandmother taught me: when the glow comes, get out of the pan. So I did. You stayed in yours a little too long. I watched you run when the white curtain hit and the lightning began hitting the domes one by one, each crack shaking your ribs.

Do you remember the rain pools at dawn? The ones that were perfectly still and beaded glass? You knelt to drink and saw a sky that wasn't ours — a paler sun, too many small moons scattered like spilt salt. You looked up, quick, and there was only our yellow-white sun and proper shadows. Looked down again, there they were again, those other moons. You flinched.

I drank anyway. A wrong reflection still tells you where the next cloud will thicken. And thirst will make you taste pepper and protein — the mist shrimp in the moss — if you squeeze a handful into your mouth. I did that once while you were watching the sky. You thought it was strange.

We collect honey differently, too. We don't keep bees; the Fist Bee does that. Matte black, drunken thrum, an inch and a half of ground-effect flight two inches above the moss through wind that would tumble your hawk. She stores her honey in abandoned bathtub bromeliads — those enameled leather cisterns three feet across that hold eight gallons of leaf tea and salamander birth-water. Each one a world. If I find one capped with wax before the ants do, we eat for a week. If I find one flowering — that terrible scarlet spear twelve feet straight up into killing wind for one day before it shreds — I hum.

We hum back to the wind. We have pierced chert pipes for that. When the basalt pillars organ-chord to each other across the domes, we answer. The glider-hawks hear. It tells them we are still down here, listening. Listening is the whole job.

My work is grinding. Inside my lean-to there is a hollow worn into the granite where hands have worked for generations. Chert roots, bromeliad hearts, fermented moss. The hollow was shallow when my grandmother found it, now it's a smooth bowl you can rest a baby in. I grind there every evening after the steam rises. My capybara-hide smells of smoke and tallow. My hair is cut short against lice and wind.

My people are very few — thirty at most, thin as the one tree where there should be a forest, one boulder where there should be a moraine. We tie red grass where a child took first steps. We place a smooth river stone on a ledge above a sink where water vanishes into cracked granite, so the river remembers to come back. The spirals and eyes and wavy lines on the quartz veins circle those maws. We don't need to explain the glowing moss or the other-sky in the water. We use it.

Last night, while you lay on warm granite soaking slowly through your shirt from the moss underneath, while the Milky Way got bright enough to throw your shadow and the reeds roared and hushed and roared — I was watching you from fern-frond bedding not yet rotted.

I was deciding if you knew to keep your silhouette down.

You did, eventually. You flattened when the pan-capys flattened — those shaggy amber-water stones that become vegetation when they hold still. You didn't run when the laterite bank collapsed in the night with its wet thud. You lay and let the 3 a.m. thimble frogs peen — that one pure, lost note a mile off, all the carpet singing that the pools had refilled.

So this morning I left you something.

Not food. You have moss water for that.

A bundle of red grass, tied with vine.

It's at the edge of your pan, where the moss is thickest, just before the ground swales and the next storm will try to wash you clean off the high, flat, green, roaring table of the world.

If you know what it means, you'll tie one back.

And move low, before the sky builds itself out of nothing right above your head.
