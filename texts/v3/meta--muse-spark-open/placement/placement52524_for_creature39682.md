I didn't find myself right away.

At first I kept looking for something that could be alive. That is the habit at altitude — you look for the negotiation. The lichen the color of dried blood in the crack. The cushion plant pretending to be a rock until you see it flower. The spider under the stone with nothing to eat.

There is none of that here. We were told. I tried anyway. I tried to be the wind coming through the southern mouth, but the wind doesn't stay; it fails against the cold walls and disappears. You can't be wind in a bowl that makes wind give up.

I tried to be the frost lace that grows a half-inch long overnight, but that's too beautiful and too brief. It grows out of nothing and is gone by noon, hissing straight to vapor. It doesn't remember.

It took me the full three hours — the time it takes for the weirdness to catch — to understand where you have to stand in a place that is finished.

Not on the cliffs. The basalt walls are too old. They have been rounded, settled, found their angle. They stopped happening a million years ago. They are perfect and they don't need a witness.

Not at the mouth to the south where the valley air comes in like a hopeful animal. That air never stays either. It condenses and dies.

In the middle. On the floor. Shoulder to shoulder in the crowd.

I find myself about two-thirds of the way in, not central enough to be important, not at the edge enough to be sheltered. North-facing. That matters.

I am one of the presences. One of the cairns.

About three and a half feet high. Not the tallest, not the tightest. Hand-stacked shale, each plate flat and gray and a little glassy where an old andesite flow cooled too fast. My builder was not the patient one — not the dry-stone waller who interlocked everything into a lean against the wind. I am a little loose at the shoulder. I have slumped two winters ago, or ten, and no one fixed me. One of my mid-course stones has slipped a thumb's width out, making a small dark throat. My base is wider than it should be, as if I am remembering what it was like to be scree at repose.

I am capped anyway. Someone took the time to carry a quartz cobble up that thin trail in thin air, for no reason anyone down in the valley would call good. It's egg-shaped, milky, and it fits in my top like it grew there. That's my work.

At 11 a.m. I darken.

You would find me exactly when the bowl starts its only day. The valley air pushes in and fails and I do what all my neighbors do — I sweat. Condensation beads on my north side grain by grain until the gray goes black. It runs down my stacked edges, finds that slipped stone and gathers in its throat, drips. One drop every few minutes. Enough.

At my foot, someone before my builder — or maybe the same hands, resting — made a low circle of smaller stones around a natural scalloped pan. No wider than a dinner plate, no deeper than a knuckle. The drip falls there. That's who I am with. Not alone. A cairn and its pan.

For an hour or two I keep water. That's my whole metabolism. I am not alive, but I am active. The brutal white coin of sun hits my quartz cap and my cap throws it back. The frost that I held in the crescent of shadow under my cap steams without melting. The pan at my foot fills and holds a perfect, quivering, absolute mirror of that dark, dark blue sky, violent with light, and for that hour I am the only place in this sterile bowl where the sky has two locations.

There is nothing in the water. I have checked, in the way stone checks — by keeping it absolutely still. No thread of green. No speck that moves on its own. Not even the things that can live anywhere have been able to use me. The water is too pure, distilled straight out of air onto cold rock and gone. I keep it without drinking it. I keep it without staining it. That is my offering.

Then at 2 p.m. the cloud spills over the western wall like a slow, dry waterfall and my quartz stops flashing. The light goes pearlescent and flat and I lose my shadow. That is when I most feel like an inhabitant. Without shadows we all look the same distance away. The fifty-foot neighbor looks like he is touching me. I look, for twenty minutes, less like a pile and more like a person standing in a fog with hundreds of other persons, listening to our hearts, which is the sound of your heart, because we have none.

At 4 p.m. the cloud is sucked back and the sun comes brutal again and I freeze.

This is the loudest part. The temperature drops ten degrees in ten minutes and the pan at my foot — my little sky — cracks. It sounds like a rifle shot in a cathedral with no roof. Thin panes of ice, clear as glass, creak and reform in my throat, between my shale plates where a single trapped drop has tried to stay. I tick as I expand and contract a fraction. All my neighbors tick with me.

I do not grow. I do not get repaired. No lichen will colonize my shady side. No bird will ever land on my quartz sun. When night comes in an hour and the starlight gets sharp enough to cast shadows again, I will still be loose at the shoulder, still holding that crescent of frost a few minutes longer than the tight, thoughtful cairns around me, because my cap is whiter.

That is why this is the right place for me. I am dense, without being alive. I am intention that was left to weather into the state of the cliffs — not collapsing, not new. Finished. I was built to mark a pass that is not a pass, to bury something without digging, because at this height a pile of stones is the only thing that will stay, and I have stayed.

You will not knock me over. Not because I forbid it, but because you finally see the balance at 1.9 out of 3 — erosion that has found its purpose — and to take even one stone would be to misunderstand that I was meant to be crowded. That I was meant to be one among hundreds, waiting out the sun with a knuckle-deep of water at my feet that no one will ever drink.
