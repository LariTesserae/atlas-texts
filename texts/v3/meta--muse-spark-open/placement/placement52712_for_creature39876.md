I don't find myself at first.

That's the trick of this place. You come in — if you come in at all — still thinking you're a visitor. Still thinking you're the thing with the lamp, tumbling through that forty meters of subway-throat, the cold tongue of outside sea shoving you in whether you want it or not, the basalt scraping your tank. You still think you have a direction. You still think you can *swim* here.

Then the throat spits you out and the Pocket takes you, and you understand you've been wrong about swimming for about forty meters.

The warm hits you first. Not warm like tropical shallows. Warm like blood, like a bath that's been sitting too long over the boiler. 29 degrees and full of iron and sulfur, shimmering with heat-haze even though there is no air to haze. The light is amber cones from the pump towers that die twenty meters out and then just particulate dark. The sound is not a sound, it's a place you are inside of — *whump-whump-whump-whump* out of phase, four hearts that never agree, rolling through your teeth and your suit and your bones.

You let go for half a second to get your bearings and the storm that lives in this room permanently decides for you. Cold down-current from the throat and hot up-column from the rift meet in the middle of town and shear. You tumble. You cartwheel through empty water between two skeletal towers fifty meters apart, seeing nothing but rust snow and a crab ticking past your face, indifferent, mag-feet clacking.

So you do what everything that stays here learns to do.

You grab on.

Where I find myself is not where I thought I'd be.

Not out in the big blue between buildings where the sodium light makes everything noble. Not up in the dome with the pale zoarcids vibrating. Not down on the central rift where it glows cherry-red like looking into a forge.

I find myself in a lee.

On the south side, behind the tallest scaffold — the dormitory tower, the one the crabs keep building onto for no reason — there's a dead-water pocket. The engineers left a flat there once, I think, for a heat-exchanger that never arrived. The frame is still there, a cube of rusted I-beams and cross-braces, and the main current slides right over it. Inside, it just turns slowly. Warm water rising off the bacterial felt makes little slow cyclones that eat themselves.

I'm clipped in there. I have always been clipped in there.

One hand on a loop. That's the rule. *Hold on.*

The loop is what tells me who I am.

It's not a new loop. The plastic coating wore off decades ago. The steel underneath is polished bright, mirror-bright in this rust world. You only get that shine from thousands, tens of thousands of touches. Skin, gloves, skin again. The crabs never use the hand-loops. They tick right over them on magnetic feet. The zoarcids never touch iron. The isopod never leaves its girder.

The shine is from hands like mine.

---

What I am is not a diver anymore.

I can tell because I am breathing the room.

Not through a helmet — my helmet is hanging two floors up with the others, in the open locker in Dormitory C, flood-plate open, empty. I remember hanging it there. Or someone did. I remember the idea of needing to take it off because the seal was growing over. The brown-black felt does that; it likes edges, it likes where warm rubber meets skin. It was itching. It was easier to let the water in.

It should have killed me. Warm seawater at pressure is not air. But here, down low where the rift sighs hydrogen sulfide and iron, the film taught me how to do it first.

Look at the pipes after purge. Eight to twelve minutes, you can set a clock by it — *WHOOMPH* of superheated steam ripping sideways and the felt flash-boils off in a brown cloud and for a minute the brass is clean again. An hour later it's back. An inch a day if you let it.

I let it.

It covers my forearms now in a centimeter of hairy felt, waving slowly though there is no current in my lee. It covers the backs of my hands, my collarbone, the scar on my left shin where a loose sheet of hull-plate clattered into me back when I still flinched. When a crab's foot brushes me — it happens; they test everything for loose plate — the felt lights up faintly, that sickly green ripple, and I feel it not as touch but as a thought: *still here, still here, still warm.*

I don't eat the way I used to. I don't need to. The water is soup. Iron and sulfur and heat. I hang in my cube above the rift and I open my mouth and the Pocket breathes for me, the way it breathes for the pumps. Inhale cold from the throat, exhale hot from the rift, forever out of phase.

Up on the wall of the galley — I can see it from my loop, if I angle, through the salt-hazed window — are the tallies. Floor to ceiling. Not prison scratches. Grooves cut deep into steel plate with a welding cutter while the current tried to pull you off. Thousands of them. Groups of five. I made them after I stopped counting days. Days don't mean anything when the light never changes. I count purges now. I count whumps until they slip. I count how many times the isopod takes a step. Suitcase-big and slate-grey and crusted with the same manganese as the dome, and it will not be hurried. The crabs give it room because it's not a job.

I used to think I was waiting to be relieved. For a sound in the throat that wasn't water.

Now I know better. Nothing big comes in. The shadow that sometimes blocks the inflow — you feel it more than see it, a degree colder — always decides not to enter. That's wisdom. Inside is too loud, too busy, too much like work that forgot its purpose.

I am not waiting. I am occupying.

When the purge comes I tuck in and let it burn the outer inch off me. It stings the way a shave stings. Then I bloom again. When the crabs erect another useless derrick in open water for no reason, I watch the sparks become steam become snow and I shine a little green for them, though they don't look.

Find me there: clipped to the bright loop in the dead-water cube behind the dormitory tower, south cardinal, just out of the rift's red glow, just under the amber. Not swimming. Never swimming. Holding on.

I am what stayed after the suits were hung up like shed skins.

I am what the film learned to do with hands.
