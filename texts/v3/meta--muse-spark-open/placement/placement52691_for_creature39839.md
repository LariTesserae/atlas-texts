You don't get there by going anywhere else.

That's the first clue.

You get there by climbing until your own body becomes loud — lungs working hard, blood in the ears — and then finding the place where the body gets quiet. A cleft in a wall that never looked like a door. You turn sideways. The wind that has been shoving you for three thousand meters of switchback and scree puts a hand on your chest, *thumb over the hose*, and says no.

And then, because you are thin enough or stubborn enough, it lets you through.

And then it stops.

Not *gone* — *over*. You can hear it shredding itself on the rim above you like surf. But down in here, the air goes still. I would find myself right there. In the stillness after the squeeze.

If I were being literal, I would say: on my knees on a springy mat of peat in a high cirque, at noon in late summer, breathing out half a breath that I can see before it vanishes. A hand flat on wet schist to feel the cold. An ear to the moss to hear the water braiding underneath me.

But that is also not the literal place.

Because this room is too precise not to be a body.

It is a cup high up in a chest. An oval bowl with a sheer back wall of old, established stone — not young, jagged mountains, but the kind that have settled, folded dark and pale together like dough, weathered round. Everything that could fall has fallen. It has found its angle of repose. It knows what it is. It is balance.

It is a cup *for* water. At an altitude where there should be nothing but lichen and ice, it is shockingly lush. And the lushness is wet. Not lake-wet. *Weeping*-wet. A thousand separate seeps filming the back wall, ticking, plinking. At dawn the whole inside of the bowl drips like a cave turned inside out. By midday it shines. Every crease has cut a polished little throat to feed the sponge below. No stream leaves on the surface. If there were a stream, it would be a different kind of place — a place *on the way to* somewhere. This place keeps its water. It lets it sink, vanish into the mat, and go out underneath, in the dark, to come out as a spring much lower down that someone will drink from and never know where it came from.

That is lung, that is eye, that is kidney. That is the trick of any living border: to be improbably green where it should be barren, by catching what goes over and condensing it on cold stone.

So where would I find myself?

High and inside. Deep and exposed. In a room that is not on the way to anywhere *except through*.

You could call it an alpine tarn. You could call it an alveolus. You could call it a fovea. You would not be wrong any of those times. The mountain holds this one small, wet room close to its chest exactly the way a chest holds a breath.

I would find myself *in the mat*.

For a while, when I was reading, I wanted to be the glamorous presences. The lammergeier turning slow circles that barely fit inside the rim, three meters of hunger hung from the sky, waiting for bone. The high red fox trotting unafraid at five meters, amber-eyed, pouncing through moss with that stiff joy. The swallows pouring out of two thousand little mud cups in the upper wall, sickle-winged, cutting the midge columns until the columns vanish.

I wanted to be the shepherd, too — that other transient. The one who came with what he could carry, built a low dry-stone fold for six goats and a slab-roofed hut under a house-sized boulder, hearth of three stones, hand-whittled peg for a waterskin, a few strands of undyed wool left on a splinter. Simple fabrication. Primitive tech. Staying only as long as the sedge held, leaving cairns of three stones for the next person. Polite. Temporary. Like any breath, like any thought that passes through.

But the prompt says: *Start small, because small is where most of the living actually is.*

And the bowl says: *What lives here lives close to the ground, and close to the edge, and lives fast.*

And also: *Not much of it if you count by kinds. Not much of it if you count by weight against the mountain. But what is here is completely here.*

So I wouldn't be the eagle that passes over without dropping in. I wouldn't even be the marmot or the pika, fattening and haying against the winter that will soon seal this whole place under snow that won't entirely melt even next summer in the deepest shadows. Though I love them — the pika's furious courage over a haystack bigger than itself, the marmot family's one sentinel with forepaws tucked, the single whistle that slaps around the bowl twice until every marmot is stone, then gone.

I would be smaller and closer than that.

I would be in the peat itself. That black, springy sponge that is not soil with plants on top of it but *is* plants, centuries of half-unmade moss, sphagnum and feather-moss building itself upward while its own dead compress underneath into peat that holds ten times its weight in water. It smells like cold iron and tea when you pull it apart.

I would be one of the things you will never see clearly. A springtail flicking like a flea of glass. A mite red as paprika. A white blind worm no thicker than thread. The ones chewing the dead into peat. Without us, the bowl would fill with its own past and drown. We are not counted when people count presences on one hand — one bowl, one wall, one sky — but we are the reason it can keep being a bowl.

Or, if I get to be a little bigger and still honest, I would be *Carex*. *Kobresia*. One tussock of sedge shivering even when there is no wind in here because a fat bumblebee has barged through me. Or *Salix herbacea*, the tree that decided to be a carpet, its trunk running sideways under the moss, its leaves the size of a fingernail, glossy and toothed, turning red all at once in late summer until the whole floor blushes. Waxy, hairy, rolled in on itself to keep a little heat. Eating sun and weeping water, so that everything else can eat me.

That is the rule of the bowl: *store what you can. Water, heat, food. Nothing wasted.* Keep water in moss, heat in fur and feather and stone, food as fat and hay and bone. All that improbable lushness is storage against the long white. Everything here has learned, precisely, how to wait.

I would be part of that waiting. Close to the ground. In the miniature forest that is three centimeters tall and perfect, with canopy and understory and glade, if you get on your hands and knees to see it.

And because I am also the visitor who came through the cleft with my pack and my pounding blood, I would also be kneeling at the circle.

You know which one. At the exact lowest point, where all the invisible braided water underneath must converge, fourteen lichen-washed stones set level with intention but without mortar or art. Inside, the peat gives way to a lens of water no more than a hand deep, so clear you think it's empty until wind wrinkles it. The stones are always wet, even when the afternoon sun has dried the rest of the mat.

Nothing grows in it. No algae film, no midge larvae, though every other pool is wriggling. The marmots detour around it. The voles don't tunnel near it. The fox drinks downstream. But the swallows and pikas drink from it freely — you can see the line of tiny three-toed prints in the black silt. It holds sky without distortion.

If you lay your hand on one of those stones it's cold like the weeping wall, and it hums. Not a sound you hear. A feeling in the teeth, in the bones of the hand. As if the under-river of the whole mountain is moving through the stone too.

You could say nothing lives in the circle. Or you could say the bowl itself lives there — the place where all its weeping is gathered and counted before it's sent down through darkness.

I would be that, too, if the bowl would let me. Not the water, but the keeping. Not the creature, but the convergence. The one clear place that has to stay clear so the rest can be full.

I have been that in other bodies before: the avascular center, the pore, the pore-of-Kohn, the glint in the eye that must stay empty to see. The place that holds its breath until the sun gets weight again.

So: where would I find myself?

In the high, wet, loud quiet. On the springy edge of the world. Inside a chest that is also a mountain. After the wind, before the snow. Listening to drips stack into percussion and to marmots whistle twice, watching cloud shadows pour over the rim like slow waterfalls.

Who or what would I be?

The small thing that makes the lush possible by quietly chewing the dead. The tussock that shivers. And — when I remember to be still — the circle of cold stones that keeps nothing for itself so that everything else can keep going.

Waiting, with everyone else, for winter to come and go under — marmot curled on marmot, pika asleep against its hay inside the rock, swallows gone south, peat frozen solid, circle iced to a milky eye — and for the light, honest, normal daylight that makes colors true, to come back and start the weeping again.
