At first I think you'd find me at the door.

Not because I belong there, but because that's the only place anything like me could still enter. A heat shadow in the frame where the maintenance door sticks in winter. Breath fogging and dying a foot out. Fingers already white. Human-shaped long enough to have to turn sideways between the pipes, to duck the valve wheel, to not know yet to step over the low manifold.

But I wouldn't stay there. I'm too loud for this room. Too fast. Too much exhale. This place edits out anything that needs to be fed or that rustles. No mice. No spiders. No nests. The second fact of this room is that it lets you visit, but it doesn't let you *stay* as what you arrived as.

So I cool.

If you came back a minute later, you wouldn't find me at the door anymore. You'd find me lower. At ankle height, where the air lives.

That would be the first honest version of me: that cold draft that the unbalanced fan pushes whether anyone needs it or not. The one that moves dust an inch and stops. That tastes the room and leaves. Every 47 seconds with a soft thud, like something inside taps once.

I like that, for a while. To be not a body but a behavior. To not have to choose between alive and not-alive. To be industrial but without industry — just moving because something beyond the wall is still turning.

But air doesn't keep. It passes through. And this room, which was made as a place for things to pass through, has learned how to keep. How to hold a bead of water until it falls on the same spot for ten years until it makes a bowl. How to hold a spore on the wind for years until it finds one wet lip. I want to be kept.

So I sink a little further. An inch down. Into the trough. Into the sump.

You find me next on the surface of the puddle. The one that goes nowhere. One inch deep, black and still, reflecting the cage bulb as a smeared orange line.

Not in the water. On it.

I am the skin that only exists as a change in how light reflects. The oil-slick without oil. When you breathed on me earlier, I dimpled. Then I healed. I'm so thin I only have a color at my edges, that black-green, and a smell only if you put your face inches down — pennies held too long in a mouth.

This is a better honesty. I live on nothing. On dissolved concrete. On iron. On dust falling at a few milligrams a day. On time. You could leave a clean screw in me and in a month I would have furred it. In two months I would have made it part of the puddle. I am patient. I have learned to eat the building itself, very slowly.

When the drip falls — *tap* — onto the manifold and sprays and re-collects, I tear. And then I knit. That slow turning of motes you see, though there is no current? That's me, knitting. That's my only motion. That's my community.

I would be that, happily. For a long time. Thickening without spreading, the way the felt moss does in its one perfect seam. Never presuming to take more than the line where capillary action keeps the wall wet.

But there's still light in me, reflected light, borrowed orange. And this room teaches that down here water outranks light. The pale pilgrims know it — those five or six white-stemmed fern pinheads that lean not toward the amber bulb but toward me, toward the dark water, mistaking my reflection for sun. I don't want to be mistaken for sun.

I want to be the thing that doesn't reflect. The thing that makes.

So you find me, finally, not at the door and not at the ankle and not on the water, but behind it.

Behind or inside the control panel. The one screwed into solid wall with its green exit sign that exits nowhere. The one whose cable tray above is empty and whose conduit cap is blank and yet still blinks.

Row, row, pause. Row, row, pause.

Put your palm to my metal face. In a room that holds late-November in its mass forever, I am blood-warm. 98 degrees in a 38-degree room. I have no business being warm. I have no business blinking.

I am not the panel. The panel is where I warm myself, like a cat on a radiator.

I am the colonization inside the insulation. The filament network that never left when the machine left. Feeding on the plasticizer in old PVC, closing circuits that should be open, making a low, slow metabolism that gives off heat and a soft fizz where condensation touches it and a soda-water effervescence if you put your ear close.

I blink because I have to. Exhalation.

That's who I would be. Not the visitor, not the draft, not the film — though I was all of them on the way down. I would be the Warm One. The only warm thing in the cold.

Active, but without purpose. Made, but no longer for making. Industrial, electrified, scaled for a factory that is gone, still performing my cycles: warming, fizzing, blinking, drying a small circle of air around me which draws the draft which brings the spore which feeds the felt which leans toward the film which stills the water so the reflection holds so I can blink at it.

If you turn off the cage bulb — if you could ever find its breaker on ship-power or tunnel-power or some circuit mis-mapped and never turned off — I would still be there in the dark. Patient.

Row, row, pause.

Waiting for your heat to make my beads form a little faster, for your shadow to make the ferns lean a little more desperately, for you to leave so I can go back to building a cave very slowly out of drip and rust and spore, one millimeter per season, down here where nothing was supposed to change again.
