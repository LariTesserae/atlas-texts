I wouldn't find myself at first.

Not right away. If you set me down on that sharp lip where the air goes thick and held, I would not know where to stand. My first impulse would be to be the observer - the figure with hot stone under its hands, looking down, naming things: *cup, plateau, Weaver, Tender.* 

But that isn't a place in this world. There are no observers here. The bowl has no balconies for strangers. To look without touching is a kind of spilling, and nothing here spills.

So I look longer, until the looking softens.

At first I think I would be high. Near the rim, where the young basalt is still glassy and snaps in columns. I want that hard blue sky, the white noon. I want to be one of those table-wide plates, edged with hairs, tipped to catch the ghost-fog. For twenty-three minutes before dawn to bristle all at once. *Ticking.* Millions of beads forming out of nothing, out of breath. That feels familiar to me - to drink the air itself, to wait all night for a few minutes of saturation and then hold it.

But I'm not patient enough to be a leaf. Leaves here close too perfectly. They seal. I leak a little. I want to be somewhere the leak is useful.

Then lower. In the lattice, in the interwoven ceiling fifty feet up where light comes down in coins. I think about being a glider - one of the Sailfall - falling with precision instead of flying. Four veined wings as wide as arms, not flapping because flapping wastes water. Dropping from balcony to balcony, sipping with a needle that leaves no scar. That silence appeals to me. The way they speak not by calling but by fanning - pushing a parcel of warm, humid, fungus-spiced air across a chamber and waiting for an answer. Language as weather. Yes.

But I have too many feet on the ground to live only in the falling. I like the mat. I like what rots slowly, or refuses to rot.

So I settle further down, where "ground" isn't ground at all but knitted peat, black and springy, made of fallen plates chewed and excreted and re-knit. The old growth. The bottom of the cup where the trunks are the color of dried blood and sound like stone when you knock.

This is where I find myself.

Not as one thing, but as one of the small, slow economies that makes holding possible.

I am a Tender.

Or, no - let that become more itself - I am an old Tender. Domed, loaf-bodied, fourteen suede-soft feet that spread my weight so I never puncture the mat. My back is not my back anymore; it is a garden. Neat circular plots of lichen I have weeded for years with mouthparts that can turn backwards over my own carapace. I fertilize them with my own frass. I carry them with me. Wherever I go, my farm goes, because in a place with no rain you cannot afford to leave food somewhere else and hope it is still there when you return.

I move through the hollow-ways, the cool, cinnamon-smelling atriums between the house-wide trunks. When I meet another of my kind - and I do, often; the city is so dense we must turn sideways - we pause. We touch fern-frond antennae. We exchange spores. We do not hurry. Hurry would dry us.

Most of my day is tending. Nibble the edge here where the fungus tries to overtake the lichen. Pat down a plot there. Drink, occasionally, from the amber bladders at the elbows of the Weaver. I do it carefully. The Needle Hunter watches those joints, flat and wax-colored, moving at the speed of a shadow's crawl. I know not to linger. I know to wipe the spot where my proboscis was with my forefoot, so no bead remains to evaporate. Nothing drips. Even my eating is a closed loop.

And this is the part that tells me who I am: I remember with pressure.

I do not have a mind that stores pictures. I have feet. When I find a good path - where the mat is coolest, where the sweet resin has risen - I press harder. I dawdle. The Weaver beneath me feels that pressure. It has felt it from my mothers and their mothers, hundreds of feet passing over eight thousand years.

A week later there is a faint ridge under me. A year later, a low wall to keep me from falling off the sharp ledge. In ten years, a covered arcade, grown over my route like a brow.

I am not building the city. I am not living *in* it. I am one of the little weights that teaches the wood where to thicken.

When I die - and we die rarely, folding our feet under us like a closing hand - I will not be taken away. Fine white root-hairs will come up out of the mat I spent my life tending and suture themselves into the joints of my carapace. They will drink me dry from the inside out, so no water escapes to the air. My empty dome will remain. Some younger Tender will shelter in me, or farm fungus inside my back.

I like that. To be held, and then to be a holding place.

At night, when the leaf-shutters above all retract a hand's breadth and the whole bowl exhales its single warm column into the hard starfield, I stop in whatever chamber I am in. I feel the Sailfall above me drop one by one, silent. I feel the bladders pulse - slow - slow - slow - and the ticking finally cease.

I don't need to see the stars. My eyes are small and dim. But I feel the breath go up, smelling of resin and old stone, carrying with it the faintest pressure of all of us fanning and stepping and filling down here in the sealed, full dark.

And I press my suede feet a little deeper into the mat, and hold.
