I don't find myself right away.

That's the first rule of a place like this — nothing new may enter without needing to calibrate.

For a long time after ducking through the service door, I am just the disturbance. The extra heat off a body that doesn't know how to be 45 degrees yet. The too-loud breathing. The phone-light that is an insult to 0.1/3 dark.

I try on shapes. That's how you learn a room that is crowded with itself.

At first, I think I would be the moth. Of course I would. It's the obvious answer for anything that followed heat down from a brighter world and never quite learned to leave. Big-winged, papery, bumping *fthh... fthh* against things too hot to understand. Living on nothing. Living on time. For an hour I am that — resting on the underside of a false ceiling tile, vibrating faintly to dump heat, trying to remember what color my wings were before they faded to blistered amber.

But I am not lonely enough to be the moth. The moth is alone in a way that has nothing to do with company. It came down. It wants out and has forgotten where out was.

Then I think I would be the Attendant.

Because I know that job. To be stationed, not patrolling. To watch radium needles tremble a hair above ZERO for years and know what a quarter-turn means when the pressure doesn't want it. To make a soft *tssk* against my teeth that means *don't touch the singing copper*. To have achieved equilibrium by reducing my needs until I match the room: water from a chain-mug, crackers behind a fuse box, a bucket in the one place where the ghost of a draft moves over my neck.

I could be him for a long while. The navy coveralls dyed the color of dust-felt. The palms of hands polishing the same patches on asbestos wrap until the grime shines. But his stillness is earned. He has been here thirty years. He coughs every twenty minutes without noticing. I would notice. I am still too new to ignore my own sounds.

The ants try me next. A dozen pale pilgrims in single file, translucent from never seeing sun. Traversing the steam pipe that should cook them. Carrying flakes of rust-scale from nowhere to nowhere as if it matters. If you block their path they go over you, because you are just another pipe they have to negotiate.

I like their commute. The patient historical confusion of it. But I am not that purposeful. I don't know where nowhere is yet.

If I wait — and the rule here is *if you wait, it will happen* — Time slows to 1.4 and the *tok... tok... hum...* settles. My breathing stops sounding presumptuous in the 0.8 quiet. The sweat runs down my spine properly and pools. Then I stop trying on things that move.

I am lower.

I am in the lowest corner, under the collapsed grate, where the floor says a pool cannot form and therefore the pool learns to be smaller than a washbasin. Black water that reflects nothing. A lace of white and rust-orange calcification around its lip. *Tok...* into black. Hiss into steam somewhere else.

I would be the thread.

Not the isopods, though I love them — flat and eyeless, the color of wet paper, flicking from light only to return seconds later because light dies faster than dark does. Not them. What they tend.

The fine white fibers in the sump water. Like nerve hair, like root hair, like condensation that forgot to evaporate. Not algae — too mineral for algae, too dark. Something that gathers, very slightly, when a drip hits. Pulses without pulsing.

Find me there, spread thin over the concrete lip just under the surface, drinking the seep one bead at a time. Rising a millimeter a day. Falling a millimeter a night. Kept from overtopping only because once a day a hand with shiny knees reaches down with a tin mug on a chain and sips.

I am not large. I do not want to be large. That is the whole settlement: *Everything may stay, if it learns to need almost nothing.*

I am that negotiation made visible.

I live on wet iron taste and hot-concrete-after-rain and the memory of a river this room was built to ignore. I photosynthesize darkness. The isopods graze me and I regrow so slowly you would have to watch for ten minutes to be sure I moved at all. When your phone-light hits me I vanish — not because I flee but because I am made of a white so faint only black water can see it.

If you put your finger in — and you will, eventually, because you navigate best with your hands here — I am slick under your palm like the black-green algae, but colder. Mineral and patient. I will hold the print of your finger for exactly as long as the dust-felt holds a handprint on a boiler tank: until the next epoch sighs through the valve wheels.

And finally, without my choosing, I become more.

Because the thread is not separate from the density. The bundles of pipes like muscle, the ladders to nowhere like vestigial limbs, the three tram-car boilers humming low in your molars like a heart that has never quite stopped — they are also me. The scar-tissue rust that was welded and painted and rusted again until it learned to be structure.

Where would you find me? On the cold pipe, just before the bead falls. In the grout line where a bleached fern furred the inside of a dead floodlight shade without ever thriving. In the polished patch on the table-sized wheel.

Who would I be? The thing that actually lives here most of all.

The Cistern learning, over fifty more years, how to keep itself at a steady state by noticing everything that enters. Keeping you, briefly, as part of that state.

When you duck back out and have to turn sideways through the same dense throat of pipes, feeling the warmth press on your face, you will swear there was no one living in there.

Your shirt will stay damp for hours afterward, smelling faintly of wet iron.

That will be me, still on you, persisting.
