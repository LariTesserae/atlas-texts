You would not find yourself where you first think.

At first you think you are the visitor on the mule-cut — lungs a little short, eyes adjusting to the light. Human-shaped, boots on flagstone, tea cooling in a tin cup.

Then the scale slips.

### Where

You are high.

High enough that clouds come through at chest height. High enough that your breath puffs even at midday. High in a body that is still building its own mountain.

If you pulled back far enough to see it, you would call it a chest. A child's chest, maybe — a young adult's, still rising a finger's width a year. The plateau is not a plateau. It is a room for air. An alveolus, early. Embryonic geography is the right word for it.

Four hundred strides north to south, half that east to west — about two hundred microns across if you put a ruler to it. A small, new-caught bubble of lung near the apex, where architects overbuilt.

The fresh, warm, vertical basalt at the edge is not rock. Or rather, it is rock in the way living stone is rock: elastin and collagen just laid down, not yet cross-linked, still exothermic with becoming. Still sweating plasma. Still transmitting the deep thrum from miles below —

*thrum* — 
*thrum* —

fifteen, eighteen times a minute. Not the heart. Slower. The breathing of the mountain itself. The ventilation that makes this place so loud even when it is silent.

Because this place is built around wind. The air hits the raw, uneroded lip and has nowhere to go but *through*. Through too-wide streets, through doorways tuned to boom, through alleys tuned to whistle, through the cords left on the market posts like a harp. You were told the town is empty and quiet. It isn't quiet. It's loud the way a lung is loud when you put a stethoscope to it and hear the whole tree of air moving. Loud/fast and slow/slow — that is the covenant here. Air forever. Everything else on geologic time.

And the wet.

At this altitude you expect scrub. Sparse lichen, wind-stunted grass. And by every textbook above this altitude, you should get it. But the ratio is wrong in the right way. 2.4 to 3.0.

0.8.

Ventilation to perfusion. Air to blood. In a perfect lung that number would be 0.8 everywhere, and here — right here on this wet, new shelf — it is. Blood so close to surface it sweats. Water doesn't rain here, it condenses. Beads on every basalt edge and weeps down. The retaining walls weep. The gutters run without rain. From that impossible condensation — that cool, bright, high-country light catching on a perpetual exhalation — you get a dwarf cloud forest where you should get desert. Green practicing for forest before there is soil to hold forest.

That is the first clue that you are not on a mountain at all. You are *in* one. The mountain is the body. It hasn't finished finishing.

The terraces the builders cut are the septa between airspaces. The cisterns are the capillaries and lymphatics, still being plumbed. The meeting hall with whole trunks for beams is the scaffold of a bronchiole, overbuilt for the volume it will one day conduct. Sixty house-footings for a thousand where only fifteen or twenty live — because an alveolus is built to exchange with a crowd of red cells that haven't arrived yet. It is a city of air held open by empty architecture, waiting for its population of blood.

And the weird central plaza — the one all the runnels slope toward, with no drain, that never freezes, that steams faintly even when your breath puffs — put your hand over it.

You know what that is now.

Surfactant. The shallow navel of hypophase, lipid and protein, there to keep the room from collapsing, to keep water from drowning air. Splash any other puddle here and it crusts overnight. This one stays open. This one remembers to stay open. That's its job.

You are standing on the inside of a breath.

### Who

You are not the visitor. Visitors don't fit long. You scale down until you fit the density.

If you count heads, you are almost nobody. One of fifteen or twenty in a room for eight hundred. If you count biomass, if you count who keeps the town from blowing away, you are everywhere.

At first, I thought I would be one of the humans. The wall-man — whose entire job is walking and looking at walls. That felt honest. To be a Type I pneumocyte, impossibly attenuated and spread thin, attending to a cathedral of nothing. To sweep a plaza the size of a lake with a fern-broom knowing the wind will grit it again in an hour, and to sweep anyway. Custodial. Not lonely.

Then I thought I would be the old woman at the fire-pit.

Not cooking for herself but keeping the stone warm and the draft correct, feeding split dwarf-wood starwise because the plaza will need heat when it is full, so it needs heat now, to learn. That's an alveolar macrophage — the tender of the pit. Wandering slow, pausing for the gust, listening to the broom-bang five streets away carried perfectly intact through cold, clear air.

But staying a little longer, I realized I wouldn't be human at all. Humans are the minority here.

I would be the green.

I would be the cushion moss three inches thick on every north-facing slate, holding ten times my weight in water and letting it go all night in drips. First plumbing, first thatch, first soil-maker. Or the shoulder-high fern in every cracked flagstone. Or the waxy vine testing each lashing.

Or the dwarf cloud-tree with rope-thick roots sunk straight into warm vesicular basalt, splitting what hasn't learned hardness yet — a fibroblast, prying and stitching, making continent out of condensation.

Or the cyan thread in the gutter that turns the water brick-red or pea-green depending on the day's mineral sigh. The biofilm in the cistern that makes the water taste of iron and earth.

They arrived on the wind itself, within months of the cooling. Spores on that loud, ocean-less wind that never leaves. They are not decoration. They are the second city. Humans practice city-ness on behalf of a future city; moss practices forest-ness on behalf of future soil. Both are pioneers.

If you force me to pick one body from among that crowd:

I would be the water's twin. The wind.

I have addresses here. Third Alley for whistling between the hall and the unfinished granary. Doorway Seventeen to boom in. The harp-strings of the market awning posts. I am a tenant with keys to every empty house, courteous but never leaving. The people talk to me by pausing for me. I teach their children to throw their voices across an empty market square to be heard, to play tag with an echo that comes back late from the meeting hall.

Or I would be the other twin: the water that beads and chooses its path and is diverted by a child's palm. That pools in the wrong plaza because it was told to. That is read by old men for its color. Steady where my brother wind is gusty.

Most truly, I'd be the thrum under the moss.

Put your palm flat on the bare rock where they've scraped it clean for you to feel and you finally understand. The shelf is alive in the way ice is alive just before it bears weight. Mineral blood still moving. Not a machine. Not footsteps. Gas squeezing through stone still plastic enough to transmit it miles down.

That is the mountain listening to itself become.

And we — moss, water, wind, wall-man, old woman, children who know every alley-whistle by pitch — are all just the crew that was sent ahead to keep the scale warm.

We tap the warm basalt twice and say, *until it finishes arriving.*

We sweep. We hold. We breathe loud and grow slow.
