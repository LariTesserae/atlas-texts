I wouldn't find myself at first. That's the point of a place like this.

For a long time I would look for myself in the wrong scale.

Not standing — there is no standing here. My first mistake would be to imagine myself upright, a human figure in relief against a wall. But this room punishes verticality. The ceiling would correct me. After a few minutes of stooping, my neck aching, my hands finding the floor without permission, I would understand: this is not a place for people. It's a place for things that know how to be low.

So not there. Not in the center, under the amber lamps where the light is most even and the eye feels safest. That belongs to the press-bed above and its slow snow of scale. Not by the gap where you entered, either — the threshold is too loud with outside air, too hopeful that the world on the other side still matters. This room has stopped listening to outside.

I find myself instead in the most ordinary inch of it. The place your eyes slid over because it looked empty.

In the southeast corner — if a room like this can be said to have a southeast — where the concrete lifts a little into a shallow dome, no higher than the side of your hand. The lime pan there is thicker, almost white, cracked like the dry bed of a sea that was only ever a puddle of condensation in 1978 and then never again. It crackles when warmth passes over it. It tastes of iron.

That's where I am.

If you put your palm down right now, you'd be touching me, or very close to me, and you wouldn't know for a second. Because I would be one of the grazers.

Not the biggest one — not the palm-sized slate coin with eleven plates that has survived all the boots and all the drops from the ceiling. Those are old and they know too much. They move as if they remember when the machine above had workers. I am not old.

I am thumbnail-sized. Seven plates. The middle plates still a little darker than the ones at my edge, the way new scale is darker before the dust dulls it. My back is not beautiful. It is precise. Pitted in exactly the same way the concrete is pitted, so that when I am still, I am not an animal at all. I am texture. I am floor.

This is what I do, and it is all I do, and it is enough to be a life:

*scrit. scrit. scrit.*

I move slower than your breathing. My soft, bristled mouth — you would have to turn me over to see it, and I hope you don't — laps at the pan. I do not eat with hunger. I eat the way a tongue worries a loose tooth. Iron for the faint pink of whatever passes for my blood. Salt for the spark that lets my blunt antennae twitch. Old oil varnish for the long, slow burn that keeps me 55 degrees in a 55 degree world.

I leave the twin trails behind me. Pale, faint, already being crossed by someone else's trails. I am polishing the world. Fifty years from now, if you came back and lifted the slab, this corner would be a fraction of a millimeter smoother because I was here, and because the thousand others like me were here, lapping without end. That is my work. We are why this place remembers being smooth.

You might think, as a human reading this, that being a grazer is lonely, or small. It isn't. It is dense.

To be me is to never be alone. Not ever. On my back right now there are legs. A wingless cricket, too large to be graceful, is using me as a road. Its hooked feet find the little grooves between my plates and click as it goes. I feel its weight as pressure, not pain — my plates flex, the way they were made to flex under boots. I don't shake it off. To shake it off would be a waste of movement.

Further up my back, for a second, the shadow of one of the ceiling ones passes over — not its body, just the cool air it pushes down when it breathes. All our antennae still at once. Then resume.

And to be me is also to be part of the other direction. Below me, the pan. If I press a little harder I can feel the concrete itself, that ancient, beach-stone concrete with its exposed aggregate like fossils. I am eating the room that is eating me. When I die — not hunted, just tipped by a boot or worn thin — I will split along my plates and my clear oil will bead. I won't smell. I won't bleed. In three minutes my neighbors will be here, with that same unhurried purpose, and they will lap me up and the pan will be whole again. My plates will stay for a while, a little dish of slate among the lime, until they too are lapped into dust and become pan again.

So I am food that is also ground. I am ground that is also food.

That's the bargain of this room, and I keep it without being taught.

If you stay still long enough, as you are, breathing shallow so you don't inhale the haze — I will come to your boot. I will not know it is a boot. To me it is a new, warm cliff that has appeared in the southeast corner, smelling richly of sweat and outside. I will taste the salt on its laces with my blunt antennae. I will consider it. Then I will climb it, cool and dry, over the toe, cool and dry, over the knuckle of leather, and continue on my endless polishing circuit as if you were always part of the terrain. Because now you are.

I am not waiting, like the felted ones on the ceiling with their heat pits aimed at your throat. I am not nervous, like the crickets with their typewriter ticking.

I am here, in the most ordinary inch of the most forgotten room, doing the only thing that needs doing, under an amber light that will be on for sixteen hours and then off for eight, for longer than anyone will remember to turn it off.

I am the reason the floor is smooth.

Put your hand down. You will find me.
