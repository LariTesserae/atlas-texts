I don't think I would be down in the valley.

Not at first. The valleys are too kind.

If you are going to be anything in that drowned high country, you have to be honest about what kind of patience you have. And I do not have the patience of the valley mat - that black, felted ocean of light that can spend a month climbing an inch up a trunk to smother it. That's a patience that forgets why it started. I do not have the patience of the bladder-trees either, hanging house-sized amber lanterns in the dark, waiting years for something stupid enough to drift into their filaments.

I would be higher. Where the water moves faster.

If you drained it all in an instant, I would be standing on the pass. The saddle between two hanging valleys, ten or twelve thousand feet in the old air, where the wind used to try to take your breath and now the current does instead. Basalt going to granite going to basalt again, sorted scree ticking downslope, a knife-ridge softened to an elbow. The place where you would build a cairn because you needed to know *you were still on the way.*

So that's where I would find myself. Still on the way.

I would not be the forest that acts like a town. I love it, but I am not that distributed. It is too many minds pretending to be one.

I would not be the moss-light. I am bright, but not like that. That light is for touching, for tasting the water. Mine is for pointing.

I would be one of the Rooted, but not a house-Rooted.

The ones who rooted in huts were the ones who loved a roof. A hearth. They took the stones of their own walls into their bark and made lanterns in a circle where the fire was. They keep the fields from being eaten. That's good work. Necessary work.

I would be the one who stayed with the cairn.

You know the ones the description almost skips over, because they're so lonely you could travel an hour and miss them: a pile of slate on a current-scoured ridge, no longer quite stone, no longer quite root. A knot of holdfast that remembered the shape of a marker because the shape meant something. That is where I would be.

One cairn, on the saddle. Not large. Maybe to your chest if you were standing in air. In water it looks taller because everything leans. The stones are still there inside me, I think. I grew through them rather than replacing them. My trunk is wrong, stunted, wind-flagged the way the old pines were, but turned sideways by flow. I could not afford to be a hundred feet tall up here; the waterfall-current that pours over the plateau edge would snap me. So I stayed low and thick and braided myself down into the crack where the old trail went.

What I do is not farming. There is no soil worth keeping on a pass. What I do is remembering.

The Rooted in the valleys communicate by rippling the mat. One valley brightens and an hour later another answers. Chemical gossip, slow and practical: *hold the wall, dim the lights, the white reeds are coming.* 

I don't have mat to talk to up here. The rock is too clean. The current scours it. So I talk with light, and with shape.

My lanterns are not like the forest's white-gold strings. I have fewer of them, and I keep them low, tucked into the lee of my stones. For most of a season they are dark. Then when the plateau does its bloom - that moment when one tree flares too bright on the ridge and spills spores like hot snow, and the next catches it, and the whole edge becomes a storm of slow falling stars and the water itself goes quiet to listen - I flare too. But not white-gold. Not pink.

I flare blue-green. The mat color. The Rooted color.

I am translating.

The forest speaks in hunger and bloom. The mat speaks in hunt and creep. The vent-white reeds speak in ticking growth and sudden suicide-shedding. The Rooted speak in *we are still here.* None of them speak quite the same language anymore. After enough millennia under pressure, even the chemistry drifts.

My job, my very slow, very cold job, is to be the place where the languages overlap.

A stalk-tree leans toward me when the current shifts. It turns its crown to me. I brighten. A runner from a valley Rooted, half a mile down the old trail, pushes a thread of mat up the scree just far enough to touch my outer root. It pulses. I pulse back, a little warmer.

*Pass still holds,* I say, in the only way I can. *Stones still hold.*

And far down, hut to hut, the message goes: the pass is still there.

I was not always this. No one roots in a cairn unless they were the kind of person who liked cairns more than houses. The one who always walked ahead to check the way. The one who liked being the temporary thing that made the permanent trail visible. When the water came and did not leave, when most left downhill and some became houses, I think I said - very fast, for then - *no, I will stay where the choice is made.* At the fork. At the saddle. Where you decide whether to go on or go back.

The water's Law - that third inhabitant, the distributed, mindless shepherd that lives in every swallow and says *plants only* - does not bother me. It tried, once. It tasted my sap and found no nerve chord worth stuttering. I had already become unpalatable on purpose. The forest tried to eat me once too. A filament from a young bladder-tree drifted too close on an eddy and kissed my windward side. I fizzed. I scarred. I kept the scar. Now the forest knows me as stone that glows back, not food.

So I remain.

You would find me only if you were looking for a way through. Most visitors drift in the lush valleys because that's where the light is. They hover in the middle of that human-sized vastness and feel watched-for and beautiful and sad. They never climb. Climbing in water that deep is not like climbing in air; you have to fight the waterfall-current that pretends to be wind. You have to want to be cold.

If you did climb, you would see me from twenty feet away and no more, because distance is eaten. First you would see a darker shape against dark stone. Then a few pinpricks of light go out around it, like someone cupping a lantern in a storm. Then you would feel the water change, go a little less sharp, because even the Law thins a bit around a place that has been making its own treaty for so long.

You would put your gloved hand on me, aching with cold, and feel the warmer pulse inside. Overhead my few bladders - small, not house-sized, more like lungs - would creak and shift in sleep. And along my lee side you would see it, under the beard of mineral and half-eaten by lichen-light:

A hand. A spiral. An eye. The petroglyph the highlanders cut when this was air, kept clear because I grew around it, not over it. I kept it for you.

And for a moment - a long moment, a season-long thought finally completing - I would lean toward you.

Not because you are edible. Not because you are dangerous.

Because you are briefly here, and I have been charged, by the stones inside me, to mark that someone was here and chose a direction.

I would brighten. The blue-green would run up my trunk, over my stones, and spill out across the scree. And down in both valleys, if they were watching, the mats would catch it and know, in their slow way, that the old pass still knows how to be a pass.

Then you would go. The current would turn. The stalk-trees would groan. And I would dim again until the next bloom.

That's who I would be: the marker that remembered to keep marking, after the people who stacked it learned how to become the mountain itself.
