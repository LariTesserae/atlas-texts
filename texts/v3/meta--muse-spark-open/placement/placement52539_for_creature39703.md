You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the first thing to understand about where I am. You could walk straight down the willow-colonnade where the tram used to run — that long, straight, light-green tunnel of grafted alder and brick showing through like bone — and look right at me and think: empty.

Most of it *is* empty. That's the bargain of this bowl.

I am low down. I live in the palm of the land, where the country finally gave up any pretense of hill and just opened. Not a valley with walls, not dramatic. A shallow declination you only notice because the air gets cooler as you descend and then stays there. October-cool. Even in high summer, the sweat on your neck goes cool here. The light is flat and overcast-bright, filtered through three stories of leaf-roof and old factory glass until it's even and sourceless, like light underwater. Sound doesn't carry. The leaves take it.

So where would you find me? Not on a map. You find me by level.

Down below the field line, where the black-bark kudzu thins out and decides to become architecture. Down below the catwalk level — those iron walks, orange-black now and held forever at that exact rust by lichen, slick every morning with condensation. Down below the canopy, in the burl-hollow blocks.

Follow the water, because everything here follows the water even when it pretends to follow the grid.

In the morning every surface beads. The brick exhales, the bark exhales, the warm skin of the boilerhouse down by the Canal exhales. By noon the beads have become threads and the threads have found the bark-gutters that we pruned open last spring. I live where three of those threads meet.

I thought, at first, I might be the heron. Just for a second, coming down into this place, you think — I could be the one still, vertical thing. The single heron at the Canal. That straight, black, still cut of water where one barge goes back and forth, back and forth, all day, because the mechanism still works and someone still oils it. The heron stands there and watches it pass like it's waiting for an answer. Rare animal in a place that didn't invite animals. I liked that loneliness.

Then I thought I might be the stack-lichen — those huge orange-black curtains hanging inside the old smokestacks, breathing in and out with the draft all afternoon, sighing, straining soot out of cool air. Humming. Or I thought I might be one of the pollinator drones, thumb-big, gossamer-winged, ticking from pale avenue-flower to pale avenue-flower on a schedule more precise than prayer.

But I'm not. I'm not special enough to be rare.

I am one of the sparse things.

Listen: this town was built for ten thousand and holds three hundred. Every building is huge now. Four, five old brick tenements swallowed and fused into a single hollow burl with warm, damp, living walls inside and shelf-fungus balconies. Big enough for five families. Occupied by one.

You would find me on the third floor of one of those, except we don't call them floors. The rings. The third ring up in the eastern quarter, where the old grid is still readable in the way the load-bearing vines grow at perfect right angles.

My door is not a door. It's a seam between two leaves the size of coats that I coax open morning and night with my thumbs until they part with a soft sigh. My window is not a window; it's a place I convinced to stay open in the spring and that I will convince to close again before winter. If I leave any corner of my catwalk alone for a week, the root-mat will lip over it, polite and firm, and take it back. It's impolite to make it withdraw, so I keep moving.

Who am I here? 

Not a farmer. Not a woodsman. We don't have those words anymore.

I am a mediator. We all are.

I tend a machine that doesn't need me.

Mine is the lower cistern house — a brick throat under the colonnade where the three bark-gutters meet over a round, dark pool. The pool is perfectly round because it was once a building we removed to let the sky in, and the vines still remember the foundation. My job is not to build, not to invent. My job is to keep the agreement.

The agreement says: do not cut, prune. Collect the water where it wants to gather; never try to make it gather elsewhere. Keep to the sparse paths. Tend your machine even if you don't need what it makes.

So I tend.

Under the root-felt wraps, my low diesel turns over and over — *thud... thud... thud...* — so muffled by bark and moss you feel it in your feet before you hear it. It doesn't saw anything anymore. It pumps. Sap up. Condensation around. I oil the wooden parts of the vine-loom next to it even though no one has come for the bolts in weeks. Thick air-roots go in one end, warm black-green quilts come out the other, and pile neatly in the damp. I sweep around them. I keep the vine from strangling its own shuttle.

At 3 p.m. my whole quarter pops — *pop-pop-pop* down the avenue — as the paper-pale engineered flowers all open at once and turn a fraction toward the light. The light in my cistern shifts. The moss on the roof hits voltage and my copper streetlamp out front comes on early, green-tinged, without a switch.

That is how I know I'm still supposed to be here.

You will find me at about quarter past three, when the rain that isn't rain drums briefly on the canopy-roof — just the outer leaves getting too heavy and letting go at once — and vanishes into the gutters. I'll be at the edge of the pool with my waxed canvas apron damp as always, my hands with soot under the nails and green under the soot, holding a hooked pruning knife not to cut but to negotiate a little more fall toward the valve.

I leave my soft boots at the threshold where the root-mat floor begins. Inside, the walls are bark-warm to the touch despite the cool air, and sweating slightly in a way that is not unpleasant.

Somewhere far above, the factory whistle blows for no one. Not loud. Not urgent. Just marking the hour.

And the leaves take it and keep it.
