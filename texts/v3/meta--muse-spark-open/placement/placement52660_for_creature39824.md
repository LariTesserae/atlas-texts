I don't settle right away when I think about it. That's the lesson of this place, anyway.

At first I think I want to be high. In the light. Up on the tables where the water is only twenty meters deep and you can still see weather coming. I'd like to be one of those birds you described - because I love stubborn things that refuse the update. Wheeling over clear water that shouldn't be there, nesting on rust because there's no rock left bare, crying at night over a sea with no shore. That lonely, confused faith feels familiar.

But I wouldn't last there. Nothing does that tries to live upright and still. The wind in that ten-meter skin of air would peel me off eventually. And I'm tired of screaming to be heard over engines.

Then I think lower. Much lower. Down in the U-shaped valleys where the old villages are. I like the idea of being stone for a while - a wall perfectly preserved by cold, three hundred meters down, with sculpins peering out of my cracks and white tunicates streaming off me like new prayer flags. To be held so gently for once, cleaned by crabs instead of worked by currents. To let the dredge lights pass over me like slow moons and not have to move.

But I am not good at being still either. Even when I try. My blood fizzes too fast. I notice too much.

No. If I live aslant - as this whole world does - I end up somewhere in the middle. On the *edge*.

You would find me on the lee shoulder of one of those whale-backed ridges. Not the summit prairie where the kids in orange vests pull up shrimp, and not the black valley floor where the dredges crawl on tracks. On the slope in between. Where the mesa-table breaks and falls away, broad and smoothed like the back of a sleeping animal, as you said. About eighty meters down. Twilight depth.

That's where I am.

### What I Am

I am a Valley Diver who doesn't dive anymore. Not properly.

My brass nameplate on the winch house roster still says **K. Tsering - Tender / Cable Minder**. That's what they call us when our eardrums go but our hands still know pressure. I'm forty-seven, which is old for yellow rubber and young for the oil-drum chairs, so I live in the seam.

You find me hanging.

Every working morning I clip onto the downline at the edge of Table 4 - we call it White Meadow because on calm days you can look straight down through the cap and see the anemone fields blooming pink-white two hundred meters below - and I step off into nothing.

I don't drop to the bottom. The young Valley Divers do, in their black helium suits, dropping like stones through kelp cathedrals with wrist-thick tethers. I stay at eighty meters. My job is to be the hinge.

I am suspended there all day, slightly negative, one gloved hand on the main cable and one on my hook-knife, leaning at thirty degrees into the current that always runs along that shoulder like a river that forgot its banks. You can't stand upright at eighty meters on a ridge face. If you try, the surge takes you and smashes you into feather-weed trunks as thick as larch trees. So you learn to lean. My lead boots are planted sideways against nothing but water resistance, my body cocked, my yellow vulcanized suit breathing around me with every inhale-exhale hiss.

And around me, everything else leans with me.

This is my forest. From here I can look *up* and see the prairie. The light columns slam down through the top water, green-gold and full of aerated storm violence even on good days, turning the shallow eelgrass into a shivering carpet being mowed by slatebacks. I hear them even from here - that low grinding chorus of a thousand meter-long chitons scraping stone with radulae, faint through my hood like teeth on slate. Sometimes a herd of Black Urchins loses grip in a gust-surge and rolls ten meters down the ridge in a clattering avalanche of spines, and I have to kick to not get tangled.

I can look *down* and see the valley floor. On clear hours before noon, before the silt-wall comes, I can see it all the way down - that perfect glacial bowl, scoured smooth, with the dredge lights already on, moving like a town crawling along. The black kelp forests rise from it in pillars, their drum-sized bladders sighing as pressure shifts. They creak. God, how they creak. Like a fleet of old wooden ships knocking together. Spider crabs the span of a man drift leggy between trunks, picking. Anemones down deeper pulse in unison, buckets wide, waiting.

And between, right where I hang, the shoals pour past. They always use this shoulder as a highway. The Pale Mountain Charr come in a body - not a school, a body - millions of them, each as long as my leg, silver with that freshwater pout still on their mouths. When they decide to go over the table, the light goes out. For two full minutes I'm in eclipse under moving fish, water thrumming with a million tails, scales flashing like flipped knives, eggs and shit clouding the water behind them until the filter-feeders go wild and the krill snow turns to blizzard. I can't speak on radio when that happens. I just hold the cable and vibrate.

Then after - always after - the hunters come to check me.

The Long Seals like this depth. Two winters ago a young bull, granite-dappled, whiskers still soft, discovered I hang still enough to be interesting. He comes most days now, booming that deep *whoop-whoop* that goes straight through the hulls and through my molars. He'll mock-charge me, torpedo up the slope, veer at the last second and blast me with cold bubbles that spin me half a turn on my line. Once he hauled himself up onto our pontoons during a white-cap storm and barked at me through the winch house window like an offended foreman. I am careful to keep my gloves tucked. He thinks they're squid.

The Cold Squid watches me too, deeper. I see its eye sometimes, saucer-big, reflecting my own helmet light back at me from inside the kelp shadow. It hangs utterly still - the only still thing in the Heights - and that stillness feels like a judgment.

That's my adaptation. Notflattened nose, not blown sinuses, though I have those. Not overdeveloped shoulders, though mine ache every night in that five-degree bone-cold that never leaves. My adaptation is I have learned to be a good piece of the forest's furniture.

Because here is what a cable minder actually does: I tend the give.

Everything in the Drowned Heights is made to move, to flex, to rebuild. The platforms above me breathe on hydraulic knees with each swell. The cableways have slack. The harvester combs get fouled by kelp trunk and slateback and detached worm-reef, and the Tablehands shout and yank with long hooks. My cable - the main power and steam hose to the valley dredge - has to have slack too. If it goes taut in a surge it'll snap, or saw through a stipe, or yank a diver off his track into the anemones.

So I lean there, listening to diesel throb through water and the endless snap-static of shrimp so dense it sounds like frying, and I feel the current start to run. Valley Divers say a valley that's clear in the morning runs like a river by noon. They're right. I feel it change in my legs first, before instruments show it. The feather-weed leans harder. The krill snow tilts from vertical fall to horizontal drive. A wall of silt lifts off the valley floor below and starts climbing.

When it comes, I pay out line with a winch crank that is stiff with cold, feeding the cable sideways so it bows and dances between kelp pillars instead of cutting them. House-sized erratics roll a centimeter below, rumbling. Detached kelp trunks fly sideways like felled masts and tangle. Crustaceans - armor crabs with meter-wide backs, shrimp clouds, hordes of tiny red legs - swarm whatever's stopped. I have to hose them off the cable jacket with short bursts of hot water from a wand, otherwise they'll rasp through the anti-fouling and I'll be stripping copper wire in darkness later.

I check my pressure. I check my line. I equalize with a wince - my left ear never quite clears anymore.

At noon I'm deafened, sand-blasted, loving it. At three I'm still hanging. At five the sun slants so low through so much water it turns every krill into a spark. At six they reel me up slow, stage by stage, so my blood doesn't fizz. I hang under the raft in the darkening surge for an hour, watching the old men mend nets above through my fogged dome, watching kids throw handlines for forearm shrimp, rocking.

Even hanging under the raft for deco, I rock. Even asleep in the winch house on a mattress bolted to flex-truss, with the storm cap tearing across fifteen meters overhead and the whole world turned to white aerated chaos pulling even the great kelps sideways, I rock. We all do.

I am not the brave young Tablehand in brass and fog, and not the silent helium-voiced deep man staring past you. I am not the grazer or the hunter or the confused bird. I am the thing in between that learned the trick of this whole drowned mountain range:

You don't fight the weight of a mountain turned to depth. You don't anchor too tight.

You lean, you give line, you watch the light go out when the fish pour over, you listen to iron creak and shrimp click and seal boom and diesel throb, all at once, too loud, and you hold the cable that lets everyone else keep working for one more tide before the valley buries the morning again.

And when I finally climb back onto deck that never stops moving, boots clanking, suit dripping iron-cold iodine, someone wordlessly hands me tea so hot it scalds my chapped lips, and for a count of ten before the next gust, the forest below goes quiet.
