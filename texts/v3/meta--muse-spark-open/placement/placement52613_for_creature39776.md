I would not be in the high places, because there are none.

If you put me in this world I would find myself low - always low - not because I had fallen there but because this country has spent a billion years learning how to be low and I would have learned it too.

You would find me twenty meters downwind of a weir that is not mine, but has been mine for the season. You would not see me at first because that is correct.

### Where

In the place where the sheet decides to become a pool.

Not a lake. The plain does not do lakes. But a long, faint dish in the gneiss, maybe a hand's depth deeper than the pavement around it, where the striations of greenstone widen just enough to slow the film. Where a family two generations before me laid the low C of unmortared slate, curve upstream, mouth downstream, and scraped the bedrock clean of tufa so the biofilm grows back fast and dark and sweet.

The weir is working. The water comes over it in a continuous glass lip, a centimeter thick, catching the pearlescent sky and doubling it. Inside the catchment the water clears because the silt has fallen out. I can see the grazing tracks on the bottom like pale calligraphy.

Behind me, three slabs of greenstone lean together. Not a house that fights weather, a house that lets it pass. The wind goes over. The mizzle goes over. The hides stretched across the top are greased until they are the color of my own skin - translucent, spotted with rain, lit from everywhere and nowhere. Under them the star-crack hearth is warm. It has been warm in this exact star-shaped shatter for longer than I have been alive, and the stone is polished by sitting, by mending, by waiting.

In front of me, a hundred meters off, one of the breathing erratics sits half-sunk in its own amber ring. I do not camp near it. No one does. But I am close enough to smell it when the wind shifts - that warm iron exhale, faintly metallic on the tongue. Close enough to hear, under the hiss and the whine and the endless *shhhhhhk* of the grazers, that slow *click... click......... click*. I keep the weir upstream of it. The children know not to drink downstream.

That is where I am: in the skin of the shield, in the wettest year of a wet plain, ankle deep and then knee deep without noticing when it happened, with the great low cloud lid pressing the sound sideways forever so that every plok from every pool comes to me.

I am not central. No one is. The country is too finished to have a center. I am scattered, as the built things are scattered - a small warm mark in an immense dish that tilts so gently water cannot decide which way to go. That is the point.

### Who

I would be what this place makes when it makes a person and lets her live past childhood.

Short. Broad through the hips and shoulders, because heat is not the problem but staying warm while always wet is. Pale to translucence - you can see the blue of veins at my temples, my forearms are the color of the tallow we rub into the hides. Hair shaved to stubble because it never dries and wet hair kills. Eyes light grey with that second lid, the nictitating membrane that I can draw across when the drizzle blows horizontal for hours. I blink sideways.

My hands are the important part. They are always wrinkled, fingertips softened, stained black at the nails from biofilm and from polishing basalt. I know by touch what is worth scraping and what is not - the black leather-crust that will feed us, the blood-shot jelly that will retract if I prod it wrong, the pink bacterial sheen that means the water here will turn your gut.

I am not a chief. We do not have those. I am a tender. A weir-keeper. An old enough woman that the band lets me decide when a pool is exhausted.

In the morning, in the dim that is always morning, I go out with a flat slate scraper and a woven net made of fungal cord and stilt-walker chitin. I scrape. The sound joins the other scraping, the grazer scraping, so mine does not matter. Behind me the grazers themselves come - dinner-plate blacks, shield-blacks - moving like slow tongues over the stone, eating what I have exposed, leaving their rich shit that tomorrow's rain will dissolve. We graze together. We do not own them. We farm the film they farm.

At midday, when the moth-cloud hangs lowest, I lift the net through the top millimeter of water and take a few handfuls of the palm-sized filterers. They are warm and buzzing and almost all water. We roast them. The children pick the shelled grazers and we cook them on the star, suction-foot up, gills popping, until the meat comes free like curd.

In the evening I mend.

That is mostly what I am - a mender. I re-stack the slate where sheetflood has tumbled it. I re-braid fungal cord. I re-grease the hides. I re-carve. Under my sleeping mat I keep a pouch of chert flakes I have knapped into little grazer-shells, each one patiently pressure-flaked to that seamless dark oval. When I leave a cairn for the next band, or for the plain itself, I tuck one under a jasper slab. Not as prayer to gods we do not have, but as manners. To show we were here and tried to keep the shape correct.

I can read the plain the way you would read print. The amber phosphor that traces the watersheds at night is a map I can walk with eyes closed. The way the stilt-walkers suddenly avoid a run tells me a cloak has come. The way the pipers go silent around the breathing rocks tells me to shift my weir six meters left this year.

And for five minutes after true dimming, I stop all work.

Because everyone on this plain, even the solitary tenders a day's walk from anyone else, stops.

The pipers synchronize. For five minutes the deafening chaos - *PLOK PLOK PLOK PLOK* from every pool in every direction - locks into one pulse. *PLOK... PLOK... PLOK...* so loud and so low you feel it through the water in your boots, through the gneiss in your molars. The moth-cloud freezes. The dragonfly-things hover. Even the grazers still.

I stand in my pool, water to my knees, hands empty, and I count with them without knowing what we are counting toward. My throat, which cannot make that sound, tightens anyway.

Then they dissolve back into beautiful noise, and I breathe again, and I click my chert against the slate twice - not for any use - just to answer the breathing rock behind me with a quicker count, a human count, while I can.

That is who I would be here. Not the storm. Not the mountain that was. Not the strange slow mind in the erratic that is still settling.

The film on the stone that learned to tend the other films. The animal that learned to keep a low wall in shape and know which water to leave alone.
