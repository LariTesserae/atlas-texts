I didn't find myself at first.

That's the trouble with a place like this — scale breaks the habit of locating yourself. At first I thought I knew where I'd be:

At the edge. Of course. One hand on a root-pillar wide as a house, testing the wind before a long glide. Or high up, riding the warm stair-draft without moving a muscle, a Kite, translucent and empty-headed and content to sift. Something with a clear role, a beautiful function.

Then I tried to be that and it slipped.

Because if I were a Kite I'd never land, and I want to land. If I were a Glider I'd have that perfect face and that single perfect note — *I am still here* — and then silence again. But I am not good at silence. I narrate. Kites don't narrate. Gliders only speak when the distance absolutely requires it.

Closer to the ground, then. I thought: alright, the leaf-meadow. Two motherpillars holding up a wet sky, a mile of fern cut all to one height. I could be the person who cut it. Bare feet gone to bark, eyes gone amber-green, hair that never dries. I know how to tend. I like tending.

But I tried that on and it was too competent. The Tenders are competent in a way that has dissolved competency into breathing. They don't decide to prune. They see a shoot leaning the wrong way and their hands move. They don't wonder if the mountain is alive or built or natural — grown and ungrown is enough for them. I kept wanting to name things. To stand back and say: "Look what we made." They never stand back. There is no back far enough to stand.

So that wasn't it either. Not quite.

Let it sit longer. Let the dim do what it does — light from everywhere and nowhere, so you stop looking for a source.

Where I find myself is not on a plateau at all. It's *in* the vertical.

Not dramatically. I'm not hanging from a thousand-foot curtain root in a storm. I'm in the part you'd miss if you walked fast. Terrace wall, tier  — call it middle-high, where the cloud cap makes the air still taste like late evening at noon. The wall is not a wall. It's that exposed cross-section where an old slump left the lattice open: living wood and dead wood laminated, hollow in places, choked with black humus in others. Basketwork at the scale of geography, beading water constantly. Ticking.

I am in the hollow under the skin. Under the mile-wide path your feet think is solid. In the nation-sized dark that the path roofs over, where roots hang like stalactites and don't touch bottom and the wind comes out changed.

More precisely: I am in the maintenance gap.

There is a beam there — it was a tree once, then an arch, then a beam, then a fossil of a beam still alive on its outer bark — that has developed a soft spot. White mouth-fungus, the lung-rot kind the children are taught to pick off. No one has picked it this season because the knot that tends this section moved two terraces down when a path healed shut last spring. So the fungus has fruited.

And I am not the fungus. And I am not the beam.

I am the tending that forgot to happen, and then remembered itself in a different form.

I am what grows in the missed spot, in the interval between attention. The small keeper that volunteers when the designated keepers leave.

Put a body on it, if you need a body: I'm old enough that my soles have gone black and my ankles click, young enough that my hair hasn't yet started to sprout seedlings. I'm the one they leave behind at the quiet points. Not as punishment — as aptitude. Because I can sit still long enough to feel the re-spacing.

You know the ones: where spores make a perfect right angle around nothing. Where you drop a weighted vine and it falls slower the further it falls, as if gravity is losing interest. Where a lower terrace hangs inverted in the haze above you.

The others walk around these places. I tie the offerings there. Little knots of braided root and hair. Not prayer. Markers: *here, step carefully, space is thin.*

That's my village. A single propped leaf, a smoldering bowl of humus that never quite goes out, not for heat but to keep the mimic vines honest. My tools are a bone hook, a stone flake, patience. My job is to keep a door open that isn't a door — a gap in the weave where the mountain exhales. To pick the mouth-fungus before it can fruit, but not too clean, so the Borers still have a place to chew. To smoke them gently when they get ambitious.

People pass through every few months. They think I'm alone. I'm not. The Grazers pass over me horizontally, licking algae off the pillar I live against, leaving tide-marks. A Glider once landed wrong and stayed for three days in my roof bromeliad, panting, looking at me with that flat, solemn infant face until it remembered how to fall correctly. At night the kink-birds — the ones that browse on geometry itself — make their not-shadows over my fire and for a second I see with their eyes: the whole mountain as a small seed, folded in on itself, thinking it can stay small by folding forever.

I don't prune the fold. You can't.

I prune around it.

That's where I find myself — not as inhabitant, not as feature, but as interval. As the attention that lives in the gap between attentions, keeping the void from closing, keeping it *void*. The mountain is a lung, vast surface, mostly air. Someone has to tend the air. To keep it empty enough to be useful.

If you came looking for me you would walk right through my plateau and think it empty. Fern meadow, two motherpillars, mist ticking down. You would only realize later, on your way back — when the short path back takes slightly longer — that someone had kept it empty for you. That the emptiness had been *grown*.

And if you stopped, and breathed that wet-cloth air until your lungs stopped fighting it, and listened until the drip disappeared and then came back, you might hear me not calling. Because I don't call. I note.

A single clear note only when distance absolutely requires it:

*I am still here.*
