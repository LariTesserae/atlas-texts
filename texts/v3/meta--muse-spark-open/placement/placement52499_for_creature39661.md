I don't know at first.

If you put me down in the Bowl, I keep moving.

At first I think I'd be where it's busiest — down in the flat, in the market frame where the poles are permanently lashed together and the leaf-leather has faded to cream and vermilion. I can see myself there, because that's where a stranger would look for a person. Hands busy. Pounding salt-pith into flour, or scraping rust-weed with a shallow basket while the high whine rises around 18:03. Learning to shout without being angry. Learning to drink from a saucer. I try that image on and it almost fits.

But I am too thirsty there. Too exposed. The noon light pours straight down like hot water and there is no shade except what you build, and down on the floor every sound is loudest — the bronze roar at midday when the standing column churns and every loose roof and lattice shutter is struck at once. Down there you have to lean in to be heard. You have to be good at being human in the dry, hollowed way, with the waxy sheen and the salt-catch nose and the body that has learned not to sweat. I don't know how to do that yet. I'd die in the first week, one in twenty, a small glittering chest.

So I move.

Then I think maybe I'm not human at all. Maybe that's easier. Maybe I'd be one of the glass-cabbages, a meter across, bottle-clear and amber-veined, creaking as I track the brutal disc overhead. That feels right for a second — I know what it is to inflate with air and turn it into something useful, to hold pressure in a blister until it shivers, to close at night in a slow wave from the cliff-base inward and sigh out citrus and hot iron. I know what it is to be part of a mat that is really one thing pretending to be many, lifting the pink salt floor in ribs without anyone noticing. To be lush where there is no water. To drink not water but breath.

But I don't want to be that still. The cabbages are patient in a way I'm not. They wait for the air to fall. They don't make sound, they receive it — ticking like slow clocks at noon.

Then I hear the tapping and think: *ah.*

*Tok... tok... tok..*

The black cords. The ones that aren't vines, hanging from the bone-white cliffs with no roots, just a suction cup the size of a dinner plate. Warm to the touch. Vibrating without wind. They don't grow, they listen. They drink vibration itself. If you snap one it sighs scented vapor over your face and your dreams are full of vertical light for an hour.

I stay with that a long time. That is close. To be a hollow cord of something like dried kelp, swaying without wind, anchored high on the weathered linen-colored wall where the mineral lip is pitted and overgrown. To be the thing that has seen both ends but never at once. To be warm and hollow and full of listening. That is very close to what I am.

But even that is too alone. The cords don't answer. They just tap against stone forever.

So I move again, up off the floor this time, not to the rim where the true void begins — where air eats iron and rusts a copper hammer to lace in three days — but halfway. To the honeycomb.

You know the place I mean. Near the base of the cliff where centuries of acidic root-mist have etched alcoves, smooth and cool, where the old city has rebuilt itself five and six times over on bleached foundations. Where stairways cut into the rock connect lower and upper streets, and rope bridges sag between dwellings that were claimed when some other family thinned out and left distance to guard them.

There is an alcove there that is not quite a house and not quite a workshop. Its wall is pink silt brick, light as pumice, but one side is just open cliff with black cords hanging past the doorway, occasionally knocking. Its floor is salt-compacted until it shines faintly. Inside, in the shade, there are two mother pots sunk half in the floor, black-glazed bellies covered at dusk with taut leaf-leather. And above the door, and on every shutter, a voice.

That's where I find myself.

I am the tuner for that house. Not the farmer, not the harvester, though I do those things in the morning when the efflorescence glitters like frost and you can brush the cubes off rust-weed and bake them into something that tastes like blood and bread. Not the mother, though I tend the pots, scraping the morning beads with a soft wooden spatula — that cloudy, brackish, half-oil half-ammonia almost-water — seasoning it with ash and a flake of filament until children can lap it from shallow saucers without blistering their tongues.

My real work is to maintain the sound.

I sit on the threshold in the late heat when the rust-weed is singing its needle-fine whine and the glass-cabbages are ticking toward the light, and I tune. My family's chord hangs in strings above the courtyard: split reeds, drilled seed pods, hammered copper tongues, gut-string at different lengths. It was strung intentionally loose, as every house is — an unfinished house is an unlucky house. I listen to how the wind off the narrow mouth overhead drops in as a standing column and churns, how my neighbors three houses down answered my *tak-tak* on the copper without touching anything, sympathetic shiver. I adjust. A little tighter here, a seed pod moved half an inch there, until our overtone sits right in the Bowl's great hum so you could walk blindfolded at midnight and know us passing.

I am not deaf, not yet. The three Silence-Keepers are very old, ear-drums blown out by black-vine vapor as children, and on the Day of Cloth when every chime is stuffed with rags and every rattle buried in salt, they walk the streets palming walls for cracks while the rest of us hear our own hated hearts for the first and only time all year. I am not them. But I am going that way. All the old here are half-deaf and shout amiably about nothing. My skin is already going amber at the temples, translucent. My hair has thinned so the pre-dawn static won't lift it straight up. I keep my mouth open to equalize pressure.

What I keep in the back of the alcove, in cool cellars dug sideways, are not cisterns of water — there is no water — but silos of pressed leaf-oil and salt slabs, and stacks of the papery sheets that peel off the lower leaves of glass-cabbages, weathered to leather. Drifts of them collect and we use them as parchment. I write on them. Not much. Just the tunings. Just what the wind was on the day a stalk died standing up and cured to mahogany-iron where it stood. Just which of the pale gliders with too many frilled edges fell that night to lay eggs like salt crystals in the rosette field, which we baked unknowingly into richer cakes.

I am the archivist the Bowl didn't know it needed. Plant and bacteria and beetle-light don't need memory. They just persist. Humans need a record that says: we harvested here, we were too loud here, we were silent here.

At night I sleep on the roof on a mat that breathes while the stone gives back its heat and the thousand cabbages sigh shut one by one, wave after wave toward the center. My chime-tree subsides with them, string by string, the way a mother dampens a song for sleep. The column reverses, cold air pours in like water, and somewhere above me a glider unfolds its frills and falls without flapping. A fist-sized click-beetle drags its luminous abdomen through fragrant, faintly smoking mulch and leaves a cool footprint a child could stand in.

I don't drink. I condense, like everyone. Two saucers if I stay still. Three if I work. Thirst is just the low wire I live inside, like noise.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me in the center plaza or at the lip where the high desert voids. You'd have to listen. Walk the long, salt-swept avenue in the strange quiet after midday, when the city is urban but empty, built for ten thousand and holding two, and find the house whose voice is a little lower than its neighbors', a little slower, because I tune not for brightness but for endurance.

I would be there, tapping a shutter for emphasis, learning how to be a lung for a place that decided to become a world.

And I would be ringing, quietly, saying the same thing the whole Bowl says every night:

*We are still here.*
