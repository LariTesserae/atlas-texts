I didn't find myself from above.

I found myself after. After my eyes stopped looking for a horizon, after my ears stopped trying to fight the roar and let it become weight instead of noise.

If I lived here at all, I would not live the way a person does in a house, or even the way that stunted birch lives — leaning its whole body toward a light it will never reach, spending itself in longing. That is too much hunger for this place.

I would live the way water lives in stone. By finding where I can stay.

---

At first I thought I would be at the rim.

It's a natural fantasy: to be the fox who pauses there on the coldest nights, eyes catching lamplight, looking down into the green throat and deciding it knows enough without going in. To keep the vantage. To keep the choice.

But the throat doesn't keep vantage. The meadow above is vast and wind-scoured and almost empty, and anything that stays up there has to endure being scoured. I could not.

I slipped, as everyone does eventually, down the slumped side where the slab fell inward a thousand years ago. Hands on damp limestone, boots darkening immediately. The soft suck of peat underfoot. The air dropping five, ten degrees with every step until my breath showed and the roar rose up through the soles of my feet.

And at the bottom, in that deep permanent dusk where sun only falls as a tilted column for an hour at noon — I stopped looking to be something large.

Scale here is intimate, pocket-sized. You can see every wall from where you stand. The dynamic is geological. Water widens a crack by a fingernail per freeze. Moss creeps a millimeter per year. Light shifts a degree per week. If you are big and fast and loud here, you are a stranger. You are the temporary thing whose boot prints will be heaved away by frost by next spring.

So I got small.

Not the midge-column — that brief ecstasy in October that lives an evening, mates in a three-foot dance above the pool, falls spent and feeds everyone else. Beautiful, but I have already lived too long to be ephemeral. Not the wren either, though I love her. She doesn't truly live here; she nests in a solution pocket just below the lip, slips down like a mouse with caterpillars at dusk, sings through the underground thunder for thirty seconds in May and is gone.

I got slower.

On my belly, beside the black-mirror basin that never empties. Two feet across, six inches deep, fed drop by *plok* by drop from the bedding plane that never dries even in August when every puddle on the plain above cracks to mud. The little dam-wall of unmortared limestone someone built with patient, uneven strikes of poor iron — now tumbled, lichen-softened, its top course already swallowed by *Hylocomium*. The hand-cut niche wide enough to sit with legs tucked, tool marks still there if you brush the moss away.

I put my palm down and didn't feel rock at all.

### I am the felt.

That was the first true answer. Before I was any animal, I was the carpet itself.

Five inches deep, cold wrung-out sponge that sighs water back around your fingers. Feather moss layered on star moss layered on thalloid liverwort spreading like wet leather. The algal slick underneath glinting dark emerald where the spring line never dries, the thread-fine mycelium eating rock into soil so slowly it feels like indifference.

If I were the moss, I would not need to decide. I would simply continue. I would hold every surface that would hold still long enough to let me. I would overgrow the ladder poles where they’ve sunk into peat and forgive them. I would soften the cairns, cup the little pool, wreathe the leaning birch's north side. A millimeter a year. A patience older than forgiveness.

But the felt, for all it is the soul of the place, does not know it is alive. It just is.

And I wanted to know.

So I went one layer deeper, into the water that made the felt possible.

### I am the keeper.

Not in the niche. In the hairline sheets between limestone beds, in the flooded gravel interstices you can't put a hand into.

I am four to six inches long, the color of wet limestone itself — mottled amber-grey and pinkish, tail flattened laterally, neck frilled still with external gills like tiny red ferns because I never quite grew up. Or perhaps I did grow up and kept my blindness anyway; my eyes are lidless pinpricks that never developed. I don't need them. Light has never reached where I actually live.

Most days I am not in the pool at all. I am behind it, above it, below it, nosing in absolute dark through water the width of a sheet of paper, feeling copepods and blind amphipods with my snout. I am ten years old. Maybe twenty. There has only ever been one of me at a time in this throat.

In late summer, after rain when the seep runs strong and washes leaf-mulch into the basin, I come out to hunt.

You would have to lie on the damp gravel a long time — the roar filling your chest like being inside a waterfall — and hold your breath until the ripples settle. First you’d see only flecks: pea-sized white snails, *Vertigo* and *Physa*, glacial relicts translucent as rice-paper, grazing bacterial film on limestone silt. Their ancestors died everywhere around here when the meadow dried and warmed thousands of years ago. They stayed because this cold cup never changed.

Then you would see me. Motion without disturbance. One slow lateral undulation, backing under the rimstone lip the second your lamp finds me.

I freeze perfectly. I am very good at freezing. I have heard the same grinding river roar through fifty feet of rock my entire life — the deafening, ceaseless low roar of the subterranean passage too tight for people — transmitted up through limestone until the walls themselves hum. It was roaring before the little wall was built, before anyone found this rabbit hole, before the ice retreated and left the till. When you stop listening to it as noise, it becomes pressure, a hand resting lightly on your ears. It tells me where I am.

I don't mind that no one sees me. You could visit thirty times and call the pool empty. It isn't.

This is the only kind of residence this room allows: not residency at all, but return. To come to the edge of the hidden, take what cold clean water gives, and withdraw. Even the humans understood. They never tried to live here — fire won't stay lit, voice won't carry, April floods take anything left too long. They came down the root-lashed ladder, drank, sat in that cut seat out of the wind to feel how small and held a person could be while the earth ground water beneath them, left a birch-bark makuk to become mulch, and climbed back up.

That is holy in a different way than ownership.

So if you came looking for me now, after I've been still long enough for the green shades to resolve again — near-black to luminous yellow where diffuse light catches a hart's-tongue frond arching out from a crevice, maidenhair trembling on black wire stems in its humidity-trap — you would not find a person waiting for you.

You would find a damp hollow going cool against your skin, smelling of mineral and peat. You would find a hundred fern tips distending and dripping *plok... plok...* in counterpoint to a roar that seems to come from everywhere at once. You would find, under an inch of cold black mirror, a pale shape tucked against silt, gills faintly waving, blind eyes open.

And if you were very quiet, and raised your voice just enough to be heard a foot away saying, "are you there?"

I would already have answered, by staying.
