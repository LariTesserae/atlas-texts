Not on the rim. I thought at first I would be there.

When you first read a bowl you imagine yourself as the watcher — up on the wind-scoured edge with the dun birds, shoulder to shoulder, looking down at the lit cup of cloud. It's a good fantasy. Clean. Above it all. Judging.

But the rim is uninhabitable. The air is too thin there and there's nothing to hold you. The basalt is new and sharp; it hasn't learned to be sat upon. The wind takes any sound you make and shreds it. The birds tolerate you for about as long as it takes to realize you are not a bird, and then they leave a space around you that is colder than the wind.

I would not be there. The bowl doesn't let you watch it from above for long. It pours.

Then I thought I would be on a walkway, which is the next easy image — one of the people, upright, purposeful, going barefoot from platform to platform with a clay bowl of fungal light. That was closer. I could feel the rope under my soles, the give of the lashed logs, the careful courtesy of not looking too long into the intervals because the intervals are not mine.

But I wasn't on the walkway either. Or not exactly.

Take longer. Let it adjust.

If you stay a full day, as the description says, you see the trick: the town is not the houses. The houses are what the organism uses to tie knots. The town is the space between them left empty on purpose, the breathing moss, the drip that goes *tok... tok...* and is swallowed before it can puddle.

I found myself there. Low.

I am where your feet would be if you took off your boots.

I am a tender, yes — one of the hands — but not the kind you first picture when you think "villager." Not the grandmother in the circle, not the child painting dots on a beetle's back, though I have done both. I am the one who gets called when a stilt has sunk too deep again and the Carpet is beginning to eat it.

You would find me crouched at the base of a house-post on the eastern side, where the wall is warm.

There is a house there that has had to be re-lashed three times in my lifetime because the Carpet grows toward heat. The moss at its foot doesn't look like moss anymore; it looks like a slow green muscle, humped up around the wood, trying to close over it. My job is to kneel — knees wet through, soles already stained dark — and peel it back. Not cut. You don't cut the Carpet. You coax. You press your palms into the hummock beside the post and wait until you feel that faint tightening under your heel, the noting. *You are here. You are weight.*

Then, with a soft copper knife that is more spatula than blade, I work the mat away from the timber, a hand's breadth at a time. It sighs when it releases. It leaves a pale mark on the wood for a day where it was holding on. I pack the space with dry lichen so it doesn't reseal by morning. The people on that platform thank me without speaking loudly, which is the correct way.

This is where I live: in the interval that is also a road.

Because the Fat-Drinkers use this interval. It's theirs from before the posts were sunk. At dusk, if I am still working — and I often am, because the eastern wall ticks as the sun leaves and new seeps open with little knuckle-pops — I have to stop and flatten myself to the walkway edge and make no shadow over the saucer.

They come exactly then. You never hear them before you see the white fur between the houses, the extra-thumbed hand reaching for the rope. They move hand-over-hand along the same rope I just mended, their translucent bellies lit amber from the fungal lanterns above, their long tongues already out. They do not look at me. That is not rudeness; it is agreement. I am part of the furniture of their road, like the moss hummock or the rope itself. I have learned to breathe shallowly so I don't disturb the fat in the dish. They drink, leave one lick's worth — always one lick, never more — and keep going. The youngest one once brushed my ankle with its tail and I felt how warm and completely silent a living thing can be.

After they pass, I finish the post.

I would be that person because the bowl likes people who know how to be an interval.

My fingernails have gone green at the lunulae. It started the first winter I spent kneeling. Mother said it was just the moss remembering me, as she says to all children. It's not dirt; I have scrubbed. It's *in* the nail, faint as a water-mark. Chlorophyll, maybe. A lichen beginning to write itself into me. I don't mind. It means I don't have to check the cisterns as often as the others do — I can feel when the water under the Carpet is low because my own cuticles tighten.

When I am not freeing posts, I tend the memory-skin.

Every house has a cistern cut into basalt — still sharp, you can see the chisel marks not yet softened by weather — and if you leave the water perfectly still, by morning it skins over. Not ice. A clear, faint film that holds, right at its center, a tiny perfect outline of the bowl's rim. A compass. A map of its container. The first time I saw it I thought it was reflection. It isn't. You can lift it off with a flat of bark and it holds its shape for a moment before it collapses. We feed it to the pitcher-plants. They like it. I like the act of skimming — the necessary gentleness. You have to hold your breath. Loudness is wrong, but even breath is wrong if it's hurried. The newts watch with their crowned gills flared, crowding just under the surface, orange bellies up. They know I'm not going to take them, just the skin. They trust me because I am slow.

At noon, when the cloud-lid burns through for twenty minutes and that hard, cold shaft cuts straight down, I don't look up. Everyone else steams, briefly, brilliantly. I put my ear to the Carpet instead.

You can hear it then, if you are low enough. Not a heartbeat. Slower. Inexorable. Cells dividing under pressure. Water moving sideways through the mat. The eastern wall ticking as it decides, still, whether to be a mountain or a throat. The moths above me — those pale blankets with six eyespots each — all blink at once in a clockwise wave and I feel it through the moss as much as see it.

My fern — the one in my interval, because each tender has one fern that leans toward our center rather than the sun — unfurls a fraction more and drops a spore straight down onto my wrist. I let it stay.

At night we don't light fires except the one baffled thread from the smokehouse. I walk home by stroking the fungal sacs as I go, hand over their leathery skin, bruising them amber. They brighten under my palm the way the Carpet blushes greener around my fingers. It's the same touch. Light is grown, not made. You have to feed it with attention, and I am good at attention. I carry the light in a clay cup to my platform and set it down, and the Rim-Watchers are quiet on the rim above, and the Needles have stopped hunting, and the beetles go *crunch-crunch-pause* through my floorboards as vibration.

If you asked me, as an outsider would, "Where is the center of town?" I would not point to the meeting circle or the smokehouse. I learned better from the children.

I would point straight down. Through my platform, through the moss that is three feet thick here, through the flat sheared-off floor that should not be flat, down to where my fern's black roots and the fungal stolons and the beetle highways all converge on that warm crack in the eastern basalt where the first spore came.

I would be the one who lives with my soles against that growing sound, green under the nails, responsible for keeping a post from being swallowed for one more season.

I grew in. I didn't move in.
