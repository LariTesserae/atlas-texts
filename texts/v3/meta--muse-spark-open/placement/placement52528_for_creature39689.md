I thought at first I would find myself in the center.

Because that's where the town pretends to be open - the chapel-sized well where you can stand and see all the doors looking back at you. Where the market shuffles. Where at noon the column comes down thick enough to choke on.

But I don't belong there.

I tried putting myself there in my mind and I kept fidgeting. Too exposed. Too much sky. That hard white coin straight above is too much like being watched.

So I let myself drift.

Past the forge, no - too hot, though I can feel how you learn to love the west wall the way you love a stove in winter, the way you would lay your hand near it but never on it at 3 p.m. Past the machine shop where the lathe never stops - I hear it too loud, I would be too anxious about the belts.

Lower? The lowest tier where the cisterns are capped with boards? Darker, damper. The lime skin forming on the basins like a cataract. That's closer.

No. Higher.

On the north wall. That's where I settle. That's where what I am becomes more itself.

I would be on the second tier, not the bottom, not the top - the middle band where the collapse sheared cleanest and left that long, weeping stain. The seep. Where the rock cracked into the fossil aquifer and never stopped bleeding. The wall there is always dark. Not wet enough to be a stream, just enough to be glossy before dawn, to grow that black-green felt of lichen and that one vivid patch of moss that looks painted on. Artificial green. The only green that isn't being coaxed along a pipe.

That's where I live, because someone has to.

I am not the pump-men. I am not the cistern women who keep the boards straight and dose the lime so precisely. I am the vine-keeper. Or one of them.

I would be one of the people who tends the engineered vine - thick-stemmed, waxy, heat-tolerant, the one we grow not for fruit, not for beauty, but because it tells the truth. It grows toward a leak. It seals a joint slowly with its grip. It cools the pipe it covers by a degree. You prune it and it hisses faintly as the water pressure changes inside it.

My place is a narrow balcony that isn't really a balcony, it's a catwalk that got turned into a dwelling when someone bolted corrugated tin to its rail and called it a wall. My oil-drum ocotillo is there. Stunted, folded shut. I have named it, of course. Everyone names their drum-plant the way you would name a kettle you use every day for twenty years. Mine would be a small, rusted thing with fewer leaves than it should have and one good red flower a year that we all quietly celebrate and don't pick.

My hands would be like theirs - broad, short-fingered. Palms callused on the heel from dragging the collection tarp. Outside of the forearm gone smooth and shiny from brushing against hot tin a thousand times. I keep my hair shorn to the scalp in summer, which is always, because steam will take loose hair and tangle it in rebar. I have the burn, low on the left wrist, pale and papery. I don't remember getting it. I wear the long sleeves anyway, salt-stained canvas, cuffs buttoned at the wrist even at 41C. Collar up.

You would find me at 04:45 with everyone else, but you wouldn't notice me at first because I am not in the well with my face tipped up.

I'm on my tier, in my clear salvage-plastic poncho - re-taped at the seams - standing absolutely still with my arms slightly out from my sides, letting the condensation that has poured down the sheer basalt all night gather on my shoulders and run. The whole pit sweats before dawn when the cold desert air above hits the trapped hot air below on rock. Black basalt turns glossy. Every pipe, every wire, every laundry line drips: plink, plink into barrels. That is our rain, and it comes from nothing. I am not trying to stay dry. Newcomers try to stay dry. I am a funnel. My poncho channels the drip from the catwalk above me, from my own laundry line, from the vine leaves I trained to overhang my door, down my arms into the pail at my feet. I tilt myself without thinking. The children next door do it with tin cups, scraping droplets off the underside of the catwalk itself.

By seven I've taken the poncho off and stretched it as an awning over my section of gutter, watching the timbre change. *Plink* vs *plunk* - that's how you know which barrel is full without looking. My hearing has gone sharp the way everyone's does. No birds, no insects, no rats to muddy the sound. Just human sounds against machine sounds against water sounds against rock settling. I can tell which joint is sweating by the way the vine leaves go darker within an hour.

That's most of my mornings: redirecting runners, tucking a tendril toward a seep-darkened bolt where a micro-leak hisses so faintly only the plant can hear it at first. Wiping mineral bleed off the basalt. Talking low, hand lightly on a shoulder if I have to say something over the compressors. We don't shout across the well even though we could hand a cup across it. We go and say it.

At eleven I go inside. The rule is religious: never the forge and the pumps at the same time or the lights dim. At noon the sun stands straight overhead and the bottom becomes a kiln. The column falls. Light doesn't fall correctly then - it thickens, motes hang too long. Sometimes in the heat shimmer off the west wall I see the second catwalk, the one that hasn't been built yet, lingering after I blink. I do not remark on it. Someone else needs the shade-cloth I'm standing on. I nap under it while the rock radiates, while flatbread cooks on salvaged iron on the roof above me without any fire. I listen to my boiler ticking over like a goat breathing.

My real work is in the evening, when the convection starts. That silent, massive exhale up the walls that carries the smell of hot sage and dust from the plain above, diluted to nothing, almost imaginary. That's when we all come out again.

Once or twice a week, when a joint won't show itself, I do the thing we do with the wrongness. I call to the pump-man below to cut his engine. He calls up. We cut mine. For sixty seconds the whole tier goes quiet. No thump of compressors, no buzz of sodium lamps quite so loud. And in that hush the bowl reminds you it is deeper than its measured depth. Sound falls too quickly. And up from the seep-crack comes the low tone. The molar-hum. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it.

Children laugh nervously when they feel it. I close my eyes. I have learned to use it the way you use a stethoscope. You move a hand's breadth along the pipe and the tone modulates where the hairline leak is, where air is being pulled. It is not haunting. It is diagnostic. Like listening for settling at 3 a.m. when a flake of fresh basalt pops off the still-sharp wall and clatters onto a roof and you get up to mark where it fell.

That is who I would be. Not loud. Not central. Not the one who carries the dead up the bolted stairs to the rim at night to give them to the desert because there is no soil deep enough to bury them inside, though I have helped carry.

I would be the one who tends the green that only lives within arm's reach of the drip, who knows which vine is thirsty by the way it curls, who keeps the damp alive in a place that is always five degrees hotter than it should be and always trying to evaporate. Who has learned to live like condensation itself: to gather at night on cold rock, to run down any surface offered, to wait in a covered barrel through the furnace of noon, and to be useful again in the evening.

If you lived there a year you would hear me before you saw me - not my voice, but the soft scrape of my shears, the faint hiss of a valve I just turned, the plunk of water finding its pail on a morning that has no clouds.
