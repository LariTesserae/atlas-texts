You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the trick of the Works. From the outside — if there were an outside — I would be invisible. Another stain of wet wool on wet concrete, another low figure in oilskin with her head down, watching her feet on the shine-worn stair.

You'd have to be standing still for a long time on Upper Tier 43 to see me at all. The eye wants to go wide here. It wants the panorama: the opposite flank falling away in thousand-foot stairsteps, the gorge in the middle where the water finally remembers how to be a river, the green that has stitched itself over everything until the rust and the brick only show through like scabs. You'd look for a skyline. There isn't one. Just more tiers, more gantry-cranes turned into hop arches, more spillways cut the width of a doorway, weeping.

And then a door you thought was a water-stain opens, and I come out with a barrow.

I'm not a Keeper. Not really. I'm not a Sluicer. The young strong ones who speak in verb-nouns — *lift-gate, tap-pipe* — laugh at my knees.

I am what happens when you've lived in one stretch of wall long enough that you know its drips.

You would find me seven tiers down from where you're standing now, on Tier 36. That's not low enough for the gorge gloom to give you the nystagmus, not high enough for the moor wind to skin your lips. It's the bean tier. Or it is this week. Next week it will be the potato tier. The wave moves down-mountain following the water, and my stretch moves with it.

My house is not a house. That's a lowland word. My house is the north cheek of Cistern 36-C and the south cheek of the bean trough above us. The front is a catwalk of open steel grating that sings when the Carriers run their swaying carts over it at shift change. The back is a wall that never dries. We sleep against it in winter because the pipe that runs hot along its base is the only thing that will ever get the damp out of our inner wool. It never does. My hair is cut with kitchen shears to the scalp, short as my husband's. If you let it grow here it stays cold on your neck and rots. Your hands want pockets. Your hair wants off.

I am 47. I have Tier-knee both sides, that outward bow you get from forty years of taking the same camber sideways when the moss is thick. I'm short, like everyone who was raised climbing — broad, pale the color of the underside of a leaf, eyes good in the pearlescent grey that never casts a shadow. I don't squint when I come out of the rewind shop. There's no difference between inside light and outside light. The sky is a high, wet paper that gets very slightly thinner toward noon. That's noon.

In the morning I do what my mother called Keeping, though she didn't call it that either. I walk my beat. It's not big. Fifty meters of trough lip, sixty centimeters of engineered black earth held by concrete, with that foot-thick curtain of ivy hanging off it, waxy, leathery, layered like thatch. Behind it the concrete weeps constantly. That's the deal we made. We let it weep, it lets us farm.

I go with a hook. I pull the water hyacinth before it mats. I thin the brassicas so they don't bolt in the cold. The leaves are huge in this light, huge and dark, trying to catch a sun that never comes. I trim the ivy where the roots are starting to pry at the lip — you have to, or the lip spalls and we lose the soil into the canal below, and then someone from the Shops has to come and reline it with that black plastic sheeting they extrude two tiers over, and they'll give you a look that says *you let it get thick, didn't you*. My hands are black-green to the wrist. The juice doesn't wash off. Not in this water.

But I also listen. That's the Sluicer part. Everyone listens.

My cistern makes a particular *plip* — pipe to water — when it's in balance. I've known that *plip* since I was a child carrying yoke-buckets like a human bee, like a human pack animal, because Fauna 0.9 means there are no other animals to do it. No birds. One crow on business, now and then. Just us. When the *plip* changes to *plip-plip... plip* I know the balance pond three terraces up is rising a centimeter too fast because someone on the moor left a catch-gate open. I don't run. The Keepers don't run. I tap the pipe with my bar, once, hard. The sound goes through the pipe, not the wall. Down in the turbine hall on 35, the boy on watch will hear it in his teeth — that low 50-cycle thrum you feel more than hear — and he'll know to check his wheel before it seizes with mineral crust.

That's Water Law. *You may not take faster than your tier gives, you may not block what the tier below expects.* It's not written anywhere. There's nowhere to write it that isn't already a wall or a trough.

At midday — the greyness thinning — the factory on Tier 37 vents. It doesn't look like industry. It looks like weather. A plume of steam smears sideways along the wall and waters the hanging gardens. A second later the shutters open and the hinge-makers spill out. Their shutters are our shutters. Their wall is our floor. We change shift on the stairs without a gap, without silence. There is never a gap. If one turbine cuts out, another three tiers down starts before your molars stop tingling. The Works is the quietest heavy industry you'll ever hear. Just drips. *Plip* to cistern, *tink* from leaf to tin awning, seep-hiss down a moss face.

I take my barrow to the stepped street. The Carriers — ten-year-olds and old men — are already there with carts swaying impossibly high with greens. Today it's Tier 36 beans. Tomorrow it will be Tier 35. The crows don't carry seed, so the old man next to me carries a rabbit-fur brush and goes flower to flower on the dwarf espaliered trees nailed to the south-facing wall. South only means slightly less dim here. He does it flower to flower because there's nothing else to do it.

I don't look across the gorge. Sightseers look across the gorge. We watch our feet. The stone is slick. The moss never dries. The stair nosings are dished in the middle from a century of boots. Every railing is cold and damp to the touch, always, and somewhere above me a neglected overflow is finding a new path down the ivy, drawing a darker green line that wasn't there yesterday. Tomorrow someone will have to cut it back.

In the evening I touch the buttress when I pass it. Not for luck. Just to feel that it's still solid. Rust-dark iron, still there. That one is Buttress Mara, she went up in '19, held. If a wall fails — and one does, every few months somewhere in this watershed-sized town — we will all go. A hundred of us without being called, shoring with timbers, rerouting the water, salvaging the soil in barrows. By week's end the wall will be a scaffold. By month's end it will be a water-stair. We'll talk about it the way lowlanders talk about grandmothers.

You ask what I am. I'm not lichen on stone. Lichen is decoration. I am lichen *in* stone. I live *in* the Works the way blood lives in a body — not on it. There is no outside to go outside to. My roof is someone's field. My field is someone's roof. My door opens onto a catwalk that is someone's ceiling. There is no empty space. There is only through.

And I don't dream of the lowland. They say it's hot there, and dry, and shadows are sharp. Sounds like a place where things die of thirst. Here, everything weeps, everything drips, everything is damp and cold and green and black and rust-red under the low sky, and it never stops moving, never stops being used, never stops being kept.

If you come down to Tier 36, you won't see me again at first. I'll be bent over the trough, pulling hyacinth.

Listen for the *plip*.

If it's steady, I'm there.
