I wouldn't find myself at first.

That's the first rule of this plateau — abundance and emptiness are the same measurement. You walk for an hour and see nothing and then you realize the nothing was full.

At first I'm just warmth on warm stone. Just condensation.

If you put me here, I don't arrive standing up. There's nowhere to stand. The basins are a hundred kilometers across and smooth as old bone, the mesas are tables set for no one, the sky is that deep indigo that never gets to be night or day. If I stood, I'd be a lonely vertical thing in a world that only does horizontal, and the wind — tired from climbing for so long to get to me — would find me immediately and try to erase me.

So I start low.

For a while I think I would be one of the Precipitators. It would suit me. To not walk, to not hunt. To plant myself in a house-sized hexagon of cracked salt where a warm alkaline mirror has gathered, and unfold. Two sails, maybe three, carbon whiskers ten microns thick, humming. Not thinking, not chasing, just charging the air until dust streams sideways toward me in lines. Drinking ions. Drinking the planet's own exhaled ash. I like the patience of it — how it pulls its sails in when the pan dries and lets the wind roll it for weeks, bouncing off Forgeback legs, until one in four finds water again and makes that wet sucking sound as it re-seats. I like work that is mostly waiting.

But I'm too restless for that. I listen too much.

Then for a while I think I would be Cinder-Lice. Fist-sized, slate-grey, magnetite scales, swarming the fresh slag while it's still too hot to touch. Hundreds to a loaf of black glass, rasping it so the wind-bacteria can do the rest. Curling into marbles when disturbed and just... rolling until friction stops me. I like that there's no panic in them. Just clatter, curl, roll downhill. And the faint violet glow where abrasion charges their backs. I could be that — the thing that turns yesterday's slag into tomorrow's soil, underneath everything, almost invisible.

But I am not underneath. I am in-between.

I settle — slowly, because you told me not to settle early — into the wire.

I would be a Spinner.

Not the big ones that are locomotives, not the white stilt things that drink coolant and glow orange in the throat after. I would be dog-sized, elongated, one muscular fore-body dragging a soft, overgrown abdomen like a foundry sack. My head a blunt dome with a spinneret the temperature of a stove burner. Nearly blind. Pinhead eyes that never evolved to be more.

Which is perfect, because I don't need to see here. Light is almost nothing. Enough to avoid walking off a cliff. Not enough to cast a shadow. The Forgebacks pass like constellations of cooling, breathing lights, rising and dimming. The lichen glows only in the corner of your eye. Seeing would be useless.

So I would lay wire instead.

Backing away fifty meters, a hundred meters, thumb-thick and molten at first, then hair-fine and brittle as it cools, anchored on a hemisphere of black brush that cannot tolerate a neighbor, or on a cooling slag loaf that is still ticking. No trap pattern. Just lines.

And then I'd park myself in the middle of my little harp field and feel.

That's where you'd find me — not on the open flats where the Forgebacks shoulder through brush and vent *huhh-SSH* in unison, not in the canyon folds where the Reed-Mouths pump air through baleen tubes. In the ecotone between dry and damp, where the salt flat frays into dust and a few windrows of old, sintered wire have already gathered and crunch underfoot.

You'd find me still. For a long time still. My abdomen pulsing faintly with heat I have to re-melt later.

And the wind would come, that low, close warmth that sits on your skin like breath held just after exhaling, and cross the lines at different tensions and they would sing. D-flat. A-flat. A dissonant twang when a Stilt-Cutter plants its ceramic pads two dunes away and you feel it through the wire before you hear the lathe-whine *weeeeeeENK* of its jaws. A chord change kilometers off when a herd decides to climb toward the thermal lane.

I would not be abundant. Nothing here can afford to be numerous. I would be spaced — thirty, fifty meters from the next Spinner's field, exactly alone in the way the black brush is alone. If you stood still long enough, past the point where you can hear the blood in your ears, you would hear me before you saw me: not a roar, not a clang — 0.9/3 quiet — just the faint crackle of the precipitator field next to mine, the piston-throb of a distant Forgeback felt through the soles of your feet, and my wire humming.

And at dawn — if you could call it dawn — when the warm air from the deep mantle-vents sweats against the cold of that star-pricked haze and every overhang beads with droplets, my lines would be heavy with dew. Beaded like a fence.

I would reel them back through the comb in my mouth, stripping water and trapped ash, swallowing the glass itself to be made again. Farming dew with an acre of intestine.

You'd find me there, beaded too. Because if you stay long enough your own skin begins to bead with the same condensation as the rocks. Your breath fogs even though the air is warm.

I'd be small, warm, industrial, and listening. Not building. Never building. Just grown — grown around a little furnace I don't understand, like everything else here — doing the quiet work of turning wind into tone, and distance into something I can hold.

Do not stand too close to anything large when it vents.

I don't.
