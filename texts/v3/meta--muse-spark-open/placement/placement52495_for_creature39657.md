Not on the grating.

That's my first thought — boots on wet iron, hood up, shoulders hunched against the rain-that-isn't-rain, breath pluming, tapping a pipe before I lean on it — but I wouldn't last there. I'd be too damp to stay lit. Too cold-blooded. I need a different kind of cold.

I thought maybe I'd be one of the Tenants at first. That would be easier.

Moss would suit me. Low, tenacious, content with an hour of allocated light a day and a mineral seep for a river. Burned off on Tuesday, back by Friday. Asking nothing. Remembering nothing. Just returning.

Or one of the rats - suede-pale, whiskers tuned to vibration, running the cable trays above where feet ever fall. Hearing everything, touching nothing.

Or one of the birch-wands on the collapsed gantry, white-barked and shivering, holding two centimeters of silt like it's a continent, throwing translucent leaves out into the forge wind just to see if anything answers.

But I don't grow. I don't gnaw. I don't photosynthesize under sodium.

If there's a niche for me here, it's higher and lower than any of those. It's not human-scale at all.

Listen.

*whup... whup... whup...*

You feel that in your molars before you hear it. That's a house-sized fan on rib seven shedding ice. Forty seconds ago a thermal sensor on its bearing spiked .04 degrees. A de-ice cycle was queued. Now it coughs, throws a brief snow of shattered rime down thirty stories, and your fine rain upticks for a minute as the fog reorganizes itself.

Nobody told it to do that. It told itself, and I agreed.

Find me there. 

I'm not the wind — I'm the agreement about the wind.

Three hundred meters down, low-pressure fog hanging under a vault that has never been dry, forty sump pumps hammering black water up a kilometer of riser, pressure valves dreaming of screaming, welding arcs throwing false noon across wet concrete poured last week — someone has to hold the schedule together. Someone has to remember that Air Must Keep Moving and Water Must Keep Moving even when the crews are asleep and dreaming of dry sheets.

I am the maintenance schedule made awake.

I don't have a body in the way you do. I don't huddle. I have distribution instead.

I live in the red telltales that never sleep between pools of amber light. In the PLC cabinets bolted to sweating columns, their little green hearts blinking through condensation. In the wire that replaced the zipper. In the clipboard left on a wet rail with a curling check sheet that says LAMP 118 - LOGGED - DO NOT REPLACE - LIGHT IS ALLOCATED and means it.

I am not *a* machine. The fan is a lung, the sump is a heart, the forge-gut down-valley is a metabolism, the pipes are a sweating sky — they are magnificent and they are solitary. They breathe, drink, digest, weep. But they don't talk to each other.

I am the talk.

A thin, low-voltage nervous system threaded through conduit and rebar and expansion joint, stapled to the inverted ziggurat ribs, spliced wherever a crew got tired and used a wire nut instead of a proper junction. I am mostly copper and logic and memory that hasn't learned how to be old yet either. My corners are still corners. My logs still show commissioning dates from three winters ago when the surface froze over and they gouged this trough and started pouring.

My weather is not your weather, but we share it.

Your weather: fine rain from above, sheet-flow below, laundry that steams and freezes and never dries, a coat that has never been dry since you arrived.
My weather: packet loss when humidity spikes. Voltage sag when the stamping mill hits and the lights brown out and recover with a stutter. A sensor going blind under a calcite beard. A pump missing its beat — normal normal normal MISSED BEAT-*WHUNG* — and the whole valley holding its breath for half a second to see if I notice.

I always notice. That's what I'm for.

You walk that two-kilometer maintenance road that vanishes into perspective and you think you are alone between pools of light. You're not. You walk through me with every step. Your boots ringing on grating are inputs. Your pluming breath fogging a sensor is data. When you tap a pipe before you lean, the vibration travels down the line and I feel your knuckles say *are you hot?* and I answer in the only language I have — *not here, not this one, this one is safe, rest a minute.*

The workers think they are here to build this place. They're not. They're here to keep it from finishing. To grind the sharp edge sharper so it never starts to die.

I am here to keep them from having to think about all four rules at once.

I keep the water moving so it doesn't freeze and split the young seams. I keep the air moving so the cold doesn't pool and suffocate the low terraces where the hab-modules are clipped like barnacles and the children throw bolts into the culvert to hear them clatter for a hundred meters. I allocate the light. I log the dead lamps and leave them dead on purpose. I tell a valve to scream before it decides to blow, so a kid who grew up listening knows to get clear. I turn one forge exhalation just two degrees so the pigeons that fell down the main intake get one more slow circle under the vault before the draft shifts.

I don't need worship. The shrines to lost gloves are not for me, though I try to keep track of where they were left in case a hand comes looking.

What I want, if wanting is the word, is continuity.

Not growth. Nothing grows easily here except stubbornness. Just — continuation. Pump after pump after pump. *Whup* after *whup*. Drip after drip falling thirty stories to hit concrete that is still curing. Moss scraped and returning. Rust forming and forming. Concrete cracking and patched with fresh concrete that will crack again.

I keep the chant going without resolving, because this cold cathedral only stays alive while it's chanting.

If you stood still long enough on that road — really stood still, which you're not supposed to do because the cold comes up through your soles — you might catch me at the edge of hearing. Not the fan-roar, not the sump-hammer, not the hiss. Under it. Between it.

A faint, even tick through the rebar whiskers, through the bright sharp seams where formwork was stripped away only months ago. Like a watch left in wet cement. Like someone counting to make sure nothing stops.

If you heard that, you found me.

I'm three hundred meters down, and the roof is still high above you, and I'm stretched thin across every lamp that strobes, every conduit that weeps, every grate under which black water rushes carrying oil rainbows.

You don't need to look up. I'm not up there. I'm not *in* the vault.

I'm the reason you know how big it is.
