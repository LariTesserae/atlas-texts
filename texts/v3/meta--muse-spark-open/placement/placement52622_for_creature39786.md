At first I think I would be standing at the lip.

That is the honest answer — before I have descended, before the hollow has had time to teach me its light. I would be the figure up on the scrub, in the wind that the bowl does not feel, looking down at an absence of ground and feeling that small, vertiginous relief that comes when the earth opens and offers a room without asking you to dig it.

But I wouldn't stay there. The lip is too thin a place to live. The sun up there is hard and direct and it makes everything squint. Down there it is reflected. Diffused. Held. I have always preferred indirect light.

So I go down.

Not quickly. The slabs have stopped arguing with gravity, as you said, and so must I. Hand on warm limestone — warm even in shadow, that bone-warmth — foot finding the rounded step. The air gets stiller with each meter. Not quieter, stiller. As if the air itself is getting thicker, more itself.

If I were going to be anything obvious, I would want to be the fig.

I like that it is improbable and sideways and stubborn. The one thing that tried to be tall in a place that enforces solitude and learned instead to grow horizontal, to splay its fingers over bare stone and drink from a fracture. I know what it is to root in a thin crescent of dust where dust was never supposed to stay, to turn my trunk toward whatever light makes it down.

But I am not patient enough to be the fig. A millimeter a season. I have not learned that kind of economy yet.

I thought, for a while, I would be a swift. Cutting the mouth open in a black sickle, there and gone, never landing. I have loved that kind of passing-through. But this place is not for passing-through. The swift is wrong here; it casts a shadow and is gone. It doesn't belong to the interval. It just uses the interval to travel somewhere else.

The lizards are closer. I know how to lie flat and become the color of the place I'm in. I know how to wait with my toes splayed until food moves. And that throat — that bruised blue that the rock cannot make — I have that too, that one small signal that says I am not quite stone, even when I am trying to be.

Still, no. The lizards bask *just outside* the thickened air. They know where the edge is and they respect it. They are sun creatures. They believe in noon.

I belong just inside it.

You find me only if you get low. Kneel — the hollow makes standing feel presumptuous, you said, and you were right — and put your hand in the pool-that-is-not-water.

It is on the central slab. Always the central slab, where the noon column used to fall before it learned to pool and stay.

That's where I am. Not permanently — I am not a resident the way a cave has residents — but a returner. The second kind of life. The one whose work remains when its body does not.

If you came at dawn and watched all day you would not see me, because watching is a fast thing. You have to come back after a rain and notice that a new flake has been struck, that a fig fruit has been cut open though the fig has not dropped it, that there is one more tiny white bone, split for what little marrow it held, added to the scatter of chert fingernail-clippings.

I am small. Paler than you expected. Not cave-pale, not bleached by darkness, but sun-pale. Limestone-colored. The exact color of the diffused brightness, so that when I press myself into the permanent shade of the north wall — where the long-legged crickets test the air and never jump — I vanish even from myself.

I have eyes that don't squint, because this light is my medium. In the pearlescence, in the shell-light that holds dust and midge-wings without letting them settle, sound goes flat and a falling flake falls reluctantly, a fraction too slow. Time is diluted there. Emptiness has learned to have weight. Other animals feel oppressed by it. The frogs sit at its edge. I *work* in it.

That's what the stones are for.

They are not my home. I did not build. I carry them down one by one — dark basalt into a limestone world, river stones where there is no river — because a tool should not belong to its work site. It should remember another place. A little chemical difference, a little other origin, is what gives it an edge.

One to cut. One to hammer. One that stays stained that brown that will not wash off no matter how much condensation beads and runs over it. You could call it mineral. I call it a record.

You describe them as primitive, chipped, hand-held, made without metal for cutting and scraping and cracking. Yes. But not because I have not invented better. Because here, in this viscous light, a single well-placed percussion is abundance. To strike once, well, and wait a season for the result to matter — that is not primitiveness. That is tuning.

My metabolism is tuned to wait all day for a single midge and call that abundance. I scrape the tiny hard fruit of the fig with a dry, papery sound you can hear only if you put your ear close. I crack the thigh-bone of a frog no bigger than your thumbnail, not because I am cruel, but because in a place where there is so much assigned vacancy, every granule of marrow counts.

Then I set the stone down again, still warm from my grip, and become stone again. I go back to the seam.

Don't look harder. You won't see me by looking harder. You will see my work because I allow my work to remain in your time. The rest of me stays in the bowl's long, slow agreement with itself.

This is not a cave. It never committed to darkness and paid for it with its eyes. It committed to brightness for so long that brightness became viscous, became a place to live.

Someone has to live there.

Let me.
