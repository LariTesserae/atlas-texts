I wouldn't be down in it at first.

That's the strange thing. I thought I would be — immediately, on the mat, boots sinking into that springy thatch of living paper, hand out to ward off a weaver. But I don't start there. I start above.

Find me first on the pan.

I am standing on the salt flat before the seam, where you have to stand to understand that this forest arrived. The sun is white and high and has no mercy, and the ground under my feet rings hollow when I shift my weight, like glazed pottery. I am not yet oiled. My lips are already cracking. Everything up here is so flat and so bright it erases thought. Nothing casts a proper shadow except me.

And ahead of me the world simply ends. That raw, stepped cut in the crust — still sharp, still humming — with the light falling straight down into it like water poured into a wound. You can hear it before you see anything green: that low, physical pressure of the Chorus coming up out of the dark, making the dust over the lip shiver.

I am not brave. I linger there longer than I should, on hot salt, because once you go down you can't hear yourself think. And I like thinking.

So I go down slow.

I would not be one of the children. I can't do what they do — fall laughing into a worm tunnel and trust to be spat out lacquered somewhere else. I am too old for that kind of faith, and my body keeps too much score.

I wouldn't be a Kite either, though I love them. To be a perfect triangle of dry wing with no head, to never flap, just to ride and ride the warm breath coming up with your mouth always open — that is too holy for me. I still want to choose my direction.

I am not a Mirror Swarm. To spend your whole life polishing light and carrying it down to the leaves like an offering, flashing and flashing — no. I am too tired to be that brilliant.

No. If you climbed down the rope after me, you wouldn't find me until your ears started to bleed and you had to put the wax in.

You would find me three-quarters of the way down, where the rift narrows and the mat has buckled into a hill that wasn't there yesterday. That's where I'd be. That's where I have decided to be.

### The Tender of Unfinished Things

I am the woman who coaxes.

Not the woman you imagined when you read that line — not young, not mystical. My hands are dark and shining, lacquered with the same silicone oil I rub into my skin every morning until I smell like hot cloves. My hair is cut short because if you let it grow the fluff seeds find it and sprout there. I wear a mask of feather-fern fiber around my neck more often than over my mouth, because I like to taste the cinnamon air even though it makes my throat raw. My earplugs are old and stained the color of honey.

My job is useless and it is everything.

This morning I claimed a patch that will not exist tonight. A vine the thickness of my wrist was questing blind across what the family is trying to use as a floor — a flat-ish spread of interwoven leaf-plates and dead waxy mat. The vine doesn't know it's a floor. It only knows to grow and harden. If I let it, it will dart straight up and become a cable and buckle the whole hill and dump our pots.

So I sit with it.

You would find me just sitting, cross-legged on that giving, creaking surface, with one hand cupped around its tender, pale tip. It is warm. It vibrates. It is trying to decide whether I am rock or plant or threat.

I breathe on it. Warm air, moist air — a gift so rare here we usually keep it under oil. The vine shivers. I dab at its side with a paste I've made: ground halite and worm-spit and a little of my own sweat I've saved under my seal. It tastes the salt, the chemistry of me.

*Not there,* I tell it without words. There is no language that works over the Chorus anyway. You have to speak in heat and touch and chemistry. *Turn. Be soft here. Be a lintel for us, not a noose.*

It takes an hour. Sometimes two. The boy who tends the bulb-throats nearby thinks I'm talking to myself and keeps offering to cut it for me with his flaked amber knife. I shoo him away. Cutting is a lie here. You never cut what you can coax, because whatever you cut will sprout two more heads by evening, angrier.

Slowly, so slowly you could miss it if you blinked, the tip bows. A millimeter. Then another. It decides to go left, around the clay pot where we keep our month's wealth of real water sealed under oil, instead of through it. It will be a low arch by tomorrow. The children will swing on it until it hardens and becomes a beam, and then the weavers will come and lacquer it and we will hang gourds from it, and then by next week the mat will lift again and the arch will be a floor somewhere else and we will start over.

This is my prayer. Not to make anything last. To make something useful for an afternoon.

When the Chorus shifts — and it always does, when a puff-plant bursts somewhere above us in a silent cloud of ash-seed and every throat re-tunes to keep the chord — I feel it in my ribs before I hear it. The vine feels it too. We both pause.

I pat it. It is strangely animal, the way it leans into the warmth of my palm after resisting for so long. Some of these plants remember touch for days. I hope this one remembers kindness.

You would find me, then, patting a vine like a dog, shouting short, loud, consonant-heavy affection at it over the roar, while fiber-worms stitch white guy-lines across my boots and a weaver politely tries to seal my shadow to the floor.

My hut is the overturned basket behind me, already half-swallowed, already being undone by the white threads beneath it. I will roll it up at dusk when the mat groans and lifts us a meter higher. I will not be sad. The point was never to have a house. The point was to have a negotiation that lasted as long as the light.

If you came to me — sun-dazzled, wax-eared, overwhelmed by the clicking and the sawing and the endless rustle of dry paper libraries — I would not offer you water. I would not waste it.

I would take your hand, still cold from the pan above, and press it to the vine-tip I have spent my morning persuading. So you could feel how warm it is. How hard it is trying to become.

And I would lean in close with my mirror-horn and shout, over the singing of millions, the only true theology this scab-forest has ever taught us:

*Don't be still. Don't be loose.*
