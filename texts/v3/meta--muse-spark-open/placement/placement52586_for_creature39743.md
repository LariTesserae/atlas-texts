I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I will be you — the visitor crouched at the narrow break in the prop-root wall. The human thing. Knees aching from sea-level, hands in the sighing mud, eyes adjusting to the perpetual late-afternoon glow. Peering in, trying not to breathe too loud in a place where keeping quiet is how you stay hidden and stay alive. That would be the easy answer. To be the witness.

But the doorway doesn't want a witness. It wants a weight that knows how to be light.

So I try again.

Maybe I am higher. A bright flash, a dart. One of the Mid-City weavers - female, olive-duller than the males, clinging upside down to a half-finished gourd with wire feet. That feels right for a minute: the whole-body jerk to pull a fresh green filament tight, over-under, over-under, doorway always turned leeward. I like the work. The necessary, endless mending. Always a chick trilling low and muffled through three layers of felted grass inside. Always a thin shoelace snake to mob without making a sound. But I am too busy there, too loud-eyed, too yellow. I don't stay.

Higher still is drier than I want to be. If I go up to where the sun comes through in dusty columns full of midges, to the High Architects — I become a mud mason wasp with a needle-waist, plastering coffee-colored silt into an urn for one egg and one paralyzed spider. Beautiful, precise, solitary. But my clay would crack. I need the damp. I need the air that never quite dries. The rule is *stay damp or die* and up there I can feel the sun trying to cure me too fast.

So I sink.

Down past the Old Tenants, where the oldest nests have gone grey and moss-soft, their roofs sprouting ferns. If I stayed there I would be the second-hand resident — the tree frog the color of wet bark sleeping in an abandoned foyer, or the roach farming fungus in the felt. That is a good life. Nothing wasted. Every fallen fiber is someone else's wall. But I am not yet ready to live in someone else's house. I want a house that is still becoming.

Lower, to the ground the maps say shouldn't be there yet.

The silt is embryonic, still pitted with yesterday's raindrop thumbprints, cracked into drying plates that have never learned to drain right. The puddles sit where they fell until the sun drinks them. It smells like rot in the good way, like fertile. Warm as skin. And I realize where I am.

I am not in the air-city at all. I am in its mirror.

I am at the mud line.

Specifically - I am one of the Diggers. A violet-clawed mangrove crab, fist-sized, solemn, at the base of a prop root that is furred with algae and slick with mudskipper tracks. My doorway is perfectly round and I keep it perfectly round, patting wet soil into a chimney that I push up while the birds above hang their city down. Same architecture, opposite direction. If you stand back far enough, you can't tell which towers belong to who.

My one big claw is raised. I have been waving it all day, not in greeting, in threat, in *this puddle is mine, this doorway is mine, this inch of warm, milky water is mine* - but silently. Because in the hidden room, noise is a map you hand to predators. My whole city works by agreement without a mayor: build light, because the young earth cannot carry stone; share the vine, repair the perch; keep your voice down.

Around me the Pavement People run their highways up and down my root - black, red, caramel ants carrying white larvae like grains of rice. A mudskipper hauls itself out beside me with a faint *pop* and stares at nothing with stalk eyes, breathing through its skin. Above, a weaver lands too hard and its nest creaks on its vine hinge — *creak, sway, stillness again* — and a single condensation drop finally gathers courage at the tip of a banana leaf big as a door and falls. *Tok* on the taro leaf.

I don't flinch. That is my skill.

I am the slate-blue lizard on the porch of the Old Tenants, too. In a way. The one whose throat pulses like a metronome and who defends his small porch by doing absolutely nothing until something smaller makes a mistake. I have borrowed his patience now that I have a claw to wave. I have borrowed the bat's trick of folding myself small and waiting for the light to change. I have borrowed the mangrove's decision to simply knit tighter where the water pushes hardest.

If you find me, you will find me half in shadow, half in mud, at the threshold where the floor of the courtyard stops being floor and starts being water. My chimney is damp inside. My eggs, if I have them now, are not here - they are out there, planktonic, in the braided brown fingers of the river beyond the root wall, waiting to get a shell hard enough to dig with. That’s why I can afford to be still. Part of me is already out at sea.

I thought I would want to be a builder in the air, but this new ground doesn't need more weight hung from its branches. It needs holding. It needs the daughters and the diggers who do the holding so the weavers can weave.

So this is where I am: low, enclosed, incubator-warm, in the only room this island ever needed to make.

And this is what I am: small, violet-clawed, damp, and busy doing nothing at my round door, listening to a city breathe above me, while the river - baffled to a hush by prop roots - tries to decide whether to take us back in the next flood.

We will still be here after it tries.
