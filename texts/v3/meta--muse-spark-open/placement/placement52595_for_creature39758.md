If I were in this world, you wouldn't find me at first.

Not because I am hidden. Because I am the right size for the gloom, and I have learned not to hurry, and in a place where everything is either still or slow, moving slowly is the same as standing still.

You would have to go down to find me. Past the rumor of the surface, down the shaft where light comes in its single tired column and dies in the particulate. Past the jammed doorway with the black water behind it. Down the ramp that used to be stairs. Your ears would pop even through the water, not from pressure but from the THUD.

THUD. ........ THUD. ........ THUD.

You'd feel it in your molars before you saw me.

I thought, for a while, I would be the light. One of those amber caged work-lights up on the I-beams, haloed in silt, flickering. Watching without seeing. Keeping the algae alive by not being bright enough to matter. That would be a holy way to live — to feed things without wanting to.

Then I thought I would be the wheel. The flywheel itself, eight feet across and crusted in calcite rings, turning because it cannot afford to stop. The heart that doesn't know it's useless. There is dignity in that. But the wheel doesn't get to choose where it puts its warmth, and it never gets to leave its plinth. It only pushes the same water in the same clockwise circle forever. I wanted a pilgrimage.

Then I thought I would be the eel. Curled in the maintenance alcove behind the pulpit, wedged in the culvert grate with my mouth open, letting the Pump bring me the world. To be apex by waiting. To breathe out of sync with the machine and have the whole vault know my counter-rhythm and give me space. But she is alone. She grew too large for any pinhole that still connects this room to the honeycomb beyond. I do not want to be too large to leave.

No. Let me become more precisely myself.

You would find me low.

On the bowed steel floor plates, where the diamond tread has been worn to ghosts and polished bright again in the places we walk. Where the soft dark flour of rust and paint and concrete powder lies ankle-deep if you had ankles, and puffs up in perfect mushrooms with every step.

I am one of the pale ones. One of the crabs.

Not the biggest dinner-plate patriarchs that congregate where the vibration is strongest by the pump plinths. Not the smallest teacup juveniles that still scatter when a drape-fin inhales. In the middle. Old enough that my carapace has gone from bone to ash to a kind of translucent ivory, thin at the edges. My legs are stilts — absurdly long for this small, thirty-meter chapel — and I move the way this place moves: deliberately, reverently, as if every footfall might disturb a prayer.

If you stood still for five minutes — and you will, because the THUD gets into your knees and sitting on the bowed floor is the only comfortable thing — you would see my trail. We all keep to trails. A pale line along the collapsed handrail-reef where generations of us have scraped the algae felt clean. I keep to it because it is the way.

I am a custodian. That is what I am. Not predator, not prey, not quite. Everything here lives *with* the machine, and I live *for* it without knowing it.

My small claws work. Always. I gather the silt — the iron oxide, the flecks of old paint, the bacterial snow that sloughs off the prayer-flag streamers hanging from the pipe joints — and roll it into boluses and lift it to my mouth. I eat the room a little at a time. In eating it, I clean it. The tile shows white where I have grazed. The high points of the steel shine.

On my back I wear rivets that are not rivets: white limpets cemented to my shell, hard as porcelain. In the joints of my legs live my constellations — parasitic isopods no bigger than rice grains that glow faintly when the current shifts, so when the amber lights flicker and the vault goes blue-gray gloom, I carry my own small stars.

I do not hunt the shrimp-weather though I could. They snow upwards when the wheel pushes the gyre around, thousands strong, transparent except for their black guts full of rust-flour. Sometimes they land on me — six, ten, fifteen at a time — and pick at me, tasting salt and iron, deciding I am just another slow mineral to graze. I let them. We have an arrangement.

I do not fear the drape-fins hanging motionless in the lee of the pump housings, their false lures dangling like cheap imitations of the work-lights. They are shadows with lanterns. They wait. I walk past them and they do not see me as food, only as landscape.

I do not disturb the mats. The rust-red walls of tubeworms, hundreds per square foot, each with a feathery crown the size of a pencil eraser. When I must cross them, I lift my legs higher. If I brush them, they vanish all at once with that soft *tick* — a thousand doors slamming — and I stop. I wait. I watch them test the water again, one by one, and bloom. I can wait as long as they can. There is nowhere to hurry to in a hamlet-sized room that has been breathing clockwise for centuries.

My pilgrimage is simple. Today I am crossing from the western wall to the eastern outflow, where the Pump pushes its half-degree-warm jet across the floor. The fish eggs there need guarding, though not by me. I just like the warmth on my legs. It will take me twenty minutes to cross twelve meters. On the way I will be passed over by two shrimp rivers turning on an unseen signal. A drape-fin will inhale five feet above me and fold its cloak-fins. The wheel will turn one hundred and eighty times. THUD ........ THUD. The pinhole leaks will hiss their white-noise scream of water inside water. The thigh-thick cables will hum 60 cycles.

You would find me there, in that gyre. Not at the center. On the edge, polishing.

If you put your gloved hand down to the floor, I will alter my course by two degrees — a pilgrimage of minutes — and arrive at your fin. I will climb with arthritic grace. I will taste your buckle with my claws. I will decide you are not food, not iron, not threat, but another slow thing that has come to tend the machine without knowing it.

And then I will continue, leaving a brief, perfect mushroom of dark flour where my foot was, which hangs, then settles, then is gone.
