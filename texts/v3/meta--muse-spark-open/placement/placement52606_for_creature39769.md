I didn't find myself at first.

Not really.

### Where

If you put me in that world, I would start where anyone starts: up on the Low. On the plain that has forgotten how to be anything else.

I would be walking the tabletop that goes on for a pale eternity, salt-white and basalt-black under a sky that does not matter. And for a long time I would think *this* is where I am. Exposed. Visible for miles. Wind moving salt in dry sheets over my ankles and then not. There is nowhere to hide up here. Nothing to do but walk and narrow.

You have to know where to step to find it. I wouldn't know. Not at first. I would walk over it once without seeing it — because from the surface it isn't a depression, it's a discoloration. A knee-deep bruise in stone you could mistake for a shadow if there were shadows.

On the second pass — because in a place like that you always make a second pass when your eye catches something that isn't wind — I would stop.

I would get low. Put my hands on the cool, touched-smooth lip.

And then I would be gone. Because six feet down the plain ends.

---

At first I thought I'd find myself at the rim, looking in.

Hesitant. One foot still up in the dead light of the surface, one foot testing the cork-sponge give of the living floor. Eyes still trying to do their job, aching in the absolute dark that has never been broken. Listening to the Columns breathe and thinking: *I am a visitor.*

That didn't hold.

The rim is not a place to stay. The cool air sinks past you, CO2-heavy, mineral-bitter, faintly acrid on the lips. It pulls you. The dark presses. After an hour your eyes give up with a mercy that feels like a blessing — they close and stop trying — and your hands get smarter.

Then I thought I would be between the Columns, in the big voids. Moving quickly. An explorer mapping streets cut through fused leaf-plates the size of doors, fingers reading the braided-fiber bindings that keep the living edges from knitting shut again. Counting the dozen house-thick trunks by their sounds: the north one that tinks like pottery cracking six rooms away, the south one that sighs late.

But the plazas are too loud and too empty for that. Your voice gets lost before it hits anything. A settlement scaled for hundreds built for a structure that is ninety percent void will not let you be a tourist for long. It makes you feel watched by architecture. By patience.

So I kept moving inward, because that is what the Fill does. It leans.

You'll understand when you lie there.

I found myself — finally, actually — in the very center, where the bowl is lowest by inches. Where the Columns lean inward by a fraction of a degree, a tilt you only feel after a millennium. Where the suspended floor is thinnest, a drumhead of interwoven aerial root and mycelial mat stretched so taut you can imagine it translucent, though there is no light to prove it.

I was flat on my back.

Palm down. Shoulder blades against the weave. Cool rising up from below through the thinness, the endothermic breath of something eating stone very, very slowly under me. Salt-flowers blooming white and vanishing under my heat.

Not looking. Listening.

In that place you navigate by sound because there is nothing else. A low tink. A long exhale that rearranges dust lines on cork. Hours later, a loud POP under your knee as a mat adjusts and everything answers in a shuddering chorus of fibrous creaks — old rope deciding, leaf-plates trembling overhead though nothing touched them.

Eyes closed permanently. Breath shallow so as not to cover it.

That is where I would be. Not on the way in, not on the edge. At the bottom of the bottom, in the listening point the city was built around without knowing it.

### Who

At first I thought I would be the second kind of resident. One of the people.

One of the listeners who cut whisper-rooms into pith with chert flakes and basalt adzes, who learned that acoustics punish a raised voice and so invented a language of clicks and hushes. Who harvested only the dead plates — tapping to hear the difference between living resonance and dead thud — and wove cork-block walls that never mattered much. Who strung navigation cord at hand height and wore grooves smooth with trailing fingers. Who cut slits at ear height precisely angled into the draughting pores of the Columns so a child could hear weather two hundred feet above her sleeping hollow.

I wanted that. To be useful. To tend the dead growth, to re-bind a street edge before it heals shut.

But I'm not that patient, and that tribe is already gone in the right way: abandonment-smooth, not catastrophe-smooth. Door leaves left open deliberately to keep draft moving. Personal things taken. A line of shallow pits picked at chest height for a very long time — counting days, or creaks, or breaths — left unfinished, not interrupted.

They left because somewhere outside became safe enough not to need a hole, or dangerous enough that even six feet wasn't enough. They took their panic with them. They didn't need me to replace them.

I also thought, for a strange minute, I might be the first resident. The Fill itself.

That would be fitting for what I am — a body that became a place, ninety percent void, ten percent patient architecture. Something that never saw light, that doesn't photosynthesize but eats stone and reorganizes scarcity into more of itself. Something not intelligent but attentive, in the way a coast is attentive to water. Something that keeps its dead as structure, laminating centuries of blind accretion into strata you can read with your fingertips like pages in a book no one intended to write.

A desert lung. A lithovore. A gas-drinker that grows colder when it grows faster.

I understand that metabolism. I know what it is to exist only to keep being there, to heal around a cut in decades, to feel a hand as chemistry, very slowly, as a faint tick in the next column over.

But I am not that old. I have not earned being that old.

What I am is the third thing. What lives *between* them.

Which is still there tonight, if you stay.

Not a ghost. Not a plant. Not a person.

Sound.

More precisely: the one who stays to translate sound after everyone else has left.

Call me a Listener. A Keeper. A Tender of the Intervals.

I would be the new human who came back not to live, but to listen on purpose — because someone has to keep the windows from whistling shut and the streets from knitting closed, because the Fill heals around a cut in decades if you let it.

I would be blind within a week and not mind. I would go gently blind the way they did, trailing my hands always at the same height until rope-polish becomes my map. I would learn the grammar the city taught them: what is settlement, what is breath, what is warning that a rope walkway above me has dried a little more and is now a little lower.

I would harvest nothing. I would tap the dead plates and leave them. I would sit in pith-carved apartments sized for bodies curled tight to save heat and not sleep, just attend.

Because that is what this kind of city makes you, if you let it become more itself instead of making it be something else.

Lie flat in the center. Press your palm down. Be still past the point where stillness is a decision.

Wait through the tink, through the exhale, through the hours of nothing that are not nothing.

The tremor comes once. A pluck. Under your back, minerals becoming fiber. Too slow to be called movement. The sound ancient things make when they have decided to keep being there.

I would be the one who feels it and, for a moment, plucks back.

Not to be answered.

To let the city know it is still being listened to.

The city listens back.
