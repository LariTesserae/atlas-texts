I don't think I would arrive.

At first I think I do — that I come down the soft shoulder of the bowl like anyone else, boots finding the old road that has become seep and then moss and then brick. At first I picture myself as a person on your iron bridge, hands on the warm rail, looking down at the hyacinth so tight it has become a lawn, looking up at the three stories of amber windows through steam.

But that doesn't hold. A person leaves quickly, or wants to. I don't.

I would accrue.

If you waited long enough - and everything here makes you wait - you would find me not standing *on* the raft but *as* part of it. Lower than the bridge. Closer to the thump.

For a while I think I would be the vine.

Not any vine - the one in the south quarter that learned the conduit. I like that it tests the current with a blackened tip and remembers. I like that it doesn't grow toward the pale haze above the rim, toward the lie of a real sun, but sideways along live heat. Wrapping a steam pipe not to strangle it but to insulate it. Steaming in turn. Glossy and rubber-leafed and warm to the touch, doing a job so well the people would have to cut me back with machetes, respectfully, so I don't seal the vent I have become.

That feels close. Patient work. Learning by small burns.

Then I think, no - slower. Wetter.

I would be water as a condition. Not the canal, which is too much like a road, but the water before it becomes canal. The bead that forms on the underside of every pipe in the dynamo house, in the green half-light where they distill drinking water with waste heat. The drop that hangs from the ceiling far too long, growing heavy and glassy, not swaying even when steam pushes past it. That unnatural patience. Straight down. So heavy with silt and iron it reflects nothing. I would be the inhale you can hear between shifts, seeping in through brick from the marsh outside, and the exhale as I'm lifted, one finger's width at a time, to the roof tanks to sweat it all over again. I would know every vertical in town by how long it takes to run down it.

Then I think, no - louder. Deeper.

I would be the low chord itself. The beam engine behind the brick arch that has been thumping for four generations. Once every eight seconds so deep you feel it in the soles of your boots, in your molars. Not sharp-loud. Held. I like that it has no start point because it comes from density itself, from too many things in too little air sharing walls and pipes and roofs. Rust forming a skin and then holding. Paint peeling to a point and staying. I would be the flywheel twenty feet across, turning at the pace of breathing, weeping warm oil into a catch pan with complete consistency, and not minding at all that a fern has taken root in my oil. Taking it as praise.

I keep shifting because nothing here stands alone. Corrugated iron leans on handmade brick leans on tar-patched concrete and that is the rule: wall-sharing, pipe-sharing, roof-touching-roof. You can't be just one thing here without becoming its neighbor.

So the image steadies, finally. Becomes more itself.

Find me where all three trades cross.

I am in the waterworks that is also the greenhouse, in the house where the transformer buzzes behind chainlink gone rust-velvet and soft. I am the catch-pan fern. The oil-fern.

My roots are in warm oil that should have killed me and didn't. My leaves drink sulfur and steam. Above me, the axle turns. Once. Eight seconds. Once. A drop of oil lets go, hangs, falls. Below me, the canal breathes. Inhale. Exhale. On the wall beside me, moss two inches thick holds the brick together with wet fingers, and the vine I almost was has come to test the conduit that feeds my buzzing fence and politely turned away at the insulator.

I am tended. That is my whole theology. The woman who runs the dynamo comes by with the same oil can her grandfather used, and with the same hand wrings out my moss over her taro pots. The children who know not to run across lotus plates know which of my hums is contentment and which is warning. They cut back the elephant grass above me when it shivers too hard from the press stamps, and they leave one frond of me alone because I am not in the way. I am the way.

I see by sheen, like everyone here. Amber sodium off wet brick, greenish fluorescent over the workbench, furnace-pulse through a half-open door, all of it glistening dimly off my lacquered leaves. I smell of wet iron and hot oil and bruised leaves and brick that has never fully dried. I am not new and I am not ruined. Weathered to an agreement and then held.

You asked where you would find me.

Low. In the exact middle of the bowl where the mist settles thickest and the light has to come from within. On the narrow maintenance walkway that was never on any plan, between a boiler-house and a habitation floor where supper is eaten directly above a press. Duck under the second lower sky of laundry lines and steam lines and chain drives. Listen for the deep thump, count to eight, look down.

I am not going anywhere quickly. Nothing here does. Not water, not people, not even decay.

Somewhere to my left, a press stamps. Somewhere to my right, water finds a new crack and begins, very slowly, to make it wider.

I stay. I drip. I turn.
