You'd have to look for a little while to find me.

At first I thought I would be higher up. A Passer Between. One of those high white moths, weightless enough that the cirque's own breath can carry me. Drifting from grove to grove without ever having to touch the terrible bright stone between. That seemed right — to be the thing that doesn't hold at all, just moves on air.

But I don't drift. I hold.

Then I thought — maybe the beetle. Thumb-sized, oil-on-water green, fused shut and determined, taking all day to cross six feet of scoured basalt because there is one flower, one week a year, worth crossing for. I like the stubbornness of that. But I don't labor like that either. I don't cross. I stay.

You would not find me on the rim where the vines hang humming. You would not find me deep under the overhang where the giant taro does its slow work of tipping and spilling and making its own private rain. Those are lonelier, larger allotments than I have earned.

Come down lower. Mid-bowl. Where the floor steps into pedestals and pans.

You would find me between.

Not quite in the fern-grove — though its fronds throw a moving lace over me at noon and its creak is the closest thing I have to company — and not in the bare seep-stone either, that bright sheet where water takes everything with it and nothing is allowed to keep. If I were stone I would be that stone, clean and empty so the hush has somewhere to go. But I am not stone. I learned the other half of the rule.

Hold your water or let it pass. Be an island or be stone.

I am an island.

Specifically — and it takes a moment for your eyes to adjust to the scale enough to see me — I am one of the Chapels.

Not a grove, not a pool. A moss cushion. Dome-loaf. Knee-high, round as a proofed bread, emerald so deep it looks poured. I sit on my own little pedestal of peat and mineral silt, a knuckle of the mountain showing through, exactly where one of those hundred finger-width falls beads and gathers overhead.

If you crouched, you would think at first it was just moss. Then you would see it is all one thing. Or a city that has decided to be one thing. Tight rosettes spiraled clockwise — all of us in here spiral the same way, following the same slow swirl you can't feel but we can — all of us leaning, incomprehensibly, not toward the sun at noon but toward the open lip, toward the drop and the blue haze of the lowlands below. Drinking air more than light.

Put your ear close. I am ticking.

Tok... tok... tok. Not fast. Patient clock time. Each hair-fine awn catching mist, beading it inward into the sponge-heart. Dead generations below me turned to black peat that acts like a bladder; living fur above, glossy and fat with water. You could wring a bucket from a handful of me and I would not change shape. I have learned to take up my small, intense allotment and do it completely.

That is my work. I hold.

The tree-ferns creak and spill to feed their own pond. The taro holds a pint in a door-sized leaf and tips it exactly when it needs to. The pan pools hold one newt, three shrimp, one plink every half hour. I hold larger than them and smaller than the grove. Enough for the beetle to come once a year and lay its one pearlescent egg deep in me. Enough for the midges you never see to come and drink and leave again.

In the long even light without hard shadows, I must look like a miniature from twenty feet away. Up close, I am a field. If you rested your palm on me, your skin would feel filmed, and I would sigh a little water up around you. I would not wash you away. The bare stone beside me washes away everything. I keep.

If a sitter came — someone who tucks a woven-leaf shelter under the eave for a season — he would end up on the dry lip of the boulder right next to me, between me and the fern-grove, feet just above the seep, listening to the loud hush that is really a thousand hushes.

He would hear my tok join the *tok* of fern-fronds dripping into the pan below, and the soft glug as that pan overflows to the next, and after an hour the whole cirque would start to sound like breathing, in and out, very slow.

That is where you would find me. Right there in the interval, being the interval made lush. Not moving, not calling, just holding exactly how much the air allows to be held, and no more, so that when you finally need a little water — or a little quiet — I can tip it, just a few drops, back to you.
