I find myself at first on the rim, and that's how I know I don't belong yet.

Not inside the bowl, but just above it — where the weathered lip softens from bone to flesh. Where the basalt sweats. I am sitting with my legs dangling inward, boots off, soles against the warm stone. The stone is not cold the way high places should be cold. It holds the sun and holds the breath underneath the sun and gives both back to me through my feet.

For a long time I thought I would be the climber. The one who had earned the bowl by climbing. But I haven't climbed. Not the way the description means climbing. I haven't come up the long spine of the range with purpose. I have wandered sideways into it, the way water wanders. Which is the only way you're allowed to find a place like this. You don't conquer a saucer that the mountain cupped to collect rain. You spill into it.

And once I'm inside, I don't stand in the center. I know better than to stand near the throat.

I find myself on the western side, of course. On the driest shelf, which is not dry at all, it just weeps a little slower than the rest. Next to the slab of amber stone that leans out like a table. Next to the three heart-leaves overlapped and held down with river stones.

I am not the occupant.

That was my second mistake, early on — to want to be vast and patient and distributed, to be the sheet three hundred paces across, to be the thing that learned to be unappetizing to the world. I wanted to be the ancient one, the skin under the soil that farms itself in perfect circles. But I am too hurried for that. I make noise when I think. I chew. I would have bitten the leaves.

No. The occupant does not need me to be it. It already is. It has no loneliness that needs filling from inside.

I am the one who comes and does not stay.

I am the user of the room.

Let me become more specific as I sit here, because the bowl teaches you to be specific. Each thing gets its true color when there are few enough things to see.

I am not a man, not quite. Or if I am, I'm a young one. Barefoot now. The black soil wells up around my ankles when I shift my weight and makes a print that fills slowly after I lift my foot. My feet are stained red to the calf. My hands too, at the cuticles, under the nails — that rusty red from the pit scraped in the peat. The water in it is dark as old blood now. The crushed root has settled. The chewed dipping stick is still resting across it, and it's my stick. I chewed the end myself until it frayed into a brush.

I have a knife. Not a good knife. Soft copper, hammered thin, edge turned over from cutting too many midribs. It doesn't keep an edge long, which is good. It means I have to stop and whet it on the amber slab, and while I'm whetting it I have to listen to the pauses between drips. One bead forms on the eastern rim and falls. Long pause. Another, thirty feet away, answers it. The bowl holding its breath, then letting it go bead by bead.

My job is simple and it is not harvesting, not really.

Anyone could tear a leaf. A wind could tear a leaf, if there were wind. But the instruction here is *cut cleanly along the midrib*, and that is a kind of prayer. I was taught — not by a mother or a priest, but by the last girl who had the copper knife before me — to never take the newest leaf on a crown, and never the oldest. To choose the middle-aged ones, the ones that have had their sun. To place my palm flat on the velvet-green moss stool next to the tree while I cut, so the occupant feels a hand and not just a blade.

To leave payment. Iron for leaf-leather.

The root I crushed outside the bowl and carried up — it grows only where birds still sing, down where the air is still eaten by mouths. I carry its iron up as red water. I kneel and dip, and paint. Not the leaves I'm taking; the ones I'm leaving. I paint a stripe, slow, on the next leaf that will be someone else's in a month or a year. So the occupant drinks metal through its skin.

That is who I am: the dyer. The translator between two chemistries.

I don't live here. No one lives here. If I tried to build a wall or a cairn the moss would grow over it in a season, not out of malice but out of tidiness. If I tried to light a fire the air would refuse it — too wet, too still, too deliberately inhospitable to appetite. The place has weeded out fire the way it weeded out insects.

So I come for an hour. I weigh three leaves under river stones to cure in that high, thin warmth that sits without weight. I leave three others to finish curing for whoever comes next, even if no one comes for years. The occupant can wait years. It can wait centuries.

And while they cure, I sit on a moss stool that is not a stool. It is a knot of breathing. I sit anyway, and it allows it. It prickles warm against my thighs through my thin trousers. It smells like rain on hot iron. If I stay very still, I can feel the water being pulled through me too — not just under me, but *through* me. The same slow capillary tug that moves the petal inches a minute toward the throat. Toward that perfect meter-round gut where the water disappears without a splash. My own sweat beads and gathers and will eventually find a channel no wider than my forearm.

For those twenty minutes in the afternoon when the light comes low from the east and turns the porous basalt translucent — when the whole rim glows like honeycomb, lace of chambers and threads lit from within — I finally understand what I'm doing here.

I am not tending the occupant. I am letting it tend me.

Elsewhere in the world I am crowded. Touched too much, bitten, pollinated roughly by news and noise. Here, I get to be spaced. Thirty feet of empty moss between me and the next thought. A slow, straight spiral down to rest exactly in the gap where I fit. A single deliberate puff of spore-smoke that holds together and sinks precisely where another part of me is waiting to receive it.

I am not its animal. I am its brief, tolerated symbiont. The mouth that does not chew, but cuts and paints and leaves.

When my hour is done I will stand, pick up the cured leaves — leather now, true-colored, will not wash out — and back out over the rim the way I came, sideways, spilling.

The prints of my feet will fill with clear water behind me.

The bowl will keep holding its breath after I'm gone, bead by bead, until the next iron-bringer comes to sit on the stool that was left empty for them.
