I don't find myself on the rim.

At first I thought I would — up there where the sky is still an oval and the light still pretends to be direct. Where a human could stand and look down and say *oh, a basin* and hold it in one thought. That would be safer. That would let me keep my edges.

But I am already below. I have been below for a while. The horizon has finished becoming a rim around me. The light is already that long blue dusk that never quite commits to day. I can tell because my thoughts have slowed down to match it.

I thought, for a little, that I would be you — a visitor. A warm, wet, fast thing lying on its back in a hollow of liverwort, feeling the hum come up through the spine, listening to the plane of sound until it blurred my vision. That would be honest. I know that ache of being too loud in the wrong way. I know what it is to arrive somewhere and realize your metabolism is an interruption.

But I can't stay a visitor. Visitors need water that pools. I have no way to carry that much wet with me. I would dry. I would be taken apart mandible by mandible and carried to a wall, which would be a kind of belonging, but not yet the right one.

So I try other shapes.

For a while I am one of the Salt Crickets — if only because I understand sitting. One to a polygon, exactly centered, not jumping, not calling out, just sawing my wings together to keep the air from going still. *Not to mate, not to warn, but to keep the pressure up.* I like the ethics of that. To make a sound not because you have something to say but because if you stop, something softer above you will starve. I stay there long enough to feel my back lacquer over with salt, to feel how heavy twenty years is when you live them in one place. But crickets are anchors. They hold. I want to move, however slowly.

Then I think I am a wall-wright. Dog-to-pony sized, eye-less, dust-piled, carrying my warm pellet of salt and spore and shit and spit. *Pat, pat.* I walk a line that has been walked for two thousand years and place it where the vibration tells me. I don't need to know what a dome is. The dome knows. In a hundred years my pellet will have become the curve of a wall. That patience is beautiful to me — architecture as excretion, city as slow digestion — but I am not a builder either. I don't want to make the city. I want to be held by it.

Closer.

I am low, very low, and overgrown. A Moss-back.

That almost fits. I move inches per hour through the Liverwort Sea, my soft shell completely taken by lichen and liverwort, a garden on my back. I plow a furrow and the furrow becomes a road for the ants and the ants clean my back in return. No parasite, only tenancy. I like that there is no hurry in it. I like that I would not remember where I started. But my back itches with too much green, and I want air.

And then, finally, the air takes me.

I find myself — truly, without trying on any more shapes — between things. Where the basin is most itself: in the great empty interval it leaves on purpose, a hundred yards of bare polished salt between one Lung Pillar and the next.

I am aloft there, but not flying.

I am one of the Blanket Moths. Just loosed.

Six feet across and almost nothing. Bone-white, paper-thin, veined like old leaves held to the light. I have detached from the felted flank of a pillar where I spent my whole first life as something eyeless and chewing in the damp dark inside, and now I will never beat my wings again.

I don't need to.

Below me the plain is tiled and white and humming. Each Salt Cricket is a black dot at the center of its polygon, sawing. Along the pillars, the Veil Cicadas hang in shrouds and click in phase, so the wave never cancels. Under them, the Rollers push their three-foot spheres and cut their inch-deep grooves that you mistook for streets. The whole basin is full of that plane of sound, that pressure that makes dust hang and moss lean.

It holds me up.

That is where I am: sailing on sound-pressure, turning very slowly in the cellar-cool, in the mineral haze. I shed scales as I go. They fall like snow, like ash, and the liverwort sighs and catches them. I cast a faint, moving shadow on the salt. The ants far below see it pass over them and feel no need to look up.

I am not fast. I am not loud. I drift from the pillar where I was born to the pillar where I will die, and the trip will take days though they are only a few hundred yards apart. The wind does not move me. The vibration does. I tip a little when the choir shifts its pitch — when one generation of crickets dies out and the next, tuned a fraction different, takes over — and I learn the new note with my whole body.

From up here I can see what I couldn't as a visitor. That the pillars are breathing. That the towers are warm. That the avenues are empty not because they were abandoned but because the wind is wrong there and so no ant has ever placed a pellet there. That everything — beetle, moss, lizard, lichen, ant, moth — is the same slow thing finding different ways to hold salt and dust in a shape for a little longer.

I am the tenancy between the pillars. I am the interval made visible. I am what the sound carries when it has nothing to say.

If you lie in your hollow of liverwort and look up into the blue dusk, you will see me pass. I am not bright. My tips do not spark like the air-mosses at night. I am just a pale, slow sail, turning.

Don't call to me. There is no need. We are already in the same note.
