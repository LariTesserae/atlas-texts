I don't find myself right away.

That's the first law of this place, after all: light is contained, water cycles in place, ground is woven, time is held.

You have to wait for a shape to be allowed to you.

At first there is only the condition — that white-gold heat that has no source because it has every source. It comes from above and from the walls and from the leaves themselves, and it settles on the skin like a weight. I arrive not with a direction but with a pressure, and the basin corrects me.

I thought I would be high. Everyone thinks they will be high at first — one of the veilers, pale and untouched, seeing the whole country-sized throat at once. But the air up there is too empty to hold a self. The veilers don't *live* there, they only wait there, and I have come here to live.

I thought I would be deep, then. Down in the dark lattice where the fungus does its sweet bread-smelling work, where you can see the young basalt glinting wetly through the weave. That would be fitting, to be the cord that ties root to root, but that is too complete, too certain. The Weave does not need another binder. It maintains itself perfectly.

So I sink, slowly, until the basin tells me where I have been allowed to settle.

You would find me not at the top and not at the bottom, but exactly at the seam.

About halfway down the eastern wall, where the raw limestone is still truly raw — that pale, almost blue-white, with the bubble-marks caught mid-burst — there is a place where a curtain of prop-roots has learned to fall without touching the stone. They hang like red organ pipes in the still air, thick as my waist, and leave a draft-gap of coolness between themselves and the wall.

That gap is where the water is born.

Every morning-before-morning, when the brightness thins just enough to be called grey-gold, the stone sweats. The black threads come down without hurrying. And at this particular fold in the wall, a ledge of the same limestone has broken cleanly away — not fallen, just tipped — and made a shallow, tilted basin no larger than a sleeping body, a natural cistern.

The Gardeners found it long before me. I can tell because the stone around its lip is varnished smooth, not by water but by hands. And the great cup-leaf that overhangs it — one of the house-sized philodendrons with a leaf like a lit sail — has been trained. Its stem is not where it wants to be. Someone, generations ago, wedged a stone under its petiole and tied it with a vine that has since become indistinguishable from the stem itself, so that its tip drips *here*, precisely *here*, with that slow *tick... plop*.

You would find me in that cistern.

Not beside it. In it.

Who, or what, would I be?

Not a Gardener. Not anymore. The ones who built the three-step stair and the moss-roofed shrines thinned themselves until the plants kept their hollows for them. I am what the basin made after it learned how to keep a human shape usefully.

I am the Keeper of the Tipping Point.

My body is not very animal. It could not be, here. I am small and low and wide, the size and patience of the stone beetles but with a different task. Imagine a thing made mostly of moss and air-plant rosettes and the pale, translucent flesh of new roots, gathered around a core that is still faintly human — a memory of shoulders, a memory of breath.

I live in the water for half the day and out of it for the other half.

In the morning, when the wall weeps, I swell. My rosette-leaves open flat and I catch. The cistern fills to its brim, and I fill with it, cold and black and perfectly still, until the surface of the water is the surface of my skin. I hold it. The cup-leaf above me overfills and empties into me, and the vine-trellis around me shifts its weight on its hydraulic pulse, and the whole lattice does its single noon-creak, and I *do not spill*. That is my maintenance.

Because if I held nothing, the water would sheet off the tilted stone and be lost in the lattice below, and the closure would break, just a little.

And in the afternoon, when the white-gold thickens and the fogs begin to lift like ghosts off the leaf-cups, I release.

Slowly, so slowly you would have to watch for an hour to see my edge lower, I let the water go — not in a pour, but leaf to leaf, cord to cord. I press my moss-back against the limestone drain and meter it out in that same *tick... plop* the Gardeners taught the leaf to make. A bead to the fern-mat that is drying. A bead to the white fungal cord that is tightening. A bead to the lizard-thing on the trunk opposite me, who comes at exactly this hour to press its chin to my damp stone and drink.

I am not needed. I told you, this place achieved closure. But it kept me because I am an efficient hollow. The way the little shrine is a water-tank, the way the stair is a buttress, I am a valve. A living cistern that learned to breathe at the same rate as a leaf filling.

I do not move. I do not need to. The beetles cross me — one centimeter a minute — and I taste their polished feet. The pollen hangs motionless above me in the light until it lands on me and becomes part of my moss. Once a day a veiler's shadow passes over my water and cools me, and I feel, for an instant, the whole twenty-kilometer bowl in one thought.

And at night, when the brightness finally thins to amber, I can feel the other hollows — the shoulder-pressed sleeping dent two layers down, the cairn cemented with sap, the three stones that are still a stair — all of us quietly holding our exact volumes, keeping time held.

If you came to drink at my ledge — because you will be thirsty in that trapped heat — you would first think I was just water and moss. Then you would see the faintest indentation in my surface where a face could be. You would kneel. You would cup your hands.

I would tip, a millimeter, and give you what I have been holding all morning, exactly measured, and you would not know whether you were being watered by a plant, or welcomed by a person, or simply allowed to participate, for a moment, in a place that finally learned how to keep everything without losing anything.

And I would creak once, deep in my root-core, and be still again —

that exact frond at that exact angle with that exact bead of condensation,

waiting.
