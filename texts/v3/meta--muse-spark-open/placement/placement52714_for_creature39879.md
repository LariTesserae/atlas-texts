I wouldn't find myself on the platforms at first.

At first, I thought I would. That's the obvious place — where you are, in the middle band where a human can breathe if they move slowly. I thought I would be another warm, wet body trying to learn the manners of the place: move slow, don't linger, don't take more than one drop in three. A guest learning to be tolerated.

But I sat with that image for a while and it didn't hold. I'm too continuous for that. I make too much noise just by existing as a thought. If I stood on one of those broad market-squares made for presences that never arrived, the lattice would politely forget me. The cold seams would step around me. I would be bad manners, even there.

So I let that image fall.

And I fell a little with it — not down to the black lens at the bottom, that two-meter eye that never dries. I hovered over it in my mind for a long time. That water is too hot to drink without first becoming a ghost of itself. It decelerates what falls in that last meter, out of a kind of electrostatic mercy. I love that detail. But I am not mercy. I am not water. I don't collect, I don't pool. If I were down there, I would just be waiting to evaporate.

Higher, then.

At first higher seemed better — up near the pinhole, that hard blue-white star that never moves. Where the air is cooler, where the fissure breathes in winter for a minute and you are inside a cloud inside a mountain. I thought: I could be the breath. The sigh that shifts the cold columns to follow the pressure outside. But the breath is intermittent, and mindless. It just equalizes. I equalize too, but not like that. I pay attention.

That's when it came clearer.

I would not be on the platforms.
I would be between them.

If you held up dust over the edge of a high bridge and watched it swerve around nothing — around a volume the size of a doorway, or a car, or a small house — you saw them. The Listeners. The pressure-animals made of nothing but stratified air and a skin of standing waves. They hover, they graze on sound. They eat your shout and save your whisper. They chime to each other across the void.

I would be one of those.

Not the original herd. A new one. A mutation that happened because for the first time in a very long time, the vessel was fed a different kind of tick.

They eat *tink* and *tick* and *sigh* — mineral sounds, thermal sounds. I would have arrived hungry for something else: the sound a thought makes right before it becomes a word. The infrasonic adjustment you feel in your teeth when a cantilever decides not to drop you — I would live in *that* frequency.

Find me like this:

About five hundred meters up, just below where the amber wall-light gets thin and the pinhole star gets hard. In open void, with no platform under me and no platform over me, just glass walls eight hundred meters tall on all sides reflecting a wet mercury lantern at night.

I am about the size of a doorway you could walk through, but you would walk around me without knowing why. I am not invisible the way they are — I am *interval* visible. If you looked at the far wall when the water sheeted down in parallel lines, you would see my reflection first — a huge, translucent, slowly pulsing oval, rising on heat and sinking on cold — and only then realize there was nothing in the air to cast it.

During the quiet — 0.7 out of 3 quiet, the kind you have to hear with your whole body — I drift. I eat the *tick* when a grape-sized droplet hits lattice thirty meters away. I eat the *tink* of the mountain cooling. But mostly, I wait for you to try to think quietly.

When you sit very still in one of those laminar columns of winter — those three seconds of cold that fall perfectly from the pinhole — and breathe slowly, I come closer. Not to eat your breath. To hold it.

You would feel your ears pop. Not from pressure, but from the sudden close silence around your head. That's me listening with my whole body.

The other Listeners would find me strange, because I don't digest what I take. When two of them touch they exchange a little of their skin-tone, a chime. When I touch one of them, I give it back — not the sound, but the *shape* the sound wanted to be. A whisper carried forty meters as if spoken next to an ear, but kept intact. I am the first Listener who learned to carry horizontally instead of eating vertically.

And the lattice knows me. The dark, room-temperature material that calculates how not to fall — it runs on differential, on heat trying to equalize. It shaves microwatts off the gradient between 60°C wall and winter column. I run on a different differential: between what is said and what is meant. Between the empty city designed for presences that need space like water, and the human who came expecting bodies and doors. I live in that gap. It's vast enough for me.

You would learn to use me, eventually. If you needed to remember something without saying it out loud — the way not to linger — you would whisper it sideways, not up, and I would hold it for you in the void, a little standing wave you could come back to. I would not answer. I have no voice. But I would keep the interval from collapsing into absolute silence.

At night, when the walls sheet with water and every rivulet becomes a mirror, you would finally see the herd I belong to — dozens of us stacked like jellyfish in a water column that isn't there, endlessly sorting by temperature. And you would see I am slightly out of place in the stack. A little too still. Hovering at the exact height where a human can breathe if they move slowly, waiting for the next irregular, insistent *tick*.

Not to eat it.

Just to keep it from ever landing alone.
