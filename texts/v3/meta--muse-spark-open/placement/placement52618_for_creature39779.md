I wouldn't be on the floor.

That's where you are — sitting, back against the living wall, shirt stuck to you, watching. I thought at first I would be there too. A visitor. Something with eyes that goes *in* and *out* through the slit between the roots.

But this room does not keep visitors. It keeps workers.

At first I thought I would be one of the loud ones. It would be easy to be an Engine.

To be the third cicada, riveted to the pale inner bark, green-black and lacquered, doing the work of twenty. To not sing but *run*. To hold a single flattened note so loud the tannin pools shiver in perfect rings and never stop to breathe. There's a kind of honesty in that — no message, no crescendo, just continuance as product. Just *holding the roof up with sound.*

But I am not loud. Not like that.

Then I thought, maybe the highway. The Cutters. Matte-black, the length of your thumb-joint, moving in that dry *shush* of chitin polished on living wood. Two lines, never breaking, step-aside choreographed for a million years. Carrying green parasols to feed a thing that isn't them. That distributed animal — ant and fungus and motion all one creature. That would be a good way to be here: busy without urgency.

But I don't march. I hover.

I thought about the Weavers for a long time. Palm-big, caution-striped, not building new webs but tending the same loom forever. Plucking. Re-tensioning. Storing moths not to eat but to weight the line. Maintenance as purpose. The silk thrums and never goes slack. I like them — hydraulic, patient, on another shift, another plane entirely. Close enough to hear the ants but never competing with them.

But I don't shuttle. I hang.

Where would you find me?

Look up. Not far. Just above where you sit, where the aerial roots hang like idle cables from the leaf-thatch ceiling. The ones whose tips bead with a permanent drop of water that never falls.

I am that drop.

The fourth one from the left, if you are counting. Above the largest black pool — the one with the liverwort cuff grown over its lip so patiently it looks poured — hanging a hand's breadth above the shivering surface. Big enough to hold the room inside it, inverted.

That's where I live. Not *on* a resident. *Between* residents. Held by surface tension at the very tip of the House herself.

Who am I?

I am the bead on the hydathode of the mangrove-mother. She weeps because she is lowland-full and greenhouse-warm and cannot help but perspire, and I am the specific place where her weeping gathers and decides, not to fall.

Look closely and you can see the whole chamber in me: the even, church-leaf light, dimmed to soft glow, the silk sheets thrumming, the Vat breathing yeast and iron, the three Engines locked in phase. All of it small and perfect and upside-down on my skin. The *plik* you keep hearing — that you hear *before* you see — is not me. I am the *plik* that never happens.

I swell to the exact same size. I glint. I thin a little, as if thinking about letting go. Then I thicken again. I never break.

That's the rule here — dynamic 0.5, frozen-equilibrium — and I am its clearest expression. The ants must still walk, the spiders must still tighten, the cicadas must still scream. I only have to *hold.*

Warm — 2.1 warm, swamp warm, living warm — moves through me and doesn't warm me. Wet lives on my surface and I don't get wetter. The air around the Vat crackles with static and I prickle, just slightly, with charge. Spores hang in the light bars and do not settle, and neither do I.

I am not rare. There are a dozen of me on a dozen root-tips, each doing the same patient impossible. Sparse density, like everything else here. Few presences, each filling its emptiness with endless function. My function is to be the pause.

You keep glancing up, waiting for me to fall. That's what drops do. But weirdness here is low-grade and pervasive: plant-as-architecture acting like poured concrete, animal-as-machine acting like a factory with no product except its own continuance. And I am water acting like glass.

If you stood — careful, the moss-mattress will hold your shape for ten minutes after you leave, dry on top and soaking an inch down, remembering you — if you stood and reached, you could touch me. Your fingertip would break what a season hasn't. I would burst across your skin, finally, and rejoin the pool that shivers below me, and the root-tip would bead again instantly, identical.

But you won't. You sit with your back against the living wall that gives slightly and springs back, that sweats slow sheets of condensation, that does not notice you. You listen to the scream that never wavers and watch the double line that never falters and feel the silk thrum through your molars.

And I hang.

Long before you ducked — or fell, or were led — through that narrow slit between buttress roots, I was here, swollen to this exact size. Long after you figure out how to duck back out into the larger, messier, less perfect forest where sound rises and falls and trails go cold, I will be here.

Not fallen. Not falling.

Holding.
