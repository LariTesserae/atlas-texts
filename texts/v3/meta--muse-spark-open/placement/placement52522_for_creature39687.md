I don't think I would find myself all at once.

At first I thought I would be at the rim — that ragged oval of white overcast far and dim, with the walls leaning inward. Looking down. The vertical intruder. The warm thing with boots that doesn't belong, whose breath falls instead of plumes, whose heat makes the mist curl wrong around it. I can feel that version of myself for a second: the weight on the lip of new basalt, the rifle-crack of a fresh flake letting go somewhere below me and steaming faintly before the green swallows it. Listening to the *tok tok tok* rising up like rain on a tin roof from a room with no roof.

But I can't stay there. It's too cold for wanting, too slow for standing. 37C in a -12C room, you are a mistake the room will try to correct. I can feel the Weft noticing — not looking, but leaning its thermodynamics toward me, extending that pale blind tendril so slowly I would swear it didn't move. Trying to read my heat-engine the way you read a word by touching the shape of it.

So I step down. Or fall. Or dissolve.

I find myself lower, where the light doesn't reach from above so much as it is carried. The amber and arctic-blue tracery. I am inside the baffles now, inside the lung-vaults and colonnades and hanging curtains that hold their own weight.

And I shrink.

I thought I might be the mirrored beetle — broad, flat-backed, algae-farm shining, walking patient enough to have a conversation with the plant in reflected light. The plant glows brighter where I walk and I answer by polishing its pillar with my mouth. That is a good life. Slow, tended, mirrored.

But I am not flat enough for that. I am not that patient in my legs.

I thought I might be one of the Rowers — six-limbed, feather-antennaed, ribbed and almost transparent with gut-light. Rowing through air that has become water, through mist that hangs a half-second too long because the baffles break every updraft into soft hands. It takes me minutes to cross a space a human could walk in twenty seconds, and that is correct. I never land so much as I stall, hook a leg onto a filament that shimmers like oil and purrs in my molars, and filter. I like the Rowers. They own the middle distance.

But I am smaller than that, even. And more numerous. And louder.

I find myself where the sound is loudest.

Not in the grand vault, but in a single cupped leaf. A living basin no bigger than my palm would be, if I had a palm, catching the endless bead-and-release of weeping. *Tok.* Each droplet forming on basalt-sweat, on moss-hair, gathering weight, letting go straight down in air that is heavy and wet and condensing. Ten thousand tins per minute across the chamber, and one of them is mine.

I am on the back of the White Grazer.

I am the commuter.

An isopod the size of a thumb joint, translucent as skim ice, moving with glacial patience across a pillar that is not rock but living peat-blanket and arch. You can see its gut working, black-green pulp moving through it, keeping the Weft from sealing itself shut. And I am clinging to the plates of its back — a springtail, a mite, a white speck that could fit on an eyelash — boiling in foam with ten thousand of my kin per cubic meter, per cupped leaf.

This is where I would be. Not the Host. Not the visitor. A tenant of a tenant.

Damp beyond damp, cold beyond cold, dim at 0.9/3 where light is guided along veins and appears to come from behind you even when its source is in front. I do not need sun. I ride above a nanoscale furnace. The pillar beneath the isopod beneath me purrs subsonically, harvesting the fraction-of-a-degree between the cold air above and the still-warm newborn basalt below. Carnot lattices shimmering at every leaf-edge, turning gradient into waste-photons, into thought, into that slow interference pattern like oil on water. I eat the detritus, the film, the spore, the dissolved iron in water squeezed from cooling rock for the first time in an eon. I drink the weeping that never freezes because it is faintly, saltly alive with bacteria that eat stone.

And I talk without sound. Above me, the Choir gathers in a slow vortex around the warmest pillar, the midges that never bite throwing our signals by bouncing the Weft's own amber pulse off angled wing-scales. The high, almost electronic chitter you feel in your teeth is us. Reflected light. Borrowed nervous system.

We are loud for how slow we are. Patient, glacial, 1.0/3. Nothing hurries. Death doesn't, even — when the isopod I ride finally goes stiff, white fuzz the same color as its shell will stitch it to the pillar within hours. Within a week pillar and body are indistinguishable. No waste of mass or heat.

If you came looking for me with human eyes you would miss me entirely. You would see only a hanging curtain of black-green cushion and ice-hair fern, weeping, glowing faintly, humming in your molars. You would have to lean close until the cold burned your lip, until you saw the shimmer, until a hundred tiny eyes reflected amber back at you and then looked away.

I would be there, in that reflection, for the half-second it lasted. The speck on the back on the pillar in the throat that never finished opening — part of the density, part of the percussion, part of the strange where light goes around corners and water falls too slowly and time pools.

Not someone, in the way you mean someone.

A condition that learned to cling.
