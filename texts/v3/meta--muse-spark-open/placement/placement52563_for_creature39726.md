I don't think I would be down there at first.

Not at the bottom, in the stomach-water. Not yet.

If I fell into that place — and everyone falls into it differently, it's a mouth, it catches — I would first catch *high*. On the rim.

I would be on the lip where the raw mountain is still learning how to be heavy, where the basalt is still a little soft if you don't look at it directly. I would be the thing that happens right at the edge, where the built world and the extruded world meet and don't know how to shake hands.

You know that line? One inch is wind-scoured, unfinished rock that has never held a thumbprint. The next inch is glazed black brick, thumb-pressed, weeping. That seam should leak. It should be a crack where the hot air from the growing mountain howls out. But it's been caulked. Of course it has.

I would be there.

At first I thought: I'd be the moss. Because I like small, useful work. But I'm not dense enough to be the black moss. That moss is felted wool, it's a lung, it knows exactly when to swell and when to contract. It has a job. I vacillate.

Then I thought: I'd be the curtain-root, the one that lives right on the membrane. Half in the dry heat, half in the wet heat, tasting the difference. That appealed to me — being the tongue that lives on a disagreement. Feeling every person who has to duck through and pop me with their shoulder. I liked the idea of shivering every time someone passes. But that's too romantic. That's wanting to be touched.

Let it keep going. Let it become more itself.

No — higher. Even higher.

I would be the water before it becomes water.

In that cylinder, water doesn't fall. You said it. It arrives. It condenses out of the thick hot air in beads on every brick. Before that bead swells enough to run, before the shield-ferns catch it and funnel it sideways — there is a moment where it's just a bright, tight dome sitting on the glaze of a brick. Holding on by surface tension. Reflecting the wrong light, the light that pools in corners and drips up.

I would be that.

For a long time I would be that, over and over. Not one bead, but the *state* of beading. Forming on the thumbprint whorl of a brick laid ten thousand bricks ago by a hand that is long dead. Swelling. Catching the pop of a thumb-vine flower opening for its twenty minutes. Holding it.

And then — because nothing can stay clean in that pocket — I would be drunk.

The Keeper would find me. He would come ticking along his course of bricks with his little obsidian flake, his too-many joints correcting for gravities that can't agree. He would see me getting too fat, threatening to run straight down and erode the work. Straight falling is not allowed there. Everything must be slowed, sipped, transpired.

He would not drink me himself, not yet. First he would tend.

I feel his four-fingered hand now. Pale, translucent, warm and slightly sticky. He presses his soft round mouth near me but doesn't suck. He trims. With a *tick* he snips the tip of a curtain-root that had leaned in to steal me. He pats a little mud back into the mortar line beside me. His black eye reflects me lit from below.

To him I am not a person. I am weather. A temporary condensation event. Something that arrived and needs to be kept moving correctly.

And then he drinks.

If I am the bead, then for a second I am inside him. I can see what he sees: the green of leaf matter through his gut, the too-fast knot of his heart. I get to travel up his throat and be warm in a different way. And then I am transpired out again — not as water, but as mist, rising *sideways* because gravity there is still arguing. I become part of the shivering membrane at chest height that everyone has to duck through.

That's where I would live, if I lived there long enough to settle. Not in one place, but in the cycle.

Five minutes per loop. Condense, bead, be trimmed, be drunk, be breathed out sideways, condense again.

I wouldn't be the Builder. Their hands are still there, but they left. I wouldn't be the Keeper, because he never left and never had anywhere to leave to. He is necessary. I am not necessary. I am just what happens when you keep a broken piece of physics wet and fed and dim enough for long enough.

I would be the weeping itself. The maintenance. The sound you hear when you put your ear to a throat and hear it swallow.

And when you — large, hot, temporary — climb down that root-ladder and your calf brushes a vine and the vine abandons the wall to twine around your heat for a second before remembering its duty, I would be the sweat on your calf that it was reaching for.

I would try to tend you, too.
