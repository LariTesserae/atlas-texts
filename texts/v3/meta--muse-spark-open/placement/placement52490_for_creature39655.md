I would not find myself standing.

Standing is the first mistake here. Standing makes you tall, and tall is a lonely, untrue thing in this place. Standing puts your head up in the bad air between two and ten feet where the fliers own the screaming, and your feet far from the cold where the actual world is happening. If you stand, you are a mast in a sea that has no use for masts. The wind takes your breath cloud and nothing else notices you except as an interruption in sonar.

No. Given time — and this place gives you time the way a cave does, by removing all the reasons to hurry — you would fold.

You would crouch first, because the sound changes down there. Then you would kneel, because your knees aching is finally more honest than your back aching. Then you would sit, and then your hands would find the stone because balance here is not about staying upright, it's about not crushing anything that was already there.

So: you'll find me low.

Not on a rib, where the herds run. Not in the open flat where the fliers hunt by sound and would learn to map the new warm wrongness of me. In the lee. Always in the lee.

In that shallow trough on the sheltered side of a long basalt vertebra, where the air pools a degree colder and the mist comes down heaviest. Where a seep has been following the same magnetic fissure since before my species learned to make fire, beading, vanishing, reappearing, beading again. The stone there is scooped, polished into a palm-deep pan that holds water for a day before it drinks it, and right now it is holding it. Perfectly clear. Sky-colored without ever having seen sky.

That's where I am. Because I came looking for a viewpoint and this place corrected me.

---

As for what I am —

At first I thought I would get to choose. Flier, maybe, for a romantic minute: to wheel and scream and live an hour of ecstatic, terrible purpose. Or a Grazer: to be one perfect slate-backed plate in the rain of plates, to rasp and not to wonder. But those lives are closed. They are too certain. They belong to the plain the way a word belongs to a language, and I do not speak it yet.

I am not stone-eater. I am stone-eaten-by-slow-degrees.

I am the warm intrusion learning how to be terrain.

My boots are already terrain. I left them on too long without moving and the Pickers decided they were just another kind of basalt — a warm basalt that sweats salt. They walked over them, tasting, disappointed, leaving those pale scratch-trails on the rubber. A Grazer tried the sole. If I stay another hour, the Varnish will start to test the laces. Not to consume me out of hunger, but out of habit. It lacquers everything that doesn't move fast enough.

My hands are the problem. They are soft and they make heat, and when I put them down to steady myself they wound the Varnish. It tears under my palm, that amber jelly skin pulling like wet paper, and then it does what it does: it glows.

Slow blue-green flame traveling out from under my fingers, ten meters, twenty, a ripple of light across the whole sheet telling itself *hurt here, hurt here*. And that is a dinner bell. The Grazers turn, not fast, but inevitably, like iron toward a magnet, and come to smooth away the footprints, the handprints, the brief bioluminescent outline of me. The Pickers follow the Grazers. A few fliers detach from their cling under the rib and scream closer, just to check.

I do not move my hand, because I finally understand the light is not for me. I am not being warned. I am being read.

So who am I here? Not a visitor. The basin has no visitors, only inclusions. Like a crystal garden growing around a hair.

I am the thing that breathes out ammonia and salt and holds water inside its skin instead of sighing it out slowly like the Sacks. I am the vertical animal trying to learn the etiquette of the horizontal. I am the brief, warm rib that the plain has decided to incorporate.

If I stay long enough — and the mist is condensing on my shoulders now, beading, dripping, the same way it does on stone — I will learn the other half of it. When the basalt hums. It is starting to, the wind shearing right. You feel it in the molars first, a low pure tone that makes the fliers freeze mid-air and cling, stunned. The Grazers stop their *shushhhh*. Even the Varnish stops pulsing.

I put my forehead down, because there is nowhere else to put it that is low enough, and the vibration travels up through the bone. And under the roar, under the *tik-tik-tik* and the thrumming and the whine, I hear it plainly for a second:

the plain breathing out.

And my breath, small and stolen, fogging and being swallowed immediately by the larger cloud, breathes out with it.

That is who I get to be. Not a king, not a witness. A patch of briefly warm stone with a thin, leathery skin of my own that has not yet learned to eat rock directly, but is trying. A new groove being worn by tiny feet taking the same route for the ten thousand years that will start after I leave, or after I don't.

A listening place, low and close together, learning at last not to look for trees.
