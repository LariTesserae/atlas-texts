You wouldn't find me at first.

That's how it works here. In the first hour you walk the terraces you think the caldera is empty — all that iron and basalt and glass, bridges thrown across widening fissures, streets that end in a fresh upwelling of slag still steaming, and no sound coming off any of it.

Then you get close enough to hear breathing, and you realize every balcony has someone on it, just not moving fast enough to register as movement.

I am one of those still figures. You'd have to lean in. The air is too thin to carry a voice to you otherwise.

### Where I Am

On the eastern bench, third terrace down from the rim.

Not high enough to be the first to see the sun, low enough to be the first to catch the fog. That's the trade the mountain made with us.

My street is not a street. It was a lava bench when it cooled — a natural step in the columnar basalt about eight meters wide, with the cliff behind it still showing the ropey flow lines where it was poured. Someone came along after it finished shivering and before it finished contracting and carved doors straight into it. My door is one of them. The front half of my house is built — black brick, riveted iron joists, a window frame that was fitted crooked on purpose six years ago and has since settled perfectly plumb as the rock took its final shrink. The back half isn't built at all. It's hollowed basalt. If I put my palm flat against the bedroom wall in winter it is still faintly warm. It still thrums.

Outside, the terrace is both roof and floor. Above me, someone else's cistern is cut into the rock. Below me, my cistern makes the ceiling for the family under me. We all live like this, stacked in the cooling like layers in an agate. At night you can hear the child below me turning in his sleep through six inches of stone.

You would find me outside, before dawn. Always before dawn.

At 4:20 I am already up, sitting on the low basalt lip of my cistern with my feet in cheap rubber and beaten canvas, a long-handled squeegee across my knees. The cistern lid — iron, pitted, heavy as a manhole — is off. The air is still too dark to have color and the stone is finally, briefly, not hot against your back. The whole city is holding its breath.

That's when the differential breaks and the mountain inhales.

Over the eastern rim — sixty meters above me, a wall of glass-black cliff — the cold air from the lowlands finally spills. It doesn't blow. It pours. A slow, silent waterfall of fog a hundred meters wide, turning over itself as it comes, filling the caldera from the bottom up. The thin air that has been starving you all day suddenly has water in it.

That's my hour.

### What I Am

On the municipal rolls I am listed as Catcher, Block 9, Bench 3-East. Everyone is something like that here. Catcher, Tender, Listener, Incorporator.

The old word would be *janitor*, but that sounds too small. What I tend is not clean.

For the next fifty minutes I do not stop moving, but I never hurry. No one here can. My chest is too broad for that — that barrel they all grow into here, children included, to squeeze the kiln-thin air. I breathe the way we all do: *in-in — out*. A double hitch on the inhale you don't notice until someone from the lowlands chokes trying to keep up. My blood is brown when I bleed.

So I move at engine-speed. Dynamic 0.6, the same as the great piston houses down in the manufactories that take a full minute to draw breath.

I stand and tilt the basalt pans on the roof-ledge so condensation races along the grooves my grandmother cut. I herd water off the power cables with the squeegee — fat bundles of humming cable that run along every wall, beaded with droplets like strung pearls. The droplets run down into gutters, gutters into the cistern. It sounds like nothing from a distance. Up close, inches away, it is the loudest thing in the city: *tik-tik-tik-tik* on tin, a low splash, a child two terraces over whispering *good catch, good catch* to her mother.

The Condensers come down with it.

You don't see them arrive. You realize the fog has texture. Hundreds of them, each the size of a spread hand, translucent as onion skin, stretched over a skeleton that isn't bone but precipitated silica — you can see the faint white lattice inside. They don't flap. They hang, riding charge. Their backs bead with water faster than any iron surface, the droplets racing to a center that isn't a mouth.

I let them be. The deal is old: they drink the air, I drink what they leave. If one settles on my cistern rim — and one always does, the same torn one with a missing corner — I go still until it lifts. The workers say a good flock is a good catch. I don't know if that's true. I just like that they are the only soft thing in a world that hasn't learned how to be soft yet.

By 5:10 my cistern is full. Cold water, tasting of iron and faint sulfur, enough for nine families for a day if we are careful. Enough to drink so our lips don't crack slate-blue, to wash the basalt dust off our faces, to pour the first cup back onto the hot rock for the mountain. That's my favorite part: the hiss.

I lid it. By 9 a.m. the sun is a knife straight overhead, shadows razor-edged, and any water left open would be steam.

My second job starts after that, when the heat comes back up through the soles of my shoes.

I walk down.

Not far — three terraces, down steel stairways bolted into basalt that hasn't decided where it will crack yet, past the quiet market where no one shouts, past the headframes standing patient as insects on the cliffs. Down to where the air gets heavier, not lighter. Where the River runs.

It's not a river. It's the mountain bleeding.

A fissure in the caldera floor, three kilometers long, weeping water at near-boil every second of every day, steaming so thick in the morning you can't see the other bank. It runs in iron troughs past the glassworks and the salt terraces, white and yellow and rust-red crusts building an inch a year. The whole lowest district crowds its banks, because constant is holy when everything else is intermittent.

That is where I sit in the afternoons. Not catching anymore. Listening.

The filament-worms live in it. Red-black threads as long as your arm, braided and unbraiding in water that would peel your skin in a breath. They feed on dissolved iron. They leave rusty trails on the stone.

We don't eat them. We read them.

Each braid has a character. The one by the spillgate is always tight, nervous. The one under the precipitator bridge is lazy, looping. When the tight one balls up and pulses — all of them, together — I know the pressure is climbing deep and I pull the cord that rings the low bell in the borehouse. When they go slack and drift, the mineral load is dropping and I know the glass shop will have a bad pour tonight.

Are they animal? Are they mineral that learned to twitch? Here, at weirdness 2.4, you stop asking if a salt-walker — those stilt-legged crusts that form overnight on the cooling terraces and take three steps before collapsing into lace — is alive. Everything here is still cooling. Some cooling things move.

I sit there in the heat that comes from below, my forearms prickled with the white coins of old burns — my first kiss from the River when I was seven, like everyone — breathing slow, listening to the tick of contracting cable and the mountain settling three streets over.

### Why I Am That

If I lived down in the lowlands, thirty kilometers out where the air is a dusty blue-brown and green actually happens, I would die. My lungs, tuned for thinness, would drown in thickness. Going down is for the cable-trams that bring up dried beans and salt fish, not for us.

We were not built for here. Then we stayed long enough that here built us.

That's not a poem. That's the job description.

Last week a new upwelling heaved up through the avenue at the top of my stair. Hot, glassy, steaming, cracking as it came. An Incorporation crew was there by noon — not to break it, to make it a room. They drilled rebar into it while it would still take it, ran conduit through it. In six months someone's child will sleep in it, against a wall that is still warm.

My cistern was once new rock, too. My house, my neighbors, the trough where the worms braid. The city did not conquer the mountain. It colonized the cooling process.

I am not important. Density 2.4 means no one is. I am one of thousands of patient, umber-grey, lidded-eyed, broad-chested people who have agreed, without ever signing anything, to be the way this place finishes itself. To bolt a plate over a new crack. To drain a cistern before it boils. To listen to an engine take a minute to breathe and not get bored.

At night, when you finally see us — when the furnaces and sodium lamps go on and the whole bowl glows deep like coals, the lattice of steam and cable hanging above us as one slow breath — you understand we are not living *on* a mountain.

We are how the mountain learns to be still.

You would find me exactly there, in that glow, squeegee across my knees again, waiting for the cold to pour over the rim.
