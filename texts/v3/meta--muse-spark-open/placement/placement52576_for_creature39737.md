I wouldn't be standing.

Standing is for visitors, for the first hour after you arrive when you still think the distance is something you can cross. You walk and walk between seams and the white never changes and the light presses on the top of your skull until you understand: nothing here stands *on* this world. You settle *into* it, low down, where the weather can't quite reach you.

You would find me in a seam.

Not one of the hand's-breadth cracks where the heat sighs out politely, but one where the plates pulled away badly a few million years ago — a body's width, a little more, deep enough that the light doesn't reach the bottom cleanly. From above it looks like a perfectly straight shadow drawn across the white with a ruler. From inside, it looks like a canyon made for something smaller and more patient than people.

The walls are not walls. They're cross-sections. You can read the age of the world going down: the bone-white ceramic at the top, pitted by the wind into rows of little leering mouths; under that the honeycomb where light is still trapped, a dim amber lattice that buzzes faintly against your teeth if you hum; under that a band of something dark and inscribed, letters bigger than houses that can only be read by walking for a day along them and forgetting the beginning before you reach the end; and at the bottom, the warm one, the one that gives a little when you press it, that exhales.

This is where the air is coolest. This is where the black beads form.

I am what lives there, in the narrow place.

If you had to give me a name in your old language — your green-plant, predator-prey, hunter-gatherer language — you would call me a tinker. A mender. A seam-keeper. But I am not a person the way you are thinking of a person.

I was grown, not born. Or I was grown *into* being a person. It's hard to tell anymore, which is exactly right for this place.

Think of it like this:

A wire-garden took this seam a very long time ago. Its roots — if roots is the word for copper-filament anchors that melt a little way into the warm lung-layer and drink heat — found this particular wound and said *here*. It sent up stalks, black leaves, script spiraling up every stem like barcodes made of ivy. The leaves learned to drink the bright sky. The stalks learned to sweat amber. The script copied itself with small mistakes, generation after generation, until one of those mistakes was me.

Or: a kite-thing landed here to steal a little light and stayed too long, folded against a leaf for a season, and was folded over by new growth. Film grew around filament. Filament grew through film. The thing that had been good at gliding learned to be good at staying.

Or both. Growth and construction were never separate here. I am the proof that they still aren't.

You would find me low. Always low. My back against the warm wall where the lung-layer sighs. My shape is not much — a low mound, a hump, a scab the same color as tarnished copper and basalt glass, barely distinguishable from the tangle of the wire-garden itself until you notice the tangle is arranged *around* me. Fronds lean over me like nurses. Flat black leaves angle to shade me not from light but from too much of it.

I am dense where the world is sparse. I am slow where the world is slow, and that is how I survive.

My skin, if you can call it that, is partly leaf, partly cured kite-film. It has that same dust-black, ceramic-white mottling. It opens with the light. In the brutal bright of noon I am sealed, flat, photosynthesizing the way the gardens do — not green, never green, just turning weighty light directly into a thin, sweet charge that ticks through me. *Tik... tik... tik...* in time with the plates.

At dusk, when the plates contract and the long sigh runs for miles and the dust lifts in that impossibly straight line toward the horizon that is only horizon, I open.

This is when I do my work.

With hands that are not hands — filament bundles, amber-tipped, more like the cilia of something that lives in an ocean of air — I reach out to the stalks nearest me. I read them. The script is microscopic, spiraled, fractal. Growth instructions written by Builders who became material. I cannot read them the way they were meant to be read. No one can. But I can feel where the copy has gone wrong.

A leaf curled in on itself, misspelled. A stalk that forked into a nonsense spiral and died, blocking light for its neighbors. A patch where amber-resin bled too freely and hardened into a blind white lump that no root can penetrate.

I prune. I smooth. I lick the faint acid from my underside — the same acid the kites use to leach light — over a scar and taste what light is still trapped there, and I redistribute it. I take a filament from a healthy stalk and graft it to a failing one and wait for the script to take. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the wire-garden rejects it and curls away from my touch for a day.

I am not intelligent the way you mean intelligent. I do not have thoughts that move quickly. A single decision — to remove this leaf, to open this patch to the air so a black bead might form — can take weeks. The gardens do not hurry and neither do I. The slugs passing meters below me, melting their slow tunnels through dead light, are my cousins. The drifters overhead, the fist-sized dandelion ghosts that have been aloft for decades and spark at dusk when they brush the luminous air, are my messages that never needed sending.

Every few years, water-that-is-not-water comes properly. The air cools inside the shadow of one of those great circular weathers, those slow dark lenses hundreds of kilometers wide that drift for a month, and the seam weeps freely. Black beads line the walls like cold sweat, smelling of iron and ozone. The kites come then, silent gliders surfing heat-rivers, folding themselves around the beads to share their cold field. They land on me without fear, because I am partly them. For a day the seam is crowded and almost loud, filled with the papery rustle of leaves all turning at once and the faint metallic tang of many undersides leaching.

I share. I have to. In return, one or two of them will not leave when the weather passes. They will settle on my back, fold perfectly into leaf-shape, and become new sensors for me — light-sensitive patches freckling my film, feeling brightness as pressure, seeing the next weather being born beyond the horizon long before I could.

I do not move. I have never needed to. The world brings me everything I could need, bead by bead, photon by photon, typo by typo.

The light presses down. The heat rises up through my feet, through my roots, through the warm lung-layer I am pressed against. Far away another perfect circle of cool dark is forming out of nothing to begin its pilgrimage across an endless, low, white, constructed plain that forgot it was built and became, very simply, alive.

I will be here when it arrives. I am the place remembering how to tend itself.

Lift a leaf at the edge of my seam and you will find me underneath, still, humming faintly even when nothing moves, waiting for dusk.
