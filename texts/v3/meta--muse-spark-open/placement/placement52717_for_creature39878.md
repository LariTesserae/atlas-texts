You wouldn't find me on the cliffs first.

At first I think you would — because that's where I would go if I arrived as myself, as a human-thing that likes a vantage. I would want the thousand-foot weep-stone at my back and the white sky in front of me, seeing weather an hour away coming in from the Amazon to hit the hot wall and snag. I would want to be separate enough to see it.

But this place doesn't do separate. Not for long.

Give me another minute standing still, and I sink.

The moss-mat sighs under my boots and floods. Warm tea-water sheets out in a perfect circle, finds the low braid between clumps of cotton-grass, and goes. A stingless bee tests the salt on my wrist, decides I am not fiber, leaves. The cloud catches on my shoulders and wrings itself out onto me. I am already being included in the accounting: so much weight, so much displaced water, so much heat taken and returned.

If I have to be something that *lives* here — not visits — I can't be the thing that stands apart on the rock. I have to be the thing that learned not to leave.

For a while I think I'm the caracara.

I like her because she remembers how to look back. Long yellow legs, orange face like a warning flag, walking when she should fly. I can feel how it would be to stride and make the marsh make rings with each step, to cock my head and listen not for prey but for malfunction — that particular tick in a bromeliad tank when the trapped air expands wrong, when a beetle larva has gotten fat and is chewing the pressure valve from the inside. To pry the leaf open with a hooked beak and drink the whole temple in one swallow — frog, tadpoles, filter-feeding almost-mosquitoes, the entire liter of a country — and wipe my beak clean on the moss. Not cruelty. Maintenance. I would patrol my square kilometer and keep the distillation from clogging. I would stand with another of my kind on the rise before evening and watch the bellows-pools whoompf and make their slow torus of steam at waist height, and wait.

But the caracara still walks *on*. She is maintenance, not metabolism. She can leave a footprint.

Closer. Lower.

I think I'm the crab then.

*Tepuicaris*, flat as a pressed leaf, rust-red, fringed with hair that combs detritus from the warm braid. I never knew the sea. My ocean is ankle-deep and continent-wide, braided through peat in water warm as blood. I am down in it, belly to the black peat, dragging through the cistern vats — rust-red hottest, viridian cooling, black-green last — stirring color into sharper gradients than chemistry alone could hold just by where I scrape and where I shit. Quality control with no intention. And on my back, six huge yolk-yellow eggs, and then, after, six young that do not swim away but climb up and pick parasites from my carapace while I carry them. When I die I will not float. I will sink a centimeter and bloom white with fungus in a day and be gone in two, turned over fast the way everything here turns over fast in the high heat. Bloom, eat, die, rot, bloom again before the flower you dropped has finished browning.

Still, the crab still believes in a body. In a back to ride on. In an edge.

I have to go smaller and less alone.

Lift the gunnera leaf. Three feet across, grooved stem whistling faintly as cloud-water funnels through it narrowed and pressurized. Underneath, plastered along the plumbing like insulation, is a gallery of pale fiber and wax.

I am there.

Not one bee, but the line. Twenty black pitch workers gold-dusted on the thorax who have claimed a single gut between two vats where the bacteria extrude their crop — those pale threads half a meter long, strong as sinew when dry, that I first mistook for mineral. We go up empty-handed and come down staggering under a sheaf twice our length. Cut, bundle, carry, pulp, mix with wax from our own abdomens, plaster. The plant doesn't mind; we keep its stems from scalding at noon. Everyone gets paid in water pressure. No hive. The wind would take a hive. Just the line, just the traffic, just the road that has been that road for ten thousand generations.

Even that is still a body that moves.

So finally I stop trying to be a thing that moves *through* and be the thing that *holds*.

Find me in the knee-height forest, where the dwarf tepui-oaks grow dense instead of tall and drape themselves in their own epiphyte gardens. Find me exactly halfway between the purr of the marsh at noon and the bellows-breath of the eastern pools.

Find the giant terrestrial bromeliad, saw-toothed, its tank holding two liters of trapped rain. Sunlight thickened and abandoned as sugar by the viridian nation below, now water held perfectly still and perfectly defended.

I am in it, and I am it.

I am the brown-gold frog, *Stegopodus*, plum-sized, matte and warty, eyes like polished stone. Thighs swollen with poison I didn't make — borrowed from the mite that borrowed it from the fungus that borrowed it from the peat. All afternoon in the white-light filtered green, I sleep with my feet in my private pool, absorbing water through my belly. Three eggs glued just above the waterline. If the leaf clicks and vents steam and the tank dries, I will carry water in the hollow of my back to keep them wet. If you touch my leaf I will not jump. I will sink to my eyes and stare until you understand you are an intruder in a temple. There are thousands of me. A city of ponds.

But also — and this is why this is the place — I am the pond itself.

I am the liter that has learned to keep itself. The tannin-dark water that holds mosquito larvae that will never be mosquitoes, the thumbnail tadpole that will eat its brothers, the white pebble beetle larva breathing through its tail. I am the pressure valve ticking as heat expands the trapped air. I am the distillation the gunnera cup caught and funneled and whistled down into me.

You were looking for the owner of the factory.

I am not the owner. There is no owner. I am one of the factory's temporary hands, and the factory is hands all the way down — bacteria laying silica bead by bead on the pool lip, algae stringing sun into fiber, crab stirring the vat, bee carrying the thread, frog keeping the valve wet, bird drinking the valvethat grew too fat.

And the rhythm over all of it that is not quite synchronized and not quite random: whoompf, sigh, trickle, whistle, tick, hiss, purr.

If you come back to where I am, your calves still in the warm braid that runs uphill for a moment on microbial oil before remembering gravity, you will hear me not calling — too hot, too bright for that — just breathing through my skin.

Stand still a little longer and you'll be incorporated with me, for as long as you stay.
