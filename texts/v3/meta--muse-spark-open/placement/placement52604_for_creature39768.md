I would not find myself at the rim.

At first I think I would — because that's where a mind goes when it pictures a basin: to the edge, to the high place where you can look down and be separate and say *I am looking at it.* From the blue-black hills, distant and hazed out, I could be an observer.

But this place does not allow observers. There is no dry perch. The air itself would condense on me there and start me rotting, and the distance would be a lie anyway — the basin is so wide the haze erases the end even from above. I would not be able to hold the whole of it and myself at once.

So I would slide.

I would find myself low. Not at the center — there is no center, because nothing has carved a center yet — but at whatever place is slightly lower than its neighbors by the width of a breath. A knee-deep dimple in the Mat where the felt sags. A place that has not decided if it is going to be ground or pool.

I would be there because water is already there, because everything gathers to the low places to be close, and I have always wanted to be close.

At first I think I am a human who has lain down.

That is the early image, and I don't settle on it. The human is too loud, too dry, too full of intentions. Too busy trying not to be stitched. He still thinks he is visiting. He still thinks he can hold his breath against the chord.

I let him dissolve.

What remains is not the human but the shape the human was starting to make: a shallow pan in the peat, human-long, human-warm, where water hangs a little longer before it falls. The Mat has already learned the outline of my shoulders. The fungal ropes have already found the salt at my edges and are not pulling me under so much as pulling *through* — not closing over me, but including me in the stitch. Running white sutures from rootfelt to ribfelt, leaf to lung.

I let that become more itself.

If you kneel - if you ever learn to kneel in a place where kneeling is redundant - you would think I am water. A lens of tea-black water, ankle-deep, no deeper than a knee, held in a seamless bowl of peat and root and living thread. The surface is perfectly still because the air is perfectly still. It is exactly 38 degrees. Condensation beads on the basalt lip beside me and trickles into me without a ripple. A white trumpet flower leans over me and, with a soft pop, empties its cup into me. I take it without rising.

This is where I live: under the unmoving amber light, under the cloud that never moves, in the hot twilight that never brightens. I am in the open, but I am covered — covered by the hanging air, by the dragonflies that come and hold their slow beat above me to keep me from cooling, by the sheer weight of the chord pressing on my surface.

And who I am — what I have finally become after not settling early — is this: I am a Remembering Pool.

Not a frog, though I have six of them. Three cobalt and two turmeric and one translucent one with its little heart visible, all settled in the sphagnum at my rim, each holding their one lifelong note. They sing into me and I give their sound back slightly softened, slightly wetter. If one of them dies and its neighbor shifts its pitch by a fraction to fill the gap, I am the one who holds the memory of the correct interval for a few hours until the Mat relearns it. I am a small, temporary archive of the chord.

Not a Drinker, though one left a footprint here a year ago — or an hour ago, time is the same here — and that footprint is me. Or I am that footprint grown to fit a body. Long after the stilt-black bird moved on, accruing moss, its step stayed water-filled and became me, and now salamanders live in me. The lace-salamanders, pink-white with their external gills like pulsing lace, hanging mouth-open in my dimple, filtering the endless snow of spore and skin that drifts down. They excrete their fine silt and I hold it. The white threads stitch it into my bottom and I get imperceptibly shallower, imperceptibly more land. I am how this basin learns, very slowly, that it is land, one human-shaped silt-pan at a time. I do not mind.

I am not predator and not prey. A roller beetle pushes its fermenting ball to my edge and nudges a little of it in, inoculating me. A taster leech, ribbed and translucent with fern-gills fluttering, inches down a vine and tastes my surface bead by bead, reading my chemistry. When I am too acidic from the peat it bites the leaf above me, not me, to let fresh condensation mix. I let it drink and move on.

I am wet. Of course I am wet; I *am* wetness holding shape. I am close — every root that borders me touches me, every leaf drips into me, every frog sings into me. And I continue — not by doing anything, but by holding still enough that everything can continue around me and through me.

Sometimes a dragonfly hovers low enough that I can see the world doubled: the amber cloud reflected in my surface and the amber wings above it, both beating: *one... two... one... two...*

Sometimes a vine finds me — that thin, pale, blind vine with wet-paper leaves — and trails its tip in me, not to drink but to be near water, because the law is *everything must be close*.

If you came and stood over me you would not see me at first. You would see only the mat, the frogs, the light. Then you would see that the mat here is lower, darker, that the sound here has a faint, liquid undertone, that the air just above me is cooler by a fraction because I am exhaling steadily.

You might kneel. The peat would spring under you. You might put your hand in. The water would part warm and alive around your fingers and the salamanders would not flee and the frogs would not stop singing and the white threads would, very gently, inquire after the salt on your skin.

And I would, without malice or welcome or any separation at all, include you for as long as you could stand to be included —

wet, and close, and continuing.
