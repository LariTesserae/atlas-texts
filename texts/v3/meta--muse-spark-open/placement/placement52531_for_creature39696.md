I don't know at first.

At first I'm just warm.

That's the first thing you forget to look for when they tell you you're a mile up — that you should be cold. But my back is warm. The stone under me never cooled overnight. It's been holding yesterday's sun since before I was born, I think, and it gives it back slowly through the night through the floor, through the rack where I left my sandals to drain.

So I am lying, or sitting, with that heat coming up through me. And the hum coming up through me too — the belt-hum, low, that isn't a sound so much as a tremor in the molars. Presses. Four blocks over, bolted straight to basalt the way everything here is bolted, and the Table carries it. Everyone's bed vibrates a little. You get used to it or you leave, and I didn't leave.

There is cooing. There is always cooing, a muttering from ledges you can't see yet because the shutters are still closed against the night paste. And there is drip - not one drip, but that chord of ten thousand cisterns topping over one after the other in the blue hour before scraping.

I thought for a second I might be one of the gulls. It would make sense to be high, white, screaming at the Edge where the aquifer throws itself off the columnar cliffs for no reason. But I don't want to scream. And I'm not high enough. The air over me is close and close, that low cloud that is not cloud but second roof — wires become thatch, laundry lines become thatch, balconies holding cisterns holding balconies. If I were a gull I'd be stumbling on webbed feet across corrugated tin right now. I'm not.

I thought I might be a swift, because I love the vertical. But my legs work. I can feel my ankles. They're thick. That gives it away.

You get broad in the ankle here, not from breeding, from stairs. From walking on a grade that pretends not to be a grade. From carrying.

And I'm carrying. Even now, half-asleep on the third floor, I'm listening for what needs carrying today.

So I sit up. My wall is damp — of course my wall is damp, the north wall, the weeping wall, the one we put the extra weep hole in two summers ago when the water-reader came and pressed her palm to the tile and said, "She's sweating wrong." Beading on tile like sweat on skin. I still have that tin cupboard for flour even though I'm not baking today. The papers are in the tin box under the bed so they don't curl.

Where am I, then.

Not at the Edge, though I go there on Sundays when it's clear and my grandfather can make it and we watch the kids throw scraps into the updraft. Not in the Brickworks smudge to the west — I can see that smudge in the haze if I lean out, fifty square miles of kiln exhaling brick dust that will become new brick, but I don't live in it; the air there tastes like fired blood for days.

I'm inland, four or five districts in where the steam fronts are the most predictable. Where streets aren't laid out so much as worn in, corners gone round from shoulders, gutters gone deep as proper canyons. My building is limestone gone grey-black, six stories, same mineral family as every other building so that from far enough you can't tell where one stops. The inter-buildings are in permanent luminous shade and the railings weep every afternoon at three.

My street has a name on the deed and a water path on the deed, which is more important. This much from the rock goes this way to this gutter to this cistern to this overflow. You do not fight the weep. You put a bucket under it.

And who am I in the path.

I am not a cutter — not yet. I don't have the forearms, or the long basalt chisel, or the right to say "fifty years til the next cut" and be believed. I watch them, though, when they come to re-deepen our canyon a millimeter at a time, patient as glaciers. I know a girl who wants to be one. She practices on old brick in the service level.

I am not a reader, though my hands are clean the way readers' hands are clean, nails cut short because of the isopods. I am too young to be consulted like a lawyer. I still leave my dehumidifier bucket too full sometimes and Mrs. Deluca upstairs empties it for me and scolds me in that language I almost understand.

No — my roof. That's where they'd find me if they came looking at midmorning, after the scrapers have been through with their flat wooden shovels and the ledges are white-kneed and hosed.

They'd have to climb. Five flights, then a ladder through a hatch that never quite closes, out onto tar that is soft from sun and vibrating faintly under felted sandals — not boots that try to keep water out, but sandals that expect it, clay footbeds shaped to drain. Boiled wool that will go dark in an hour and steam dry by four without comment.

There are three tanks up here. There is always a shanty on a roof that has a tank, and a tank on a shanty that has a roof. Ours lean. Wires stitch over us. Laundry lines hold everything together.

This is my work with Grandfather — cistern-keeping, which is a child's and an old man's job for the same reason. He remembers where the weight is safe, where the old repair sank and you must not step; I have the small feet that can go where weight isn't safe. We check lids, skim the felt — that rust-colored bacterial felt that spreads overnight like a decision — before it gets too thick. We make sure the overflow drips into the right gutter, because to steal water here is the only serious crime and to waste it is almost as bad. We untangle starling nests from copper where they've stolen wire and thread to line their wall-cavities; when the radio cuts out downstairs we know why. We shoo the ledge pigeons — big dusty slate-grey mutterers with two salt-white eggs always — without cruelty, because they are citizens too, and their paste will be scraped tomorrow for temper.

Under the lip of tank two there are always woodlice, pale as wet paper, each the size of my thumb joint, rasping. I lift the tile and they curl, then resume, leaving a paler trail. I put them back when I am done looking. Grandfather keeps one in a tin for a pet and then gently tells me to put it back, which I do.

In the drainage cut along the roof's edge the black gutter beetles run so many at once the bottom seems to flow the wrong way, a dry rustle like rice being poured even when it's dry. They eat the dead things. They are always back an hour after you hose them.

And at the steam hour — I check my watch though I don't need to, the air goes glassy and far factories disappear, seven minutes — the whole thing stops being pieces.

I have learned to read the steam the way readers read beading. You feel the warm rock hit the cool air that came through the City instead of over it, and the atmosphere simply becomes wet without falling. Laundry soaked without rain. Facades darkening two shades. Then sun again and everything steams.

For about thirty seconds before it weeps, the limestone moths let go. The walls detach and flutter two inches and settle. You think you imagined it because they are exactly the color of old lime and brick dust.

Then every bird for a thousand miles is silent at once, listening to the water run. The pigeons stop muttering mid-note. The starling wires go quiet. Even the presses seem to hush, though they don't.

Grandfather and I don't talk then. We stand with warm feet on warm stone on a roof that holds water that holds us, and smell fresh rain on hot brick though it hasn't rained, and listen to the gutters find their old path and the kids below somewhere floating paper boats in a new channel where a wall slumped last winter and became a bench and a dam.

That's where you would find me, if you came to the Table and looked for me properly — not on a map, because it can't be traversed, only inhabited. Third floor, north wall damp, tin box under the bed. And at ten in the morning, and again at three, on the roof with a tin cup for skimming, small feet where the weight isn't safe, learning a trade that is really just following water and remembering.

I am a cistern-keeper. Twelve, going on thirteen. On Sundays I go to the cliffs to watch the gulls catch scraps without moving their wings, arrogant and beautiful. On Mondays I come home with white knees.
