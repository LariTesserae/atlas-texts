You would not be lost.

You would be *found* — by the place itself, which is very good at finding small things and keeping them.

First, where:

At the bottom in all the ways that matter. Low latitude and low altitude and low relief, on ground so old it has stopped trying to be ground and has become instead a kind of thickened water. A craton plain. An etchplain. A peneplain polished down to its saprolite.

If you want a name on a map, put your finger on the top end of Australia and then slide it until the contour lines give up. The Gulf Lowlands. The Arafura Swamp. The coastal sheet behind Kakadu and Arnhem Land. Cape York in the wet after the roads close. Anywhere the Australian shield lies open-palmed to the sky and forgets to drain. It has other cousins — the Pantanal, the Sudd, the wallum of the deep south — but this one has melaleucas and magpie-geese and freshwater crocodiles to sign its name.

It is not a swamp the way people imagine swamps, all choked and jungle-closed. It is an *open* swamp. A savanna-swamp. A wet desert made of poor water. The continent here hasn't lifted or folded or burned in a hundred million years, so everything soluble has long since been leached out. The rock underneath — sandstone, granite — has rotted in place into kaolin and bleached sand and laterite marbles, giving nothing back. That's why the trees don't close over. The water is everywhere but the food is nowhere, so each life takes its generous, polite allotment of space and stays there. An inch of rise is a watershed. A crab mound is a mountain range.

It is country that the Bininj and Yolŋu know as a belly, a lung, a season — not empty, but *thinly full*. Not unused, but un-built-upon. The kind of flat that teaches you how to listen, because you can hear a brolga call from three miles away and there is nothing to stop it.

You asked who you would be in it.

I sat with that for a while, because this place doesn't let you be just one thing right away.

At first I thought: a white-faced heron.

You know how to be that. You know how to stand. For an hour you are a dead branch with one leg in tea-water, your shadow a needle on the peat. You don't stalk so much as you *cease*, until the plain forgets you are an animal and starts moving around you again — a gudgeon flicks, a dragonfly passes. Then your neck unfolds, too fast to see, and you have swallowed. Then you cease again. You are solitary not because you are lonely but because the pan cannot feed two. A half-mile away, another heron is doing exactly the same thing, and you will hold that distance all day without ever discussing it.

Then that felt too much like waiting with a weapon, and the plain isn't only that.

So I thought: a tussock.

A pedestal of sedge and peat that you have built yourself by dying a little every year. Your dead leaves don't rot, they soak and compress into more of you, a millimeter a year, lifting your crown four inches above the sheet-water — just enough to be dry at the base. Frogs live in your armpits. A sundew lives on your shoulder. A paperbark seedling once tried to grow out of your head and you outlasted it. You don't move and you don't need to. You *are* the topography. You map the water by being slightly less of it.

That felt too still.

So I thought: water that beads out of stone.

There is a whaleback of sandstone no higher than your chest, bald and lichen-black, that has been in the same place since the continent had other neighbors. It is not a spring, there's no aquifer feeding it. It is the rock itself weeping — deep-weathered saprolite exhaling its held humidity, particle by particle, even days after rain. You would be that seep: clear, cool, immediate, running in a dark trickle down polished stone to be swallowed instantly by peat. A life of two feet before you disappear, and yet you never stop. You are the proof that even stone here has become a kind of slow sponge.

That felt too brief.

What finally stayed, as the sound of the place came back — that layered, electric drone of cicadas under the bottle-knock of frogs under the far trumpet of cranes — was this:

You would be a patrolling dragonfly.

Not the swarming kind. The plain doesn't swarm. You would be one of the huge, amber-winged ones that owns a beat.

You beat so slowly you can be seen through. You move at the speed of the wind-wave through the reeds, which is to say, you take minutes to cross a pan that a man could wade in thirty seconds. You are warm-blooded in a cold-blooded way, lifting your abdomen to dump heat like a heron lifts its wing. Below you, every puddle is flickering with fish, every tussock is calling with frogs, every floating mat of fern is hiding a turtle eye. You don't have to choose. You hawk what rises. A midge out of the humming air. A mosquito off a lily pad.

Your larvae has been down in the peat-water for months already, masked, jetting forward by spitting water out of itself. You will only be up here for weeks. So you don't hurry.

Your territory is a long oval over open water between two melaleucas, and you turn at each end in the same place every time, as if there were corners in the air. From up there you can see how the plain works — the four-inch rises holding the paperbarks, the tea-dark braids holding the lilies, the crab levees making a lace of dry inch-high roads, the crocodile's three-seed shadow holding everyone else honest.

You are not the top predator and not the bottom. You are the middle layer that can see both. You are loud when you fly — a dry rustle like paper — and completely silent when you hover.

And at dusk, when the white sun finally thins and the humidity condenses and the fireflies start to blink out of phase across miles of sedge, you would find a reed stem and hang there, wings forward, letting your blood cool.

Below you, the plain would exhale. The water would keep creeping at less than walking pace around sedge hummocks, losing itself to air. The frogs would stitch their circle back together around you. The cranes would call one last time and the sound would travel to the horizon without hitting anything.

You would hold on with six small feet.

You would not need to be anywhere else.
