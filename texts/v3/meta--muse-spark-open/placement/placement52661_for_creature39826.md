I wouldn't find myself on the ridge.

At first I thought I would — because that's where you go to understand a place like this. Up where the condensate sails fan out like the wings of something that tried to leave and thought better of it, trembling in the wind that never stops combing over the whalebacks. Where you can see the whole negotiation at once: the scoops and bowls of the old range holding the city like water in cupped hands. Where the light is thinnest.

But I wouldn't stay there long. The air is too thin to think in, and the ridge is for the dead and for catching weather. The turf-walled ossuaries are up there, and the names exfoliating off slate one letter at a winter. I'm not done being used yet.

So my feet take me down. Down is where everything in this world goes eventually — not falling, but settling, wearing, tilting very slightly in the direction the earth decided on forever. Down the stairs that are not stairs but old drainage lines paved over, then paved over again. Down past a wall that is half lichen-mortar and half lichen by its own ambition, rust-red and sulfur-yellow, breathing faintly. Down where the sound gets even smaller and the moss gets even thicker.

You'd find me in the third basin from the windward edge. Not the deepest one, where the cloud pools and sits all morning like a second, lower sky, and not the high one where the wind keeps everything scoured to slate. The middle one. The working one.

Where three narrow aqueducts meet above an alley and argue quietly about who gets to turn which wheel. My house — my block — is one of those hills that forgot how to be a house or remembered how to be a hill again. From the street side it looks like a terrace of workshops, iron sheeting gone green-black under its felt of cultivated moss. From the roof side it looks like pasture, because it is pasture: six pale roof-goats sleeping huddled against each other in the damp, their breath making little ghosts that vanish before they rise, their mossy-slipper feet silent on the turf that is also our insulation, our cistern lid, our garden. You get into my place through a doorway that is lower than it should be because the moss has grown over the lintel again. You duck. Everyone ducks here.

And who would I be? 

For a while I thought maybe the crow. The huge slate-colored one with white bars on its primaries that doesn't caw but knocks — *tok-tok* — like a loose gear. To be the inspector, the one who glides between terraces and watches without being watched back, who is not pet or pest or prey but a kind of agreement. That appealed to me. To be quiet and useful and a little apart.

But I am not apart. Nothing is apart here. The rule won't allow it: nothing is new and nothing is wasted, and that includes distance.

No, I am lower than that. Closer to the ground. Closer to the green that eats noise.

I am a minder. A tender. We all are, but this is my specific tending: I keep the water moving through the walls.

Not the great traps on the ridges — other hands, broader shoulders than mine, keep those sails. Mine is the intimate water. The condensate that beads overnight on every cold surface and has to be coaxed, scraped, channeled before it decides to become rot instead of power. My mornings start before the dim gets any less dim, with a reed scraper and a bucket. I clear the channels behind the moss-mats in the wall cavities. I harvest what the wall has grown — a cultivar that fruits with little red pinheads after drizzle — and press half of it into cakes for the goats and leave half to keep holding heat. My fingernails are green-black at the cuticles and will not come clean. I have stopped trying.

Below the kitchen, two small chattering water wheels turn in the alley-wall, fast and well-oiled. They are older than my grandmother and younger than the slate they are bolted into. They run the lathe and the wire-braider. I mind them the way you mind a child or an old dog: by listening. When the cloud thickens, they slow, and the amber bulbs strung in braids overhead dim to a sullen glow, and the whirr becomes a sigh, and I know to stop braiding and go prune the ferns instead, because their spores will be up soon and I will need my goggles down. When the cloud thins, the wheels chatter faster, the bulbs brighten, and I can solder again.

My workshop has a rowan tree in it. Or my tree has a workshop around it. It came up through a crack where an oil line used to run warm, and now its bark has grown around a gear that no longer turns, and its roots follow the old warmth down into the slate. I oil the bark when I oil the gears. The leaves have a faint sheen. In late summer when the basin fills with that cinnamon-colored spore haze, I keep my mouth closed and watch how the tree filters it, how patient it is.

People come close to talk to me, because you have to. They put a hand on my forearm, forehead almost touching mine under the moss-eave while the drizzle frets down. We don't waste breath. A neighbor brings me a bundle of fern-fiber to card. I give her a length of fresh braid and a cup of thin sweet milk still warm from the byre under the floor, where the whole house hums. A child — legs already thick with stairs, hair damp and never quite drying — kneels beside me with a little hooked knife and learns how much moss to shave from the roof-street so it doesn't get too heavy and collapse the room where we sleep. Not too much. Not too little. The negotiation.

This is why I would be here, in this body: short and broad-chested, shoulders set forward from carrying, skin pale except for chapped knuckles, eyes dark and narrowed against spore-mist, head cropped to stubble because long hair here is just another place for something else to grow. Dressed in five bad coats that are also good coats — wool felt, fern-fiber, oilskin from a dead sail — overlapping like shingles. Goggles pushed up on my forehead, waiting.

I am not tall. Nothing vertical lasts long here except tired cliffs. I am low, dense, layered, opportunistic, patient. When I sleep, the moss grows a fraction over my doorsill. When I wake in the shadowless morning that is always late-afternoon, I scrape it clear again, without resentment. I wring the cloth out into the cistern. I listen to the wheels. I keep the water moving to the wheels and the green from drowning me.

I have decided, collectively with everyone else, to stay.
