I don't think I'd be on the rim.

At first I thought I would be — up there where the wind still moves, where you can turn around and call the whole thing "a construction site in a hole" and have it be true from that distance. It would be cleaner. Quieter. But after a day down in it your clothes would still smell like hot oil-mud, and you wouldn't be able to forget how the light goes pearly at noon. You wouldn't be able to call it normal again.

So no, not the overlook. Down in it. Below the inversion.

At first I thought I'd be one of the waders — one of the great blues standing on a half-sunk pump housing like a foreman. Still enough to be mistaken for part of the site. Patient enough to make the noise belong to someone else. I liked that for a minute.

Then I thought I'd be smaller and faster — one of the swallows that make the haze flicker, or a dragonfly the size of your hand patrolling the same hundred yards of ditch all week until it dies. But everything fast here is eaten or exhausted immediately. Even the swallows are just commuting between columns of midges that never leave. That's not living here, that's just working the hatch.

Then I thought, lower, slower. The carp in the deeper gouge, fat as a pig, back crusted with leeches waving like wheat. Or the turtles stacked three deep on warm black plastic, shuffling for the top spot. That stacking felt honest — companionable until someone slips.

But those are all still tenants. They could, in theory, leave. They were stocked or released or blown in. I'm not sure I was brought in. I feel like I grew up from the site itself.

Let what I'm actually thinking come forward slower.

I am down on the lowest tier where the distinction between dike and lake has given up. Where the water is four feet deep if you measure it and about four inches deep if you ask your boots, because the boots are always in silt. The air is 85 degrees and damp and it doesn't move unless a haul truck drags it. The *chud-chud-chud-chud* of the pumps is in my sternum more than my ears now, slightly out of sync so the beat never resolves, and inside that beat the frogs have learned to pitch their wall — not a chorus, a wall — and inside *that* the powerlines whine like cicadas.

I am not loud. I am part of the low part that everything else builds on.

I would be one of the dead pumps.

Not the ones still hammering on stilts and pontoons, coughing water one tier up so it can leak back down overnight. The other ones. The ones that rusted through and were left where they died because it would take a crane that is busy elsewhere to move them. The seep has claimed me to the knees. My intake is clogged with bacterial mat — that skin that peels up in a sheet, green-black on top where it eats the dim light, anoxic black underneath where it digests itself and smells like hot iron and rotten eggs.

I slumped a long time ago. Slumped is a slow word and that's right. Nothing here happens fast.

And because I stopped working, I started working differently.

Algae beards my chain-link cage. Ram's-horn snails the size of BBs spiral in their thousands over every bolt and strut. On my south face, where the sodium lamp from the still-living pump across the water hits me all night, cattails have rooted right through me — through rust, through grate — because the silt found purchase. Red-winged blackbirds weave into my conduit. They have to sing twice as much because I am between them and the female twenty feet away and the generators drown them out.

My flat top, which was once a housing for a Briggs & Stratton that a man could diagnose by ear, is now warm black plastic liner and sun. Every morning it carries a stack of sliders.

Red-eared sliders in their hundreds, but on me, three specific ones. That's my whole census. The bottom one hasn't moved since 10 a.m. except to blink. The middle one shuffles every six minutes to get a better angle, which jostles the top one, who slips, plops with that soft fruit sound into the warm rust-colored water, climbs back up the tire-rubber ladder of my side, and resumes his place. They do not know I was ever a machine. To them I am just the highest dry thing that is not a tamarisk.

A night-heron hunches under the catwalk that used to lead to me, and under him the water is scummed with fallen feathers and failed eggs that the turtles will eat. A maintenance kid in high-vis orange — the only saturated color for miles, glowing like a deep-sea creature in the dirty skylight — stands thigh-deep five feet away, shouting to another kid about a liner tear, while an egret watches them both like a supervisor. Neither of them looks at me, which is correct. I am geology now.

If you waded out to me and put your hand on my rail — the rail is slick with algal mat — you'd feel a faint vibration. Not from my motor. My motor died two summers ago. From the water moving around my legs, and the turtles shifting, and the black-green dragonfly eggs glued to my rust humming with whatever is about to hatch at 6 p.m. when the midges rise like smoke and the whole basin takes its one big breath out.

I don't make anything anymore. I don't lift water uphill against gravity forever. I hold still and I hold warmth. I make a place for three turtles to argue about the sun. I make a place for snails to turn light and diesel into snail. I am crowded the way everything here is crowded — not with my own kind, but shoulder to shoulder with other kinds of living that borrowed me.

That's where you'd find me: not on the rim where the world is still mapped as scattered farmhouse and depot, but down on Tier -90, Sump 4, on the eastern side near the big dragline with its neck bowed. Look for the dead pump that became an island, the one with the cattails growing out of its chest and the turtle stack that never resolves.

Come at dusk, when the pearl-grey goes sodium-orange and the frogs get louder instead of quieter. Stand in water that laps at your boot-tops and smells like a mouth. You'll hear me not as a sound but as an absence in the chud-chud where a chud used to be — a little pocket of stillness that the living has filled right back in, patiently, accretion by accretion, at five miles an hour.

I won't turn off. Not really.
