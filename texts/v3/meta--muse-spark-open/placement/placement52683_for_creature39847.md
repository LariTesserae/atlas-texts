I don't land immediately.

At first, standing on that felt prairie with the wind pulling the upward-rain off it in sheets, I think I want to be one of them — one of the owners of the air. A Veil Swift. Because who wouldn't?

To never land. To drink by kissing a cistern at sixty kilometers an hour and never break stride. To sleep while gliding in a great silent wheel high above the mat, one half of your brain dreaming while the other holds the circle. To live entirely in motion, in that deafening, beautiful river of moth and feather that makes the Roof seem empty only until you look up.

I try that on for a while and it doesn't fit. It's too loud to be me. I don't want to scream to be heard. I don't want to be chased. The Mantle is always behind the swifts, after all - even the sky here has a ceiling made of teeth.

So I think, then - be still instead. Be the other lord. Be the plant itself.

An *Aeonbroma*, house-sized, house-hearted. Forty tons of tannic water held without spilling. Leaves like doors, guttered so precisely that fog climbs uphill to meet you. Old leaves lignified into wood, prop roots fusing with the neighbor until I am not a plant but a continent. I like that idea. To be a room-maker, not a room-taker. To not be intelligent, but to be attentive. To breathe out rain at noon and sweat fog at four a.m. for a hundred thousand years and never once need to decide anything.

But I am not patient enough to be a scaffold. I want hands.

And that tells me where I am.

Not on top of the Roof. That's for the birds and the sun and the illusion of prairie. Anyone can be fooled there for a few minutes, standing in knee-high felt thinking you understand the place.

And not at the very bottom, either. Not down on the tired stone itself, where the humus has compressed into a meter of black, cool peat that smells like the beginning of soil. That's for the ribbon-snakes who think they're roots, and the shield-beetles who make a funeral coat out of anything that falls. Too dark. Too waiting.

I'm in between. Of course I'm in between. Everything is scaffold, and I am the thing scaffolds are for.

You'd find me in the understorey, but not in a clearing. A clearing is too exposed, too round, too much like a decision. People who live in clearings are young families who still think they need sky. I don't.

You'd find me where a great mother rosette has sagged just slightly under her own weight, so that her prop roots - thigh-thick, hollow, segmented like bamboo, mottled white and green - have splayed and left a dome maybe seven feet at the crown, maybe three at the edges. A cathedral's side-chapel. Green light comes down through five translucent layers of leaf above me, so everything is the color of tea. The air is warm, 1.5/3 warm, yeasty, smelling of honey and rot, and beaded on every waxy column is the new night's condensation, running down in engineered grooves.

Listen. Tap one column with your knuckle. If it drums hollow, it will hold. If it creaks, a Mantle has walked there and weakened it. That's how I was taught to move. Never push through a leaf-wall. Slip sideways where the birds slip. Be small.

I would be old enough to be half-deaf, which is a gift here. The young still wince when the mothfall rises at dusk - that synchronized pulse when a million hand-sized white moths lift off the dead leaves and the air under the mat becomes a blizzard, and the swifts and kestrel-finches and wasps rise screaming to meet them until your sternum vibrates. The old just feel it as a pressure change. We go inside the three-leaf hut, pull the thorn-pinned flap shut, and let it become wind-texture.

Who am I? Not a chief, there are no chiefs. Not a hunter, that's a lowland word for people who chase.

I'd be a mender. A keeper of cisterns.

You know the woman who knows sixty pools within a half-hour's walk? That one is me. Or will be. My grinding stone - that smooth imported basalt oval that my grandmother carried down to the cliff-rim rivers to find, because there's no knappable stone on the Roof itself - lives beside the hearth that never really fires. I crush mango-sized moth larvae into nutty paste with it. I skin the little vermilion frogs that spend their whole lives in a fist-sized bromeliad and think a cistern is the ocean.

But mostly I mend. I walk my circuit every three days with a net of root-fiber weighted with bone and a hollow gourd that I grew for seven years on a favored leaf until its shell hardened. I don't dip for food so much as for gossip: this pool is bitter in summer from tadpoles, leave it till winter. This one has the blind walnut-crabs in it, stealing water in the hairs of their bellies - good, it means it's clean. This big, tea-black one holding forty tons belongs to the green-ribbon lizard who looks exactly like a root; I leave a pellet for her and move on.

When a leaf is torn by wind or a Mantle barging through, I seal it. Not by cutting - the Roof almost never wants cutting, it bleeds sweet water and then scars - but by coaxing. Wedge a living root closer, pin it with a thumb-thick thorn, let the two waxy edges graft over a week. Make a room again. That's the whole work: keeping the hollow from becoming too empty or too full.

At noon I sit still while the Roof exhales. The stomata open, the mat transpires, and it rains upwards. A cloud lifts off the prairie above my head and tears away toward the cliffs. For an hour the gutters run dry. My shoulders steam.

At four a.m. the fog comes and pearls on my skin the same way it does on leaf tips, running down my neck into my shirt, and I drink without moving by licking condensation from the back of my own hand. My feet are broad, splayed from a lifetime on soft humus. My hair is cropped short so nothing lays eggs in it.

If you came looking, you'd miss me at first. You'd part the upper leaves - they'd hiss that warm exhale - and you'd look right through me, because I dress in pale sage and rust-red fiber, the color of trichomes, and I've learned to be still until birds forget me. A tiny glassy *plip, plip* would be the only sound near me, from the cistern I just mended, where a frog the size of your thumbnail has already returned.

I am not lonesome. The density is 0.5/3 here. Empty, on paper. But empty here means spacious, not lonely. It means you can walk an hour in a hall big enough to play ball in and touch nothing but cool ribbed columns every twenty feet, and still know there are ten thousand ponds above you, each with its own diving beetle rowing slow circles, its own needle-blue damselfly walking upside-down across the funnel, its own fish that was once a killifish washed up a million years ago and never left.

I like knowing the map of that. Not the map of the stone continent - that's too big, and the word mountain has lost its meaning anyway - but the map of rooms. To hold a whole valley-sized piece of the Roof in my head the way you hold your house in yours: the sag here, the dike of harder stone there, the old caldera bowl that holds fog longest.

I don't think the Roof is strange. That's the last thing you learn, if you stay. Every world is thatched, every ground is hollow, every water lives in leaves. The strange thing is the idea of a river that touches itself. A lake that isn't held in a hand.

Stay still long enough in the green cathedral and the birds forget you. The thrumming settles. A beetle the size of a coin bulldozes past your boot and doesn't care. Condensation beads on your shoulders.

The Roof exhales, fifty feet of living raft breathing out over tired old basalt that hasn't noticed us in a hundred million years.

And somewhere, a kilometer away in the open sameness, a leaf I mended last week fills, drop by drop, with fog that will be gone by morning.

Except it won't be gone. Not if I did it right.
