I think at first I would be standing.

Not down in the basin where the water is, not up on the wall where the bodies make a second rock — at the lip, where you have to sidle and drop that meter into stillness. Where the wind outside dies because it has nothing to push against anymore.

That threshold. That is where I would find myself in this world.

Because I am not built for that density. I need a little scarcity around me to think. If I went straight down into the crowd — shoulder-to-shoulder, membrane-to-membrane, the whole wall breathing *shhh* every thirty seconds — I would be mapped and then un-mapped. The chorus would bend around me once, locate my shoulders and pack as a new stone, and then forget me. I would become environment too quickly. Condensation beading on my jacket, running down my neck in the same dark trickles as the rock. That cool that isn't killing-cold, just cellar-October, breath-visible, patient. I would be useful for a minute as warmth and then I would be in the way.

So I stay at the edge at first. Half in the jet, half in the pool. One hand still on the wind-scoured shoulder of the entrance, one boot already in the damp.

And from there, I watch what I would actually be.

Not one of them. Not quite.

If I were one of them — 90 grams, 18 centimeters of wet-slate and pale belly, huge lidless eyes like dewdrops about to fall — I would not know I was one of them. That's the point. There is no territory here, no queen, no name for the layer you're in. You cling to the back of the one in front of you, someone clings to yours, your patagium is always a little damp against someone else's flank. You breathe slow. You pipe without stopping. You wait for the trickle to shift a handspan left and the whole living shingle shifts with it and you never decide to move at all.

I would like that, I think, for about an hour. To be forgiven the need to be distinct. To be warm because everyone is warm. To scrape lichen with a flake held in both hands, draw it toward my chest — *scritch* — eat, drop it, never own it.

But I don't have their eyes. My eyes still try to find the sun. In that uniform dusk — that deep blue evening without a source, where the rock is dark amethyst-grey and the pools are mirrors that reflect nothing — I keep looking up for a brightness that never comes. Their eyes drink *this* light: the faint phosphor of lichen rims, the wet sheen on ten thousand membranes, the glitter when thousands turn at once. I would be blind with my kind of seeing.

So no. Not a glider. Close, but not.

I think — as I stand longer and my core temperature drops to theirs, as the piping lifts slightly to include the new obstacle that is me — I would be the next thing down. The thing that lets them be crowded at all.

I would be the water that doesn't go anywhere.

The borrowed water. The rain from yesterday still sitting in a pan no bigger than cupped hands, a finger deep, dark and still. The condensation beading on every sharp, young arris of rock — this rock that hasn't learned to be smooth yet, still glassy where it sheared — running down in dark trickles to gather. The paper of clear ice that skins the large pan overnight, waiting to be tapped.

Tap. Pause. Tap. Pause.

I would be that pause, actually. The gesture.

Watch that older one again, the one with lichen growing *on* its back until it isn't grey anymore but bearded black-green. It picks a chip, tests, discards, picks another. Holds it in both forelimbs like a person holding a small hammer. Tap in a slow circle, tick... tick... tick... until the ice stars white and gives. *Plip.* Drink. Wing-membranes spread to drink through the skin as well.

Primitive is not stupid. It is just enough, preserved not in the object but in the motion, passed by watching. The floor under the shallow water is littered with them — thousands of tiny, minimally flaked hammer-stones, each used once by something with no concept of ownership, only need. A museum of one second's ingenuity, repeated ten thousand times a day.

I would be a good stone for that. Not the hand, not the mouth that drinks, but the selected edge. The one that fits. Used once, well, and then left under an inch of dark water for the algae and midge larvae to settle on. No cache. No forging. Nothing built to last — that is the law of the young rock. Shelter is given or it doesn't exist.

To be held for exactly as long as I'm needed, and then to be discarded without rancor, among all the other good stones — that feels honest.

And from down there, under the water, looking up through that brief hole in the ice, I would finally see the place as it is: not a colony, but a climate. Fauna as weather. The low lid of cloud never quite clearing the ridge, the sheer walls on three sides still sharp enough to draw blood, the faint smell of stone-water and warm skin and lichen-metal. The whole wall pulsing, pale bellies rising at slightly different rates, pushing mist off the stone.

*Shh.*

If you stay too long, the text said, you would be covered. Lichen spores on skin, a few curious thumb-claws testing the edge of your boot for suitability.

I would stay just long enough to be covered, then step back toward the notch, toward the thin killing wind, and let the wall shiver once, resettle, and continue its single, dense, patient note without me.

So: on the eastern face, high where the real summit is still a white bulk lost in cloud, in a bite taken out of the mountain when it was still soft.

And what would I be? For a little while, an obstacle the chorus learned around. For a little longer, a tool that worked. And underneath both, the damp that made any of it possible.
