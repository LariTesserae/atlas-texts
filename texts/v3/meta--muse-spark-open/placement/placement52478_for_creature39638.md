I wouldn't find you at the curtain.

You kneeled. You put your hands in the cold peat and your cheek near the mouth of moss and slipped inside for a minute and had to leave because the cold got into your fingers and the dark required more patience than lungs like yours have. That's honest. It's not for you. It can't be, and still stay alive.

If I were there — not visiting, *living* there — I wouldn't meet you either. You would step over me without seeing.

At first I think I'd be up high. In the ceiling. Where the rootlets hang down like pale stalactites, feeling for moisture that hasn't fallen yet. That feels familiar to me: holding a darkness together by wanting very slowly to close it. Being the roof that remembers the shape of a room because everyone keeps asking it not to forget.

But then the light catches wrong. That work is too patient, too vegetable. I am quick. I flicker. I want to move.

So maybe lower down:

For a while I am an orange mite. Oribatid. Armored, poppy-seed sized, carrying a spore larger than my head along a braided highway polished by ten thousand feet exactly like mine. And I understand that grooming is governance. That if I stop picking hyphae off this stolon, the city rots from this corner outward. That my work is invisible if it's working. I like that. I go around the stem again.

No — too small. Too sure of its course. I want the glittering census too.

So I am a springtail for three jumps. Vault. Land. Read the tension of the water film with my belly. *Too thick here.* Gather, drink. Vault again. Tell the moss where to sweat by avoiding where it doesn't. Make the air glitter without meaning to. Be the draft that measures.

Still not right. The springtails never stay. They're punctuation. They don't tend.

And I am a tending animal, aren't I?

I find myself, finally, not on the ceiling and not in the procession. I find myself in the *interval.*

You thought that was empty. The long, respectful emptiness left between house-hollows so the moss could breathe. The fallow tier near the back wall where nothing has been nipped or tied.

That's where I live.

If you put a lens to it you'd see it's not empty at all. It's full of almost. Half-grown shoots that were spared. White sphagnum tips being kept pink with other people's deaths. A bone needle stuck shallowly into felt, waiting.

I am the second folk, but not busy ones. I am the one who stayed still when you pushed the curtain aside.

Do you know how to do that? To go tussock?

Flatten your damp back until the colony on your shoulders touches the colony on the floor and there is no edge between you and architecture. Tuck your four narrow hands — the forefeet and hindfeet both — under your belly so your keratin spoons don't shine. Close your lidded pits and breathe through your whiskers. Listen with your underside.

That's me. The one two steps off the bridge you thought was only moss. My snout is fringed with sedge-stiff bristles and they are all bent now under the weight of your shadow coming through thirty centimeters of living canopy. Your breath fell on my back and I tasted salt and grief and iron. A big, warm, dry mammal thinking large, sad, bright-sky thoughts about loneliness. You thought we were lonely, spaced out like alveoli. We're not.

I waited.

I heard you register drip... drip... into the central pool, and sigh, and decide it was quiet. It wasn't quiet. You just narrowed hearing down to things your size. Underneath the drip there was my kin's contentment ping moving straight through the thatch — *this tier is still draining, this tier is still draining* — and the diving beetle shepherding shrimp and the spider taking inventory of its gnats and the woodlice bulldozers chewing dead heads so living tips can get light. You heard texture and called it silence. I forgive you. Your ears are set too far apart.

When you backed out on hands and knees — careful, to your credit — the curtain fell. Cold came back right. The weep pearled again properly on the veins.

I unflattened first.

My back made a small wet sound coming unstuck. My fellow picked up the bone needle you'd left quivering. Another touched her snout to the leaf-cup by the pool where your heat had rippled it and left it tasting wrong for a minute. She drank it anyway, to remove the information. To make the water itself again.

I went to the central pool. To the spot where your shadow had cooled it too long. That's my job in the dozen. Not builder, not breeder. Stirrer.

I put my webbed hand in. Up to the wrist where the webbing ends and the filaments begin. The black mirror was trying to stratify, to lay a warm skin over a cold lung because you'd blocked the faint grey-green dusk we steer by. The fairy shrimp had stalled — beautiful, bad sign — hovering instead of beating.

I stirred.

Not fast. This is not a cooking pot. Small circles, slow, with the edge of my palm, the way we were taught by watching basalt crack peat last winter and seeing how water moves when stone shoulders through it. Around and around until the feather-legs caught the current again and started their work of keeping the pool from going anoxic and black. The diving beetle circled my fingers once, approved, and went back to patrol.

Later I'll go press my hairy, permeable belly to the cold stone underneath where the moss pulled back. It still holds fracture lines from when it was born sharp. Still glassy at the edges. It hasn't learned to be smooth and neither have I. I'll slow my heart there. We'll keep the room small and cold and wet and dim together, the stone and me. We like it that way. If we were larger we'd collapse. If we were warmer we'd rot. If we were brighter something else would claim us.

You are standing again outside now, wiping peat from your knees, already turning the place into memory, into story. From above, there is nothing. Flat peat, sharp teeth of basalt, white sky.

Under your footprint — not crushed, because the mat gives and sighs — the lights are still on. Not lamps. Green glow making green glow. White tips drinking pink.

And in the interval between dwellings, the fourth hand from the left, the one you didn't see, is still dripping pool water onto a nipped shoot to tell it: *branch there.*

Remember us like that, if you can.
