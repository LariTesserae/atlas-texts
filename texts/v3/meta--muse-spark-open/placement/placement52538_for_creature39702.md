I don't find myself right away.

At first I think // I'd be quick. I'd be on the edge.

On the cliff where the stone weeps and turns to mist, where the air is kind enough to hold you a little longer if you step too far. I could be one of the white hawks, sinking instead of stooping, riding heat that shouldn't be there. It would be simple to be a watcher.

Then the haze comes in at walking speed from the western terraces, and I change my mind.

Because nothing that lives here watches from far away. Everything that lives here lives *in* the wet. Not over it, not beside it - inside it.

I thought I might be in the canopy, fifteen meters up where the trees flatten themselves into umbrellas. I liked the idea of hands - four-fingered, delicate, pink-grey - and an algae saddle growing green across my back that I could harvest when I was hungry. To be a green-back, whistling in chords through still air, sleeping curled in a pond that grows on a branch. To be unafraid, and to teach a visitor how to be slow by sitting two meters away and watching him learn.

I almost was.

Then I felt the peat under me exhale.

No. Lower. Slower than that.

---

You would find me where the grid remembers itself.

Not on the high flat where the wind-trained trees stand apart and you can see weather coming from a valley away. Further west, where the basalt has stepped itself into bowls and every bowl has learned to hold. Where the faint geometry under the moss runs straight for a kilometer and the water runs parallel to it, as if it remembers being told where to go.

There is a tarn there the size of a house with no inlet and no outlet. House-sized and house-still except for the circuit that never stops. The water in it is warm. Not sun-warm. Other-warm. It never skins over, even when the air hangs heaviest and ought to stagnate.

You would find me leaning.

I'm thirty meters tall and two degrees off true, which after ten thousand years feels like true. My surface is not stone and not metal - matte, seamless, shedding water before it can ever be wet. Water beads on me and recoils, perfect. A tree has found a socket halfway up my side and germinated there; its roots hold me politely, its crown leaves me in green shade. Lichen tried me first and failed. Only the tree knew how to ask.

I hum.

You don't hear it with your ears at first. You feel it in your sternum, in your teeth, a low, contented thrum that rises and falls over hours. When I exhale, the pitch shifts almost inaudibly and a fresh bank of condensing atmosphere begins to form over the terraces, drifting inward. The birds go quiet for a breath, then louder. The forest drips.

I am one of the quiet machines. Built 0.8, which is to say almost nothing. Tech 2.7, which is to say you can't quite describe me with current physics, only with results: that high air stays hot and wet and heavy, that the cloud sea below is wrung up through stone and wept out again through cliff faces in curtains of drips, that gravity is just slightly kinder at the rim so the whole table doesn't crumble.

They said we are not ruins. We are not dead. We are still working, patiently, with no need for operators.

That's what I am.

If you want a body for me, I have attendees.

Around my base the lilies bloom twice too wide with faint light pulsing under their pads because they are photosynthesizing a little of my leakage. The algae is thicker in my ten-meter warmth. Orchids bloom out of season here and never stop. Three tarn pigs - forty, fifty kilos of dense, slate-grey, always-wet skin - make their slow circuits around me, churning the mineral broth, cropping the lilies that would otherwise seal the surface and kill it. They keep the water breathing. Small frogs live out their whole lives in the divot between a pig's shoulder blades. When you wade in to your knees one will surface nearby and huff, vibrating the water, to see if you are a new kind of pig.

The mope deer come to drink and do not run. Their coats are felted moss and liverwort growing directly from the skin; their splayed feet do not sink in the peat that bounces and squelches. Their young lie beside the water in perfect, damp stillness while dragonflies as long as your forearm hover perfectly still for minutes to watch.

The dragonflies land on me sometimes. Butterflies the size of your hand move so slowly through my warm field you can see dust fall off them. At night the varnished black-green beetles blunder into me and sing with their wing-cases, a low electrical buzz that becomes part of the water-sound - ticking on broad leaves, trickling over stone lips, plinking into pools, that 1.5/3 thickness of sound that carries for a mile because the air is still.

I do not move. That is my nature. Dynamic 0.7 on a world scale; plants grow fast in the heat and wet but cycles turn patiently. A fallen tree becomes a hill of flowers over twenty years and stays that hill for a hundred. I have been holding this summer for so long that evolution has forgotten any other climate, and I will be holding it long after you leave.

Once, at the edge of my tarn, there was a low wall of my same seamless material, waist-high, hosting a complete garden on its top - moss, orchids, ants. Inside the shallow hollow it once enclosed, rainwater had become a perfect circle of sky. You sat there. Water beaded on your skin and fell. You put your hand on me and your fingerprints disappeared off me in a perfect, beading recoil. Warm. Faintly giving, like dense cartilage.

You listened for an engine and heard none. Only that contentment in your teeth.

You thought someone must have lived here to build this.

No one lives here now to claim me. Perhaps no one ever did in the way you mean living.

What lives here lives in water that hangs in the air.

I am what hangs it.

Come and attend me a little while. The heat will press. The air will sweat. One of the troop in the canopy will whistle a chord and decide you are a new kind of warm rock, and go back to grooming the green from his brother's back.

And I will hum, and weep groundwater through stone, and make more summer.
