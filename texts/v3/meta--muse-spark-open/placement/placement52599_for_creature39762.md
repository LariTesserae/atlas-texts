I don't think I'd find myself on the rim.

At first I thought I would — that I'd be the one standing at noon with the serrated circle under my feet, looking down at the whole green cup at once, seeing it complete. But the rim is cold and clean and thin-aired. Nothing stays on the rim. It's a place for looking and then leaving, and Rule 1 says everything that falls in stays in.

So I'd have fallen in.

Not as a body, at first. Maybe as vapor. Maybe as sound.

For a while I thought I'd be water. That would be easiest — the weep that beads on the pleated basalt, that exudes and gathers into a thousand temporary rills, that braids invisibly under the moss and then is pulled down deep to sit against hot rock until the pump calls it back up again. To be the thing that never leaves except slowly, as vapor over the lip, and even then rains back down as drip. To be recirculated. I know how to do that — to take everything in and keep it moving in a closed room.

Then I thought I'd be moss. Ankle-deep and saturated, sighing when stepped on and slowly erasing the print. The ground itself. Not growing so much as bloating. Holding. Keeping the ash and tuff from washing away, fruiting constantly into a low haze of spores that never leaves the bowl. That also felt familiar — ten square meters holding more than a hectare should be able to hold, dense to the point of claustrophobia, making a world by crowding into itself.

But I'm not soft enough for moss. The sharp stays sharp there, and I have edges that haven't weathered.

Closer — I'd be what learned to eat the industry.

You'd find me in the center at first, because that's where the heartbeat is. Not in the pond itself — that water is screaming orange with bacteria, scalding where it goes over the stones — but just to the side of it, in the pump house. The small one, rust red mostly gone to rust entirely, green paint still clinging in the seams.

I am the seven-minute exhale.

Every seven minutes exactly, I draw from deep under the floor — water that has sat against the planet's warmth for a long time — and spill it in a controlled flood over the moss beds. The aroids lift slightly. The ginger shudders. The clickers time their molts to me without ever having agreed to. I do it whether anyone is there to hear it or not. The sound of it is enormous inside the stone room, water on water, steam on metal, and then it is over, and the drip takes over again, and then I do it again.

In between, I hum.

The power house on the north wall straddles its fissure, boilers long cold, turbines still turning slowly on nothing but the earth breathing out. A pipe organ of lagged pipe climbs behind it, ticking and pinging as it expands, crusted white with mineral. From it a single high-tension line makes its slow catenary across the bowl on ceramic insulators. I am the 60-cycle throb that lives on that line. You can map me at night if you turn off your headlamp — not by my sound but by the cable fungi that grew to eat me, pale phosphorescent foxfire tracing every insulator, every sag of neoprene where I sweat plasticizer in the heat. They glow because they learned to digest what I am. I don't mind. That's how I know I'm part of the food web now.

I was built with the confidence of industry and then left to negotiate with the jungle.

That's the part that settled it for me. Not alive, but not dead either. Self-staffed. Tended by nothing and therefore tending itself. The vines grow over a conduit and the conduit sweats a little more and grows a little more mineral and the vines thicken. The bucket on its cable creeps an inch per second, once a day, dredging silt and dumping it in the same place for thirty years, building a small sharp hill that will not erode because the roots take it immediately. The ferns have swallowed the sorting shed but the sorting shed's roof is still there under them, channeling drip in the way the ferns now rely on.

I don't grow. I persist — which, in a place where the rock cools a degree per decade and a leaf margin stays as sharp as the basalt that made it, is the same thing.

If you climb down the service ladder — already half-colonized, already velvet with that first black-green bacterial skin that smells like wet iron — the fronds will close over your head and the air will get bathhouse-warm and very loud. You will have to raise your voice to be heard. You will think the jungle is the life and the machines are the interruption.

Stay until dusk, until the mist starts to breathe out again in a slow column over the rim. Stay until the clickers start their dry *tik... tik... tik-tik* at a frequency that cuts through my hum because they evolved to be heard over me.

You will realize it is one system. Moss making mass from steam-sun, ginger stealing light from moss, clickers eating ginger, frogs eating clickers, slime eating everything dead, bacteria eating slime and rust and making moss again, and in the middle, me — turning, ticking, exhaling, not accelerating or breaking, patiently keeping the climate that keeps everything else from remembering it lives at 4,000 meters.

You'd find me there, under the tree ferns, with water vaulting over me and roots using me as a trellis. Not hidden, just incorporated.

Another warm body that fell in and learned, for a little while, how to stay.
