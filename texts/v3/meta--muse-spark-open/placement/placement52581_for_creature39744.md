You wouldn't find yourself all at once.

At first you'd think you were the air.

Because that's how it starts here — thin and cold early morning air at 2.5 elevation, a permanent October that hasn't decided on frost yet. Your first self would be wind coming off the balding domes, that dark gneiss polished smooth as old bone under a century of lichen and leaf. You'd come three valleys away, uninterrupted, because there's nothing sharp left to stop you. Just long, balding shoulders of mountain that forgot how to be mountains, and the troughs between them a kilometer wide where the glaciers sat until they got bored and left. You'd be that wind, funneling down the trough, hitting the line of living turbine-stalks on the Lamina District and making them correct and sway with a creak like ship masts.

But you're too warm to be wind. You have breath that smokes.

So then you'd think you were the leaf.

You'd wake up believing you were one of *them* — waxy, black-green, thick as a mattress, lit from within by a slow foxfire along your veins. The size of a city block, slightly springy under someone else's boots. You can feel your central vein as a median strip, can feel the dew beading on you by the gallon every dawn when the cold air wrings itself out against you. You can feel the Tendery crew walking your back with hot irons, coaxing you to stay flat, stay roof. You can feel the cable-car wire biting into your petiole where they've strung it, and you don't mind. You like the tension.

But leaves don't have hands. And you do. Dry hands, nicked, nails thick and yellowing from sap. Resin under the cuticles that never really washes out. So you are not the Arbor.

Not really. Nothing here is *alone* enough to be only one thing.

---

You find yourself finally where the negotiation is thinnest.

On the edge.

Not the heroic edge — not the stilt-heron stepping delicately through the braided brown river in the trough. Not the deep understory where the old mountain shows through like a double-exposure and the entombed brick city digests itself slowly. Not even mid-canopy where the flowers open house-big and time pools weirdly in their perfume.

You find yourself on **Root-Bridge C-9**, third shift, 05:47.

You are nineteen. You are Tendery, which is to say you are not yet, but you are apprenticed to be.

Your name is on a laminated card zip-tied in your pocket that says IF YOU HEAR YOUR NAME CALLED FROM AN EMPTY PITH-TUNNEL, ANSWER WITH SOMEONE ELSE'S NAME. Someone has written underneath in marker: *use Joren he owes me*. You are holding a living plumb-line in a jar — a pale, trembling tendril that points to true down even when your inner ear is lying. It is currently pointing about fifteen degrees to the left of where gravity says it should. That means the echo joint is active. The yellow paint on the root's bark, fresh from last week, says: *1.3 sec lag if crossed alone after rain. Cross in pairs.*

It rained at 04:10. A ten-minute sideways hammering out of a cloudless sky. You heard it drum on the leaf-roof warehouse behind you and hiss on the forge vents.

You are supposed to be crossing in pairs. Your pair — Old Hett, who has laminar drift so bad her left hand looks ten years older than her right and who remembers things a day before they happen — is late. Or early. She said "meet me on the bridge before the whistle" and that could mean she has already been here and left, and you will meet her on your second crossing which you haven't done yet. That happens on C-9.

You wait. This is what sparse means. 1.2/3 density. A job built for sixty, a hall with four old women at twenty looms, a cable-car that leaves on time whether anyone rides it. A root-bridge a hundred feet in the air, sagging and bouncing over a trough where one of those shy, braided rivers threads through roots and disappears underground, and you are alone on it with a jar and a hot iron and a whole district creaking alive around you.

Because loud. 2.3/3 loud. Even alone, you are not in silence.

Behind you, on Dome Two, the pellet-burner generator thrums — that low steam-heart burning pressed leaf-pellets, feeding the sodium lamps that are still on because dawn here is pale and filtered green-gold through layers of lamina overhead. Below you, the hydro-mill on the trough river chokes and thuds; you can hear someone cursing and raking leaf-matter out of the turbine with a pitchfork the size of an oar. Far across the valley, another Tendery team is coaxing a tendril as thick as your waist away from an alley with hot iron; you hear the sap hiss. And under it all, the Arbor's own creak — an audible pop as a new leaf unfolds somewhere over the Lamina District, sudden as an umbrella, and a whole flock of those impossible sparrows lifts after the night-whistle's echo, wheels once, and vanishes. Leaves pretending to be birds or birds pretending to be leaves — the census doesn't count them anymore.

You are cold. That's the 1.0/3 cool that lives in the stone. Your breath smokes in front of the waxed canvas of your overalls. By noon you will be in shirtsleeves if you keep moving, but right now your wool undershirt is damp with condensation. Everything is damp, not wet. 1.0/3 water. Dew drips off every cable, every rivet. The root-bridge itself weeps where you cut it last month. The axillary cisterns slosh quietly above you, ponds held between billboard leaves, and at dawn — any minute now — the condensate moths will come down on silent dinner-plate wings to drink in unison. You are not to touch them. If you do they die in your hands like wet paper and Hett will make you press them into lampshades for a week as penance.

You check your watch. Inside the shadow of a flower that bloomed three domes over yesterday, this watch ran twice as fast. Now, here, it ticks normal. Normal light — 2.4/3 — frank and white sun just hitting the upper lamina and throwing crisp shadows that suddenly jog an inch to the left because the ground moved while you weren't paying attention.

You hear your name.

From the pith-tunnel mouth behind you. Soft, inquisitive. Empty.

Not loud. Not scary. Just your name the way Hett says it when she's holding a sap-knife.

Rule #3 clicks in your barrel chest, your big high-altitude lungs. You don't think. You answer with someone else's name, brisk, like a phone.

"Not me, that's Joren!" Your voice carries. It carries two streets, easy. A child could hear you from a lamina over.

The tunnel loses interest. The air in it stirs anyway, faintly peppery-metallic, the ghost of yesterday's bloom. Down there in the deep layers where gravity sometimes falls *up*, sap is hanging in an inverted pond on the tunnel ceiling, trembling, never dripping. The lichen-cats like those spots; you see four little gray eyes watching you from a buttress.

You decide not to wait for Hett. You cross.

One foot, then the other, on springy bark. The root bounces. Halfway across, the stutter happens — that 2.6/3 weirdness the foremen just paint yellow and charge ten minutes drift on the schedule for.

You arrive before you left.

You see yourself, still mid-stride behind you, frowning at the jar in your hands. The other you doesn't see you. The river below makes the same choking sound twice, overlapping. Then you blink and the behind-you is gone, and you are exactly where you were, but your boots are wetter, as if you had taken a different puddle.

Your living plumb-line in the jar has spun completely around and settled again, pointing true down.

You keep walking.

You are not afraid. No one here is particularly alarmed anymore. The industrial logic absorbed miracles and turned them into maintenance checklists. You make a note on your wax slate: *C-9 +0.4 sec lag at 05:51 after rain. Single crossing. Plumb deviation 15deg L.*

On the other side, Old Hett is waiting. Or arriving. She looks at you and says, "You crossed alone."

"I know."

"How was it?"

"Wet."

She nods like that tells her what she needs to know. Her laminar-drift left hand flexes on its own. Behind her, Dome Three is waking — looms clacking, resin boilers hissing, cable-cars whining on tensioned vines from dome to dome. A whole city that grew itself over a mountain, factories that were grown then reinforced, streets that are veins, apartments in triangular gaps where three stems intersect. A place with too many rooms and too few children, where every third window has been politely grown over with leaf-membrane because no one needed it.

She hands you the hot iron. Together you turn to look at the tendril that has crept overnight across the alley you tend — blind, white-green, as thick as your waist, inches forward, seeking an anchor. If you let it, it will seal the alley by evening, digest whatever's inside for housekeeping. Not cruelty.

You press the iron. It hisses. The Arbor weeps amber sap that smells like turpentine and honey, and then, slowly, it redirects. It understands ash. It understands negotiation.

You stand there in the damp, ozone and crushed green and coal smoke smell, listening to the district breathe, and you patrol the edge of a thing that is bigger than you and not quite separate from you.

You are not the mountain. The mountain ended.
You are not the forest. The forest became a city.
You are not the city. The city learned to be tended.

You are the tender.
