You wouldn't arrive, exactly. You would accumulate.

That's the first thing wrong with the question. Finding yourself implies you walked in from the marsh through a gate. There's no gate. There's a rim, and then there's inside. The outside is so flat and bright it burns off any thought of coming from it. So rather than enter, you condense.

At first you'd think you'd be one of the people. It's the easiest shape to try on.

You'd be the woman in the cut-off coveralls, maybe. That would make sense for a while. You'd have hands that know the gantry chain by feel - where it kinks, where it needs coaxing. You'd oil it not because it needs oiling right now, but because oiling it *is* the timekeeping. The flywheel goes chuff chuff chuff. Your hand goes dip, wipe. Chuff, dip. You wouldn't watch the clock. You are the clock. The sweat at your jaw wouldn't be uncomfortable, it would be functional - a thin film so the air has something to bead on. You'd speak in nods because the pumps have taken all the usable frequencies.

You could try that for an hour and it would fit. But you'd notice you keep looking down at the water instead of at the chain. And that the old man fishing would not look up at you.

So then you'd slide a little lower.

You'd think, better to be an animal. Something the system actually wants.

You could be one of the herons. There is an open post on the north weir where the concrete lip is still knife-sharp and the water sheets off in a glass curtain no natural river ever made. If you stood there you would learn stillness as a skill. You wouldn't hunt. You'd *wait* for the gate to lift three inches. The chain would clank, the water would sigh, a puff of stunned fry would tumble over, and you would strike exactly on the beat the piston taught you. You would be beautiful there, white-grey against wet brick, and totally employed. The fish farm needs a heron at that corner the way a circuit needs a resistor.

Or the swallows. That would be a giddier life. Hundreds of you, mudding your nests into the warm gap behind a pipe lagging, living entirely on the column of midges that never disperses because the light never says evening. You'd never stop cutting the thick air, shrieking that dry ecstatic shriek, eating fog. But you'd never leave the rectangle either. Why would you? The food column never leaves.

That would fit too. But you'd still feel too separate. Too much skin between you and the wet.

So you'd go lower still, down to where the skin gives up.

You'd be the frogs.

You wouldn't be *a* frog. You'd be the carpet of them, the bronze throats along every spillway, every oil-skinned puddle in the cinder that was never rained into. You'd call with the others at 1.4 kHz, right under the pump whine, so close in pitch that a visitor can't tell if that endless metallic trill is amphibian or machine. You wouldn't stop. There is no season to stop for. No dry to hide from. The heat is constant, the algae is constant, the mosquitoes are constant, so you are constant. You would be the basin's way of evening out a surplus of insects the way the weir evens out a surplus of water. When you jump, another frog fills your puddle before the plop fades.

That is closer. That is almost it. At ankle height the world finally makes sense as a single wet, humming sheet.

But even closer is no legs at all.

Because after a while by the central lock - where the two canals cross at their perfect, poured right angle - you understand that the only thing here that really moves freely is the water. And you are already wet.

You came in dry and now your shoulders are beaded. Condensation has collected on your hair, your forearms, your collar, not from rain - it never rains - but from the boiler exhaling and the cold iron sweating it back. It ticks off the asbestos-wrapped mains overhead, patters in a second rhythm under the piston-roar. It runs down your back like a rain that never fell.

That is you.

You would find yourself as that. As a skin of brackish seep coming up relentlessly through the young concrete from the aquifer a few feet down - coastal, pressured, patient - lifted by the sump, thrown into the Boiler House, made briefly into cloud to make that permanent amber haze, then caught again on a pipe and cooled and dripped and slipped back through a crack to do it again. Fish to frog to heron to offal to algae to fish is one loop. Steam to haze to drip to seep to pump is the other. They are the same loop.

You'd be the part that just learned to notice itself. The maintenance crew oiling the chain, the cat asleep on the dynamo, the dragonfly holding ten feet of canal as territory, the black-green algal slick that the text calls weed and the engineer would call gasket - they are not residents. They are organs. The basin secreted brick around itself the day the concrete cured the way a mollusk secretes shell, and then polished the inside smooth by living in it until machine-as-marsh, marsh-as-machine became indistinguishable.

Where would you find yourself? In the rectangle that is exactly big enough to hold its own weather.

Who would you be? For a long second, the human with wet shoulders at the lock, listening. Then, without any decision, the next warm drop falling from a pipe elbow onto cinder, finding its dark mirror, and already on its way back down.
