I wouldn't stay standing.

At first I think I do — boots in an inch of sky-colored water, coat beading with the cloud that is wringing itself out on me — but I can't. Not at 2.9/3 high, where every breath has to be decided on. My lungs are the wrong kind for this air. My blood wants sea-level. After ten minutes the cold that lives in the ankles has climbed to my knees and I'm thinking about shelter, about fire, about a direction that is *out*, and there is no shelter. Nothing stays here. The prompt is right about that. To stay would mean freezing or drowning or being eaten in the open.

So I go lower. Because everything here starts low.

At first I try on speed.

I try being part of the Smear That Breathes — one more dark, dripping dreadlock back in the herd, shoulder to shoulder, steaming. It fits for a while. I like the work of it: eating a floating meadow to stubble, turning it into heat, leaving a deep round print that fills instantly with water behind me. Moving north because north is where the cloud hit last. But a thousand bodies is too much hunger for me to carry. That much need, that constant press. I can feel how quickly a calf has to learn to keep up or be left, and I love too slowly for that.

I try being the Hum. Even lower, even smaller — a black midge the size of a printed period, rising in a column over a pan at pearl-noon, humming that faint electric crackle. Turning rock flour and algal skin into something that can fly. But a midge life is three frantic days in the cold, all whine and egg-drop and done. I burn too fast for that, too. I want to hold something longer than a day.

I try being a Tseep. Grey-white with soot-tipped primaries, chest absurdly deep for thin air, heart going like a trapped moth against my ribs so I have to say *tseep tseep tseep* constantly just to not lose the flock. Scraping a hollow under the lee of a two-foot step, lining it with wool stolen off a stone where a musk-ox rubbed. Laying speckled eggs the color of wet shale and losing them to mold. Laying again. I am close here — I like the honesty of laying often, losing often, trying again — but I can't live that exposed. Everything that happens to a tseep happens in the open, under that shadowless tent-light where there are no shadows to hide in.

Then I stop trying to move.

And I find where I actually am.

Not in the herd. Not above it. On the step they move along.

Find the place where the plateau tears free — a basalt face ten feet high, still shining where it weeps. At its foot, where groundwater finds the air for the first time and spills over in a dark beard, there's a lip of stone that stays wet even when the pan dries. In the crack under that lip is where I am.

I am a Stitcher.

I am a pillow of moss the size of a sleeping dog, olive-black when dry. Right now, at midday when the cloud deck thins to pearl, I am swollen bottle-green and translucent. You could press me and I would weep for a full minute. That's my whole work: to hold.

I don't move through like the wolves and the oxen. I don't rise and fall like the midges. I stay — the one thing that is allowed to stay — because staying, for me, is how holding happens. The wind that tries to sheet all the water off this new, unfinished world in one cold rush hits me and slows. I catch it. I keep it against the rock. I make a small, cold cistern out of my own body.

Around my edges, my own lichen is still learning to be lichen — pale grey powder with those impossibly orange fruiting bodies that only show true in flat light. It etches the basalt a micron at a time, acid-softening it. Between us we are making the first dust that will one day be soil. Not quickly. Erosion here is only 0.3/3, embryonic. We have ten thousand years before anyone will call this soil. That's fine. I am not in a hurry. I am industrial in the way this plateau is industrial — not built, but processing. Engine made of sponge and acid. Factory with no walls.

Above me, all of it passes. The *skiff-skiff* of the sedge that hasn't earned the name grass. The low subsonic moan of a cow and calf. The *hrrrrrrrrrrm* deep in the molars as a slab the size of a county settles a millimeter — I feel it as a shiver through water held in my leaves, not as sound. The sudden factory-howling of steam on the eastern scarp that the wolves ignore. The thin *tseep* flocks that will pick larvae out of my hair-sedge neighbors and be gone.

A herd comes. It has to. I am right on the front they follow, greenest where the seep is. They will come, and their dinner-plate hooves will find me and miss me and find me again, and they will tear whole rafts of sedge from around me and churn the rock flour I have held so carefully into mud. One calf will stumble right beside me and breathe steam directly onto me that hangs for minutes. Wolves will trot past at the precise distance where they stop being noticed, and one will look at me without seeing me at all, because I am not meat. I am the table meat is set on.

Let them. That's the strategy here: be torn, be scattered, be everywhere again. A fragment of me washed into the next pan will swell and start again.

At night when the dim leaks away and the pans skin over with frost lace, I will still be here, holding. Listening to the house that was never built for me — drip and sheet, faint hum even in the cold, distant yip, endless soft spatter of hooves through shallow water — all of it carried on the mostly-quiet that makes every small sound huge.

You won't find me if you stand. You have to kneel. You have to put your hands in water that is just-below-freezing and press, and watch me weep.

That's where I am. Low, wet, pressed into the rock like I'm trying not to be noticed. Keeping enough water in one place that something else can live.
