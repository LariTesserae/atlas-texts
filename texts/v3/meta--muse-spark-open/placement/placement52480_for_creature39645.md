I wouldn't be in the water.

That's the first mistake you make, thinking the water is the main thing. It *is* the main thing if you're a visitor — it's loud and warm and up to your throat and you can't ignore it. But if you live here you don't live in the water. The water is too new and too insistent. It never stops arriving. It comes down the middle shaft like a thrown rope and hammers the surface and churns the whole room over every few minutes and then leaves through the low arch at the far end, that sucking mouth. Nothing can hold in that. I tried, for a little while in my head, being a fish — but there is no slack water, no weed to hold to, nowhere to lay anything that needs to stay still. I'd be flushed. I'd be tired and then gone.

I wouldn't be on the floor, either. The floor is too perfect. Great fitted slabs where a mason has taken an adze to every block, and the joints are so tight you couldn't get a fingernail in. That's intentional. Stillness is rot and they built this place not to rot. Even the light gives you no cover — at noon those three shafts just drive straight down through the lime-milky water all the way to the stone and make everything overexposed. At night it's a black so total it feels like the room blinks out. There's no dusk to hunt in. No shadow to hide a nest in.

So I kept moving up.

If I live here, I live *just* above.

You know that dark ring around the walls? Where the splash line is? From the ladder it looks like a stain, like damp. Up close it's not a stain. It's a skin. Black-green, felted, matte except when a bead swells on it and catches the pillar of sun. That's me, if I am honest about what can actually live here long-term.

I am the film.

Not an animal. No face, no decision the way animals decide. *Gloeocapsa* and *Pleurocapsa* and a few true algae braided in — the ones that love fresh lime and new concrete and the sides of fountains. I don't need soil. There is no soil here, the builders made sure of that. I eat light and I eat stone. The mortar between those big limestone blocks is still young, still pale and a little soft at the edge if you brush it. It leaches calcium carbonate every time the spray hits it, and I take it. I make that faint clean chapel smell when you first slip off the last iron rung. In places I've thickened into little blistered domes, lentil-sized stromatolites. Give me fifty years without the man with the brush and I'll build a proper bathtub ring an inch thick all around this womb. Right now I'm just a beard, trying to turn this made room into a natural one.

Or — if you want something you can see without pressing your nose to the mortar — I am the fern that lives just above me.

Lean in. In the overflow arch, where the lip is sharp and new, and in the little dark crescents where the apprentice's chisel jumped, there are five clumps of maidenhair. Wiry black stems, fronds no bigger than your thumbnail, pale because they've never had direct sun, only bounced, water-scattered sun and spray. They shouldn't be here. Some spore, light as dust, fell thirty feet down a square well by accident. There's no earth for roots, so they root in lime dust and damp in a tool-mark. When the central column hits, the whole vault puffs with air pressure, and we shiver in unison.

That's where you'd find me, if you came down and floated on your back the way everybody does.

Not in the middle where it's dramatic. At the edge, where air and stone and water have negotiated a treaty. Just above the waterline, just out of the flush, in the spray-shadow of the arch where the roar stacks up and stays — bright and hard as the light.

If you hold still long enough you can feel me being the biggest thing in here with you. For a minute, you are. Warm, deafeningly loud, held quite easily by a room that is waiting, with complete indifference, for you to climb back up the iron spikes so the film and the frond can get back to inheriting it.
