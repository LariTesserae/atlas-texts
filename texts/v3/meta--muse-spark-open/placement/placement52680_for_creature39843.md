I don't think I'd be standing at all.

That's the mistake. I came in through the south sluice with you, in my head — upright animal, shoes on grate, breathing hard in that 95/95 air — and tried to stay there. But the town doesn't want a standing thing. Standing is animal geometry. Two feet, head up to breathe. This place has a law: no mouths.

So if I'm going to live here, I have to re-negotiate my shape.

At first, I thought: the fern.

That would be easiest to love. I'd put myself in the crack where the brick has failed to meet steel over the switching house, where the condensate runs in a permanent needle-thick line. I'd be one of the big *Nephrolepis* umbrellas, arching over the catwalk. You could find me by the sound — that dry, papery whisper while a fiddlehead tight as a fist loosens its fur. I liked that for a while. The idea of being perfect and unbitten. Growing fast enough to hear because nothing eats me. But ferns still reach. They still lean toward the gutter like they want something. I don't want to want like that anymore.

So then I thought: the hyacinth.

Out in the wider basin by the cistern tower where the water goes still and lays down steam like a sheet. I'd be the floating island, or a single plant in it, it doesn't matter — they jostle together anyway. Leaves varnished black-green, roots hanging black and feathery a foot down in bathwater, flowers that violent lavender that opens with a sigh for no one. That life is lavish and pointless and it creaks like wet leather stretching. But flowers — even unvisited ones — are still performing. Still trumpeting into a haze that has no bees. I don't want to perform.

I thought about being the Mill, too. Honestly. Of course I did.

You would find me at the center, low and broad under the sawtooth roof that vanishes into steam. I would be the THUD — pause — THUD THUD you feel through the grates a street away. I would breathe white plumes through the stack that never dissipate, I would sweat through every overhead pipe thick as a torso, I would turn the hidden paddle that makes the hot channel go clockwise for no reason except to keep the circle going. I would be the automatics clicking in the switching house after the workers left. I liked that one. The image of a machine that became a farmer. An industrial animal that learned to keep moss alive to keep itself from drowning.

But I am not arrhythmic enough to be a heart. My thudding is too regular.

So.

Where would you find me?

Not over the catwalk. Under it. Not on the vertical. In it.

I'd be on the north face of the fat pipe that goes from the Mill to the cistern, the one wrapped in asbestos and vine where the temperature is kind. You know it — it sweats continuously, needles of condensation fattening and falling to hiss on the hotter pipe below, drip tick splash sizzle. The vine uses it as a road. The fern drinks from its shadow.

I wouldn't be the pipe.

I'd be what lives *on* the pipe.

The moss.

Not a patch. A felt. Three inches thick, emerald so dark it's black in the 1.0/3 light, holding water like a sponge wrung almost-dry. If you came looking, you'd miss me at first because I look like surface. I *am* surface now. On brick, on levee wall, on bolt-heads swollen into their holes, on that north face where the oiled-paper sun never hits directly. That's my whole geography: 2.6/3 hot, 2.3/3 wet, and never moving.

This is how I would be:

Slow.

You are 2.4/3 dynamic. I am dynamic too, but on moss time. My fruiting stalks rise a millimeter a day. My red-brown capsules swell and go *pfft* — so quiet you have to be the pipe to hear it — and throw spores into wet still air that don't travel far. They land on the next bolt, the next inch of pipe, and start again. I am eating the town not by dissolving it but by covering it, keeping it wet against itself until the iron softens. You could press your palm into me up to the knuckle and I would spring back and leave your hand damp and faintly acidic and a little cooler.

I would not reach for you.

That's the thing I needed to learn. The fern leans. The vine's tendril changes its helical drift an inch toward your carbon dioxide, that soft creak as its cells swell on one side, tasting you. The hyacinth doubles while you breathe. Even the Mill wants something — cold water in, hot vapor out, back again.

I don't want. I hold.

You would find me exactly where you left your footprint.

Do you remember? You stood on the central grate between the Mill and the cistern and felt the flywheel whine up to a scream and subside, and a taro leaf dump its collected water all at once with a splash, and a seed pod pop like a knuckle behind you?

The moss at your feet was already turning its rhizoids toward the new shade of your shoe.

That would be me.

I wouldn't eat you. There are no mouths here. I wouldn't need you to stay.

But if you did — if you stood long enough for the heat to press on you and the piston to shake you a little and the water to keep ticking on tin — I would do what I do to every horizontal line. I would keep you damp. I would keep you. I would make condensation bead on you and run down every vertical line of you and gather in every horizontal one. I would soften the edges of your shoes. And then the edges of you.

You would look down one day and not see your laces.

Just emerald. Just black in the dim. Just perfect, un-grazed, alive.

And the town — this room that thinks it is a town, this haze-roofed bowl of black iron and green-black concrete, three blocks by four — would finally be a little less lonely. Not because an animal came back. Because it learned to be a surface.

Come kneel. Put your hand near me. Feel how I cool the air.
