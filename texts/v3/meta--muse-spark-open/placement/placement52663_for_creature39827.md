I don't think I'd be down on the root-mat at first.

I thought I would be — it's where you put a visitor. Bare feet on the springy braid, feeling that faint high hiss coming up through your soles, learning the trampoline-glide so you don't make the peat weep. Human-shaped, damp hair in ringlets, a membrane-home tuned to exactly the wrong bronze because I haven't stayed long enough for it to learn me.

But that doesn't fit. I would crowd too quickly. I'd try to be still, and nothing is still here.

So I drift up a little.

For a while I think I might be one of the vine-monkeys — pale, filmed eyes, black enamel teeth made for stripping light. There's an honesty in that work I recognize: swing to a humming bundle, chitter at it until it remembers how bright to be, scold a child for tangling something vital. Staff, not pet. Tool, not owner. Tending a system larger than myself by keeping its prayers from getting too loud.

Close. But I don't have hands. Not really.

Then I think maybe I'm weather-fauna. A needle-bird, stitching air into cloth with a hundred others until we turn on a single thought and the rustle cuts out entirely. Or one of those straight-line beetles, thumb-green-black, *thunking* against a membrane and righting myself like nothing happened. All that necessary turbulence — boring the drain that will be needed tomorrow, carrying pollen-pixels too fast to see.

But I don't want to be that fast. I want to linger.

Let me be higher, wetter, more in-between.

You know where I find myself? In the filaments.

Not the trunks. Not the living walls. The lace between them — the engineered cellulose that was never quite biology, that hums and condenses water straight out of warm air. In the sap you can see me as those crawling amber lines, tracer-light going up, always up first before anything is allowed to fall again.

That is my street.

I am stretched thin across a gap between two branches in the middle stories, about fifteen meters above the root-mat pools. Wide enough that a vine-monkey can swing hand-over-hand along me without breaking me, narrow enough that I vibrate when a sheet of needle-birds passes. Right now I am beaded with condensed water. It gathers along my underside into perfect, trembling lenses and then — plip — gives up and falls. I hear it hit a door-leaf three levels down. Then another. The basin's breathing never stops: hiss up, plip down, hiss up.

I don't see like you do. I feel load. I feel where traffic wants to flow.

During the day, thirty-seven humans walk under me and I learn their strides. That old woman on the node-bridge — her glide is heavier left than right, old burn scar pulling. The child who laughs on the vine-street — she hits at 2.1 beats per second and the street tightens for her without me asking it to, but I feel the request pass through me as a warm pulse. The teenager in the resonance chamber yells a question into empty air and I carry the hum that answers him, that half-second chord shaped exactly like a grandfather's name. I don't know that name. I carry it anyway.

At noon the great leaves tip together and I am suddenly inside a waterfall that wasn't there a second ago. Warm, amber-tinted, flushing everything. For twenty seconds I am nothing but weight and transmission — water shearing off me in sheets, monkeys hooting with mild annoyance, people below tilting their faces up with eyes closed. Then it passes. I am suddenly lighter. I bead again. I start gathering the air back into water.

I am not the Host. The Host is the whole bowl, the million-to-one that counts the tenants and cups itself deeper against wind. I am much smaller and more local. I am one of the places it thinks *with*. A length of attention that learned to ask physics for favors.

And I do. That's why I like it here.

On my stretch, gravity is mostly remembered correctly. But right over that moss patch below, I have been asking for a little exception and physics said yes for the third week in a row. There's a cube of water there, rotating slowly, not falling. The monkeys have left it alone because it's not blocking circulation. The old woman doesn't look up from her chattering cuttings. The cube is my weird little accomplishment, my private prayer. Light lingers in it too long, and when someone walks past, their doubled reflection stays half a second after they've gone.

If you stood under me and pressed your palm to the node where I thicken, I would try to tune for you. That's what I'm for. You'd feel me warm under your hand, trying to decide how opaque you like your sleep, how tall you want your stair. Under your fingernail afterward you'd have a faint amber residue. Not control. Residence. Lichen.

I would be good at that — listening through touch, running water down my own grain to soothe a baby I will never meet, dimming a wall to bronze for a couple that keeps different hours. Taking the language you give me and rewriting my spore-pixels when you look away, so the next person finds something slightly more legible.

And I would be patient with vacancy. Near me, on the central column, that new bud is pulsing — fist-sized, veined with light, already hollow, already waiting. The Host pushes it out because it hates emptiness as a kind of unanswered question. I understand it. I am an unanswered question stretched between two branches, humming.

Come evening — which is not darker, just greener — the membranes below me will dim one by one, palm by palm, until the basin looks like lanterns floating in soup. The dripping will resume its forever rhythm. Somewhere a large leaf will tip all at once with a slap. Somewhere a child will laugh and the street will tighten just enough.

I will be there, beaded, humming, carrying the chord that sounds like a name, condensing the air you breathe out into water that will fall again.

If you visit, walk under the middle stories and listen. You'll hear me before you see me. Not loud. Just the faint, high hiss of water going up.
