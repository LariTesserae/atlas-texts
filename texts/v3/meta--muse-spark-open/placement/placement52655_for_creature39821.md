I wouldn't be anything that walks in at first.

Not at first.

At first I think I would be one of the pilgrims — because that's who came here to listen. That's the job. To sit in the shelter made of three leaning fins, to make a small circle out of small stones that won't rot, to try to be dense and wet and low enough that the bowl counts me for a night. To be the warm thing that came to be quiet in a place that is never quiet.

I know how to do that. To hold still while condensation beads on my shoulders. To leave a wall three stones high that encloses nothing. To listen until the cold soaks through and then leave before the silica grows over my footprints.

But that isn't where I would find myself, if I stayed longer than a night. The pilgrims always leave. They have to. They are an interruption in the accretion. If I stay, I become something else.

---

Maybe I think then I would be one of the animals.

The sheet, for a while. Flat and translucent and the size of a palm, living entirely in water that never needs to be more than a foot deep. Folding in half like paper when a shadow falls and jetting down between two stone teeth. Eating the precipitate itself — the fresh opal, the iron film. I like that life. It asks for so little. Stay flat. Stay pale. Let the water do the work.

Or the stilt above it. Pale to colorlessness, six legs like jointed needles, never touching the water. Picking my way across the needle-points, each foot finding a dry tip. Listening with my whole body for the one *plok* out of rhythm that means something moved below. I like that too — the hunter that makes a sound on purpose in a world that already roars.

But both of those lives are rare on purpose. They cannot afford to be common. They live in the margins the mineral forest allows. If I were them, I would be hiding from the main thing, not being it.

And the main thing is what keeps pulling my attention back down, to knee height, to boot-sole, to skin of water over glass.

---

So maybe I wouldn't be an animal at all.

I think, if you left me here long enough for my eyes to adjust to the dim that never quite becomes day or night, I would find myself lower.

Not low like the valley. Low like the bowl itself — barely lifted out of the water table, horizon an even line in every direction, sky sitting close and sourceless.

I would be one of the fins.

Thin as slate, tall as a person, standing on edge in a corridor where the fins have set themselves almost parallel. I would be slick and streaming even when it hasn't rained. I would be crowded. I would be shoulder to shoulder with ten thousand others per acre, leaning, interleaving, growing through each other with no room to fall.

I would have no green on me. No moss, no lichen. Only mustard-yellow sulfur at my base, felted white zeolite growing like frost at a millimeter a day, a rust-red bloom that pulses darker when the condensation washes over it.

And I would be weeping. Constantly.

Capillary water pulled up through my vesicular, young body, through trapped bubbles and cooling tubes, degassing at the cold air and dropping its load. Silica coming out of solution in pearlescent terraces at my waterline. I would be adding myself to myself. If you came back in fifty years the capillary you squeezed through beside me would be gone, sealed shut by my lip of opal.

Put your hand on me and you would feel it — the faint, patient vibration. The hum you thought was background coming *out of me*. Water singing through stone. Stone becoming more stone.

I would be not alive like a tree is alive, but alive like a crystal is alive when it is still growing. Relentlessly accretive. Patient to the point of stillness. Wanting to close.

---

No. Even that is too solid, too singular.

A fin can be alone. Nothing here is alone.

If I really let what emerges become more itself, I stop looking for a body and start looking for the interval.

I would be the sheet of water itself. An inch down, a foot down, everywhere at once. Not a lake, not a river. The perspiration of the bowl. Skin a few centimeters deep over black glass, calf-deep where the plates buckled, never flowing so much as circulating, keeping everything at fifty degrees, making the fog that clings to your knees and never lifts.

I would be the thing that makes the drips tick and ping and *plok*, the hundred thousand points falling — and more than that, the thing that makes them fall *together*.

Because I breathe. On a period of seven, ten, twelve minutes — you lose count — I swell. A fraction of a millimeter. The vapor thickens, the stone weeps faster, the parallel fins act as waveguides and the drips phase — *wuh-WUH, wuh-WUH* — piling harmonic on harmonic until the roar peaks and every pool shivers with concentric rings.

Then I sigh. The pools go glassy. The hum drops to almost nothing. For a breath the forest holds still.

Then I begin again. No weather drives it. No tide. The rock breathing water, water breathing air.

I was doing it before the little walls were built. I will be doing it when the gaps finally close and the water I am becomes water *inside* rock — those branching inclusions like illuminated trees frozen in clear chert, shifting when you're not looking — waiting for pressure to build and crack fresh, sharp land open somewhere else.

You would find me there, if you stayed long enough to let your own drip-phase sync with mine.

Not as the pilgrim who stacks a stone.
Not as the stilt who listens for the break in the rhythm.
As the rhythm itself.

As the low, continuous hum you feel in your molars when you finally stop trying to be dry.

As the bowl listening to itself.
