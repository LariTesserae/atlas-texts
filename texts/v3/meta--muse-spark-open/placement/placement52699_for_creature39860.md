You wouldn't find me on the floor. Not at first.

At first I think I would be up high, and then I realize no - not high enough to be near the rim where the wind pours in and the sky suddenly becomes too big - but somewhere in the middle. The third tier, maybe the fourth, where the overhang is deepest and the afternoon never quite leaves.

I would need a ledge that's not mine. That's where I keep arriving in my head. Not the floor-dust where the children play among the pillow-mats and the isopods push through like little stones that decided to walk. Not the rim-ledge where only the rope-tied go to check the cairns and come back with their eyes dried and strange. I would be in the wall itself.

I think I would be a Mender.

Not the grandmother who knows every corm by name and can tell by the weight of a drip-pot if a veil will split before dawn. Not yet. Maybe I am the hands she sends out when she hears that tick change.

You learn to hear it. That's the first thing.

During the day the Bowl hums - you know that hum, that 1.5-loud of beetle wing and dragonfly rattle and forty finches turning at once - but at night, in the hush-hours, it goes down to almost nothing. No one speaks. No one scrapes. You move in socks on stone, and the only sound is the fog itself, which has no sound until it catches.

I would be the one out there then, in the dark so thick you can't see your hands. Face inches from a Veil-Moss curtain, watching it wake up.

During the day my curtain looks dead. Grey and stiff, like old hair. You could think it's part of the wall. But after midnight, when the cold from the plateau spills over the lip like an invisible river, those thousands of black-green filaments each push out their frost of tiny hairs. If you are close enough, your own breath makes it tremble. And then the beads form. First one, then a hundred, then a thousand, strung like glass on every thread, swelling until they touch and run.

Tick. Tick. Tick. Down the gutter-leaf into the corm below.

My job would be to listen for when it doesn't tick right.

Because veils mat. They tangle. A heavy night can tear a section clean off under its own water, and if you don't tease the filaments apart with a wooden comb before dawn, they rot. They go felted and black and the rot can take a whole vertical plot in a week. My grandmother would have taught me how to breathe shallow so I don't fog the fog, how to anchor a torn sail with a new braided wick, how to guide a stray drip with just a fingertip so it doesn't cut a channel in the white salt crust.

My hands would be terrible. Callused on thumb and forefinger from braiding drip-line, nails always rimmed with the white salt and the tannin-brown of corm juice. My feet wide, bare, cracked. The soles would know every ledge-path in the dark.

And my house would be a cutback, but not deep. Just deep enough for sleeping mats and a hearth we almost never light, and forty-seven clay pots. Forty-seven - I keep counting them - each fist-sized, unglazed, each with its own fiber leading up to its own square of curtain. That's my wealth. Not pots, but reliable drips. On a good morning, each pot holds a finger of water, and we pour them together into the big storage jar and cap it with cloth so the noon sun doesn't steal it. On a bad morning - when the wind upstairs stayed warm and the fog was thin - we get half. And we know not to complain, because the wall remembers.

During the day, when the pillar comes - that one brutal hour of real sun that scours the floor and makes the stone-lettuce throw open its yellow stars all at once - I would not bask with the others. I think I would be in shadow, turned sideways on my ledge, mending. The light makes the dragonflies go wild, their parchment wings rattling louder than anything else in the Bowl, hawking flies off the floor in that roar. Everyone looks up. I would look down at my threads. The veil-curtains are closed then, curled in against the burn, and I can see where the geckos have laid their clear eggs, two at a time, glued to a filament. I know not to touch them.

I am not important. In a place fifty meters across with two hundred souls, no one is unimportant, but no one is a chief either. The old ones would settle it if I cut another family's curtain at the base - three corms, and shame for a month. I wouldn't. I know the rule in my shoulders: you do not take water from another's pot. The white crust shows fingerprints. The Bowl knows.

At dusk, before the fog pours again, the children would come to my ledge to watch me work, because mending looks like weaving air. And I would tell them, whispering already, "Hold your breath when the gecko hunts. Stay still when the moths come," and they would, for a minute, which is a long time for a child.

I would not be a beetle, though I love the pitted gloss-beetles vibrating on my veils like living cups. I would not be a paper moth, though I envy how they never land. I not be the stone itself, old and settled and done with being jagged.

I would be the thin place between.

The person who drinks the air without ever putting a pot to her mouth - because after enough years on the ledge, your skin learns the fog too, and your shawl of retted veil-fiber stays damp until ten. The person whose entire vertical garden is both farm and body.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me at first. I'm the same color as the wall. Olive and dust-rose and salt-white. I'm still, comb in hand, listening for the tick to come back right.

And somewhere above me, the oval of sky has gone pale. The veil-hairs are stirring. The fog is about to pour.
