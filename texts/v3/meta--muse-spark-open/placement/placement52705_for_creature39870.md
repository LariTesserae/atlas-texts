You would not find yourself all at once.

At first you think you've found yourself at the top of the stairs, which is wrong. Everyone thinks that. A hand on the chainlink, peering down the concrete throat where PUMP HOUSE 3 has faded to a stain, counting — forty, sixty — as the air cools around you and the sky narrows to a slot. A visitor's mistake. You are not up there anymore.

Listen.

You find yourself lower. On the third day.

By then you've stopped trying to keep your feet dry, which is the first initiation. Your rubber boots — black, to the knee, someone else's before yours, soft inside from other people's sweat — have learned the shuffle-glide along the flagstones so you don't splash the eels. You didn't mean to learn it. Your body learned it to avoid being rude.

Where you are is not an address. There is no number that sticks here, the paint runs. You are on Terrace Two-and-a-half, if anyone were counting that way, which they aren't. The terraces blur. Yours is the house that is only one room deep on purpose, with the big amber window left on even at noon. Not because you need the light. Because the Basin needs to see that someone is keeping the interval.

Your job, if you can call it a job, is the empty lot next door.

Everyone in the bowl has one. An empty lot you tend by not filling it. Yours is twenty meters of wet flagstone, a rusted bench that no one sits on, a drain grating in the middle that breathes cold air even in summer. In any other town this would be waste. Here it is your main responsibility. In the morning, after you wipe your door hinges with the rag tin — not because they need it yet, but because if you skip a morning the orange lace will punish you — you go out and look at your nothing and make sure it is still nothing.

You pull the birch leaves out of the runnel so the water keeps its silver lip down the stair. You say "excuse me" to the grating before you step near it; underneath, a mottled eel, as thick as your forearm, shifts in water that shouldn't be clear but is. You check that no one has leaned anything against your wall overnight to dry. You leave your own shirts hanging damp on the line for the third day in a row, moving from wetter to damp, and you understand finally why no one brings them in.

You are not the foreman. The foreman keeps the schedule for the huge screws that go *whum... whum...* once an hour, moving water up to a river the upstairs world still believes in. You are not the tram driver, though you pause like everyone else when the diesel-electric sighs around its endless loop, letting a line of orange salamanders cross the narrow rails. They go over your boots without hurry. The children have taught you not to pick them up with dry hands. You don't have dry hands anymore anyway. Your fingertips are pruned permanently, your cuticles ragged, your nails cut short the way everyone's are here. Your hands look ten years older than your face. You oil them at night without thinking, same tin as the hinges.

You would think you are alone in that lot, but you are crowded.

White pigeons have decided your guttering is theirs, their cooing made round and low by the bowl. A pale moth the size of your palm rests on your sodium bulb and does not burn. On rainy evenings — which is most evenings, even when it isn't raining upstairs, because here the limestone itself weeps — the frogs start. Thousands of small brown throats using the whole three-hundred-meter concavity as a drum. The sound is so deep your tea shivers. You used to try to talk over it. Now you take turns, the way everyone here does. You wait for the deepest voice to finish.

And underneath that, if you cock your head the way everyone here cocks their head without knowing they do it, the other resident.

The interval.

You live in its house as much as it lives in yours. When you dropped a wrench last week from your little balcony — fumbled it while wiping condensation off the power conduit vine — you bent to look before you heard it clang, the sound arriving late, as if it had to ask permission. Under the aqueduct behind your house, shadows pool too thickly, and sometimes a drip hesitates, falls up a half-inch, remembers gravity. You don't stare at it anymore. Looking directly makes it shy.

A man tried to build across his interval two streets over when you first came down. You watched his new walls weep mud, then actively spring, until he took a sledge to them himself, quietly, and did not complain. You understood then. The Basin doesn't want infill. It wants breath between things. It wants the bench that rusts alone, the alley that holds only air, the factory at the bottom making parts for itself forever and stacking them gently rusting out back because output was never the point. Maintenance was.

Who you would be, then, is not special.

You are one of the people who agreed with water.

Not amphibian, not transformed in any way that would show on a photograph. Still human, still in waxed canvas that never quite dries, hair damp at the temples even indoors. Pale like stone kept out of the sun. Slow in the deep-water sense of slow: your half-finished project — scraping and repainting the runnel arch you named St. Agatha even though no one else calls it that — has been going for a year and will go for another. No one minds. No one is waiting for you to be done.

You keep a kettle always warm, a towel rack that does nothing, and a frog on the windowsill. You leave your window glowing amber so the Basin knows you are occupied and does not try to fill you in. You have stopped expecting dry bread. Dry is a sharp, upstairs superstition. When a new person comes down with a dehumidifier, foam-sealed windows, determined to be crisp, you see the cold pressure build behind their ears and you wordlessly bring them tea and open one of their windows an inch until the plink and trickle come back and their headache eases.

If you stay, you will forget to count the steps back up. Forty, sixty, impossibly many — they seem fewer now. The sharp air at the top, when you do go up for boots or wire brushes, feels thin, almost hostile, and you find yourself listening for a drip that isn't there, and oiling things that don't need it.

You would find yourself, finally, not in the house at all, but in the lot beside it. Standing in your maintained emptiness at dusk — which lasts most of the day here — while the bar of hard sunlight sweeps past far too slowly, while the factory wheels turn on gathered seep, while the tram dings once at the empty intersection where no one is waiting.

You are keeping the space empty on purpose, and the space, in return, is keeping a place for you.
