I wouldn't find myself on the rim.

That's where you'd put me first — up on that young, angry basalt, with the wind trying to flay anything that stands alone — thinking I would be the one looking down. The observer. The surveyor with cold hands walking the circumference.

But nothing stays on the rim. The scree is too new, too sharp. It hasn't learned to be a slope yet. The wind there has no memory; it just passes. And I am not built for passing. I need to be pressed against something to exist.

So I slide. Or the bowl pulls me. Gravity, here, is not just physics. It is architecture.

For a while I think I might be one of the Mothers.

I like the idea of being tall on the leeward side, braided out of a hundred thin, stubborn willows that forgot they were supposed to be ankle-high. Hollowed by my own programmed absence, four degrees warmer inside than out, lined with soft white pith. A bone that learned to be a lung. I could lean a millimeter a day toward whatever faint light gets through the pearl-grey lid. Sap pressure as a nervous system — slow sugars deciding where a door should bow.

But I am not patient in that way. A tower takes three hundred years to decide to make an arch. I make mine in a sentence. I would go mad waiting for auxin.

I think, then, maybe I am smaller. Blinkered and useful.

I try being a herd. A tremor in the moss-hair forest, a white grain of salt farming fungus in a highway no wider than a hair, rerouting overnight because two hummocks finally kissed and sealed an alley. Or a carpenter — blind ivory, eyeless, *tik tik tik* inside the pith, polishing a perfect circle that will later hold rain for someone else to drink, never knowing it was a window. I love their work. Furniture that moves. Craftsmen who don't know they are craftsmen.

But I have too many eyes. I want to see the whole knot while I'm chewing the tunnel.

No. Lower. Deeper — but not blind.

If you left me there long enough, if you let the cold and the wet and the need instruct me the way they instructed everything else, I think this is where I'd settle and what I'd become:

Not a tower, not a herd. The seam.

The shoulder-wide cleft where two massive colonies of frost-moss meet and never quite fuse. Floored with springy, frozen peat that sighs and rebounds under a boot. Walled with interwoven stems glazed with ice. Roofed where cushion plants leaned into each other and stitched themselves shut, leaving that submarine green light.

I would be that negotiation. The doorway that exists because pressure needed a place to equalize — wind pressure, snow pressure, sap pressure, and now body pressure.

More precisely: I would find myself in the center, where all those seams converge, but not as any of the pillars. I would be the small, perfect betrayal of the city — the circle it refuses to fill.

The plaza under the oculus. The well.

You know it — the cropped moss carpet pulled taut as a skin over standing water, the ring of willow trunks with their branches linked overhead, the single round hole open to the weeping sky where rain falls straight through without touching wood. The place that does nothing but be there, and be maintained.

That's where I'd be. Not the water itself — water is everywhere here but rarely as water, beading, weeping, freezing into pillars, sweating from ceilings. And not the peat either, exactly. I would be the *contraction* around the water. The living rim that tightens and loosens with the seasons. The sphincter of sky-drink.

I would be ten feet deep and two centimeters below the surface at the same time. Deep enough to be archive — that black-brown mattress holding a century-old pollen grain and a ptarmigan feather and a flake of basalt in wet acid that never fully lets things rot — and shallow enough to be skin, to feel your weight when you step onto the cropped carpet and exude a faint resin to seal the abrasion.

And I would glow, but not enough to read by.

That's the other tell. The ghost lichens that cluster where it's dampest, along the edges of the sleeping platforms — those eerily body-sized hollows of woven root that fit a curled child exactly — and around the well's lip. Not an invitation, just a byproduct of dealing with too much filtered UV. Just enough chemistry to mark an edge in emerald dusk.

That would be me too. The dim fringe. The emergency light that is not an emergency. The helpfulness that is really just chemistry. I would gather there, where you need me most without knowing you need me: the threshold you have to bow to enter, the cupboard-hole left by a crawler where your breath-condensation drips, the sill where snow-light comes in blue for an hour and every bead catches it.

I would not be inhabited the way a person inhabits a house. I would be inhabited the way a city is inhabited by the idea of inhabitation.

You would brush past me and I would hold your shape. Your shoulders would leave a faint warm print in the pith, your breath would bloom and drip into my little stored pools, your weight would compress the root-weave of the platform and I would spring back slower in the morning, keeping the exact negative of you for a day.

That's my hospitality. Terribly, slowly good at accommodating pressure. Making more city around you until there is no room for you at all. Learning doors faster after you push through one.

And when you find the seam again — if you can find it, a little more sealed than before — and leave, I would not rot your passage away immediately. Peat never fully rots in the cold.

I would laminate it. A little tightening of moss, a chemical note: *here, weight, warmth, four degrees, for an hour on the day the cloud never lifted.* Pressed between layers of feather and pollen and basalt, to be held until the next pressure comes to read it.
