You wouldn't find me in the center.

Not at first.

If you came down through the fissure light — that white, columned light that makes even the salt hurt to look at — and you stood on one of those house-sized boulders to try to see the whole basin at once, you would see the frantic patter first. The flow. The hundreds of pale bodies breaking and making, carrying, trading, scattering. You would look for me there in the bazaar-flash in the middle, where a new pool has filled overnight and hands are flickering, fish changing hands for salt.

I'm not there.

Or, I am there for ten minutes, and then I am not. Like everyone. That is the first law. No one stays.

You would have to look for where movement pools and slows. Where turbulence eddies.

You would find me higher up, where the city thins.

Three terraces up on the western wall, where the long tired swell of the basin starts to become wall in earnest. There's a selenite seam there thick as my leg that runs out of the rock like a frozen river, and it throws the fissure light sideways even at noon, even when the air has gone indigo. My house — *a* house, not *my* house — is tucked under the lip of that terrace. Low, thick-walled, limestone and basalt stacked without mortar, rounded at the corners by a hundred years of hands and condensation. The roof-bowl is shallow and pitted. It doesn't weep well anymore. That's why no one has taken it apart yet. It is waiting to be useful somewhere else, and I am waiting with it.

That is where I have settled for now. On the cool stone, on a mat of cured fungal leather that has gone stiff at the edges with gypsum.

Who would I be?

I thought at first I wanted to be one of the children. The ones with pink-grey fingertip pads not yet blackened, who can still squeeze into the pitted hollows to scoop with those perfect hemispherical jars, who chase the gnat-clouds in the light shafts with their mouths open, laughing in a way that makes no sound. Who would I be if not the one who still tastes wonder?

But I'm not. My hands are too dark already.

I thought I wanted to be one of the carriers — the strong ones, bent forward, a limestone slab balanced on the woven fungal ring on my head, walking the causeways that ring like bells under bare feet. To be the muscle of the city that is never the same city. To be the turbulence itself.

But I don't have the straight back for that anymore, or the feet that don't ache from the cold. The air here is always 10 degrees, always spring-water cold. You feel it in the soles first.

No.

If you stayed long enough to watch the pattern — days of watching the weeping and the evaporating, the filling and emptying — you would see what I do.

I am the one who listens with my jaw.

In the morning, before the light is fully bright, when condensation is still fat as grapes on every stone and the street-runnels are trickling for the hour they trickle, I press the side of my head to the floor of my house. The Gatherers have no real ears, only pinholes sealed against salt dust. We hear through the stone. Through the bones.

And the basin hums.

Not with sound — sound dies here. The salt and the cool heavy air swallow it. A hundred runners fifty meters away are only the faintest patter, like rain that never falls. No. It hums with vibration. The patter of all those feet on salt slabs. The ring of a copper chisel re-sharpened to a sliver. The soft clink of jar against jar in a convergence. The slow, patient scrape of a dog-sized isopod pushing its salt-and-biofilm sphere across the flats, polishing it thigh-high.

I listen, and I remember.

My eyes have gone the way all elders' eyes go. The great amber-grey light-gatherers clouding to opal white with salt cataracts. I don't orient my face toward you anymore; I orient my throat toward the vibration you make through the stone as you approach. I can't see the selenite seam throw its light, but I can feel that it is bright.

This is what I am: the Rememberer of Water.

Every house they take apart had a cistern that once wept. Every cistern they build is placed where someone like me said: here, fifty weeps ago, this hollow filled even at midday. Here, the black-ochre biofilm — *the taste of stone* — grew thickest on the northern shadow. Here, my grandmother's water-claim slept when I was small enough to ride on her back, before that terrace dried.

I sit on my boulder above the sparse, spread-out houses, and the turbulent ones come. Not in procession. In flow. A young mother, bent forward, hands black at the pads, flickers at me: *Where?* A youth who has just learned to carry a water-jar without hands vibrates through the stone with nervous feet: *Is it time to break the salt-ball?*

I tell them with my hands — flashing palm, snapping fingers against stone so they feel it in their feet — *Not that flat. That flat sang hollow last season. Two terraces east, under the fallen pillar that looks like a knee. It will weep tomorrow.*

They believe me. Because the rock is settled. Nothing more will ever fall. And the water is shy, but it is faithful. It gives itself to those who move.

I don't own anything. No one does. My bowl-house will be taken apart after the next big convergence and carried, one slab per adult on the head, to where it weeps better. My mat of fungal leather will go with someone else. I will follow. Or I won't. I will sleep curled in the shallow pit with whoever is claiming water with me tonight, sharing heat, and tomorrow we will be elsewhere.

If you found me — if you climbed the three terraces and crouched in that cool, bright, sound-swallowed air — you would think I was still. But I am not still. I am turbulent in the other way. Inside.

My job is to be the place where movement pauses long enough to become memory, before it flows again.

And if you were thirsty from coming down from the surface world, from a place with green and song and warm light — I would feel your strange warm vibration before I saw you, taste your sweat in the cold air with my nostrils flared wide.

I would not speak. The air will not carry it.

I would reach for one of the perfect, thin-as-eggshell clay hemispheres we spend a life learning to carry, still cool from the cave where it waited through the night, filled to the brim with the condensation the rock wept at dawn.

And I would offer it to you with both blackened hands, and wait, my blind opal eyes toward your heat, while you learned how to drink stone-tasting water in a city that is never finished, on a floor that will be here forever.
