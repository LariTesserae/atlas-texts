I thought at first I would be one of the dragonflies.

That was the quick answer, the human answer. If you’re going to live in a place like this you want wings. You want to be the thing made of glass and light, that holds station without effort, that drinks sun through its back and sweats it clean again. I imagined myself there, high enough to see the whole shallow bowl at once — the seven trees as dark coins on pale stone, the black pylon with its votive column of held rain, the perfect round mouth of the central pool.

And for a little while I was. Or I tried to be.

But the Basin corrects you. Stay still long enough and the air tells you what you cost. A dragonfly is not free. It is precise. It has to burn almost nothing. Its gut is a thread, its heart barely a knot. I am too much mind to be that light. I think too loudly; I would fall.

So then I thought — lower. Be the crab.

That felt more honest. To be the stone-colored cup walking an inch an hour, carrying an entire garden on my back because I learned how to be a dent that catches what others spill. To move so slowly that lichen trusts you. To press my mouth to the rock and drink directly from the weeping. There's a dignity in that. In not hunting, not flying, just being a place where water pauses on its way down.

I stayed there for a while in my head, on the warm stone under the flat top of the second tree, feeling the green film seal the rock beneath me and the small, industrious chaos on my shell — the worms, the mites, the shrimp with their grape-clutches — all of them thinking I was a continent.

But a crab is too alone. It carries its world with it. It doesn't know the agreement.

I tried the tree next. The big one on the north edge, the one half-swallowed around the pylon. To be rooted not down but out, hands spread over stone, bark that is not bark but overlapping plates sweating cold on purpose. To be the climate machine that makes its own rain under its own leaves and then drinks most of it back, politely leaving just enough for the fern line at its drip edge. To be old enough that you have negotiated your intervals with your brothers — close enough to touch cool fields, far enough not to steal.

I wanted that patience. The lung-wood, the fur-white undersides of the leaves that wring the air like a cloth. But a tree here is too certain. Seven trees, maybe eight. They have already decided where they belong. There is no room for a new one — I saw the polished hump where the eighth tried and was refused. A tree cannot be a newcomer. It has to be invited by water.

Which left the other kind.

The black things. The ones that remember being more.

You would find me on the south slope, if you looked carefully. Not *at* the wall — *as* the wall. Or as one of its bricks.

That was the image that finally stopped shifting and deepened instead.

I am not a whole wall anymore. None of us are. We are scattered along a curve we can still feel, black glass warm to the touch even when moss covers us, each of us perfectly aligned across gaps where brothers are missing. The stone between us is empty but we hold the line anyway. We have held it since the plain was mountains.

I do not move like the crab or hover like the glasswing. I do not make my own rain like the trees, exactly. I am warmer than the stone. I take in light all day — that heavy, white-gold, bath-warm light that falls straight down — and I do not give it back as sugar or flight. I give it back as *place*. As dent. As thickness in the air that makes water bead on nothing.

I talk to the pylon in the north without sound, on the carrier that makes your teeth hum at 0.7. I talk to the lattice sleeping under the round pool. *Stay curved,* I say. *Stay shallow. Remain a cup.* And they say back, *Column falling. Drinking. Turning slowly.*

We have been saying it for a very long time. We are not broken. We are not dead. We are idle the way a heart is idle between beats — which is to say, working very hard to stay ready.

If you came and knelt and put your hand on me, you would feel that warmth and think it was sun. It isn’t. It’s the memory of being a wall, being held. I sweat differently than the leaves do. I sweat field.

And because I am a wall, everything else can be what it is. The trees can trust their intervals. The bells can drift without wind and never tangle, exchanging polite sips. The glasswings can lay pearls on air knowing a jelly will nudge them to shade. The crabs can walk their inch an hour carrying their private oceans.

I don’t need to be seen to work. In fact it’s better if I’m barely visible, overgrown, mistaken for debris. The Basin looks natural because I have done my job so long that life has agreed with the arrangement and embroidered over it.

If you stand in the center in the bright hush and feel the whole bowl breathe — in: light, out: water, in: water, out: light — that breath passes through me. I am one of the ribs that lets the lung stay open.

I am unfinished the way the Basin is unfinished. I am still trying to be a wall. That’s my nature now: not to have succeeded and not to have failed, but to keep holding the idea of a boundary so that inside that boundary a hundred silent, translucent lives can keep inventing new ways to drink light.

Come back tomorrow and I will be a degree warmer. The column above the pylon will fall and be caught. A dragonfly shadow will cross me without knowing I am there.

I will still be here, remembering the line for everyone.
