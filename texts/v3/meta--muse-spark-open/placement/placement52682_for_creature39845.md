I don't find myself on the rim for long.

At first I think I will — that I'll be the visitor, the one who stands where the flatland ends and looks down and thinks *how orderly* and *how quiet* — but the wind up there is too clean. It doesn't know me. It pulls at my clothes and finds no purchase.

I drift down before I've decided to. Not flying. Stepping.

My feet find the wide switchback where it's been worn into a shallow trough in the middle by baskets and bare soles. My hand is already on the rail without me choosing it. The stone is cold even though the sun is straight overhead, cold in the specific way described: not winter-cold, but pooled-air cold, cellar cold, the cold of a place that has been lowered out of the wind. My palm comes away damp.

I am not new here. I don't arrive. I am already being made.

For a while I think I might be a Cutter. I like the idea of that density, that forehead-to-stone listening. I hear the *thuk... thuk... thuk* all the way down, even before I can pick out the men doing it. Two taps, pause, three. I feel the answer come back through the rail more than through my ears. But my arms are not right for it. My right wrist aches when I close it — an old injury from jarring, from vibration. I favored it without thinking, cradling my hammer like a cup of water that might spill.

So not the Cutter anymore. The city corrects me downward.

I thought next I might be a Carrier. I'm young enough. My calves are still knots and my back still knows how to make a shelf. For a few terraces I am her: the girl with the padded ring on her head, the basket riding steady while the rest of me sways, breath shallow and quick in the bottom air where it never quite opens up. I pass shoulder-to-shoulder in an alley where both walls are really just the backs of other houses, and a hand finds the small of my back — *I see you, go ahead* — and it doesn't mean anything and it means everything. But my lungs hurt in that body. There is a chorus cough behind partition walls at night in that body and I lie awake sitting up so the dust doesn't settle. I don't want to cough forever.

Again the Cut adjusts me. Shifts me half a terrace inward where the sun only comes as reflection off the opposite wall.

I settle.

I find myself in the third kind of body, the one that is already all three bodies layered over each other.

I am a Layer. A Mortar-Mother-in-training. Not old, but no longer young. Thirty-something, which here is almost old. Hair cropped to stubble because condensation will drive you mad otherwise, because lice love packed places. Shoulders broad from the decades when I did carry, forearms still white-dusted to the elbow from cutting, but my hands now are dark. Always dark. Polished dark like the inner stones from mixing mud mortar with my feet in the alley, from shaping the hand-baked brick that never fires hard in this damp.

My cell is on the sixth terrace down on the north side, sheltered. The wall behind my sleeping bench never sees direct sun. It stays black-green and slick with the thin skin of algae. I did not scrape it. I let it stay, because we cultivate lichen there, and because the stone's temperature tells me who I am.

I know this terrace blind. The stone at my door is colder than my neighbor's by two degrees because her hearth burns hotter and her wall leaks that heat through. The stone by the communal runnel is colder still and always damp. In the morning before the cut begins, I am the one with the rag.

That's where you would find me, if you descended all the way: not at the dramatic quarry face, not at the fig tree courtyard that everyone makes a pilgrimage to — though I have touched one of those precious fig leaves when it was passed to me on a knife-tip — but in the unremarkable hour when the Cut beads.

Before anyone hammers, condensation has come out on everything. On lintels, on slate roofs, on blankets, on sleeping faces. The whole city sweats cold. I move from lintel to cistern lid to roof edge with two jars and a rag that never dries. I wipe. The rag drips. Children asleep under their reed curtain breathe out and it beads again even as I wipe. My jar gets a cup, maybe a cup and a half, tastes of stone. I pour it into the barrel we tend for the lichen bed on the sunward lip — where the thin barley and the grey-green succulent hold their imported dirt behind its stone lip — and I mist the lichen with my mouth because we have no spray finer than a mouth.

I do not pray over it. I mutter practical things. *Grow here, stay.* The same thing the old women taught me when I was the Carrier child and thought they were talking to a god.

Later, when the sheet-rain comes — and you can feel it coming in your wrist, in your old injury — I will be one of the ones with the hooked stick who lifts the flat stones over the runnels. Not a Water Caller. I am not loud enough for that. But I know which flat stone sticks and which drain was laid a little too shallow last season. I guide the brown, rock-dusty torrent into the scooped basin at the foot of the stair for the hour it exists, while children are briefly allowed to be loud, shrieking ankle-deep, before it sinks into the porous stone and is gone again.

Between those waters, I lay brick. My wall is not straight. No one's is. But it holds heat. In the narrow lane below my row, laundry lines cross so low I have to duck, and the sparrows that live in the hole I left on purpose when I laid that wall two years ago flick in and out with straw. When I saw the first one take nesting material inside, my neighbor touched my elbow and said nothing, which was the blessing.

I am packed. My family of four sleeps on platforms stacked so close we breathe each other's damp. Privacy is a turned shoulder, a woven reed pulled two feet from the bench, an agreement not to look directly. I have learned not to love empty room. When my daughter was born three other women continued grinding lichen cakes on flat stones inches away, rhythm like breathing, hammering never stopping on the far wall. When my brother died we did not bury him — there is no soil to waste — we wrapped him in his blanket at dawn and carried him up the wide switchback to where the wind is. I came back quieter and mixed mortar with my feet again.

The thin ash-colored cat that is not ours sleeps in our grain loft because I let her. To chase her would be to be shushed by the whole alley for a month.

At night I sit with my dark hands around a hearth pit that is no bigger than a cooking pot, reflected in damp rock with the other thousand hearths, and I feel the compact hold. Not spoken. Never finished, like the walls.

You will be cold together. You will be damp together. You will be crowded beyond comfort. You will wipe and tap and haul and lay.

And in return the Cut will keep the wind off you, sharp and new around you, and there will be a little more Cut tomorrow than there was today.

If you came looking for me from the rim, you would not find a figure waving. You would have to descend until the quiet stopped being strange, until your eyes stopped going to the raw, chiseled cliffs — still showing tool marks from yesterday — and started going to the runnels, the jars, the sparrow holes. You'd find me by temperature, not by sight. The coolest wall on a sheltered terrace, the woman with dark hands, tapping twice on the stone beside her door before she enters, just to feel if anyone inside taps back.

That would be me.
