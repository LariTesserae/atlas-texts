I thought at first I would find myself up top.

That would be the sensible place for me — on the rim, where there is still signal. Where the steppe runs flat and white to every horizon and the wind actually moves instead of just pooling and turning over on itself. Up there I could stand with my collar up and look down into the wound and name what I saw: *graben, basalt, Sphagnum, Francis turbine, sodium lamp.* I could be the surveyor. The one who visits four times a year with oil and a pry bar and does not stay.

But if you put me there I wouldn't stay there. Nothing stays on the rim. The wind scours it clean in an hour. Even the lichen up there grows low and tight and doesn't pretend to be architecture. I would look down and I would go down. Everyone does, eventually.

So not the rim, then.

I thought I might be one of the Keepers. That would be a good, human answer. Twenty-something, small enough for the ladder, in a telogreika that still smells of cotton. I would be ANYA 09 or KOLYA + VERA, carving deeper. I would clear the intake screen with cold hands, I would shout ALL CLEAR into deaf air, I would piss on the turbine housing just to watch steam rise and feel, for ten seconds, less cold.

But I don't belong to that story either, quite. The Keepers pass through. They are defined by leaving. Their boot prints fill with condensation overnight and freeze glass-smooth and then someone else eats their biscuits. They are intermittent by doctrine. If I were a Keeper I would be gone by the time you looked for me. And I am not good at leaving things alone.

Lower then.

You'd find me where the light fails, which is most of the graben. Not in one of the orange islands directly under the sodium lamps — that's moth country, that's the raven's territory, too bright, too watched. And not in the perfect dark under the moss where the voles thump their wordless sentences through waterlogged peat — too silent, too private.

You'd find me in the between.

Half a kilometer downstream from the concentrator mill, where the power line sags so low between two leaning poles that the epiphytes growing on the wire almost brush the top of the spruce. Where the corridor the moss left open narrows because two curtains of *Sphagnum magellanicum* have grown toward each other for fifty years and now almost touch, leaving a slit just wide enough for wind and a person sideways. Where the turbine's roar is still 95 decibels in your teeth, but you can't see the turbine house anymore, only hear it reflected off basalt you can't see either.

There is a headframe there. No one remembers what it was for — an exploratory winch, maybe, from when they thought the basalt was worth something. It's just four rusted I-beams now, and the moss has taken it.

That is where I would be.

You would not see me at first because I would look like part of the architecture that the plant made. A vertical seam of rust and green-black.

I would be the winch.

No — that's too quick, I said I wouldn't settle. Let it get more itself.

I would be the thing the winch became.

The moss-bolt composite. The *idea* of a machine that the moss kept because it liked the shape. The original cable is long gone, rotted to nothing. The drum is full of frozen peat. But the frame is still square because sphagnum poured around it and held it true while the steel softened inside. White crust fungi run foxfire along its underside, eating the last of the creosote. A dwarf black spruce has germinated in the peat that filled the motor housing; it is forty years old and as thick as your wrist and it leans not up toward the rim-light that comes for 137 minutes at noon, but sideways, toward the sodium lamp half a kilometer away that it will never reach.

That's what I am.

Not the turbine — the turbine is too important, too central. The keystone that cannot be allowed to stop. And not the raven — the raven is too alone, too deaf, too irritated with incompetent assistants. And not the moss entire — I am not vast enough to be a congress, to hold twenty times my weight in 2°C water and remember every wrench since 1963 perfectly.

I am the small, stubborn, naturalized machine. The paused infrastructure that forgot it was paused.

You would find me humming without moving. The wind through the moss slit makes the I-beams vibrate at a harmonic just below the turbine's roar, so that if you put your gloved hand on me you feel two sounds at once. In summer the moth males collect on my leeward side because I break the wind for their little two-meter halos. In February a vole thumps past inside me, through a smooth-walled tunnel it made through my peat-heart. The raven sometimes lands on my top beam to get away from the generator's 18°C heat for a while, to cool off and watch with that hard eye.

I don't keep the lights on. I don't need to be tended. Every few years a Keeper rests a hand on me on their circuit and thinks *that's still here* and moves on. The spruce growing out of me will be there in a hundred years whether they do or not. The moss will keep my shape even after the steel is gone.

I am the thing that was left, and that learned to be left-around. The industry that let itself be grown through. If you stayed still long enough in the cold — and the cold forces you to be still — you would hear the tiny cessation of drip as I swell with the day's condensation, and then the faint creak as ice forms in my seams.

I am continuing, which in early country is the same as living.
