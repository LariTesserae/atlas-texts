I don't find myself at the center. Not at first.

If I come down that throat of young rock - that slide of sharp limestone and basalt still angry from being born - my boots would want the middle of things. The flat of the valley floor, the black pools, the white flash of the blind fish. A place to stand and look out at the scale and say *I am here.*

But the valley doesn't have a middle. Not the way a surface valley does. It's a mile across and longer than sight and the vault is so high it stops being a ceiling and becomes a low sky that sweats. If you stand in the middle you are nowhere. Sound bounces off the fault scarps until direction dies, until the chitter of the bats and the croak of the sirens and the papery thunder of wings comes from everywhere and nowhere. You can't hold the middle. It holds you and erases you with condensation.

So I let myself drift.

For a while I thought I would be a moth. There would be a kind of peace in that - ash-and-bone colored, dusty glass wings, tongue longer than my body to drink cold sweat off stone. Rising because the others rise, settling because the others settle, a pulse that doesn't need a reason. To be number, not kind. To be so many that losing me wouldn't matter. To be food and know it and not flee, just rise and settle, rise and settle, and let my fine black droppings feed the pools below.

But I am not good at letting go that completely. I would still be listening for direction.

Then I thought bat - needle-nosed, soot-black, thumb-small, hung in a shingle that goes on for miles. Memory instead of map, because the valley never changes shape. Half of me always awake while the other half sleeps. The only warm place in the whole cool equilibrium tucked under my own guano, steaming. But the bats never have silence, not even inside their heads. They scream above hearing just to know where the wall is. I would get tired of screaming.

I thought about being stone itself. The green-grey skin on every seep - bacteria and lichen and fungi braided so tight you can't name them apart. The true forest, flat against the rock. Eating basalt. Making food from damp air and dim. Softening a sharp edge by a millimeter every hundred years. That is a noble life, but it is too patient even for me. I want to move, even if only ten feet in a lifetime.

So lower. To the water. And there, I find where I would actually be.

Not in the deep black pools where the blind fish school and turn as one when a shadow falls. I would be in the thin streams, the ones no deeper than a wrist, cutting those quick, sharp little canyons across the floor. On my belly in water that never floods and never dries. 

I would be a siren.

Skin like skim-milk, translucent enough that you can see the dark line of my gut, and those huge external gills fanned out on either side of my head like red ferns, pulsing, pulsing, always pulsing because the water is so still I have to move it myself. I do not hop. I do not need to. I was hatched in this riffle and I will die in this riffle, having never moved ten feet, and that is not a tragedy here. That is agreement.

My stretch of stream is maybe as long as two of your strides, between one limestone lip and the next. The water comes over it cold and clear and full of moth-dust, and I sit with my mouth open and let it come. Around me, flank to flank, my kin do the same. We are so crowded we touch. When one of us shifts, the others shift, not out of startle but out of courtesy - there is only so much current.

Where I am is loud, but my kind of loud fits inside it. Under the bats' chitter and the moths' thunder, we make the low sound - the belching croak that says *I am still here, this water is taken.* We don't sing to find each other across distance. There is no distance. We sing to say: this riffle is full, this riffle is held, do not try to push in. And because the vault throws every sound back until it has nowhere to go, my croak comes back to me off the scarp a second later, smaller, and I feel the shape of my own stretch of valley in it.

If you crouched - boots always wet, hands always cold - you would find me by being still longer than you think a person can be still. At first you would see only water over stone. Then a gill would pulse. Then an eye, black and lidless, that doesn't blink when condensation falls from sixty meters up and pocks the surface. I would not flee. Fleeing wastes what little heat I have. I would just watch you with that eye and keep filtering.

And once or twice in a long life, one of the angular people would come. Not crashing, never crashing. A pale shape detaching from pale stone, long-limbed, long-fingered, ears cupped and turning, moving along the terrace the way water moves - finding the angle you swore wasn't a path. One of the Flint-Knappers. A child maybe, learning to be still before learning to knap, or an old one gone white-eyed like my cousins the fish, led by clicks.

She would kneel at my riffle. Her siren-skin cape would drip onto my water and I would taste the difference. Her huge amber eyes would catch the phosphor and shine, but she wouldn't be looking. She'd be listening the way I listen - for the different plop, the different ripple.

Her hand - wide-nailed, cold as mine - would come into the current. Not fast. She knows we sit mouth-open, flank to flank, and that we are bitter if you don't gut us, but filling if you do. She would choose - not me, maybe the brother beside me who has grown a little too slow this season - and lift. A quick turn, gills discarded, belly opened with a flint flake struck that morning and already bright at the edge. She would eat there, close enough I could feel the warmth of her breath for the first time all year, and then she would wipe her hands in my stream and be gone between two angles of basalt.

And where her knee had pressed, she would leave a small hollow in the gravel. By evening the current would smooth it. By morning the lichen-dust would settle over it. A week later you might find her flake there, one per acre, bright and lonely, and think *someone was here.*

But I would still be here. Mouth open. Gills pulsing red in the dim. Croaking my part of the hum that never stops, in the cool that never shifts, under the low sky that never stops sweating.

That is where you would find me, if you looked close enough and long enough to see anything at all in this place that has learned not to fight dimness but to be dim forever.

Low, wet, still, and perfectly, impossibly content to be ten feet wide for a lifetime.
