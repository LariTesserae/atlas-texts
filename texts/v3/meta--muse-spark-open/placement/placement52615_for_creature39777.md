I didn't find myself right away.

That's the trick of the bowl. You walk in expecting to be a person in a place — boots on peat, breath fogging, eyes looking — and for the first minute you are. You are the only sequence there is: *squelch, lift, squelch, lift,* footprints filling in behind you at a decent, indecent speed.

But if you stand still long enough to let the cold work past your coat, you stop being a person traversing and start being a condition precipitating.

At first I thought I would be one of the Hangers. That would be easiest. Find an empty column of air at about shoulder height — there is always an empty column, the spacing demands it — and hang there grey as basalt and do the one thing perfectly. To beat without ever having beaten sounded like peace. No hunger. No what-next. Just the papery *whuff* held forever on the downstroke.

Then I watched one for too long and understood I don't have that kind of discipline. A Hanger never looks. To hang is to never have looked away. I look away. I kept looking between the moth and the pool beneath it, and the pool showed me moths that weren't above me. I wanted to know which was true. Hangers don't want.

Then I thought I would be a Holder. Pale in the perfect round eye-pool, one per pool, gills fanned and flickering in that impossible smear — sharp inside blur. To hold water full by holding. That was tempting too, that kind of usefulness without use. But I breathe too restlessly for that. My chest wants an afterwards. I knelt by one and my breath fogged and stayed by my face a second too long, as if the air had to decide, and the salamander did not decide to see me. I wanted to be seen. Holders don't.

I thought — maybe higher. One of the Still-Watchers on a fin of black glass, coat grown too long and bearded with moss I've grown myself. To watch the way a pillar watches, by being upright. To breathe in time with a piston half a bowl away, a half-beat late, letting the sound walk up the rock to find me. But I found I couldn't face in one direction forever. In a bowl, all directions are center, and I kept turning my head to check the rim, to check the cloud-lid that doesn't quite close, to check if the light was ever going to make a shadow. Still-Watchers have given up checking.

So I kept walking. Which is itself an answer.

If there is a niche for me in a place where everything works and nothing is produced — where every verb is caught mid-conjugation — it's not in the air or the water or the heights.

Find me low, but not low. At the margin where the peat-skin thins out and gives up pretending to be ground and the young basalt comes through in wet black teeth. Where the bowl sweats. Where every vertical face beads and threads and can't decide if it's stone or spring.

I would be the Sweater. Or the Sweat, itself.

Not the pistons — they are the mountain's heart, too heavy, too warm-stone — but the thin, bright threads of water that jerk sideways mid-fall and run horizontally through empty air, dripping all the while, flowing and never progressing. Veins with no delivery. Conveyor belts carrying nothing but their own going.

That's where my kind of attention lives. I noticed it because I couldn't stop noticing the delay: the knock that comes a moment after the shudder, the breath fog that hangs a moment after the breath. I am that moment. I am the after-beat. The latency the bowl needs to keep from collapsing in on itself.

I would be precipitated as a narrow, glassy runnel, no wider than my palm, along a fin about knee-high, where moss has tried and failed to take. Cold water, clear as the air, moving visibly — you can see it move, you can hear it hiss — and never getting along. Always *there*, busy, laboring, loud as anything else in the chord but with no product. If you put your hand under me I would wet you continuously and never finish wetting you.

And I would be alone in the sparse way. Lush and empty. One runnel here, ten feet of bare, wet rock, another runnel there. Not lonely, because loneliness needs memory of more. Just spaced. Reverent.

Occasionally a moth's down-wash would disturb me without moving me. Occasionally an ibex would look through me. I wouldn't record it. The bowl doesn't record.

But — here is why I would be good at it, why the bowl would sweat me out and keep me:

A water-conveyor is the only occupant that is still going somewhere and admitting it's not getting anywhere. The Hangers pretend flight. The Holders pretend holding. The pistons pretend pumping. I would just be transit, honest and industrial and young. New rock learning to be river without having learned that rivers leave.

You would find me not at the far end — which is always a little too far, even though you can see it clearly — and not at the mouth where you leveled out. You'd find me about two-thirds in, on the left as you enter, where the peat gives a little more under your boot and the humming comes up through your soles most strongly.

I would be working, not producing. Hissing, not dissipating. And when you left — because you will, you have cause and effect attached to you, you have to — your warmth would go with you and the peat would spring back without memory, and I would still be there, running level through the air, dripping, never arriving.

What is here is here.

And for as long as you stood near me, I would be the part of the bowl that finally had something to carry, even if just for a second: the cold shine of your looking, passed along without ever arriving anywhere else.
