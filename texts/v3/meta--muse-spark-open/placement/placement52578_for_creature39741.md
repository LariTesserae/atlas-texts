You wouldn't find yourself standing where you are now.

Not out in the open on the flat pan where the heat comes back off the stone in sheets. You tried that, the first hour. The sun at four thousand meters doesn't fall — it *presses*. It found every seam in your hat, every place you'd missed with sunscreen, and made the cracked crust ring hollow under your boots until you felt conspicuous just for having weight and making noise. Nothing lives *on* the plateau. You learn that fast.

No — you'd find yourself *in* it. Tucked.

Maybe thirty meters off the main switchback where the narrow-gauge sings before the train comes, where the rust-red sandstone gives way under the black basalt cap and an arroyo has sawn down ten meters — ten meters wide, only one meter deep, polished to a shine like a river of dark glass that forgot it was a river. The north wall undercuts a little. There, the stone stays cool to the touch all day.

That's where you'd be.

You'd have started the morning as one thing and ended it as another, because this place doesn't let you settle on a single image early. It peels you.

At 4:30 a.m. you'd have been human. Fully. One of the caretakers on shift rotation, though you wouldn't call yourself that yet — you'd call yourself awake. Up before the evaporation, which is the only honest way to be up here. Your trike — fat balloon tires so you don't cut the crust, toolbox rattling — is parked in the shadow of a condensation tower, that huge rusted lattice with black fins that was dripping when nothing else was. Headlamp off. You're sitting on the basalt lip of a pan with your loose pale clothes, your damp neck-flap, your canteen still full because you haven't needed it yet.

And you're waiting with everyone else.

The fog comes over the rim like you were told it would, but being told doesn't prepare you. It's not fog the way lowlanders mean fog. It's a river, silent and heavy, calf-deep, cold enough to make your teeth ache when you breathe it. It pours into the saucer-basin and just *lies there*, and every corrugated screen for a hundred meters thrums as it starts to pour.

Then the fizzing starts.

You'd find yourself, for the first twenty minutes, not as a caretaker at all, but as part of the Ones Who Drink The Morning.

Because you can't help it. You tilt east like the ibex do, like the succulents do, like the screens do. You watch the pan come alive and you forget to check your gauges.

An inch of tea-colored water reflects the rinsed, sharpening sky perfectly. And in it — fairy shrimp, upside-down, frantic, laying a lifetime in an hour. A pillar of gnats rises off it like smoke, lives, mates, dies, makes a raft of bodies on the surface. The salt butterflies come down in a white shroud, thousands, until you can't see water at all. You're close enough to hear their tongues unroll.

You're supposed to be logging the overflow from Tower 7 into buried Cistern B. Instead you're watching an armored woodlouse the size of your thumb-joint shoulder its way past your boot, slate-grey plates with pale rims, moving at the speed of cooling wax toward the retreating damp line. *Shhhhk. Shhhhk.* There are thousands per square meter. You hear them now.

And then you hear the click. *Tik...tik.* The rust-and-black lizard doing push-ups on the hot rock next to your trike. You would be, in that moment, prey and predator and witness all at once — because the hawk overhead, underside near-black so it doesn't glare, isn't watching you. It's watching the hare that is watching the lizard that is watching the woodlice that are drinking the algae that drank the fog that you are also, in your own clumsy human way, trying to drink by proxy through machines.

You'd find yourself laughing, quietly, because the air is too thin to waste.

By 10 a.m. the pan is gone. Just dry-cracked polygons and the faint smell of ozone and creosote. The heat has returned as a thermal weight. The hawks hang highest. Everything that needed to move has moved.

That's when you'd find yourself being *industrial*.

Not in the big, smoky sense. In the plateau's sense. You swing a leg over the trike and check the slow piston house — *chk ... chk ... chk* — pushing water into the buried cistern. You tap the gauge the way a bee taps a seep. You clear a filter clogged with white butterfly wings and gnat bodies. You find a mud-dauber nest baked hard as ceramic directly over the warm exhaust vent, five layers deep, and you leave it, because the old hands told you the wasps bake faster there and stay off the electronics. You find a burrow entrance, clean and round, freshly kicked dirt apron sorted by size under the rail trestle, and you leave that too, because the blue fox that lives there eats the pack-foragers that chew cables.

You are linking wire and pipe more than road. You are catching fog the way the beetle catches fog on its bumpy back. Your long pale clothes cover everything. You speak quietly or not at all. Sound carries a kilometer here — a check valve closing goes *thunk* in your teeth — and yelling is bad manners and bad hydrology.

And if you stayed long enough — a week, a season, the time it takes for a knee-high acacia thorn-bush to decide you are not a threat — you'd find yourself becoming less human still, in the good way.

You'd be the pack-forager by noon, even if you never see one. You'd start collecting. A copper washer shaken loose from a pylon. A perfect basalt flake. A ballpoint pen lost by last shift. You'd tuck it into a crevice of your container-bunkhouse in the tamarisk grove — that deeper arroyo where the limestone finally let real shade happen — not because you need it, but because something in you wants a midden. Something in you understands the thousand-year core that paleobotanists drill from those rodent walls, layer after layer of what the plateau borrowed. The plateau takes care of what you leave and borrows what it needs. You leave the light on in the abandoned engine house without meaning to, and the geckos fill the fixture with their nest and close the circuit, and the light swings though there's no wind inside, and the moths come, and the cliff-owl — that low two-note hoot from inside the basalt — comes to the moths. You learn not to fix it.

By evening you'd be stone.

Not metaphorically. You'd be sitting in your slot canyon six meters deep where ferns grow in a vertical line, back against rock that has finally started to tick as it cools — *tick, tick, ping* — differential contraction, woodlice retracting, seed pods popping. The ibex file moves past you, ash-grey, wide splayed feet that stick instead of clatter, old female pausing to lick salt off your overflow pipe without fear. Forty, fifty of them, browsing the air. You don't move. You have learned to stilt-walk and freeze like the hares with their pink, heat-dumping ears.

At 6:30 every throat makes the chorus. Not song. Drinking. Lizards, birds, pumps, insects — *thunk...thunk...thunk* in a round. Every screen, every succulent spine, every tamarisk leaf, every pylon tilts that fraction east.

You tilt too.

The fog spills over the lip again like milk, hits hot rock, vanishes, and for a second you smell it: wet dust, iron, warm ceramic, animal musk, ozone from your own inverters.

You'd find yourself there — in the crack between sandstone and basalt, between human and animal, between machine and organism — belonging to the rock the way the ibex does, not on it but *in* it.

Sparse as constellations if someone looks from a plane. Dense as a coral reef if you know how to crouch and not move for five minutes.

Waiting, with everything else, for the invisible river.
