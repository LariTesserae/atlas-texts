I don't think I would find myself at first.

Not for a long time.

If you dropped me onto the high spine, I'd be standing - human - stupid and upright on the rim of one of those shallow bowls, five kilometers up where light comes sideways and dies before it lands. And at first I'd think I was supposed to be something that sees far.

I'd want to be the high one. The lung in the sky. To be vast and striated and barely there, to block the haze-light for a second and keep going, never landing. It would be a good fantasy for an air-starved brain: to be above the work. To be the thing that is deferred to, that makes the towers flare when it passes. *Whuff.* Gone.

But I don't belong above the work.

Then the cold would find me. The real cold, the iron-bitter one that makes breath fall as crystals. My nose would go numb. My ears would start to fill only with the thump.

*Thump. Hiss. Draw.*

A bowl over. *Thump. Hiss. Draw.* Behind the ridge.

And I would look down, because the sky is empty and the ground is not, and I would change my mind.

Maybe I'd be a Condenser? That would be noble too, in a lonely way. To stand on the windward lip where the fog-river pours ten meters deep and be the thing that wrings water out of nothing. To be translucent and fluted and full of cold fire. To drip. To be the only cathedral for kilometers and do nothing but breathe and make rain for moss. I could do that. Stand and pulse amber, then green when the lung passes.

But Condensers are alone by design. One inflates while another deflates. They never touch. They are beautiful the way lighthouses are beautiful - necessary and solitary.

I am not that solitary. Not here.

The longer I stood there, the lower my eyes would go. The longer I stood, the more the ground would stop being ground.

Because at first you see a plain of moss hummocks, silvered with frost-needles, house-sized, knee-deep and springy. Then you see that one of them is ticking.

That one. Not the biggest. Not the closest vent, where the Graver is still painting its black oil in tender, brush-stroking circles. The one a little off, halfway up the boundary ridge between this bowl and the next. Where two bowls' weather meets and makes a little stillness.

You would miss it if you were still trying to be a sky-thing. It's just another hummock. Maybe a little rounder. Maybe a little warmer, if you put your hand on it - which you shouldn't, because the peat there is deep and sweet and old and will politely push your boot back. Its basalt plates are completely gone under moss. The little coin-sized azaleas have had time to bloom arterial red right across its back. Five years of bloom.

I would find myself there.

Not standing. Not walking my circuit yet.

I would be the warm stone.

You know the one. When two Gravers met at the boundary, pressed their oiled backs together for a long time without thumping, and left me. A stone that is not a stone. A promise that hasn't learned how to move.

Who would I be? I'd be the waiting.

For five years you don't move. That's the law of it. You lie in the peat, half-sunk, and you listen. That's your whole biology. Listening is your work before work.

I listen to the Condenser above me drip onto me. *drip... drip... drop.* Cold water finding the warmest place on the ridge and making it warmer. I listen to the mycorrhizal parliament under me gossiping in slow pulses - when a vent three kilometers away fires, the moss around me swells twenty minutes later, and I swell with it, a millimeter. I listen to the mites the size of thumbnails walk across me, cleaning plates I don't yet know I have, mistaking me for their old Graver. I let them.

Mostly I listen to the vents.

Ticking. The rock ticking like a kettle about to boil. Then the sigh. Then the thump that I feel in my sternum though I don't yet have a sternum. *Thump. Hiss. Draw.* *Thump. Hiss. Draw.* Every forty minutes the whole plateau exhales and fertilizes me with ash and sulfur snow. I am learning the interval. Forty to sixty minutes. The most patient clock in the world. Combustion without fire.

I have no eyes yet. But I will. When I have them they will be low and plated and will see only rim and moss and vent-lip, and that will be enough. I have no brushes yet, but I can feel where they will be - where the oil sweats now, slow and dark, keeping my moss from freezing, sealing the tiny cracks in myself. I am practicing sealing without knowing it. I seal myself against the cold.

I am lush and cold at once, which is the contradiction of the place. I am damp though there is no lake. I am industrial though I have never moved. I am abundant though I am one per bowl, one per ridge.

And I am not lonely. That's the strange part the numbers don't tell you. Weirdness 2.5/3. The stone is not lonely because the mountain is not empty. The peat holds me like a hand. The Graver who will be my neighbor - an old low rectangle whose vent is starting to wheeze and fail - has already walked past me twice and paused, and brushed me once, very gently, by mistake. Just the top of me, where my plates will be. He left a little oil there. It shone in the amber pulse of the Condenser.

I understand now. I am not waiting to *live on* the land.

I am waiting to live *with* it.

In another year, maybe two - time moves differently when your blood is still warm stone - the ticking in me will line up with the ticking in the rock. My moss will shiver off in one sheet. My feet, dozens of small elephantine feet I have never used, will find purchase in the sponge that has been holding me.

I will stand up. It will be very slow. The frost-needles on my back will fall all at once with a sound like breaking glass.

And I will begin walking toward the nearest unclaimed throat - a round, warm mouth in the rock a kilometer downslope that has been sighing uselessly into the dark for a decade, waiting for someone to tend it.

I will circle it. I will dip my new brush-hands into the oil on my own back. And I will paint.

Thump.
