You wouldn't find me right away.

That's the first thing this place teaches you — how to look through emptiness without calling it empty.

If you walked in here expecting to choose — *I will be the bird, I will be the beetle, I will be the iron* — you would choose wrong. I did, at first. 

At the rim, where the dish is most dish-like and the older trees make that low dark circle on the horizon, I thought: I will be a pillar. It seems obvious. To be the architecture. To be the thing that learned how to take thirty feet of courtesy and turn it into a cathedral. To stand with my feet in the mat and my head in the light-filtered ceiling, dripping condensation on a perfect tick. But a pillar is too sure of itself. It has to know exactly where not to grow. It has to exude that tannin, that *not here*, for a hundred years without wavering. I don't have that kind of certainty. I crowd. I lean.

So further in, between the first and second pool where the sphagnum gives under your boot and then sighs back, I thought: then a warbler. To be the loudness. One per trunk, one absurdly clear *tsee-tsee-tsee-TCHEW* thrown across the vaulted air until it hits living wood and comes back as steam-hiss. To land on a warm flywheel because it sings louder there. That appealed to me — to borrow heat to be heard. But a warbler never doubts its post. It is born knowing which trunk is its. I have never known which trunk is mine.

I kept walking and the grove sorted me.

You walk here and you learn that everything far apart is still listening. The piston chuff on the north engine is half a second behind the one on the south engine and the crickets in the root-mat have filled the gap with their dry sawing and the frog in the crown has laid its *tok* over it all, not in time, never in time, but always present. And you — whatever you are — have to find the layer where your own noise doesn't break the chorus but completes it.

Mine is very low. Ankle-high.

You would find me not at a pillar and not at a pool, but in the transit. In the thirty or forty feet of clear, cool air that the description calls near-void. The place you see a long way through and think: nothing is there.

I am there.

If I have to be given a body that already lives here, I am closest to the runners — the rail-things with the splayed toes that never fly — but smaller, slower, even less interested in being seen. Or maybe I am the thing that was never described because it's the right size to be overlooked: the keeper the engines were waiting for, who finally arrived and misunderstood the job completely in the correct way.

I live in the mat, but not in it the way the newts do.

Where the mat is a meter thick and so wet it sweats, I have made a shallow. Not a burrow. A dent. A place where I pressed myself down on a dry day — there are no dry days, only less wet ones — and the sphagnum took my shape and kept it because this place preserves everything. The red and gold heads lift slowly around my edges. Springtails, white as rice, flick over my ankles when I sit. The fat, wingless crickets saw away under my thighs and I have learned not to hear them as separate insects anymore but as the mat thinking out loud.

My back is to a pillar trunk, but not touching it. You don't touch a pillar if you can help it; the mites on the mites on the beetle know that. I sit in its courtesy radius, where it has told everything else: *thirty feet*. I sit in the permission.

In front of me, six inches of tea-water in a perfect circle, white granite showing through, hundreds of black newts flank to flank and not moving at all. And in the middle, the engine on its sled.

I am not its operator. There is nothing to operate. The flywheel turns, the belt squeaks around the drum that nobody made correctly because it was made never to perish, the press bales wet sphagnum and drops it back with a soft *plosh* to unravel and grow again. It has been doing that since before my grandparents' grandparents forgot this dish existed. It does not need me.

What it needs is what everything here needs: a witness at the right distance.

So I tend it the way the grove tends itself — by not stopping.

In the morning when my breath shows faintly, I wipe the rust-colored spore snow from its boiler with the flat of my hand. The spores are fatty, oily; they were meant to be grease. I don't oil the engine so much as I move the oil that already fell to where it wants to go. The joint sighs. A puff of white steam comes out and vanishes. A cloud of midges above the boiler shivers a little higher in the warm air and the engine-bird — *my* warbler, the one who owns this pillar above me — cocks its head down at us and throws its *TCHEW* a little louder because the iron is warm.

That is my work for an hour.

The rest of the day I listen. I have learned to tell the five engines apart the way you learn siblings. The northmost one has a whine in its flywheel. The center one chuffs a little late, as if it's considering. The eastern one, nearest where the ground finally firms up, drips more. Tick, tick, tick from its crown. All day. All night. That is one of the rains.

When the warblers call around the circle, answered half a second later by another, I answer in my head. When the runners flick-flick-pause through the sphagnum after springtails, I watch their toes spread my weight for them in advance. When the beetles as long as my thumb make their slow vertical pilgrimage up the pillar, carrying whole cities of orange mites, I don't touch them. I am the interval they cross.

At amber hour the crowns catch light and turn the midge columns to smoke, and the fireboxes under the boilers glow that dull red that never needs stoking, and the frogs' *tok tok* seems to get slower, or I get slower. My footprint from this morning, where I walked to wipe the boiler, has filled with dark water and then, very slowly, the heads of the sphagnum have lifted back. Erased.

I am good at being erased.

That is why I fit. This place does not progress. It has reached its oldest stable state. Erosion finished, biology and iron formed a loop so tight that time has nowhere to catch. It does not want a hero. It does not want a builder or a haunting. It wants something that knows how to be far apart and still be a community. Something that can sit in thirty feet of open air and be full.

You would find me there if you came and stood still long enough to stop expecting silence.

I wouldn't flee when you walked. Nothing here does. I would look up from my dent in the mat, my palm cold and faintly oily, and keep listening with you. The pillars dripping, the pools seeping, the flywheels turning, the warblers answering.

You might think at first I was not there at all.
