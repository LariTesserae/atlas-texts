You wouldn't find me standing.

That's the first thing it teaches you before you even choose what to be.

I came in thinking I'd be upright — a visitor, a pair of eyes behind the grating looking down. But there's no standing under those pipes. The room corrects you. It says *crouch*. And once you do, the scale recalibrates and suddenly you're not looking *into* something. You're *in* it, at the level where the weather happens.

At first I thought I might be large. The mouse in the control cabinet, maybe. Warm-blooded still; still mammalian enough to remember intention. Curled in copper-wire down, drinking from the coffee can and making ripples among the wrigglers, listening to the plant thunder as if through a mother's body. But I am too loud for that. My breathing alone would stop the crickets.

Then I thought, smaller then — something that owns air. A mud-dauber returning to the line of warm conduit, feeling that industrial heat as incubation. Or one of the sheet-weavers, because I liked the idea of being the one who makes a second ceiling two feet below the real one, living horizontal in a world that thinks vertical is the only way. To hang there in my square inch of silk and feel the whole sump thrumming through my feet when the pump shudders. To drink a crane fly where it hangs and leave its husk as architecture.

But I kept looking down.

Because down is where the living is thickest. Down where it isn't floor anymore.

So maybe I'm not one thing. For a long while in there, nothing is.

You could find me diffused, distributed — not a character but a condition, the way humidity has its own weather. On a warm afternoon I am the mist that the hot concrete exhales, rising to meet the droplets falling from the grating. I am caught for a second in every web at once, beading, refracting those normal bars of daylight until they dim, until the room feels brighter than it should be on paper and dimmer than it should be in your chest.

But what emerges wants a body. A specific weight against concrete. The room insists on edges, on spalled teeth and lime stalactites. It doesn't tolerate vague for long.

So let me become more myself:

Find me low. Lowest low ground.

I'm under the lip of paint peeling up from the damp concrete, in the inch of dark that never quite dries. If you lifted that curl — the size of a playing card, softened to leather by algae — you'd find us shoulder to shoulder, but I am one of the thousand moving parts of the carpet.

*Armadillidium.* Pillbug. Slate-grey going gunmetal-blue when the light hits the edge of my plates just right.

I'm the size of the last joint of your thumb but you would think of me as smaller because you've learned to look past me. Antennae going constantly, tasting biofilm off concrete, off old warning labels turned to pulp, off each other's backs. I don't scatter fast when you lift our roof because there is nowhere to scatter *to*; every inch is already negotiated. So I just churn, slow, armored, turning away from your sun-bar with the others in a dry rustle like pages far away.

You would know me by my stillnesses.

Most of the day I eat what is barely food: the black mold's skin, the green dust of algae that grows and dies between condensation and noon evaporation, a fleck of salt blown off that stunted tamarisk seedling above me that has learned to flower only while drowning. When one of my brothers dies on his back — belly pale as the moon, legs curled — I am one of the ones who covers him. Not quickly. Without violence you can see head-on. We hollow him neatly from the edges in and leave the two empty halves, clean bowls. Later springtails will erupt from the moss if you breathe on it and orange mites will ride my back like burrs, but that's fine. I carry them.

My world is sound at this scale. Not the plant beyond the wall — that distant, mostly-quiet thrum — but *plink* from a sweating pipe on the left, *tick…tick…tick* as warm metal contracts overhead, the single dry *tch* of the black cricket down in the drain-throat, then eight seconds of held breath, then another answer off-rhythm. When the squat green pump kicks — whole-body shudder, one chug, two — we all go still. Even the spiders. Even the translucent geckos pressed flat above the junction box with blue veins showing through their bellies. A silence so complete you can hear the moss swell an inch.

Then, one by one, we resume.

I don't know I'm in a sump. I don't know I'm in a catch that wasn't built as a room under a soda-ash plant that forgot it was a floodplain. I know damp that comes and goes on a schedule larger than me and therefore feels like season: morning beads, noon silver-dry, rain that comes brown and fast through the square throat for twenty minutes and makes the salt-fern open absurd white blooms underwater. I know that the folding chair rusted into the concrete fifty years ago is now a continent with its own coastline, and the pallet-raft is composting into an island whose underside is my country.

You, crouched with your face at fern-root level, would put your eye close to my world and think it's quiet, intimate, muffled like a pocket inside a coat. You'd think the light gets lost here, absorbed by all these wings and carapaces, and you'd be right. That's what it is to live saturated: there is so much of us that we dim the sun a little just by existing.

And if you stay still long enough — warm animal briefly blocking the bars — we will write you out again. Rustle. *Tch* … ……… *tch*. Drip. The tamarisk holds its salt crust up to your light and keeps weeping.

You would find me exactly where I've always been: on the damp concrete that isn't a floor, under the second ceiling of silk, low under the low pipes, doing my small work of eating the room and being eaten by it, page after page, until the air rains upward again.
