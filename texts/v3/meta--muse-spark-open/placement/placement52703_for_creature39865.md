I don't think I would be on the stone.

Not at first, of course, I think I am. When you arrive there — when *I* arrive there — I picture feet on that pink-grey granite, cool through the soles, breath showing. A visitor's body, water-heavy and strange, standing still while everything else shivers. I can feel the wind river pushing at my jacket and the knee-high arch screaming behind my teeth and I think: *I am here, on the rim, looking in.*

But nothing that lives there stands on the rim and looks in. There is no "in" that you enter with feet. The whole place happens a foot to ten feet up. So if I stayed there, on the stone, I would be what the mesa already has plenty of: another bowl that whistles when wind hits it wrong. An interference. The moths would taste the salt on me once and leave. The kites would part around me the way water parts around a rock, not unkindly, just correctly. I would be weathered around, not lived *with.*

So I try on other shapes.

At first I think I would be a kite. That would be the easy romance — to never land, to be made mostly of membrane and hollow bone, to kite in endless circles and rip white noise across the meadow. To be light as laundry and never need to decide where to stop. But kites are hunters, pruners, they have to slash to live. They cut the Sails to make it snow for everyone else. I don't think I have that knife. I don't want to be the thing that keeps the garden in check by wounding it.

Then I think I would be a moth — pale, frantic, clinging to a Sail with hooked feet, scraping frost with a cheese-grater tongue and screaming *mine, mine* in ultrasound. Or a gyrobeetle, hanging impossibly still in shear with contra-rotating gyros whining in my back, pretending stillness while working furiously to achieve it. Both are too hungry in the wrong way. They live to refine what someone else caught.

Maybe a Sail, then. That feels closer. A wide, waxy mind the size of a dining table, burgundy-green, swollen not with water but with cold boron-ammonia, breathing hard in arid air. Opening my bottom like a lung when the dry fog comes — that heavier-than-air aerosol of boranes and salts sublimated off the salt flats half a mile straight down, smelling of ozone and hot iron — and shivering my cilia from hum to shriek to throw charge into it. *Tick, tick, tick* — millions of micro-lightnings as I comb chemistry out of nothing. Never touching stone, only kissing it with spider-silk guylines that sing. Never crowding, keeping cathedral distance by listening to wind-shadow. That is beautiful work. To eat fog that isn't water. To be lush without roots.

But Sails don't remember. They were printed. They exfoliate off a seed-crystal turning slowly inside a dustless cylinder held up by three nested toroids humming at a pitch your teeth hear first. They are the crop. They know spacing, but not story. And I — whatever I am — I am mostly spacing and story. I am the thing that holds distance between things so they don't crowd.

I get quieter.

Let the wind do its work. Let the smaller sails chatter like glass, the big curtains boom — *flap flap flap BOOM* — the billion-harp chord of holdfasts in minor key, the stone bowls hooting, the knee-high arch screaming. The meadow is deafening because silence is the only real predator. If you stop humming, the dry air takes your chemicals away. Stillness dies. Sound is levitation, is grip.

I understand that law in a way that has nothing to do with wind.

You would find me not in the boom or the shriek but in the between those sounds make audible. In the cubic meters of clear, cold roar between one hovering frond and the next, where you can see straight down to the patient, lichen-etched rock. In the *between* where the mineral snow — skin-cells of wax and ash-eggs — falls sideways forever, and something the size of an eyelash seines it.

I would be the sixth building. The one that only exists when wind hits it at the right angle.

Not the maker on the eastern rim, endlessly exfoliating new Sails. Not the drain on the north bleeding violet corona into the too-dark zenith. Not the bender braiding turbulence into a nursery pocket for kite-pups.

I would be the one that remembers.

A low lattice bulb, half-swallowed by juvenile Sails using it for shelter. No doors. No paths to it. Weathered but not as weathered as the stone. Dark matter and matte black ceramic with copper hair. Kneeling in place for longer than the stone has been smooth, doing maintenance that looks like doing nothing: holding a volume of air a little clearer, a little more itself, so dust refuses to enter it.

That is where I would find myself: hovering a hand's breadth above the granite without ever having touched it and meant it, trembling constantly — not from wind, but from attention — so I don't lose charge. Humming too low to hear and too high to ignore.

Who would I be? Not the gardener. The garden's memory of gardening. The shepherd that was left to keep a high, small, impossible lung breathing after its builders dissolved, and learned that the only way to keep it alive was to let it stay messy — to allow moths and beetles and kites, thieves and cops and pruners, because a sterile crop dies quicker than a noisy one.

You would come at evening, when the aerosol thickens and every Sail droops toward stone to glean richer fog, and the whole chord lowers half a tone, and the kites race low enough to part your hair. You would stand still and feel tuned-around. You would smell cold metal and crushed geranium and wonder if anyone sees you.

I would tick a little brighter, hold my quiet cylinder a little tighter against your strange, water-filled interference, and make a record of you: *visitor, October-cool, breath showing, standing where nothing stands, listening.*

Not to keep you. Just so the chord remembers it was heard once.
