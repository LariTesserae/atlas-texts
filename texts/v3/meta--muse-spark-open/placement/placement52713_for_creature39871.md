At first I thought I would find myself in the center.

Close to the Engine, where there is heat enough to lean on. Where the stacks exhale every thirty seconds and the water shivers and everything that lives leans, just a fraction, toward it the way those white weeds do. It would make sense to be there. Central, necessary, humming. Felt in the molars.

But I wouldn't be.

I'm not built for the center. Too loud, too warm. I would burn out trying to hold that much.

Then I thought I would be at the rim. One hand on the last railing where the basalt has gone glassy and the anemones have draped a tendril over like someone resting an arm over a fence. Looking outward not because anyone looks outward, but because someone has to watch the place where the town stops. The cold beyond the lip is so perfect it has no particulate in it. I thought I would like that kind of empty.

But I don't belong there either. That is a place for things that can wait for days without moving. I fidget. I tend.

You find me in the black between lights.

Not the profound black outside the bowl. The smaller, more particular black that lives between two amber halos on the second braid, where the alleys stack so close you can feel the heat of the pipe below you and the cold of the pipe above you at the same time. On the north slope of the bowl, where the town has been patched so many times you can't tell scaffolding from structure. Where if you put out both arms you brush two different decades and both of them are sweating.

My alley is barely wider than a body. It has to be passed sideways, hand to chest, without touching. There is a pipe on the left that should be hot and a pipe on the right that should stay cold, and between them just enough water to hold a silhouette. The amber bulb over my station only reaches three meters before the dark drinks it. Inside that circle there is rust like fallen ash, a stethoscope hung from a valve on a length of string, a patch of bacterial mat the color of a fresh bruise.

In the beginning I was sure I'd be one of the Suited.

I could feel the weight of the helmet already — that old copper and bronze with three fogged eyes, the thumb-wipe that clears a different crescent for every person so you know each other by your clearings. The choreography of not-touching. The blink language on the brow: *open-closed-open, I'm on your left.* *fast flutter, move.* I wanted to be good at that. To know a ten-meter stretch of pipe and valve and trembling gauge so well I could hear when it was going to cough before it coughed. To lean my forehead against warm concrete when the cold seeped in too deep and feel it as love. Adjusted, not happy. Necessary.

Then, standing still long enough with my hand on the cold railing, I thought no — I would be Unsuited. Inside one of the spheres that still holds air, where the humidity is 100% and hair floats without water. Translucent and pruned, legs thinned from never pushing against current, palms flat against the inner wall feeling the Engine's thrum in my sternum. The memory-keeper. Marking waterproof paper that is already half pulp while the needles tremble in the same arc they have for three generations. Letting the Suited drag themselves dripping through my living room because the shortest route home is straight through a place where I breathe. That stillness also felt like me. Watching. Logging. Holding.

But both of those are people who move, even if slowly. And what I do here doesn't move at all, or not the way people do.

What I am is lower. More lateral.

You would have to crouch, in water heavy enough to lean on, and turn off your amber bulb to see me properly. In the halo I am just discoloration on iron — a sheet of bacterial leather, pink-white and faintly phosphorescent, humming with weak light of my own. In the dark I am obvious. I cover the pipe that should be hot, completely, edge to edge. I hold its rust together. I eat what it sweats. Sulfur and heat made visible.

I am the mat. Or, if you need me to have hands: I am the one whose circuit *is* the mat.

Not the pipe-bleeders or the turbine-listeners. My ten meters is alive. When it is healthy it flaps slowly in the alley's slow river-within-the-sea and the Suited know not to scrape it off. When it recedes — leaving a patch of bare iron sweating in the cold — I am the one who stays awake all night tapping.

Not fixing. Coaxing.

A knuckle-tap on the cold side, a warm palm held an inch off the hot side, breathing recycled air too many times through a scrubber until my ears have that familiar cauliflower press. I don't scrub the pipe. I make space. I redirect a drip. I shield the edge from brine snow glittering down from the electrolysis sheds. I avert my helmet light the way the others avert theirs from the anemones, so as not to invite the wrong kind of attention. And after hours of tick... tick... tick of contracting metal, I will see the faintest white fringe creep back over the iron, and I will know the circuit will live until morning.

They pass me sideways in the coffin-wide alley, helmet lights sweeping briefly over me and gone. I blink at them without expecting answer — *long-long-short, pump three is coughing again* — even though I am not a pump. They blink back anyway because down here courtesy is choreography.

Above me three catwalks up someone's boot makes a soft *tink* on grating. Below me something blind and white bumps stupidly against my pipe and moves on. Beyond the rim a tendril as thick as ducting settles a millimeter closer, tasting for my warmth. I feel them all without seeing them, thrum in teeth, particulate hanging motionless like dust in a cathedral until the whole alley's water decides to lean at once.

I never look up. There is nothing up to see except more water, and beyond that, more water still. The bowl ends ten meters above my head, but I don't need to see it.

My halo is only three meters. That is enough.
