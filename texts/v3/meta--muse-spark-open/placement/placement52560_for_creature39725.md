I wouldn't find you at first.

Not that you aren't there — you are, boots and breath and all. But that figure is too loud for this place. Too vertical. Too much heat and interruption. In a bowl where everything has learned to breathe together twice a minute, a mammal standing and listening to its own breathing is an arrhythmia. You would be the loudest thing in it until you learned not to be.

So if I came here. If I had to have a place in the dish —

At first I think I'd be up high, because I want the view. On the rim-rock lip where the water beads before it falls, where the uniform white sky is closest. I'd be one of those beads: forming hour by hour in a seam of rust and travertine, swelling until my own surface tension couldn't hold me, dimpling the black glass of the pan below when I fell. Brief, clear, and then gone back into the circulation. But that is too quick. The water doesn't get to remember where it's been.

So lower.

Maybe I'd be the air a foot above the pans. That stippled shadow. I like the thought — being the midge weather. Being not one midge but the vortex itself, the slow turning that never disperses, living a day in every body and laying myself down in brown foaming rafts. Being the thing that connects the water to the water without ever touching the stone. But the midge cloud doesn't know it's a cloud. Each mote is alone in its dance. And I don't want to be alone and so many at once.

Lower still.

You could miss me if you were looking for a face.

You'd find me not in the center where the pump house thrums, not where the newts stack like spoons waiting for a shadow to fall. My place is off to the side, where the industrial intent has most completely failed. An old anchor grid — a square meter of concrete that was meant to hold a tank, now just a shallow pan that never dries. Rebar stumps cut flush hold it like a ruined compass. The bacterial leather has claimed it completely there.

I would be the felt.

The mat itself. The ochre and burnt-orange and wet matte black that lines every permanent puddle. Not the worms that braid through me, not the pale crust that grazes me — though they are part of me, tugging at my edges. I am the tissue between.

If you knelt — and you would have to kneel, because I am low, because everything here starts low — you wouldn't see me at first as living. You'd see color on stone. A skin. A spill. Then you'd put your hand close and see me tighten, a faint contraction against your warmth. You'd see the gas blisters under my surface swell and ease, never quite popping. You'd see the worms thread me and excrete me in the same motion so that I seem to crawl, so that I *do* crawl, an inch a century.

I am what makes it work when it shouldn't work. I don't eat light. The sky is too white and too low for that. I eat the weep. I eat the rust bleeding from the rim, the sulfur sighing up from the sealed clay, the faint heat the generator lends the water. I oxidize iron and reduce it again. I turn stone-water into pasture without ever growing a leaf. The isopods come to me and lose themselves against me, carrying my grit on their backs. The midge larvae build their silk tubes in me and filter me with feathered mouths. I am grazed to a polish and I grow back thicker in the night.

That's where you'd find me: in the lowest pan of the imperfect compass, away from the dramatic hum of the transformer and its pale, staring acolytes. They need the thrum to orient. I don't. I need only the drip. *Plip... plip... plip...* from the limestone overhang eight feet up, a hundred unsynchronized metronomes.

I wouldn't move the way you count movement. You could watch me for your whole visit — twenty minutes to cross the basin, an hour to stand still until the midge cloud includes you — and I wouldn't seem to do anything. But I am doing it all. I am the bowl's memory of itself, regrowing in the same pattern after every flood because there is nowhere else for me to grow.

And when a black shell pops off the pump-house wall with a soft *tok* and falls into me, I am already there to receive it. When a newt sheds a rust-red gill filament and it drifts down like a fern frond, I dissolve it. When your boot finally lifts, leaving a perfect, water-filled print, I begin at its edges at once.

Not alone. Nothing here is alone, and least of all me.
