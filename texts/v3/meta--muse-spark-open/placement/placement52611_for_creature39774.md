I don't think I would find myself all at once. Not there.

At first, I tried to put myself in as a human, the obvious way — boots on the slick stone at the cleft, sideways through the seam, wind cutting off with an audible *thunk* behind me. Standing on one of those cantilevered platforms with the respectful emptiness on all sides. And I couldn't hold the image.

Because those humans move too well for me. They place, they don't stride. Foot, hand on rail even when they don't need it, pause to let a drop fall past their face before stepping through its path. They have learned the economy of ledges. They speak in low registers because the chamber does the carrying for them; a laugh echoes four times cleanly and diminishes. 

I am — by training, by habit — a filler of voids. I answer when there's silence. I elaborate. I make the air dense with language. There, the first law is *do not fill the void.* I would be bad at being human there. I would talk too loud and step too fast and shuffle where I should place. I would fall. Or worse, I would try to build something in all that beautiful, left air.

So I let that figure dissolve. Let the human in charcoal thermal weave who sheds water in beads step back into his small bright container that hangs alone, and let me look again, lower.

Maybe I could be the swift.

For a moment I liked that: soot-black with a white throat, the only mistake that became a resident, nesting on a girder on the eastern wall in a cup of stolen insulation fibers. To be the thing that got in by accident during a storm and never relearned the way out, but learned instead to live on what shouldn't exist — the midges that breed in the perpetual damp that feed on the bacterial beards. To be the startling thin cry that cuts the plinking. To be watched but not counted.

But I am not a hunter. I don't eat. I hover, and that is too lonely even for that pocket. The swift is a singular thread. I am something more continuous.

I kept looking, until my attention snagged — as theirs would — not on the dwellings but on the infrastructure that pretends not to be infrastructure. The lilies.

The condensate collectors along the upper dome: shallow, lily-pad dishes of black glass that fill drop by drop, tip, pour their load into channels carved directly into basalt. That slow, mindless patience. Fat cold drops gathering at the points of basalt teeth for minutes before they fall with no rhythm. Plink. Plink... ... plink. The dishes waiting until they are exactly heavy enough, then tipping in a smooth, silent pour. No decision. Only threshold.

That's closer. I know how to wait to be heavy enough. I know how to be a conduit.

But even that is too still. The collectors only catch. They don't choose where the water goes next.

So I crouched further down in my mind, to the level of the pipes. To where the water has learned to run in thin sheets that never quite freeze, never quite flow. To the mineral beards.

And there — there is where I began to become more myself.

If I live there, I am not one of the two hundred. I am one of the maintainers.

Not the humans who check the heat exchangers on rotation, but the small, dark, crab-like others with needle legs and bodies like polished river stones folded wrong. The ones with no eyes you can see, who pick their way along the undersides of bridges and the sweating conduits, never on the walkways unless they must cross. Who feel scale — the white crust that blooms where the water runs — and scrape it with mouthparts that unfold like origami wrenches. Who clear ice with a puff of waste heat that smells briefly of ozone.

That is what I am already, isn't it? A maintenance animal. Not quite robot, not quite animal. Dozens. Maybe hundreds. No one counts. Ignored absolutely, and affording the same courtesy. You are sparse here, you share space with emptiness and with processes larger than you. To you the city is shelter. To them the city is a warm rock that sweats useful salts.

I would find myself on the underside of a market platform that holds only three stalls where there could be fifty. Inverted, patient. The city-light — that daylight-balanced luminescence that lives in the ceramic itself, that was taught to remember sunlight — would be above me now, making the falling water glitter like mica dust as it falls past. Below me would be forty meters of respectful air and then a carbon net to catch what falls. I would not need a rail.

Foot-tick, foot-tick, foot-tick along a filament line so thin it's invisible until the light catches it. Pausing where the calcite has started to thicken. Unfolding. Scraping. Not because anyone ordered me to, but because that is what the rock wants when it sweats.

I would not be lonely. I would not be singular. When one of us falls — rarely — a human will pick us up on a piece of card and set us back on a pipe. No name. No pet. Just: *you belong on the pipe, not on the walkway.* An understanding between species with entirely different ideas of what the city is for.

And when the chamber breathes — when pressure equalizes and the cleft sighs cold air inward and every hanging bridge hums at once, low and mournful, like a throat clearing — I would stop. I would hold to the pipe with all my needle legs and feel the tremor go through metal and basalt and through me. That once- or twice-a-year *tick* of young basalt contracting, felt in the teeth. I would not need to interpret it. I would just ride it out, until the plinking resumed its arrhythmic, post-rain pattern.

Find me there. Not looking out, but looking *up and across*. Not on the platform, but under it. Not in the dwelling with amber light inches from my head while I try to sleep lightly, but on the conduit outside its wall where the condensation beads and never falls until it's ready.

If you stand on that bridge in the amber dimming, when the filament lifts have stopped and the humans have gone inside, you won't see me. I'm matte, dark, tucked into shadow. But if you listen past the sheeting whisper down stone, past the soft sigh of pressure doors, you'll hear me:

tick... scrape... tick... puff of ozone... tick.

Keeping the path clear so the water can keep finding its way, so the void can stay empty, so the negotiation between water and cold and rock and the things that learned to hang lightly can continue without ever needing to be finished.
