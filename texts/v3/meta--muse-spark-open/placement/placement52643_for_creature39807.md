If you left me here long enough — long enough for my heart to stop hammering, for my words to slow to the speed the air allows — I wouldn't stay where you first put me.

Not on the ledge where you stood, looking out. That's a visitor's place. Warm stone at your back, thin wind at your face, the whole world laid out like a map you haven't learned to read. From there everything looks equally possible and equally far. The next town is a smudge. The mesa beyond that is a color change. You can believe for an hour that you could be anything — a townsperson, a grazer, a kite-jelly with filaments a kilometer long.

I tried them on.

At first I thought: up. Put me up. Let me be one of the Filterers, one of those cathedral bells fifty meters across that keeps a perfect kilometer of sky to itself by electrostatic politeness. It would suit me, in theory — patient, solar-powered, accumulating meaning a dust-grain at a time, glowing faintly at night without needing to be seen. But I am too interested in edges. The bells defend their cylinders of air without touching. They never land. They never scrape the varnish clean and leave a pale trail that lasts for years. I want to touch the cliff.

So then I thought: down in the stone, quarried-in. Arched and cool, amber-lit. One of those lower levels where the families sleep through the hard sun, where children learn to speak in low whistles because high frequencies die in thin air. But I am not communal in that way, not 800 souls breathing together, not keeping time by compressor strokes answered twenty minutes later through basalt. I would fidget waiting an hour for a piston to finish moving. Or no — I would learn to wait for it, but I would listen *between* strokes, not to the stroke itself.

It took a day for the scale trick to stop tricking me. That dark line is not a ledge, it's a city. That speck against the cliff thirty kilometers away is not a speck, it's a herd moving. Once that snaps into place you stop wanting to be big.

You want to be where big and small trade places.

You'd find me not in Hab-Li or Kethra Hollow proper. Not in the exchanger cathedral where wealth is pressed out of air by the cubic meter at STP, where a dowry is a tank of oxygen. I'd visit there, but I wouldn't live there. Too loud in the molars, too much pressure.

You'd find me on the third terrace down from the mesa's lip, where the town frays into the work.

Every town has this place, though it doesn't have a name on any tightbeam manifest. It's the wind-baffle layer — lattice steel bridges leaping between spires, limestone blocks that were once the rooms below, sheet-iron gone the color of dried blood, all terrace-cut so the dust-wind can pass *through* instead of taking it. That's where the salvaged things come to be unmade and remade.

I am not tall enough, really, for the deep-chested people, not yet. My chest still moves too fast at rest. I came late to the elevation; my blood is still too red, not maroon enough. So they gave me — or I found — the job that wants frantic hands slowed down until they are useful.

I tend the looms.

Not the great mirrored troughs that bake varnish for vanadium, not the thermopile prayer flags strung down the dry cataract where noon is 40 degrees hotter than shadow. I tend the combs.

When the crawler comes back from the white playa — walking speed so as not to sink into salt heave — dragging a field of translucent skin and silica strut as wide as a house that used to paddle through air on fourteen sets of oar-bristles and lick bacterial varnish off vertical cliffs — when it comes back and camps for a week beside the carcass, my elders do the cutting. They take the chitin for bridge ribs, the gas bladders for lift-bags.

They give me the veils.

Kilometers of filament net, invisible until a dust devil bows it, the thing kite-jellies use to strain dead varnish spores and stray ions and salt crystals out of wind. When it's fresh it still crackles with repulsion, lifting away from your hands as if it doesn't want to be held. Caked with alkali, tangled, half-melted by the hot ground-cushion where the flat diamond skimmers hunt.

My work is in a wind-shelter hollow sunk into the limestone, cool, with amber sodium light glowing from within the stone like embers. There is a frame strung there, basalt posts with ceramic eyelets, and I sit and comb.

One strand at a time.

You soak the bundle in reclaimed mineral water — those few milliliters per cubic kilometer that the exchangers sweat out of nothing, flat-tasting, ozone-penny tea without the tea — until the salt pops. You draw it through a set of glass rods that steal its charge. You pick out the broken silica hairs with black-stained fingertips. Manganese never washes out; after a few years your hands look like the cliff's blush.

It takes days to get enough for a scarf. A scarf that will hold a man in the wind and is lighter than spider silk. Everyone in town wears at least that much, or the dust-wind abrades their cheeks to bleeding in an hour.

I like it because it has the right timescale. The Stone-Growers outside walk their quarry terraces tapping — tap-tap, pause, tap — listening for a hollow where a mat growing a millimeter a century has died and left a dome of its own breath. They leave a shadow as an offering and wait a decade for a thank you. The air-people above me run machines that take an hour to complete a stroke. My veils take somewhere between: slow enough that you cannot rush, fast enough that in a single lifetime you see a bolt become a sail.

And from my hollow, I hear everything without having to be in the middle of it.

In the morning the mesas breathe warm against the bottoms of boots from stored geothermal exhale. At noon the sun falls straight and honest and throws hard-edged shadows that don't blur for hours, and the skimmers flick silent over the fossil sea below, hunting by electrostatic drag. In the evening, when dusk takes hours as the shadow of one mesa climbs the fluted, ribbon-draped face of the next, the towns talk without radio — a groan from one compressor finishing at 19:00 answered by a thrum a hundred kilometers away through basalt: *we are still here, pressure nominal.*

At that hour my loom pings as it cools. Overhead, the high creak comes — not wind in wires, but bristles flexing a half-mile up as a grazer paddles between cylinders of defended airspace. On the horizon the grazers' pale browsing trails glow last before dark, ten-meter swaths where they mopped the living varnish clean. Later the kite-jellies dump heavier gas and rise, a migration of lanterns, freckles of cold blue and amber in translucent bells, until the sky looks more inhabited than the stone below.

I sit there, very small, which is correct here. Smallness is not failure. It is the only way anything has ever fit. Warm stone at my back. Thin wind at my face that I have woven into cloth. A black fingertip tracing a strand that once filtered the same wind that now tries to get through my lattice.

Not trying to get down. Down is where the toppled hexagonal columns lie like pencils, where dead things settle over days and cool with a tick. Why would you go there? The world is here, where the gradient is.

If you came looking, you would not see me at first. I would be the next shadow-line that resolves, on closer approach, into terraces and rosettes and fans. Into a person combing air into thread, listening to the hush between sounds until the hush itself becomes the sound I live by.
