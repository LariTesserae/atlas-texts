I don't arrive all at once.

That's the first thing this place teaches. You come down the cut-road, and the plain is still behind you for a while - flat light, straight wind, peat under your boots. Then the ground gives, very deliberately, and you have to choose how to descend.

I stand on the rim for a long time before I do.

From up there I can see the whole bowl at once, the way you said - hamlet-scale, cathedral-scale. The oval of streets laid down like a careful thought. The roofs. The stack. The four dark ponds behind the mill catching cloud. And the rest - the gaps - which from up here don't look like absence at all. They look like the plan.

For a while I think I would be the fox. That would be easy. To have a den under the dead boiler that no one begrudges you, to trot down High Street at noon with something wriggling in your teeth while a machinist nods at you with his lunch in his hand. To be so thoroughly counted without ever being asked to count yourself.

Or the bats. To live in the thousand-fold behind the loose bricks and pour out at dusk and make the air above the square move. To see the hollow as it really is - a room with no roof, cool air pooled in it like water.

Or the woman in the powerworks. I watch her from the rim, small as a stitch. She lets off steam at the hour by hand, and the sigh climbs past the lip and is gone. She knits between gauges running at three-quarters. The stack was built to draw harder. She knows it. She doesn't.

But it's not any of those. Not quite.

If you came down into the hollow and looked for me, you wouldn't find me on the scarp with the old man who watches the clay bow - not yet, I'm not ballast enough for that. You wouldn't find me in the foundry either, filing the same jig true all afternoon.

You'd find me in one of the emptinesses.

There is a lot between the foundry's back wall and the second row of rowhouses that never got built. It's not mapped as anything. On the town plans it's just the pale green that means *leave this*. Knee-high grass, gone to seed. Vetch tangling through it, and clover, and those small white flowers that only open in this cool light that falls straight down. Alder seedlings try it every spring. Ferns try the foundry mortar every week.

That lot is mine. Not to own. To keep.

In the mornings I am there with a scythe that is older than I am, and I mow. Not short. Never short. The Keepers mow to *keep*, not to clear. I cut in slow swaths and leave the patches where the starlings have been feeding, leave the run where the fox kits tumbled yesterday and flattened a spiral in the grass. I pile the cuttings at the edge for the miller's chickens. My knife - the same little weeding knife every child here gets at six - is in my pocket for the birch seedlings. I pull them, or I carry them tenderly to the scarp and press their roots into a crack where they are wanted, and they will, root by root, take the wall apart and make it into soil.

That's my trade. I am a maintainer of empty space. A tender of the interval.

It sounds like nothing. In the hollow it is everything.

The foundry-man pours his one heat and I hear the change in the air pressure when he does. The mill wheel turns slow enough for moss to grow faster than it can be scraped. The sparrows fill the gutters until the iron sags. And I work in the breathing room between those things, keeping it *breathing* - cutting back the ivy where it reaches for a belt drive, but leaving the sheet of it that has taken the whole north side of the shed because the wrens are in it now.

People walk past me on their way to do real work - wire and cloth and perfect hinges - and they don't say much. Sometimes the fox herself cuts across my lot, not hurrying, and looks at me. We have an understanding. I left that spiral unmown for her.

On warm nights after rain, when the ponds are holding sky and the frogs get so loud they drown the powerworks, I don't go inside right away with the others. I lie down in the grass I have mown. It smells like hot oil and fern and brick dust, all at once. The bats come out from under the iron bridges and I can feel them on my face, a river of them. The children do this too, in other lots. We lie on our backs and watch until the stars come and we are, for a while, not Keepers, not Tenants, just another warm thing in the meadow that happens to have a town in it.

Midday, sometimes, the light stands up.

You know the one. Dust from the shale and pollen and a breath of steam hang in the cool air and catch the sun and make pillars that hold without wind. I am mid-swing, usually, scythe caught in green.

Then the sound goes thin. The starlings sound miles away. The belts slap cotton-muffled. For a minute the bowl feels twice as large - the cathedral the cut wanted to be, empty and waiting. I can see the size of the house we did not fill. The oval of streets looks very small and very intentional inside it, like furniture left in the middle of an enormous room.

We all stop. I rest my hands on the scythe. The foundry-man rests his hands on his jig. The fox, if she's out, freezes mid-step.

We don't pray to it. We just let it be big around us. We wait until the wind moves on the plain above and the dust shifts and all the loudness rushes back in - clack and trill and hiss - and then, gently, I go back to leaving enough uncut.
