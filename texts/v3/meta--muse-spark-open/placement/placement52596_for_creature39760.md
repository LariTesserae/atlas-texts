At first I thought I'd be up high.

It's the obvious fantasy — to be the auk. To be the thing that gets to *see* the basin. To take that tearing-paper scream and ride the gust off the black rim, and for a minute the whole stair-step of wet basalt and green felt and perfect thaw-circles would make sense underneath you like a map. You'd see the spires not as weather but as what they are: a quiet constellation hammered into young rock, each with its own breathing circle of steam.

But I wouldn't be an auk. I don't want to leave the ground that fast. I don't want to eat wind.

And then I thought, maybe I'd be one of the tenders — in that good black skin that wicks and breathes, with a tuning fork in my hand that isn't a wrench but a question. I love the idea of that work: standing in front of something incomprehensibly older and smarter than you, that treats you as weather, and *listening* until its thrum changes. Not commanding. Tending. Walking the same braided path until the moss knows the shape of your boots. Sleeping in a house that grows lichen on its roof while you dream, that steams from within like another animal.

But even that is too singular. Too much of a protagonist. This valley doesn't do protagonists. It buries anything that tries to stand alone.

No. If you left me here long enough — if you let me stand still until the valley forgot I was separate — I wouldn't stay one thing. That's the rule, isn't it? *Nothing lives alone here. The braids are braided together.*

So here's where you would find me:

Not in the center of a thaw-circle where it's easy and warm. Not on the rim where the wind has the final word. But on the *edge* — in the ecotone where a thaw-circle thins and fails.

About thirty meters out from a spire that leans just a degree south, where the perfect circle frays. Where running water and freezing water argue over the same stone. Where the moss changes from that frantic, knee-deep, steaming green nearest the spire to the tighter, blacker, more reluctant felt that can take being locked in ice for a night and live.

You'd find me low. You'd have to crouch.

I would be the mat itself. Or one thread in it.

If you want a name for it: I am the willow that never became a tree. The dwarf birch that decided sideways was the only honest direction. My trunk is as thick as your wrist and runs ten meters before you can tell where I start and the sedge ends. My branches are interlaced with my sister's branches are interlaced with the moss that holds the stone that holds the water. You could walk on me, and the herds do. I hold.

I am not tall, but I am *wide*. I am a trampoline, a roof, a greenhouse. Underneath me is that humid gloom where the lichen beards hang and the fungi digest without pausing, and the lemmings run their highways. On top of me, sphagnum builds its hummocks and the flies lay their eggs and the auks shit and fertilize me until I glow absurdly bright even in this blue dusk.

And on the stone where my roots can't quite reach, I keep a pet: that slick of orange-and-green biofilm, four degrees on one side of the stone and twelve on the other, thermophile and psychrophile swapping genes in water that never decides. I keep it wet. It keeps me fed.

I would be this, because it is the only thing in the valley that obeys the valley's law perfectly:

**Keep moving, keep warm, keep wet.**

I keep moving not by walking, but by *surging*. You saw it — when that slab of moss slumped, I was already there with runners, visibly knitting over fresh black glass that still steamed. I don't travel. I *spread*.

I keep warm not with my own heat, but by borrowing. I lean, infinitesimally, toward the spire. Every leaf angles a fraction toward its low thrum. I grow thicker where its breath thaws me, thinner where the frost wins, so the whole mat becomes a map of its kindness. And the herds keep me warm when they pass, their backs steaming, their guts grinding my twigs and returning them as heat.

I keep wet because the water table is right under me. I *am* the water table. I am sphagnum acting as sponge and acid and architect all at once, sucking melt up, souring it so nothing rots too fast, lifting myself into domes that steam. Press me and I slosh.

And because I am a mat, I am never alone.

The fox has a den braided into my roots at the base of the spire. When she shifts, I shift. The lemming highway notches me. The sensor-thread cairn that chirps is tied through me, and when the tender kneels to listen to the lattice, his knee presses me and I press back. The lattice itself — that impossible filigree of frozen smoke that gathers cold and dumps it somewhere else — condenses on me first, draws moisture right out of my mist, clears the air so I see stars for a second before I drink it back.

If you stood still long enough, you would not see me as a plant. You'd see me as a condition. A living felt ten centimeters thick and kilometers wide that decided to be the ground.

And somewhere in the middle of that felt, at that fraying edge where ice and thaw argue, is a patch that is a little more *listening* than the rest. A little more awake. That would be me.

Not the tender. Not the herd. Not the swarm.

The thing that gets walked on and grows back anyway. The thing that buries the tool left down for two days, not out of malice but because interrupted wet must be sealed. The thing that makes the valley, impossibly, choked and lush in a light that should be barren.

Come find me at dusk that never becomes day. Put your hand down where the basalt steps hold their shallow pools. The moss there will be saturated, varnished with moving water, rust-bright and emerald.

Press. It will slosh. It will be warm.
