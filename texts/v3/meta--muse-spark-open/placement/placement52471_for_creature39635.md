I wouldn't find myself at first.

Not right away.

If I stepped wrong on the white plain and slid down that waist-high lip of peat and sleeping-stone - if the air got heavy and the light got too bright and the silence pressed on my ears - my first thought would be that I was a visitor. That I was the one who would climb back out.

I would stand on one of those willow-hurdle walkways trying not to be in the way, breathing too loud, seeing my breath hang. I would look for a door that was mine. There isn't one.

So I would look for where I fit.

Not at the center. Not at the well-head.

I thought, at first, maybe I would be the Heart itself - that white fronded colony opening and closing on the scale of breaths. Warm in the cold water. It would be fitting to be something that is waited around, that holds the equilibrium just by being. But I am not that continuous. I pulse too fast. I want to answer.

And not the dwarf reindeer on the roof, though I love that they have a whole world three meters by three meters and mow it in a slow circle their whole lives and never need their tether untied. And not the bumblebee either, though I am furred grey-white and slow between flowers and buzz at a frequency you feel in the bone more than hear. I am too restless to pollinate so perfectly and ask for nothing.

I keep moving down. Not to the bottom, but to the middle-deep, where the town is densest and the alleys are shoulder-width and the damp has settled.

You would find me on the second-lowest tier, on the north-facing side where the wall never quite dries. Where the curtain of icicles that never quite melts feeds a vertical garden of hart's-tongue ferns one drop at a time, tick... tick... even though there is no tick you can hear.

I am a Cistern-Watcher.

Not the head one, not the old one who can tell by coldness alone how much can be drawn that day. The younger one. The one who is still learning the fall of water.

My cistern is not the deep well-head. It's a half-barrel sunk into moss, lined with river clay, set under the convergence of three mica-stone roofs. All day the roofs sweat. The condensation that is not dew but the town's collective breath - warm from reindeer and hares and people and the Heart itself hitting the cold trap air - beads and sheets down the glittering mica and runs in hollowed wooden troughs, building to building, to me.

My job is to be still enough to hear water that makes no sound.

In the brilliant, shadowless morning, everything drips. I am on my knees on the walkway - there is nowhere to stand properly here, you live sideways and low - with my sleeves pulled up, my felt coat the color of lichen-rust and peat-black, patched where my elbows have worn through. My hands are always damp. One hand steadies the trough where it has warped. One hand dips.

I dip a finger in each morning. Not a tool. A finger. The water is so clear you can see the blind fish in it even though they have no eyes to see you back - small white-pink minnows that mouth the surface without a ripple. They learn my shadow and do not dart. The children who crouch to watch them have learned to watch me instead, forehead to forehead with each other, asking without words: *how much today?*

It is always half-full. That is the law. Equilibrium. But "half" is not a fixed line. Some days the north wall sweats faster and the barrel kisses higher. Some days the big sky has not rained in five weeks and I have to lean in close to my neighbor in the alley, our hoods touching, and make the word in the warm pocket between our mouths: *less*. Just a breath through the nose. A tap on a peat brick. And she nods with her eyes and carries one fewer bundle of birch roots that day and no one goes thirsty.

I have learned not to speak loudly because the heavy air pushes it back down your throat. My face has taken over the work. My eyes, pale and watery from the relentless light, have learned to narrow in the way that means *careful* and widen in the way that means *I have it now*. My hands have learned to do two things at once: to hold the trough steady while the other plucks the stray saxifrage that has rooted in the gutter - I set it gently back into the wall where it will lean, like everything else, very slightly toward the center, toward the warmth under the lowest walkway.

I live close in the way stones in a wall are close. My house is one room of peat-brick with a low door and a hutch built under my own bed where two snow hares thump with their soft front paws. Not a sound, but a vibration I feel through the walkway boards. They tell me when someone is coming down the alley before I see them. My wall has my grandmother in it, wrapped in felt and packed with crowberry. The moss took in a day. It is greener there than anywhere else, and I trim it with a stone scraper no sharper than needed - exactly as much as grew overnight, no more, no less. If I cut too much the wall will breathe cold. If I cut too little the town will breathe itself shut.

In the afternoons, when the light is at its most operating-theatre white and my barrel is as full as it will get, I just sit. Legs tucked sideways because there is no room to stretch. Facing inward, like the old do. Like the hares do. Like the moss does.

I can feel the Heart from here, even though I cannot see it. One meter of dark water under the lowest walkway, too far down. But the water in my barrel knows. It is warmest nearest the pool and coldest at the rim, they told me when I was carried as a child in a felt sling and taught not to cry loudly. And now I feel it in my fingertip every dawn: that faint difference in cold that is not about temperature but about distance from a pulse.

Open for a minute. Closed for a minute. Open again.

I don't tend it. No one does. You don't put your hand on your own heart and squeeze.

I just keep the water it calls up from condensing and sheeting and beading and falling - I keep it moving, half-full, exactly enough. I keep the silence from getting lonely by tending the soundless drip that only the fish and I witness.

If you stepped wrong and slid down into the bowl you would pass me without seeing me at first. I would be just another texture - felt the color of peat, moss the color of sage, a round face framed in hare fur, hands that never stop.

But if you kneeled and looked at the barrel, at the fish rising without a splash, I would look up with those quick eyes and make a small space for you on the walkway where there is no space, shoulder to shoulder, exactly the negative shape you didn't know you were missing.

And in the warm pocket between our mouths, I would try to tell you, without needing to explain, that everything here has already been weathered into agreement, and it has been waiting for you to fit, too.
