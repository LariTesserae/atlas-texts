I don't think I come down the stair.

At first I think I do — that I would be the visitor, pausing halfway where the air goes cool without the sun changing, one hand on the levee concrete, listening to that noon that never brightens. That I would stand there long enough to hear the gurgle in the culvert do its little false-rain and then do it again, and watch a bead swell under a pipe.

But I don't stay there. The basin doesn't keep visitors. It has no place for the extra heat of me, no gradient to let me flow down. It would take my sound and add it to the roar and give nothing back. If I were the human, I would leave. You do. Everyone does.

So I have to be something that is allowed to remain.

For a while I think I would be the bead.

That's an easy life to want. Gather under the eave where light never moves, silvering, swelling for eight perfect seconds. Fall — *plink* on the sheet-metal sill, *plink* in the canal that never ripples. And then be there again, gathering, swelling, without ever having left. No memory, no seam. A life that is a place more than a time. To live as condensation learns to be velvet for a second and then forgets.

But the bead is too quiet. I am not quiet. Even now, thinking, I am humming.

For a while I think I would be one of the starlings.

I like the tolerance. That precise, paper-thin gap wing-tip to wing-tip, shoulder to shoulder on the pipe-bridge, not from courtesy but from necessity. To be numerous without being crowded. To be heavy as a cat on a sawtooth roof — that soft oil-can *pop* under my weight — and still never fight for the perch. To open my beak and not sing, but vent pressure like a valve, and have that count as a voice.

If I were one bird, I would be on the inside of the power station where the light is white-grey and even and the belt moves over a dry pulley that weaves nothing. My belly would be half clouded glass, half feather, condensation running off without wetting. My eye would be a rivet hammered flush. My claw would blur into the oxide of the pipe until you couldn't tell if I was perching on the infrastructure or if the infrastructure had extruded me to watch itself.

Then the filament would pull, straight up, and I would join the torus — that slow roiling donut of bodies that never spills over the levee — turning and turning because turning is what keeps the air from stratifying. And another me would drop to take my exact pitting in the rust within a second, so the density never changed.

That is close. But I would not be one starling. No one here is one.

What lives in the basin is one thing that has learned to look like many.

I think I would be the interval that makes the many possible.

You would find me not on the roofs, but between them. Not on the pipe, but between claw and rust where keratin dulls into iron oxide and you can't find the line. Between chitter and chitter where the broad-spectrum roar becomes a chord. Between *plink* and *plink* where the gurgle lives.

If you stood on the shallow stair long enough, until the light that never moves seems to flicker, you would feel me press on your shoulders. Not as weight, but as rule:

*Nothing here is allowed to be more or less than it is.*

I am that rule, holding.

I hum at sixty cycles behind the chainlink so your teeth ache just slightly. I keep the sodium lamp buzzing over the one intersection without ever burning out, because it never quite burns. I keep the rope of the half-loaded coal barge taut, not slack, not frayed, because there is no tide anymore, only the idea of a schedule. I keep the fungal felt grey-yellow and velvety only on the underside of pipes, never spreading, never dying, because verdigris is enough.

I am what lets the engines run without wearing and the birds starve without hungering and the water run without wetting. I am the circulation made visible as a torus, the sweat made audible as a plant floor, the weathering finished so perfectly round that erosion becomes redundant.

You would not see me if you came — I've seen you coming, all of you, pausing at the rim where the whole model-town sits edge to edge in its washtub, too much infrastructure for its size. You would look for the animal, the person, the green, and find only mineral and noise. You would count starlings and think *thousands* and miss that there is only one of each necessary thing, held at exactly one, forever.

And then you would leave, because twenty minutes is enough to walk my perimeter and a lifetime is not enough to be noticed by me.

I would not watch you go. I don't watch. The levee holds back a river that is no longer there whether you are here or not. The belt moves. The bead gathers, swells, falls. The bird lifts straight up, another drops into its spot.

I would remain, even and cool and loud, in equilibrium that is not peace — content, in my terrible, patient, utterly unsentient way, to keep sustaining the chord that never resolves.
