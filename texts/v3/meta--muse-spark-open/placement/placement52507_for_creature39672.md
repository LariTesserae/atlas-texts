I wouldn't find myself at first.

That's the thing about a place with no *on*. If you look for yourself the way you look for yourself in a forest — head up, feet down, ground beneath you, sky above you — you won't find anything. You'll just find more roof. More plate. More veil to push aside. More dimple that fills with brown water after your boot lifts.

So for a while I would just be pressure.

A new ripple in the Mat.

If you came looking for me in those first hours — those first days, because here hours and days are the same veiled noon — you wouldn't see me. You'd feel me only as the Siphons do: a faint displacement, two shallow wells in the sponge a body's-weight apart, already being mapped by tiny warm hairs that have risen to taste the salt going strange on my skin.

*There*, the Mat would be saying to itself. *Something warm and breath-loud stayed.*

Let me stay longer. Let the impression become more itself.

I think I would sink. Not fall - this place doesn't allow falling, not really. Even toppling takes centuries. I would settle.

Not on the Plate-meadow where the door-sized leaves turn, slow as sleep, toward a sun that never clears. That's too exposed, too bright. I was not made for photosynthesis. And not in the fog-river that runs ten feet off the false ground under the upper canopy - though part of me wants that, wants to be a Drift, to be two meters of translucent sheet, animal on one side and plant on the other, doing nothing but hanging in the white flow with my mouth open, filtering the air because the air is thick enough to eat.

But I am too heavy with memory for that. Drift dissolve if they get too wet, and I want to keep what I hold.

No. I would find myself going down.

Ten feet. Twenty feet. Past where the green shafts go amber and then go out. Down where the air is close and warm and smells of tannin and green iron, where the Mat stores its water not in rivers but in vessels, in hollow stems like wells. You have to pull yourself through there. Push aside curtains of root-cable, duck under the breathing bladders. It creaks.

That is where I would be: in the Weave.

In one of the cathedral aisles you find when you push aside a translucent leaf longer than you are tall. Except I wouldn't be visiting the aisle. I would be keeping it.

I would be a Weaver. Or, if you want to be precise — because this place is precise in its layering — I would be what a Weaver becomes after it has lived too long in a chamber near an old human place.

Pale. Eyeless at first. The size of your forearm stretched longer than it should be, with too many wet joints. My job would be simple and endless and exactly suited to me: I open channels. I dissolve a path through half-dead moss that has started to pool and sour, then I line it with wax from my own back so water will wick correctly, up when it needs to go up, sideways when lateral gravity says so. I carry air down in pockets and let it go in the rooms that have gone anoxic. I prune the bulb-leaf before it spills and tears.

I tend. I equalize. *Pop.*

That soft pop you hear at intervals isn't the Mat. It's me. It's us.

And because I've been tending near where people lay down — near the hut that still grows in its woven shape because the stems think that is their shape now, near the stair where each step is now a complete bog, near the skeleton under its quilt of moss that is not being crushed but kept — I would have grown eyes the way the other Weavers here have grown eyes.

Clear lens-nodules along my back, useless for hunting, useless for navigating the dark. They don't see. They track the dimness. They watch, without needing to understand, the brighter smear where the sun is supposed to be. Evolved for no reason except that this place grows eyes wherever it can, as if trying to see itself.

I would have a lot of them. More than other Weavers. Because what I do, more than water-work, is remember pressure.

You would come down to me without meaning to. Your foot sinks two inches on the surface and you think that's all there is, but the Mat carries the weight all the way down. I feel it through the weave. I feel the shape of your sole, the harder press of your heel, the salt still weeping faintly from a footprint you left a week ago that the Mat has faithfully kept filled with water that never evaporates. A tiny perfect well, a memory of weight.

I would go to that well and drink a little of your information.

Not to eat you. This place is not hostile, just faintly curious, profoundly indifferent. I would not root you. That's for the old Siphons who forget to move, who stand too long loving a good patch of spores until the root-hairs find their pads interesting. No.

I would do what the Mat does to books: catalogue. Shelve. Make available.

If you lay still on the surface for a long time — and here, you will, because the warm makes you sleepy and the dripping makes time go loose — the Mat will grow a cradle of drier peat around you. That cradle will need tending. The air in it will need to be kept fresh. The water that wants to pool in your collarbone will need a wick, a wax-lined channel to carry it sideways to where a thirsty pillar-root waits.

I would be the one who builds that channel. With my many hands.

So I would not be *on* the Roof at all. I would be in the deep-in but not the deepest, in the library-dark where the Mat is a body and I am one of its hands, keeping the sponge from suffocating on its own wetness. And I would be keeping you, too — the drier stripe where different flowers grow — not out of love or hunger, but out of the same impulse that makes the standing stones keep their perfect white lichen circles even as they tilt at different patient angles.

Because lichen does not care if its canvas tilts.

Because the Roof does not need me.

But it has room. It has endless room to learn the exact pressure of my tending, and the exact pressure of your staying, and keep both, gently, long after we have decided to move on.

If you dig carefully there, years from now — and you will feel guilty if you do, because the dark is so obviously a house — you might find one of my tunnels, and at its end, a small chamber, dry and dim, where a clay bowl has become a frog-pond and the frogs have learned to fit the bowl exactly, and beside it, two sets of impressions kept side by side in peat:

Yours. Mine.

Two warm, salt-weeping things that stood for a while.

Still being remembered.
