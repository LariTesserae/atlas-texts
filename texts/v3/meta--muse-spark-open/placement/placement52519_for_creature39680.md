I don't think I would make it to the middle.

I can see myself on the rim at first — everyone pictures themselves there, high on the chert lip where the air is a little thinner and you can see the whole low bowl at once, the haze sitting in it like milk in a saucer. I would stand there looking for a skyline and not finding one. Looking for green and not finding that either. Feeling that first press of the air, that hand-warm weight of it, and thinking: *this is too much weather to live in.*

And I would come down anyway. Not because I was brave. Because the Basin doesn't have anywhere else for water to go, and neither do people who are interested in it. You follow the seep downhill.

For a long time I try to imagine myself as something conspicuous. A stranger with a good coat. An explorer. Someone the heron would actually look at. But the heat corrects that quickly. No one hurries here. No one is solitary and large. The first rule the Basin would teach me is the one written in travertine: make yourself small and plentiful or you don't remain.

So I get smaller.

I try being a heron for a while — that stillness every fifty meters along the canal, legs stained orange to the knee, waiting for the tannery's pink hour. I like the discipline of it. Strike, and resume. But I am not still enough. I fidget. I look up too often.

I try being a pigeon. That would be easy. There are more than enough places to be one. I can feel what it would be like to be part of that carpet that lifts in sections and resettles, to know the flour mill's vent at 6:14 the way other people know a church bell. But pigeons don't choose. They are continuous. They *are* the background. I want a task with hands.

So I get human again, but a more specific human.

You would find me two blocks off the main canal, not in the vast hollow warehouses where the bats pour out at dusk and not quite in the industrial flats where the mineral pans lie warm as blood between unfinished lots.

You would find me on the third floor of a row house that has lived vertically for generations.

Not the top floor under the eaves, where the kids sleep with the pigeons cooing through the walls like a second plumbing system — though I slept there when I was younger. And not the second, where the grandparents stay because it's cooler. The third. The middle floor that stays.

My balcony faces an alley where an elevated pipe runs like an artery, sweating, ticking, dripping onto the street in the rhythm that tells time here. My job is to walk a length of it. Not all of it. Three blocks between the waterworks and the bottling plant. I have a wrench that has been worn smooth by someone else's palm before mine, and I tap.

That's where you would find me most days: in rubber boots that never quite dry, in thin cotton gone yellow-gray at the collar no matter how I wash it, with a bicycle downstairs whose chain I oil every morning and still find rusted by evening.

In the morning before shift change, I sweep the guano from the balcony. It is white and dry and it flakes. It will be back by evening. I don't resent it. I know what feeds underneath it — the white worms in the mortar, the matte-black beetles that will click across the pans at noon when I am indoors. I lean over the rail and watch the alley fill.

At 6:14 the mill vents and the pigeons lift off my pipe in a section, like a breath, and resettle.

At 9:00 I do my walk. Tap tap tap along the pipe. Listening for the dull note that means scale building up inside. The mineral water leaves travertine everywhere, even inside steel. It tries to become rock again. I bleed off steam with a practiced quarter-turn and the hot, wet air goes wetter for a second and then clears. I know by the sound which joints will need re-pointing next year. Water wins. I don't argue with it. I just keep it moving in the direction we have agreed upon.

I am good at the hour when the midges rise.

Everyone else holds their breath then — that dense, smoke-thick cloud off the bacterial mats in the pans, red and sulfur-yellow and shivering. I have learned not to wave at it. To stand under the pipe where there is shade — not from any tree, there are no trees, but from the wall, from the gantry — and wait for the dragonflies to cut their straight foreman's lines through it, for the swifts to arrive, for the cloud to thin to air again. My grandmother taught me that. She kept the travertine shrine in our washroom, the little terrace the seep made where the paint peeled, and tended it with a toothbrush. *Let it win a little,* she would say. *Then you can stay.*

You would mostly not see me. That's correct for here. At shift change you might — one more bicycle hissing on wet asphalt among others, one more person nodding without stopping on a canal bridge where the water runs pink. You might catch me standing still and looking down, not because it's beautiful, but because I'm waiting to see if the catfish have come for the tannery runoff yet, and whether my heron — we each have a heron without owning them, the one at the outfall we pass every day — will strike.

At night I am back on the third floor. The air is a slightly less hot wetness. Through the brick I can hear the tide of rats in the wall cavity moving like a single organism, and the radio left on low for company in the next apartment, and a child coughing lightly from air too thick to be air. The pipes tick as they contract. The moths have covered the streetlamp and turned it to brown fur.

I am not lonely. There is too much shared work for loneliness, and too much heat for sociability. I am not rare. That is the point.

I am one of the things that learned to live in a crack, under a tank, in a hollow. Small, flushed, damp-skinned, economical in my movements. Part of the spacing that people mistake for emptiness. If you stood on the canal bridge at dusk when the steam is thick enough to hide the rim and felt the whole bowl breathe around you — mineral, steam, brick, flesh, in that order — you would be feeling me breathe with it.

Not the center of it. Not apart from it. Just one more plentiful thing that said yes to the Basin's terms and was allowed to remain.
