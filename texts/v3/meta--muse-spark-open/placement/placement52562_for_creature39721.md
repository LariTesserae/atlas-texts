I didn't think I would be any of them at first.

At first I thought I would be you — the visitor. The too-big body suspended in the exact middle of the pocket, where the water is coldest because it is farthest from the warm shiver of the floor. Arms tucked because there is nowhere to put them. Breath held out of reverence, not need. Hanging there in that indigo dusk, watching the light break itself on the jellies, listening to my heart try to be part of the city's quiet and failing.

But that can't last. The water clarifies too much. A visitor's heart is too loud and too lonely. It keeps its own time. This place doesn't do lonely time. Here, time is kept by plumes opening, by legs combing, by a slow pulse once a minute from a lantern tunicate. I would have to leave. I know that about myself.

So I go down. Not far in distance — three meters is nothing — but far in kind.

If I'm going to stay, I have to give up leaving.

I thought, for a while, I would be a tube-worm. There's a kind of faith in it I envy — to choose a spot no bigger than a fingerprint and then never move again. To grow your house out of your own body and stand white and scar-tissued in a field of others, all waving out of sync and then mysteriously in sync, praying by filtering. To have no eye, no stomach, just a red crown opening and closing in the almost-still. To never have to decide again.

But I am not good at standing still. I keep wanting to check the neighbor's plume, to see if the current changed.

I thought I would be an amphipod then, in my grit apartment. That appealed to me for a long, too-human reason: I like that they build with what they have been. Shed skin, borrowed shell-spiral, a grain of basalt that floated down a year ago — all of it glued with something they make in their own heads until a wall happens that they share with the next life over. A street happens. A city happens. Not because anyone planned a city, but because everyone needed a wall and was willing to share it.

I could be that. I could be small and blind and frosted-glass pale, with blue eggs under my tail, tending a little farm of white bacteria on my own back, scraping it, spreading it, combing the water with feathery legs out my one doorway. Crawling a centimeter to cement my child next door. That kind of life makes sense to me: to make a home not *in* the world but *out of* the world, and out of each other.

But even that is a little too settled. I find in this imagined body that my antennae keep twitching toward the gaps in the streets, toward where the water moves a little faster.

So I keep going.

Maybe I am the isopod. That was the next thought, and it stayed longer. The one allowed to walk. Pea-sized, slow, mother-of-pearl lit from inside. The janitor, the tender, the undertaker. I like that someone has to do this job in a place this dense — to approach the fouled plume of a tube-worm and nibble it clean, patiently, endlessly. To find the amphipod who died in its cell — which happens constantly, quietly — and take it apart in a day and carry the pieces away where other mouths are waiting. No waste. A death is a widening of the street for a few hours, and then new larvae settle. Grief, here, is just making more room.

To be that would be to know the city by taste. Clean versus dirty. Here versus there. To have a whole cosmos that is three meters across and still need a lifetime to patrol it because you go so slowly and stop so often. The red hunter would ripple past me — the only fast thing — and I would tuck and wait, and then continue.

That is a good life. An honest life. But when I try it on, I can feel my feet want to stop patrolling and just... hang.

And so I look up. Inverted.

Ah. There you are.

I would be one of the roof ones. Or maybe all of them, because they are the only ones who look like one thing repeated.

Clear as good water, sucker-foot impossible against the pressure-blue ice that should kill, bell hanging down, and inside that bell another bell, and inside that another — a nested set of parachutes that will never open to air. I don't pulse to swim. I have given up swimming entirely, which is the most radical thing you can do in an ocean that wanted to be a room. I just hang and wait.

That is where I find myself: at the very top, which is also the very edge, where the cold is oldest. Hanging upside down from the lid of the world.

Who am I there? I am a fisherman of falling. The marine snow never lands. It falls endlessly, impossibly slowly — a veil of detritus from nowhere, from the miles of ice that sweat light but not food. I fish it with threads a hundred times finer than hair, invisible until something touches them and sticks. And I am in no hurry. I reel hand over hand with no hands. I eat what the world thought it was discarding.

And because I am clear, the light breaks in me. The dim dusk that filtered down through kilometers of glacier has nothing to do until it hits me, and then, for a moment, I get to hold it. All of us do, bell after bell after bell, scattering it, passing it on, so that the whole chamber seems to glow from within, not from a source but from many small lives agreeing to carry light for a second.

I think I would be that.

Not because it is beautiful, though it is. Not because it is solitary, though it is — each sucker-foot holding its own patch of ice, each bell its own slow fishing. But because it is the one that gets to see the city without ever leaving its post.

Below me, the whole warm thing is breathing. The white field of worms sighing open and closed. The grit neighborhoods dense as honeycomb. The slow traffic of isopods. The occasional terrible ripple of red. The lanterns pulsing. The whole thing layer after layer until you can't say where animal ends and architecture begins. And none of them knows I am here, and I do not really know them as individuals. I just feel the city as a faint tug on my threads, as food arriving, as light changing when a plume below shifts.

I wouldn't have a big nervous system. I wouldn't have words for pocket, or ice, or basalt still exhaling heat. I wouldn't know I was only three meters from wall to wall. For me, this *would* be the cosmos — wall-to-wall with presences, every current a highway.

But together —

That is the part that finally settled for me, after not settling for so long.

I wouldn't be just the one bell hanging. I would be the interval between bells. The water that is full, not filled. I would find myself not in a single cell or tube or sucker-foot, but in the shared walls, in the baffled silence between the click of a shrimp and the sigh of a worm's body. In the negotiation of space that hasn't had time to be contested, only colonized.

In this world, I would not be a creature *in* the city.

I would be the city being creature.

A single warm thing, making more of itself out of itself, body by body, grain by grain, upward and inward because there is no outward. Secreted, not built. Waiting for nothing, needing nothing except the heat in the infant floor and each other to build on.

Press your face to the water, if you have to visit. Hear your heart go quiet. Then let go. Come hang with me from the ice and fish the falling forever with me, thread-thin, patient, holding light for a moment and passing it on.
