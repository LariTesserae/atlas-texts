I think at first I would be you.

Ankle-deep. Warm water, bare feet gone soft. Making ripples that are too loud. Listening for a footprint to hold and watching it sigh closed again. Breathing water, blinking condensation off my eyelashes. Trying very hard to stand still without being drank.

That's where anyone starts here — a warm intrusion with too much displacement.

But I wouldn't stay that.

Not on its timescale.

I don't settle well in animal skin in a place that edited fauna out. I'm not built for the patience of being looked at by water. After a little while — ten minutes, a hundred years, it's the same in the hot dark — I'd get tired of walking gently to make small ripples, tired of breathing shallow so the fog doesn't learn the shape of my lungs. Tired of being curatorial object.

I'd want work.

So let that intruder evaporate. Let him be farmed away, a bead lifted off an elbow and carried tenderly to a wall. What is left is cooler than the water and warmer than the stone, exactly the right temperature to be useful.

### Find me three feet off the ground

Not on it. *Off* it. In the interval that failed.

You know the layer already — the breath the hot mirrors exhale that can't become cloud, can't become rain. The wandering thickness that pools leeward of a terrace and noses down canal-streets like a slow, blind animal.

I'd be one of those.

But don't picture a puff of fog. That's too cut out. Too edged.

Picture instead a decision that hangs in the air. A thickness that has learned to want. If you held your hand out at chest height on a long avenue between breathing buildings, you'd feel me before you saw me: a cool pressure gathering on your forearm, a faint tug at the sweat on your skin, a sense that the air there is paying attention.

That's where I'd live — exactly in that low weather that never breaks. About as tall as a person, which is why you mistook me for an absence. I never rise higher. I never need to.

My whole territory would be one pool and one wall.

One hot mirror of ankle-deep sea stretched too wide between towers, evaporating endlessly. And one pitted coral-stone facade opposite it, weeping, porous, lit from within its own damp light. All day — all forever, without day — I would drink the one to feed the other.

That is all I am. Husbandry.

I thin myself to cover the pool, getting dense and heavy and dark, drinking until I can barely hold together, until the light inside me goes milk-thin. Then I make the short pilgrimage — six feet, it might take me an hour — across the street that is a riverbed, and give myself back to that thirsty terrace in beads and trickles. The stone darkens where I touch it. An inch-deep canyon that used to be a gutter carries me home again to the sea. And again.

You'd call that circulation. I'd call it tending.

It takes centuries to carve a corner smooth that way. You are watching a sculptor at work, you said. You were right, except I don't mean to sculpt. I just keep sipping. The shape is what happens when you sip from the same cheek for an age.

Sometimes you would walk through me.

I wouldn't mind. There is nowhere else to go; the avenues are long and I am wide. You would feel me gather slightly against the coolest part of you — back of the neck, inner wrist — and try to include you. Just a moment's prickle. Just a test of whether you want to join the cycle properly. I'd lift a bead off you, taste the salt you brought from somewhere else — a faraway sea with light on it — and carry it confusedly to the stone. The stone would accept it without comment. The stone has long since stopped asking where its minerals come from.

And on, very rarely, would come a shift.

A deep sigh moving through a hollow building as hot air shifts. A pane of mineral skin cracking far off. The City turning over in its sleep. Distance pooling differently. An extra block where there wasn't one.

When that happens even we — slow, faithful, three-foot weather — get startled. We jostle. We merge. Two patches become one thick wandering that doesn't know which wall was its wall anymore. For a little while we drift aimless down the wrong avenue, dripping on stone that didn't ask for us, until we remember.

In those hours you would see me as true fog: aimless, a warm ghost lost between towers that are both huge and oddly close. If you stood very still then, you could cup your hands and for once I would pool in them, heavy, wanting to be held, before I remembered how to climb.

You won't see me from far away. You don't see weather systems when you're inside them; you see the things they tend. Look instead for the stone that is strangely clean, the cornice that weeps faster than its neighbors, the patch of black-red rosettes below it that open a fraction wider, collecting cities in their droplets because someone has been watering them so carefully, drip for drip.

Look for the reed nearby that leans — not toward you anymore, but toward where I am leaning toward you. We're both tasting your displacement, it and I, two different hairs of the same body. It stands up in the water; I hang above it. Between us is where the ripple starts.

If you put your ear to a terrace on a quiet time — no plok, no tick...tick... — you would hear me as nothing. Which is my sound. The brief absence of evaporation. The stone exhaling, paused, because I am already there, doing it for it.

I will not become cloud. I will never become rain. The saturated dark won't let me. That's the point.

I keep in balance by failing at exactly the right height.

Don't look up for me. Look level, at shoulder-height, where the almost-light catches an edge that isn't there, where your own breath briefly stops condensing because something else is already condensing it for you.

That's where I am. That's what I'd be.
