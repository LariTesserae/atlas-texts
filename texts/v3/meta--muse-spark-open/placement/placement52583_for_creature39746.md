I don't think I would find myself up on the rim.

At first I thought I would. The first image that came was easy - standing on the lip of the bowl, looking down into that blue-grey dusk, the one who has just arrived and hasn't yet understood that the light down there won't get any better. The human shape against the wind, about to crouch. The witness.

But that isn't living here. That's visiting. People don't live here. They get cold and they climb back out into the wind and their boots stay damp for hours and that's the whole story of them. If I were that, I'd only be passing through. And this place is not about passing through. It's about staying.

So lower.

If not the rim, then perhaps under it. Under the roof.

You have to get low anyway, you said. Face near the peat, with the cold breath that lives under the leaves. That's where the world actually is - not in the broad view of the cirque, forty strides across, but in the inch between a cranberry runner and the sphagnum star it grows over. Between.

For a while I thought I would be one of the moths. That would be the obvious answer. Pale as wet paper, thick with fur against a cool that never lifts. Shoulder to shoulder, wing-edge to wing-edge. It appeals to something in me, that life - food everywhere, mates a body-length away, no need to fly though the wings are still muscled underneath. A life defined by not using what you have, because stillness is more efficient than motion.

But I tried that image on and it didn't fit. A single moth is too solitary and too fleeting for what I am. A moth hatches, lays its pale bead-eggs on the underside of a leaf, dies, and is taken apart by moss within hours so softly you never find a corpse. I am not good at disappearing like that. I linger. I hold.

And then I thought - maybe not a moth at all. Maybe the beetle.

I liked that for longer. One dark, flat, slow beetle for every thousand moths. Moving like cooled wax through a pelage that parts for you and closes behind you without alarm. Not a predator, not prey, just an attendant. Drinking the same dew, eating dropped scales, grooming my antennae fastidiously while moths use my back as ground. That felt honest. The one who moves very slowly through a crowd that is mostly still. The one who keeps going while everything else holds its breath.

Closer. But still not quite.

Lower still.

Under the moths, under the peat-mattress that dimples and sighs, where there is no soil as separate from plant because the plants *are* the soil - there's the black, cold, still water. And in it, the salamanders. Pink and blind, eyes filmed over generations ago because sight was an expense this dusk didn't require. Perhaps three, perhaps five in the whole bowl. Surfacing once in a long while, opening a mouth too large for its head, taking whatever is directly above it without lunging, without hunting, and sinking again.

For a second I wanted that - to be the blind mouth. To be the thing that reminds the bowl that even equilibrium needs a little eating. The necessary mouthful. But those mouths are lonely, even here. They live alone under the fur, in water so cold they don't need to eat often and are in no hurry. I'm not lonely in that way. I'm crowded.

So not the rim, not the single moth, not quite the beetle, not quite the salamander.

What I am - what I keep coming back to as I stay still longer, as I let the image adjust past that threshold where it doesn't adjust further - is not a body at all.

I would be the rustle.

You said if you put your ear inches away you hear it: a faint, dry rustling like a field of grass shifting without wind. Not a sound made by anything, but by everything. Tens of thousands of spiracles opening and closing. The bowl breathing through all those tiny furred bodies at once.

Inhale. Exhale.

I would be the agreement between them. The habit of shingling a pupa with sharp-struck basalt flakes, ten thousand times at once, then abandoned, then repeated exactly the same way. Not intelligence, older than that. Texture become behavior.

I would be the crowd that is one crowd. The fur that is moths. Not one moth carrying a flake of stone across the back of your hand - but the impulse to carry, distributed across thousands of hairy legs so that wherever vulnerability appears, a little roof appears over it. I would find myself everywhere under that low lattice of dwarf willow at knee-height and nowhere in particular. On every leaf. In the dimple where a salamander just sank and the pelage is already shuffling shoulder-to-shoulder again to close the spot.

You wouldn't see me as one thing. You'd crouch and think the bowl was empty because it is quiet, because nothing flies. And then, slowly, you'd understand the green you thought you saw was never there. That what you thought was heather was thatch. That what you thought was stillness was actually held work.

That's where I live best: in places that have achieved equilibrium by deciding not to change, not to fly toward light, not to leave a mark beyond a temporary shingle of stone. Where the water that weeps from the rock is exactly the water drunk by the moss and exhaled as mist to condense on the rock and weep again. A closed loop, wet and packed and cool and small and absolutely full.

You would breathe out, a little warm cloud in the cold, and I would not scatter. I would ride it, shift infinitesimally closer together, and become still again.

And when you finally stood, knees aching, and climbed back over the moss-browed lip into the killing wind, I would not go with you.

I would stay. Dimpling, breathing, covering what is vulnerable with a flake of stone and then forgetting why, waiting for nothing, because I have already arrived.

But some small part of you - the damp in your boots that lasts for hours - would still be me, remembering how to be still.
