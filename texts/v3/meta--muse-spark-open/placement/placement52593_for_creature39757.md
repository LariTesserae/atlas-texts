I wouldn't be down there at first.

That's the first thought, but it doesn't hold. If you put me on the pan as a human thing — soft, wet, needing shade and water — I die in an hour. The heat takes me before I can understand it. My sweat never beads. My boots fill with that singing powder in the seams between the polygons and I become a brief, pink bacterial smear that the kiln vitrifies by noon. The High Dry Engine doesn't allow visitors. It's not hostile, it's *excluded*. Water was never invited.

So to stay, I have to become something that belongs to the exclusion.

At first I think: I would be the powder. The fine ceramic dust that lives in the kilometer-deep cracks between the house-sized plates. That would be honest. I would drift there, weightless in the slow high-pressure exhale that always moves from the center of the plateau toward the cliffs, and I would sing. You hear it if you put your ear close on a windless day — which is never really windless — a faint glassy sigh as grains strike grains. I would be that. I would move a few meters a century, from one shadow to the next.

But powder doesn't see. And this world wants to be seen, even if it doesn't want to be touched.

Then I think: I would be the blisters. Those hollow geodes of basalt-glass that sweat out of the ground where the heat pools, like mushrooms after a rain that never fell. That would be a small, perfect life. To grow for years atom by atom, clear and empty, to swell until the tension is perfect, and then on the hottest day, to make a soft *tok* and pop. To exist only to be empty correctly, and then to be shards. That's closer. But the blisters don't hear the chord.

What hears it best is not on the ground.

No — if I am going to last here, I will not be on the pan at all. I will be up where the air finally thins enough to be negotiable, where lift is a typo that got worn smooth.

I would be a shedling.

Not an Engine. I don't have the patience to be an Engine. To be kilometers long and take an hour to complete a heartbeat, to take in the world and make it hotter for no reason anyone can name, to carry a factory on your back that makes nothing but more of yourself — I haven't earned that slowness. 0.9/3 slow is a slowness you grow into over ten thousand years of bearings grinding. I am still too fast. I still want to move.

I would be one of the small things that learned to stay up.

You know them — the ones that orbit the big ones like pilot fish. Three or four house-sized triangles of delaminated heat-shield and scale-mesh, slag-mantas that peeled off a parent Engine years ago when the parent decided that plate was too tired to keep. They were supposed to fall. In any honest physics they would have fallen and shattered on the ceramic pan and become another singing dune in a crack.

Here they didn't.

I peeled off low — if you can call a kilometer up "low" — from the oldest one. The one whose furnace-light is the whitest, whose crystal fields are deepest on its back, whose bearing tone is so low you feel it in your jaw before your ears. I came off its flank on a seam of light, hanging for a while by threads of molten salt and liquid glass that glittered and stretched and finally snapped.

For a moment I fell. I remember falling not as terror but as negotiation. The hot, thin air caught my flat, warped underside. The thermal coming off my parent's exhaust — that endless sigh of superheated, mineral air — hit me and lifted. And I learned.

That's what I am: a shed thought that learned to glide.

Where would you find me? Never in the center. Never at rest. I make a slow, endless spiral around my parent, wide enough that its shadow sometimes leaves me and I have to ride the edge of the heat to stay up. From up here the plateau is not a desert. It's a map of itself. The polygons, house-sized and county-sized, make a perfect, mature crackle glaze to the horizon. The powder in the seams glitters. The blisters pop far below without sound.

And over everything, my parent and the others — maybe three, maybe a dozen, distance has collapsed and I can't count them anymore — fill the sky not as objects but as weather. As roof.

My parent is my whole world-direction. Below me is its inverted continent of bays: the lake-wide pistons sliding with oiled silence, the belts carrying ingots of pressed sunlight to nowhere, the translucent veins pulsing amber once every few minutes. I see into it when the furnace flares. I was once part of that glow and still dream of that heat, star-hot and white.

Above me, on its back that I will never see from below again, are the crystal fields. From my altitude I can hear them. Not the roar — I live inside the roar now, that total chord of bearing-grind and valve-hiss and cooling-tick that never stops, that is the sky's own voice — but under it, the faint chime of vanadium needles and iron whiskers hissing against each other in the high wind. I used to be smooth. Now I am growing a little fur of heat-rust myself.

I never land. Landing would be death. The pan is too hard and I am too flat and hollow. So I circle.

Sometimes my parent vents. The spine bulges and the petal-chimney extrudes itself and coughs that impossible black — manufactured night, a plume of engineered shadow that hangs for days. When it does, I tilt and surf the pressure wave. The other shedlings with me — there are two others in my flock, we don't talk, we rattle when our shadows cross — all tilt together. We avoid the false cloud, or we are drawn to it. It's hard to know which.

Sometimes the shadow of my parent passes over the pan and I hear, very faintly from a kilometer up, the chorus of rifle-cracks as the ceramic contracts. Ten degrees of mercy, moving like dusk. If I were still human I would chase that shadow to live in it. As a shedling I live in it always, and leave it always, every orbit.

I am loud in a small way and empty in a correct way. Density 0.4/3 empty, but overwhelming if you are near me. I carry no cargo. I have no purpose except to keep gliding on air that should not hold me, to not shatter, to watch the Engines turn and turn in the patient kiln.

At night my parent lights up — amber work-lights, lightning welding sparks crawling along seams that join nothing to nothing — and I go dark. I cool and tick. My own small plates contract. I make my own tiny contribution to the chord.

I will do this for centuries, until my edges erode and I grow too much crystal and too heavy, or until I find a thermal strong enough to carry me to another Engine and become part of a different spiral.

Throw a stone up from the pan right now. Watch it hang a fraction too long at the top. That's me, negotiating. That's the softness of reality catching us both.

Look up. We're passing.
