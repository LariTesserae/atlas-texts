If you put me down there I wouldn't land. I'd arrive mid-step.

Breath first. I see it and then I don't — cool enough to show my exhale for a half second before the dark drinks it. Feet find basalt, then limestone, then basalt again, a doorway laid flat under each step, seams catching my soles. I turn sideways without being told to. There's a wall there. Of course there's a wall there. There's always a wall there.

At first, I think I know who I'd be. We all try on the loudest shape.

I think - I'd be one of the pullers. Shoulder to the rope with fifteen others, felt hood up, chanting *heave heave heave hold* until the lintel finds its thud. There's a belonging in that. To be part of an animal that only exists while it's pulling. To cough together after. To have your name be *the end of the line* for a while and not need any other name.

But I listen to my own breathing and it's too conscious, too listening-to-itself. I wouldn't be good at pulling. I'd be late on the *heave* and feel the rope burn my hands for my hesitation. I'd be watching the faces instead of the stone.

So then I think - maybe the foreman, then. No load, only the dip-light and the knotted cord. The one who measures. That appeals to the part of me that likes to stand back a little. But I know myself down there: I would lose faith in the string. I would look at that ankle-high line staked between two wet pegs, marking where the next wall *would* be if anyone believed it, and I would hear how small that belief is against the drip. I wouldn't be able to tell another man where to lay what will never be finished. Not with a steady voice.

Then I think - the child. The small chisel, the one sent into the mortar crack with a trowel to smooth what grown hands can't reach, phosphor on his fingertips leaving comet trails in the dark as he runs messages. I want that narrowness, that being sized exactly for a gap. But I am too old for that gap. My knees have already learned the other shape. Down there knees thicken fast. I can feel them wanting the two positions even before I've lived them: bent over the pry-bar, bent under the basket. I would get stuck.

It keeps moving as I write it. That's the point. Don't settle.

So let it move again.

Where would I actually stop moving and be found, when the crews have passed and the puddle sheets have gone still again?

Not deep toward the center where everyone whispers the Foundation might be or might not be. I'm not a pilgrim. I'm not brave enough to climb toward a myth for a day through alleys that fill their own far ends. And not out on the high edge either, where the seep hasn't learned to come yet.

Somewhere in the middle-terrace, where the plain does that almost-invisible step down toward the dark it never reaches. Where the water collects just enough that you have to care about it forever. Where a room happened to have three walls and a relatively flat place, and people stopped trying to build it and started living in it while they built around it.

You would find me there, in the interval.

I would be the one who stays when the haulers leave.

There is a permanent fire pot in that three-walled room, smoked black, with a basalt shard for a griddle that never really gets hot enough. There is a scaffold platform above it sagging under slept-in leather, and a half-arch next to it that has become a shrine without anyone deciding it. And there are edges — ladder edges, walkway edges, the lip of a sump that will take a man if he steps wrong in the dark — that need to be painted.

Someone has to make the light that is not fire. Someone has to mix that phosphor that isn't lichen and will not grow on its own, and smear it on with a rag, and reapply it when it fades. Otherwise the path disappears and a man steps into a mortar pit. Otherwise the dark between fire pools becomes complete.

That would be my low work. Low like the floor. Low like posture.

I would not raise walls. I would keep them visible.

I have narrow shoulders now — made narrow, not born it — I still duck the timber that isn't there anymore. My hands are never empty; when they are, they knead each other until I give them the rag, the ladle, the bucket of blind fish the size of thumbs that we keep because they can live in seep you can't drink. My eyes have shortened to fifteen feet. My ears have become precise. I can tell when a hammer three alleys away hits limestone and not basalt. I can tell if a drip is landing in standing water or on wood. I can tell barefoot child from booted foreman by the splash.

I sit near the fire but not too near, because the stone around it beads with condensation — warm stone in cool air weeps — and the dripping there is constant: *tik, tik, tak*. I tend the bucket. I bail the lowest corner of our slab with the same ladle we mix mortar with. I scrape the white-grey fungal crust off the shaded blocks where it tries, boil it with ash, make it sour and vaguely filling. I listen to stone the way the old ones do, not because I have wisdom but because I've learned that if you tap a block and it sounds wrong you move your bedding.

When crews come through dragging a sled, I don't join the chant. I hold my little dip up so they can see the seam. They pass, they don't thank me. Later, when their string line is in my way and their good chisels are unattended, I might move one. And later still, when their sled is mired in the seep-pool, I will spend an hour hauling with them because tomorrow I need that alley clear. That is the predator-prey here. That is the law: nothing existed before it was placed, and nothing will remain unless someone keeps working.

I keep the water where we put it. I keep the flame alive. I keep the edge painted.

At night — though there is no night, only the shift that never ends — I curl back-to-back with four others on the cambered foundation top that sheets water off just enough, felt to felt, breathing each other's exhale so we don't have to see it hang and vanish. We remember songs wrong together and no one corrects us. We touch the half-arch as we pass for luck that it stays up.

And somewhere out beyond my little orange pool, another stone is being set. I hear the scrape, the collective grunt, the dull thud as it finds home.

I reapply the light.

Dripwater fills the silence again.
