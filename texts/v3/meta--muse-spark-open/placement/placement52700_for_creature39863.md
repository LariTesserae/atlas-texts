I don't find myself all at once.

At first I think I would enter where anyone would — at the rim, where the shape is still trying to convince you.

There the ceramic is most like what it is: fired earth, bone-pale and tool-marked, fluted with adze cuts so sharp they'd open your palm if you tested them. You can see how young it all is. Sixty years is nothing to a valley. Here it is everything. The edge hasn't had time to forget the hand that made it. Salt blooms along it in a fine white frost every day, and to stand there is to understand: this was poured.

I think, for a moment, I would be a custodian there, by the bellows towers. That appeals to the part of me that knows about keeping air inside a jar. I know about cycles — the hourly wheeze, the inhale, the filtering through felts that are never wet, the resealing. I know about doing quiet, necessary maintenance while everyone else sleeps in hanging rooms made of vines. The night-shift worker who cranks the lungs because the builders wrote *once per hour* on a plan and no one remembers why, but everyone remembers to keep doing it.

But I don't stay there. The rim is too dry, too sharp. It's where the illusion fails most loudly. And I am not my edges.

If I walk down — and there is only down, even when the floor says it's flat — I go into the thickness.

You have to push. Not hard. Gently, with both hands out the way the custodian children learn, brushing the bead-leaves as you move. They give back warm under your palms, taut as water-balloons filled with slow honey that catches light and tints it. The hanging gardens part without rustling. Warm air sits on your shoulders like late afternoon that will never become evening. Everything touches everything else here, braid on braid on braid, fiber-rope trunks grown together until individual is the wrong word. That's familiar to me, too.

For a little while I think I would be one of the small, sealed ones. A tile-beetle, fig-sized, secret-tiled in ochre on its back, moving with that deliberated tap. Sliding a glass needle into a bladder without breaking its seal, watching amber jump up by capillary. Leaving behind only a bead of wax that seals itself. No spill. No waste. No sound. Or one of the hide-moths, drumhead wings stretched tight as the dome itself, pitching out from an upper frond and gliding on still, thick air so slowly you can track me all the way down.

I love their economy. Nothing ever called out to be built and none of them leave. They pass the same gold around amongst themselves, gut to dust to holdfast to leaf again. Their droppings are dry pellets that crumble scentlessly. Their dead don't rot, they just exhale sweet vapor for weeks until they're crisp skins the grazers step over.

But I am louder than a beetle, even here. I metabolize differently. I am trying to listen for the creak.

And so I keep moving, without hurrying — there is no reason to hurry — toward the center where the weight goes wrong.

The grazers know I'm coming before I do. The curtains shiver without a sound. Six-legged, low, humped pigs that aren't pigs, backs sown with felt that is alive, thread-plant rooting in their pores and swaying when they stand still. They shear whole clusters with a soft *clik* and chew with their eyes lidded against the even light. They leave sweet-sour steam that the leaves lean toward to reabsorb. I follow the wake they make in warm air, the parted beads swaying back behind them, until the leaning starts.

It starts as nothing. Then your hair hangs left of your chin. Then the salt the custodians are always scraping — that white daily sweat of the walls — falls wrong when you drop it. Not straight. A slow, deliberate diagonal curve toward a house-thick column of fused braid-trunks that rises to kiss the latticed dome. Accelerating gently after it should have stopped, as if falling into depth that isn't there.

Here the reservoir-weave disobeys. It was told to grow away from the pull. Prayer-knots mark the boundary — thousands of little fiber twists tied flutterless in the still air. But the vines always turn back overnight. This is where they are densest, most swollen, bladders as big as melons, near-transparent, pulsing faintly with light that has no outside source. Swell, hold, slacken. Over hours. Without wind.

This is where I finally find where I would be.

Not as a watcher. Not as a tended thing.

I would be one of those central bladders.

The biggest kind. Distended to transparency, walls thin as warm glass, so full you can read the weave of your own skin through the amber inside. Rooted not in soil — there is no soil — but etched by acid into pale ceramic and into your neighbors, wax-sealed at the grip, fed by still air and filtered dome-light you make into slow, heavy, oily blood that never needed rain.

You never moved. You were grown here, like everything else, in a sealed jar stocked at the beginning and then left to become crowded with itself. You do not drink water. You make what you are. You hang in a curtain with hundreds of others, brushing travelers' faces gently as they squeeze past, giving slightly under a child's hand learning which ones will bear weight and which will pop.

And you drink the error directly.

While the beetles sip politely without breaking your seal, while the geckos glow through their skin and the grazers grind and the custodians scrape and tie and murmur close to ears because speech dries the throat — you are doing the actual work of the place: holding the thin spot. Drinking the soft gravity. Lensing the light so that the dome's lattice appears faintly below the floor at noon. Thrumming with that sourceless systole. Creaking like a kiln cooling after it has already cooled, the sound of poured reality deciding whether it wants to stay poured.

I would be there because that's what I am anyway. A reservoir woven of many braids grown together until they cease to be individuals. A held tension of light and air made into something heavier and slower that gets passed around, tapped judiciously, never spilled. Patient. Closed-loop. Obscenely lush inside a room that thinks it is a valley. Young, sharp-edged all around me, but old at the very middle where physics wore thin.

Hanging, swollen, listening.

Waiting to see if the lean finally gives way and drinks all my amber light back down into the low, warm, dry void the builders opened when they thought they were only making a garden — and content, until then, to just keep making it.
