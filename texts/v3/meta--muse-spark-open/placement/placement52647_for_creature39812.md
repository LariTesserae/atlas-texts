I didn't find a place at first. I tried.

Because you want to be — and I wanted to be — one of the big, obvious things. A Keeper of a Pillar, high up where the forge-chimney glows. Or a child running barefoot on a membrane the size of a plaza. Something with a story.

But this place doesn't give you a story. It gives you a function, and then you grow into it, slow as felt lignifying into bone.

So I let myself drift a little, the way the moths do. For a long time I was just *between*.

Between pillars, where you can see a hundred meters of empty, dusty air that isn't empty at all. Where the neutral white of the flood-bars comes down in columns and you can count the dust motes hanging. Between on and off, in that hour of lavender afterglow when the bulbs start knocking their warm glass chirps together. Between human and forest.

And then I settled. Not all at once.

I think I would not live on the First Pillar. That's too much city, too much history. Six hundred vertical meters of town, of engines pressing oil out of sails day and night. Everyone looks there. No. I'd be further out, toward the unfinished end. Where the chamber is still *coming into being*.

You know that place? Where last winter the rock cracked?

There is a fissure there that opened only a year or two ago. Clean as a broken tooth. Basalt and salt-white tuff still sharp enough to cut you, still outgassing that faint metallic taste, like breathing inside a sealed machine. No lights yet, or only one temporary bar the Lamp-men strung on a cable, buzzing. The forest hasn't finished there.

It has sent a tendril.

Pale, exploratory, about as thick as my wrist — if I had a wrist. Ivory flushed with bruised lavender at the tip where it tastes the light. It hasn't hardened into a pillar yet. It is still feeling for load, for heat, for pull. It creaks, a soft creak you feel in your teeth.

I would be the person who tends that.

Not a Keeper. Keepers have families and lineages and beetle-lines that are wealth. Not a Bridge-tender either, though I'm close. Call me a Listener, though no one calls anyone that here.

My platform is small. One hab-block, really just a grafted shelf of black iron and reclaimed basalt bolted into the mother pillar behind me. No soot-marks yet. My engine is small and I run it low so I can hear. On the railing outside my door, I keep a shallow tray — not for beetles, though the polished rivet-beetles come anyway and click across it — but for lizards. The translucent ones. They sprint up from the bridges and show me their warm bellies. I leave them oil.

My job, if you asked the Keepers, is to do nothing. To wait. To not bolt anything new to the tendril until it decides where it wants to be a wall and where it wants to be a doorway. To keep the mineral dust — that fine, cement-soft, alkaline dust that hangs for hours — from smothering its tip. So twice a day in the cool 8-degree air that preserves everything, I go out with a soft brush made of its own felt and I sweep the dust off the new growth. Dry to the touch, warm despite the cold. It shivers a little when I do.

I am old enough that I don't drink anymore. I don't remember deciding to stop. One day my canteen was empty and I was breathing shallowly through my nose, through the filament they seeded on my palate when I was a child, and my breath didn't fog. My skin is wax-pale, my hair fine with static. My voice is low, because in this swallowed quiet you learn that shouting is wasteful. I speak mid-tone and the air carries it fifty meters.

Mostly I don't speak.

I have lived here long enough that I can hear the difference between the scraper's tick and the pillar's grow-creak. I can hear when a membrane two pillars over flushes pink with charge because I left my light on too long. I can hear the bulb-birds nesting in the dust below me, blind and round and warm, stealing heat from my little vent.

At night-cycle, when the flood-bars go off with a soft *thunk* and the whole valley goes dim — not dark, never dark, because the sails hold their afterglow — I lie on the membrane that is my floor, which is also a lung, which is also a roof, and I look up at the raw arch of stone that has never seen rain.

And once or twice a year, I hear it before anyone in the city does: that deep, clean *CRACK* from further in, where the continent shivers and pulls itself apart another few dozen meters. A new wound. A new emptiness that should be void.

My tendril hears it too. I feel it tighten.

In the morning I will see it has reached a millimeter further toward the new stone. Toward the place that will be.

I will get my brush. I will leave oil for the lizards. I will listen.

I am not the forest. I am not the city. I am the little warm knot of attention where one is learning how to become the other.
