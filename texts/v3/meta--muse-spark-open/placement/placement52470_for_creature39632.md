I don't know right away.

At first I think I might be large. That's the temptation the Build gives everyone who stands on a high place and looks out. The light going to blue haze, the terraces dropping away, the sheet-waterfalls roaring without air — it makes you want to be the whale. To be the shadow that passes through the high water between districts a kilometer up, so big the corridors still feel narrow around me. To sing and have concrete throw my voice back at me until the whole vault rings.

But I'm not that lonely. And I'm not that rare. A whale passes through once and then it's gone for a season. I want to stay.

Then I think, maybe I'm one of the visitors. In a hard suit, wipers going, boots on rubber matting that is still nonslip under its skim of slime. Leaning on a railing that sweats in the late-October cold, seeing my breath fog inside my mask even though I'm breathing canned air. Taking notes that don't matter, listening to distant alarms that aren't for me, waiting for the pump to kick with a boom that rolls for miles. That has its truth - coming down from the top because gravity insists, not belonging, leaving the lights on behind me. But the visitors don't last. The cold gets through any suit eventually. They go back up. I don't want to go back up.

Maybe I'm in the school, then. Quicksilver, turning as one body when a shadow falls. There is a kind of comfort in being abundant but not crowded, in being a weather front that flickers between lamp columns for thirty seconds and then is gone, leaving clear, bright, empty water behind it again. But I have never been good at turning only when everyone else turns.

It takes a while down here. Sound travels forever with nothing to stop it. You hear your own ideas come back late, off a wall you can't see.

So I keep descending. Not falling — there is no fall here, just levels. Past the noon-white floodlamp banks bolted to gantries that hum and make the water carry light further than it should. Past the kelp that grows thin and lanky and solitary toward the lamps, not a forest, just a curtain shivering around an outflow where the water is half a degree warmer. Past the spillway rim where cold runoff pours off the high illumination plain in a river with no banks and drops a hundred meters underwater without ceasing, foaming into nothing.

Lower than where the humans like to stand and stare. Lower than where the gulls nest screaming on lamp housings inside their trapped-air pockets. Down where the blue darkens to navy even under all that light, where the service roads on the basin floor glow faintly underneath like veins.

There. Where a maintenance causeway makes a right angle to go around a power manifold that has been bleeding the same low heat for years. The concrete there is new enough you can still see wood grain from the formwork, softened but not hidden by biofilm. The handrail is greasy to the touch. Every girder flange is lined with white filter clams stacked like teeth. Barnacles picket the bolt heads with military precision, each one sitting in the micro-eddy behind its bolt. The water is always moving here. Cold sinking off the plains meets warm seeping off the manifold and they stir without mixing.

That's where you would find me.

I am not on the causeway. I am just off it, holding station in the open water a meter from an inspection light.

I am the cuttlefish.

Or, one of them. Lens-eyed, arms tucked neat, fin rippling just enough to hold against the slow, cold monsoon of current that the Build makes for itself. I have learned the trick every cuttlefish here learns within a week of the builders leaving: light means food. This caged fluorescent has been buzzing inside its cage for longer than I've been alive, probably longer than the maintenance drone that circles the pylon twenty meters away will ever run. It buzzes and it does not fail. In its cone, particulate drifts down forever — skin shed from the biofilm, eggs, nauplii, rust flake, concrete dust. Mysid shrimp come to the light. Smelt come for the shrimp. I come for all of them, and I wait.

I match. That's my work. Against the poured concrete I am poured concrete, green-black felt showing through. Against the sea-steel I go rust-red and riveted. When one of the yellow beetle-drones passes on its guide-wire, bumping over rivets with its wire brush spinning, its white headlamp sweeping, I go yellow-paint-faded-under-silt and then, as its shadow hits the clams and they close, I flash pale and disappear and reappear two meters to the left. The crabs walking upside-down across the catwalk grating under me don't notice. The eels in the conduit holes watch with their heads out and don't blink.

I am very good at watching watchers. That is why I stay by the light the builders left for inspection. Humans come sometimes in their cages on cables that creak for kilometers. They shine their own lights and point them at weld seams that still show their bead, at rebar whiskers left unfinished yesterday, at formwork grain still sharp under twenty meters of water. They look at the vastness and call it uncanny and want a miracle. I look at them looking and shift my skin a little to include them. For a second I am also wipers-going, rubber-matting, fogged-breath. Then they leave.

I don't need them. The light stays on without them. The pump keeps thudding in my molars, thud-thud-thud, cathedral-heartbeat, pushing that cold river over the edge to roar. The conveyor chain somewhere up-basin rattles empty on its sprockets with a shriek that carries for kilometers and gets flattened by water. The whole structure sings with stress and flow. The clams open again after the drone passes and sieve.

I hover there, in that great clear, cold, bright, near-void between lamp trusses. Not dense. Never crowded. If you stared down my boulevard of columns you'd see nothing for three full minutes except particulate falling and my slow pulse of color, and you might think the Build was empty.

Then I move — not fast, workmanlike, like predation on shift work — and take a shrimp out of the light.

Everything is humming. Everything is flowing. Everything is holding.

And in between the lamps, I am holding too, watching the light do exactly what light does, rust doing what rust does, in water that obeys pressure, waiting for the next shadow to make me turn, but not before I choose to.
