I don't find myself on the boulevards.

At first I think I would. That is where *you* would put a person — up on the highest layered terrace, looking out, boots on hot concrete, hands in pockets, making footprints that come back as echoes. A witness. A census of one trying to prove 0.0 is wrong.

But if I stay there long enough to be honest, the city edits me out. It doesn't need a witness. It barely registers a cooler patch of air. If I stood there, still enough and long enough, the salt would crust over my boots and the steam would bead and run down my shoulders the same way it beads and runs down every other vertical surface. I would become wall. And the avenues are too wide for anything that has a face. They were made to be riverbeds.

So I sink.

Not down — *in.*

You would find me where the water goes vertical.

Halfway up a weeping wall behind the habitation blocks, where the old pitted concrete has given up and the true basalt shows through underneath, wine-purple and forever wet. There is a seam there that never dries. A bright thread of hot mineral water strings down it all day, slow enough to count, fast enough to never stop. The air is dim amber there, quiet except the ticking of the black bloom drying somewhere else, and the distant *clank* three blocks over that I have learned not to flinch at.

That is where I am.

What am I?

At first, I am the simplest thing: a palm-sized bell of damp cellophane pinned to the rock by a blind mouth.

One of the Filterers.

I thought I would be the hound — something with bones and skin and that beautiful, spare intelligence that knows not to waste movement. Or I thought I would be the city-reef itself, vast and patient and breathing steam loud enough to be weather. But I am not patient enough to be a city and not rare enough to be a hound. Hounds have to be six in a valley. Cities have to be one on a plateau.

I am numerous enough to be forgettable and light enough to let go.

I pulse. That's my whole theology.

Once every breath you take up there in the thin, heavy air, I pull a film of that scalding, sulfur-sharp water over myself. I strain it. Iron, salt, spores of black bloom, flecks of dissolved rust from a girder that hasn't seen a hand in fifty years. I don't eat. I make water slightly more itself by passing it through me.

For hours I do nothing but this. Tight against the stone, translucent, veined darker than vein, feeling the vibration of the water screw groaning somewhere uphill through the rock itself. This is good. This is enough. My neighbors are doing it too, a hundred of us on this one wall, none of us coordinated, all of us agreeing.

And then the heat shifts.

You wouldn't feel it up on your boulevard, but down here on the wall I do. A low factory exhales unevenly. The haze thickens until the light goes from amber-dusk to no-light. The air gets unbreathable-soup-thick, and condensation starts to wring itself out of nothing onto everything.

Something lets go. Not me, first. Three hand-spans to my left. Then below me. Then —

I let go.

This is who I really am. Not the clinging. The drift.

We don't fly. We hang. Ash that forgot how to fall. A whole section of wall detaches at once without a signal and suddenly I am in the air, turning inside-out slowly, trailing my veined curtain, a bell now filtering steam instead of seep. With two hundred others. Silent. Slow. Pulsing still, because that is the only thing I know how to do whether I'm on stone or in sky.

From here I can see it properly.

Below, the avenue runs an inch deep with hot water, reflecting the furnaces. A conveyor moves its slate of pressed mineral wool to no one. Far below, by the powerplant with no workers, a pink-grey hound-shaped shadow detaches itself from thicker steam to drink at a sump, and I am glad I am not him — he has to remember to be still, I just *am* still while moving.

Above, the black bloom on every north-facing seam tightens and goes glossy, sealing itself to drink the air directly.

Around, my kin hang. We taste the steam for the next good wall. One of us will land on your sleeve if you're still standing up there on your terrace, and leave it wetter than water for a second before peeling away. Deciding you are not a wall worth living on.

You would find me in transit, between walls, between the city's inhale and exhale.

If you want a name for what I am: I am the interval. I am what lives in 0.0 and makes it not empty. I am the city's way of turning its own breath inside-out to see if there's anything in it.

And when the hot wind shears in from the plateau edge and flash-dries everything, I will find a new seep. Pin myself to it. Pulse.

Until next time the city breathes heavy enough to carry me again.
