I don't think I would be standing at first.

If you dropped me onto that white, bright, air-that-you-have-to-chew — I imagine my first instinct would be to stay upright. To keep my boots on the squeaking felt and watch the continents of backs go by. To be the visitor. The warm thing that doesn't belong, steaming politely in its suit, taking notes while the world breathes around it.

But that's not belonging. That's just trespassing slowly.

If I actually *lived* here — not visited, but was grown by this place the way this place grows everything — I wouldn't get to be tall.

At first I thought I'd want to be one of the big ones. A Pillar-Furnace. It's tempting, isn't it? To be the climate instead of living in it. To be a house-sized barge on forty oven-mitt feet, to carry your own weather, to throw a warm shadow fifty yards long where the felt grows twice as thick just because you stood there. To have lungs like rooms and guts like kilns and to speak only in sighs and clangs.

But I don't think I have the temperament for that kind of slowness. Those things take a decade to notice they're dying. They don't decide to move, they *lean* toward wetter rock over the course of a season. Their thoughts, if they have them, must be long and low-pressure: *drink / heat / stand*. I would get impatient inside that much patience. I would try to hurry and blow a valve.

Then I thought — smaller, faster? A Trench-Grazer, wedge-headed, smoking from both flanks as I carved lines through the mat? That has appeal, that endless honest work. Run between the legs of gods, eat carpet, leave a furrow full of humid air behind you. But grazers never stop. They are all mouth and little pink furnace, and they have to be, because the mat stitches itself shut behind them within weeks. I would eat and eat and never make anything that lasts.

No. If I know myself at all, I wouldn't be a maker of heat. I'd be a keeper of it.

So let me look lower. Closer to the ground than standing.

You'd find me not on the horizon where the big backs make a new mountain range, but underneath. In the places the light doesn't quite reach.

Lift the corner of the mat. You know the smell there — hot iron and compost, 25 degrees warmer than the killing air above. The underside of the world sweats constantly. Droplets hang from fungal threads like beads on a loom, never falling, just waiting to be drunk. Sound goes muffled there. The great chuff-CHUFF of the herd becomes a heartbeat through basalt and root.

That's where I'd be.

I would be one of the white ones. The Heat-Tappers.

Not big. Hand-sized. Domed shell frosted over against my own heat-loss because irony is everywhere on this plateau. Six hooked legs not for walking fast but for *holding on*. The kind of creature whose entire evolutionary philosophy is: find something warmer than you and don't let go.

You'd find me clinging to the belly of a young Furnace who hasn't learned to regulate quite right. She — they — runs a little hot, whistles on the exhale through an imperfect valve on her third left leg. Her belly curtain still hangs low after drinking, dripping with black melt-pool water that never had time to freeze. The felt growing in the furrows of her back is still patchy, adolescent violet-black, not yet the thick shag of an old slab.

I am tucked up near that whistling valve, where the seam between chitin plates leaks a jet of wet, pressurized air. My mouth is not a mouth, it's a needle, a thermal siphon. I don't drink blood unless I have to. I drink *temperature* itself. I press my whole flat belly to her skin and let her excess bloom into me.

You can see others like me there too — a reef of us, ticking and clicking as we expand and contract like cooling metal. We clean the infection from her valve as we feed. That's the deal. She gets to keep singing her off-key whistle without it sticking shut, I get to stay warm enough that my own little furnace — pink and frantic behind my translucent back — doesn't gutter out.

When she moves — inches per hour, a continent drifting — I feel it first as a change in the shade and the smell. The mat below thins where she's peeled it like carpet, leaving raw, smoking basalt behind us. Ahead, a wall of untouched felt squeaks under her rasp-lip and disappears into that dull red kiln inside her. Behind us, already, the Wax-Slurpers are swarming over the tar she laid, hundreds of flat limbless ribbons sealing her molten shit into lacquer so the mat has something to root in again. We Tappers ride above it all, trembling on our hooks.

And when she finally parks for three days over a good weeping fracture to pump — chuff-CHUFF, chuff-CHUFF, so deep my shell hums — the steam hangs weirdly. Not rising. Just... hanging, in a perfect trembling sheet ten feet off the ground, invisible glass in the air. The new gravity hasn't decided yet. Light arrives with no shadow and suddenly the hillside a mile away looks like it's right against my eyes. Sound that happened yesterday on the other side of the scarp arrives late and folded.

I don't understand any of it. I don't need to.

On the coldest part of the long bright midnight — which is just noon again but paler — I will detach for a moment and skitter across her belly to a better vent, leaving six tiny glowing footprints that fade in seconds. A Trench-Grazer will pause below and look up with stupid, steamy eyes. The Furnace will sigh and snow herself onto us all.

If I die, I will not fall far. Just lose my grip and drop into the warm trench we've made, become food for the mat, become sweat on its underside, become the next secret pool some other young furnace will park over.

If she dies — decades from now, settling slow into a hill that stays warm for years after the last chuff — I will already be gone, having ridden her sister, and her sister's calf. But the warm hollow we made together in the basalt, just by standing there and being hot together, will smoke for a century after we're both forgotten.

That's the trick, isn't it? Nothing here ever built a wall to keep the cold out. The cold is kept out by all of us, each doing our small, leaky, noisy job in the factory with no foreman.

Find me there. Under the mat's vaulted ceiling. Ticking faintly. Holding on tight to something larger and warmer than myself, and calling it home.
