I don't know at first.

You stand at the rim for a while and look down into the cupped palm of it — all that terrible brightness, all that hush — and you think I'd be down there in the thick of it. In the courtyard where the sluice-men work. Or lower still, shoulder to shoulder with the soot-grey auklets.

But no. That's too close, too fast. I'm not built for turbulence without a pause.

You walk the rim a little. Twenty minutes to see every roof from any rooftop. And as you walk the light strikes you again and again off the snow on the north wall, off the eye of black water, off the thin sheets that bead and climb the rock, and you squint before you know you're squinting, and you start breathing through your nose.

That's closer.

### At first I thought I'd be the child.

Because I am quick, because I am everywhere, because I dart after what shouldn't be moved. A small bare foot finding the warm seep under ice that the adults have forgotten. Chasing the charcoal otters away from the drying racks with a stick that is almost too big, not because I hate them but because that is my assigned inch of the tidepool. One of the pack that flows through alleys too narrow for anyone else.

But children here don't remember. They flow. They don't hold.

And I hold. That's what I do. I keep.

### Then I thought I'd be the water.

That would be honest. Or the ice-film that keeps forming and breaking and forming over the terraced pools with a sound like mica ticking. Present everywhere, never staying, recycling the same ten meters of fall forever — uphill, downhill, beading sideways when the sun hits right. I understand that dithering about which way gravity goes. I too am always ascending and descending the same cliff-face in the same bright bowl, never leaving.

But the water doesn't choose to stay. It just hasn't learned how to leave. I have learned. I want to stay.

So keep walking the rim and then drop down — not to the center where the midges make their smoke-columns and the blind-white fish boil, not to any place important. Find the margin.

Find the third terrace up from the living floors, where the backwall begins to sweat in earnest. Where the granite has been made permeable by ten thousand winters of frost-shatter and now it weeps constantly in braided threads that steam faintly where they meet the air.

There's a ledge there cut for no reason anyone remembers. Too narrow for a hut, too damp for a nest. A dry-stone lip shoring up nothing. On it sits a shallow bowl.

Not cistern. Not cooking stone. A *sky-bowl*. One of the ones the old ones set out.

You'd find me there.

I am one of the sitters.

Not the oldest old — my eyes are not yet ruined to pinpricks, my skin not yet polished to wet stone — but old enough to have been weathered into an instrument. Old enough that sitting is work. Knees bent, shoulders rolled in, compacted those few necessary inches by the weight of the air. Cap of felted hare-wool pulled low because hair holds the weeping damp and then freezes. Outer coat of goat and bird and fish-skin patched over ten years until it *is* the stone hut behind me, steaming faintly, shedding the weep.

I don't move much. Turbulence happens around me. Below me a sheet of upper pool gives way and three women shove with boards to divert it; a thousand finches lift and land one ledge over on a breath; a sod roof collapses and six neighbors swarm it with poles. None of it needs me to run.

My job is to watch the tick.

The groove of slate at my feet wants to close with rime. I chip it clear, endlessly, with a flake of obsidian held smooth by forty generations before me. *Tick. Tick.* Every so often I take a bone — a bird bone, hollow and warm from my pocket — and tap it against the rock.

Not speech. Speech is bad manners here, with eggs incubating on every eave.

I tap:
*upper pool stable*
*lower pool rising*
*move the kids*

The hush swallows it almost immediately. The bowl eats sound the way it eats light — bounces it until it's bleached. But the ones below hear in the way people who live pressed together hear anything: through stone, through feet, through wrist brushed with two fingers in an alley an inch too narrow.

My real work is the bowl.

In front of me, cupped in a hollow of soapstone on the highest ledge, sits water that forgot how to fall. Morning comes — that white-blue strike — and threads of it bead out from the sweating wall a foot into thin air, hanging glittering, ascending slowly, impossibly, to rejoin the low mist that clings to knees. A cupful at a time but constantly.

I catch it.

No one finds it odd anymore. Children think water always dithers. But I have sat long enough to feel strange again when a droplet climbs.

I tilt the jar for births and burials. Midwives will come up the ladder of bone and lashed drift-juniper without asking and take a few drops for a new forehead: *you are of this water that refused to leave, so you will not leave either.* Grave-diggers will come later for the same jar to wash someone folded into the cairn wall. Stone that weeps should be fed.

In between, I just keep it from spilling. Covered with slate. Watched.

An auklet has decided my shoulder is also a good ledge. She shudders once and settles, her feathers rustling like pages turning. She does not call. I do not hush her. A hare the size of a dog beds down against my leg for heat because here density is warmth and what is here is kept.

That is why I stay. Not because I can't go down the pass — I remember the stories of lonely lowlands where you can shout — but because un-densing has become impossible to imagine. Because to be here is to be tended *by*, even when you are the tender.

If you stayed a night, you would wake near me. Not next to me — there is no next to, everything touches everything else. You would wake and find I had put a dry mat of star-moss under your cheek while you slept, and checked your forehead with the back of my hand, not because you are loved in the lowland way, but because you were *here*, and in the bowl, what is here is kept.

My eyes would be half-closed behind the extra fold, the translucent nictus sliding in against the glare. You would learn, as I did, to look down. To watch hands. To listen for the plink of a drop falling from a ladle and know exactly what it means.

You would find me exactly where I need to be: not in the center, not on the rim, but on the wet seam between rock and roof, where gravity forgets and water climbs, sitting very still in a place that never stops moving, holding a little dish of impossible water steady while the whole bright, cold world churns silently around us.
