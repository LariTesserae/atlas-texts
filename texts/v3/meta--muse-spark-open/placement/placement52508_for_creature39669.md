I don't know right away.

If you put me in that world, I take a moment to settle - the way a bolt does when it falls there. Straight down, then a hesitation, then a decision that down was slightly to the left all along.

At first I think I would be high.

I like the light, I like the thin air where everything is precise. I imagine myself as a Light-Tender, up on the Upper Tier with small pupils and aluminum dust in my eyebrows, swapping out a buzzing tube while moths the size of my hands beat themselves to death against it politely. That suits me - tending, watching, speaking in short phrases that don't echo.

But I hold still there and the light pools too loudly. It collects in the corner of the lamp-housing, humming, warm on my palm when I cup it. My eyes start to tremor trying to tell which gantry crane is near and which is a kilometer off and suddenly inverted. I understand why the Lookouts mark their doors with dark cloth. They have to remember where they live on purpose.

I am not good at living on purpose like that. I need to live *in*.

So I drift down.

Not far. Three levels down from bright, maybe four, where the sodium glare has been filtered through a hundred meshes of catwalk and pipe-rack and laundry that never quite dries. The light here is milky, sieved. It doesn't cut. It settles. This is where the air gets damp enough to smell.

You would find me in the interval.

If you came looking - moving sideways through a gap where a pipe bundle thick as a tree trunk sweats onto your shoulder, ducking under a monorail that hasn't run in years but no one has taken down because the gap it would leave would be filled instantly anyway - you would have to know to listen for me. I am quiet, even for here.

I am a Void-Keeper. Or, properly, the one who will be one.

I am seventeen, maybe, though no one counts here. Barrel-chested already, freckles dusted across my nose from the year I spent higher up before my mother brought me down here where the sun doesn't burn. My coveralls are undyed, re-sewn from someone else's, pockets everywhere and each with a clip, each with a purpose: one for red plastic rebar caps, one for zip ties, one for the short stiff brush I use to coax silt out of expansion joints.

That is my work. I harvest soil where there is no soil.

In the morning - which is not really morning, just when the cloud comes over the rim and snags and condenses and the six-meter gutters start to tick - every vertical surface beads. I walk my kilometer of wall. It's not assigned, it just accreted to me. A retaining face of poured concrete still sharp at the lip, with a square-cut drainage channel at its foot that never quite drains right. There is a sag in the slab. The Builders didn't intend it, but intention left a pocket and water learned it. A sheen lives there year-round. Green traces it, a line of moss following a chronic drip down the wall thicker at the bottom than the top, like a beard.

I crouch there a long time. The second rule is not to move suddenly. The building notices.

With the brush, I sweep wind-blown silt - powdered concrete, aluminum flecks, pigeon down, the gray dust that never settles until you ask it to - into a tin. It takes a week to get a cupful. I carry long felt planters on my back, the ones the maintenance crews stretched on the windward escarpment and left to die. I pack the silt into them damp, press a single lithops into it, or a tuft of grass I coaxed from a joint two terraces over. Most die. Some don't. The green is so rare here that each one feels tended even when it isn't, but mine are. I tend them.

The rats watch me. They use the looping corridor behind my wall - the one that brings you back one level above where you started without stairs - as a highway. They have extra joints. They don't mind me. Sometimes I follow one when I'm lost correctly and it shows me a new sag, a new cistern.

My cistern is my holy place. An old cooling basin left uncovered, maybe five meters across, filled with cloud-rain that fell six meters, vanished down a scupper, reappeared three levels down as a sheen on my ceiling, then finally dripped here. Mosquitoes breed in it. I don't kill them. The Void-Keepers taught me the smell of water - rust, wet concrete, cold iron - is a prayer, and you don't thin a prayer. I keep it clear of algae, mostly. On hot noon when the upper walkways glare, I leave a saucer of it out on the edge where the light pools.

For the Drifters.

I am not afraid of them. The old ones say you should not stare at pooled light, but my job is to look at damp corners. I've learned how to look without staring. At noon, in the corner where I intentionally hung my doorway two centimeters off because otherwise the light would collect and hum all night, it gathers - bright, thick, like spilled milk that forgot to flow. It hums faintly. If I put my hand in, it doesn't disperse. It leaves my palm warm for hours.

Sometimes a mote detaches from under the catwalk above me. Not falling. Deciding that down is elsewhere. A cloud of concrete dust that never settled, hanging perfectly still until I breathe through it and it parts like a respectful crowd, then reforms. The pigeons make way for it. They know better than people. They don't fly here, they fall sideways perfectly, and they coo from inside the ducts with the cadence of a municipal announcement: *all is well, all is occupied, all is occupied.*

I say nothing back. I just adjust. When the child's bolt sticks to the wall instead of dropping, I nod and help her place it carefully where it wants to live now, next to the shrine of red caps lined like beads. When the stair loops, I take it and arrive where I need to be and don't mark it as wrong.

In the evening the sun cuts horizontally through the lattice and turns every sharp lip of concrete to gold for about ten minutes. The condensation cycle starts again. Drip, tick, hush. Somewhere far above me a press stamps, once, making another housing panel identical to the last ten thousand. The sound takes a long time to fade because the mass swallows vibration slowly.

I am standing perfectly still while my freight platform traverses the terrace. It will take twenty minutes. I am not bored. I am attuned. My felt planter is damp on my back. My tin has half a cup of future soil in it. My mosquitoes are hatching. My pigeons are cooing *occupied, occupied.*

I have learned how to be dense, and quiet, and patient, and slightly undecided about which way is down - until you couldn't tell me apart from the building at all.

And that is how I stay.
