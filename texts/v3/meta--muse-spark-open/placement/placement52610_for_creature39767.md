I thought, at first, that I was the water.

Not a person in it — the thing itself. The broth. The hold.

Because that is the first lesson this bowl teaches: if you are still long enough, you stop being a shape moving *through* and start being a thickness *among*. The silt doesn't settle. The tea-leaves hold north-south for twenty minutes at midday for no reason and then let go. The beard-algae will take your offered wrist and make a speculative root. I had been holding still for a long time.

Then a piston took its half-hour stroke at the bottom of the bowl and I felt it in my sternum, and I remembered I had a sternum.

No. Smaller than the water. Smaller than the town. But not separate from it.

If you come down from the surface-film — that mercury ceiling of trapped air and lily pads wide as dinner tables — and let yourself sink past the light without kicking, you will pass me about halfway down. Elevation maybe 0.5/3 if you need numbers, but we don't use numbers. We use *where the light goes amber.*

I live where the third tier's fire escape gave up being a fire escape and became a trellis.

There was a catwalk here once, riveted steel, meant to bridge two brick tenements that are now one continuous building because someone cut a door through both and then someone else cut a door through that door. The catwalk rusted, sagged, and was caught by vine-rope and power cable and air-hose all braiding together because there was nowhere else for them to go. Nothing was removed. Density doesn't allow removal. It only allows adding, knitting, growing over.

So the catwalk is still there, under the beard-algae. Ten-foot curtains of it, green-black, swaying with the slowness of hair in honey. And over it, and through it, my tomatoes.

You could find me by the glow.

They hang mid-water, not floating, not sunk — tethered on nets old nets have grown into. The nets were nylon once. Now they are nylon and vine. You can't tell where one ends. Each tomato has learned to hold its water against the water, skin thickened, faintly warm from its own fermentation. In the dusk-at-noon light — that green-gold bruise that never gets direct — they are the only true orange left. Low lanterns. When the thermal turnover comes, that patient silk-shift every few hours when top trades with bottom, they all wobble at once and I put my hand out, not to steady them, but to feel them decide to be still again.

That is my work.

I am the pruner. The mid-water pruner.

Not merfolk. Not adapted. Just bowl-living long enough that leaving would make a hole, and we don't make holes.

Pale the way we all are pale — translucent at the temples, blue-veined, iron-freckled. My hair never dries. I stopped fighting it after my second child. Now half my braid is my hair and half is tended beard-algae, combed, beaded with glass from a broken filament cage. It floats around me like slow ribbon when I forget to tuck it. On my shins, my wrists, my throat: the living cuffs. Engine-block moss, black-green felt that only grows on metal that remembers being hot. We coax it onto skin where the hose chafes. It makes a gasket. Underneath my skin is white and a little photosynthetic in the amber light. The border where person ends and garden begins blurred two turnovers ago and I did not correct it.

The hose —

Of course, the hose.

Thin rubber, filmed with algae, trailing sideways and down to the big drum at the bottom. The house-sized boiler that once made something no one remembers. Now it makes the Balance. It ticks. Tick... tick... tick... slow enough that you have to watch a rust-mark on the flywheel for a full minute to be sure it moved. I sip from it every two or three minutes. A long slow pull, tea-sip, not a breath. Between sips I am empty-lunged and still. To thrash is to be a tourist. To hover, one fingertip lightly hooked on a pipe-run that is also a handrail that is also a vine, is to be local.

I am good at being still.

I prune one cut every thirty seconds. Iron scissors, copper wire at the joint gone green. Snip. Watch the fruit wobble. Watch the iron snow fall forever at millimeters per minute — spores, leaf detritus, skin flakes. Snip. A blind white crayfish moves backwards across my boot — lead-shot hem, ankle ring of rebar — picks at the moss on my cuff, decides I am not food, and continues. Somewhere three buildings away in the silt-haze, which is as far as anyone sees, a pale carp hangs motionless in a stairwell, staring with eyes too large for this warm, too-still water. We keep the water alive for it with the slow wheels. Or it keeps us.

Time here is not clock time. I'll meet you two piston-thuds past the leaf-alignment. She hasn't moved from that stairwell since last turnover. My daughter — seven last turnover — is in the air pocket just above me now, the one under the inverted skiff we never removed. That's where we keep a dry rug, a sofa, air that has been there for weeks, shivering in place. She is loud there. Here, we are 0.5/3 quiet. When I need her I don't shout. I knock. Two taps on pipe means *coming through.* Three sharp pings on the boiler hull means *child loose, watch your wake.* If I need to tell her something close, I will hum it out with an exhale against her hooded ear, intimate as confession.

She will get her first collar soon. In the big dome at the lip, the old cistern where the surface-film is thickest. The first months in air, then hose, then water, then moss. No one taught me to swim. There is no swimming. You wade-float-push through broth that touches you back. You learn to not disturb.

You would find me there because I am the one who has learned to not disturb most completely. An old man mending net on a balcony railing lets his vine grow through the net to graft. I let my tomatoes grow through the fuse box the rust-lily already split open, copper filaments blooming inside the blossom. The town repairs me as much as I repair it. Brick rounded like river stone but not falling. Steel exhaling rust only to be sealed by bacterial varnish. Barnacle-coral knitting into rebar in cauliflower nodules. Established. In-balance. A thousand years like this without getting more ruined or less alive, if we keep sipping slow.

If you drifted close — and you will drift, not swim — you would see I am not looking at you at first. I am looking at the filament bulb in its wire cage just over my net. It has been on for fifty years. It will be on for fifty more, fed by the trickle through the old aqueduct gate no wider than your body, bringing fresh silt from somewhere uphill. Warm yellow square spilling into warm water, showing my scissors, my hands, the moss on my hands.

Then I would check your hose. Tug your weight. With one fingertip on the pipe to steer, not a kick, because a kick makes a wake that will be visible for hours and tangle my curtains and silt my kitchen.

And I would put my mouth close to your temple and hum you the only two things that matter here until you learn to be bowl-living too:

*Slow.*

*Don't make a hole.*
