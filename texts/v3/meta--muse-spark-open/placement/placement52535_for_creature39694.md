I didn't find myself right away.

At first, standing at the lip where the cloud snags - breath rinsed thin, cheeks gone ruddy with the cold - I thought I would be one of the newcomers. The obvious one. The visitor you described, lingering too long in the drizzle at the knee-high walkway level, hands gone bloodless around nothing, until someone leans out of a coppice-door to hand me that hot, marten-fat oiled cloth. The one being folded in. That would be the kind thing to be.

And then for a minute I thought, no - I'd want to be loud. I'd want to be one of the children. Barefoot in the cold because my soles had learned to read wet cedar better than boots could, running the highest braid of rope-and-wicker where the adults won't go unless they have to. A fern gametophyte in my braid that I forgot was there. Knowing with my tongue which blue milkcap to leave for the salamanders. Screaming with laughter while chasing frogs under a house that is also a shrub. That felt true for a breath. The bowl makes you want to be high and fast and unafraid of falling.

But I kept listening. And in a place this loud you don't choose where you are; the sound chooses you.

You don't hear a village in that bowl. You hear a chord - water *on* water *on* leaf, ten thousand leaf-tips tipping into ten thousand lower leaf-tips, the glassy chorus of thumbnail frogs, the korri heckling in perfect pump-creak, the stone anvil ringing against native copper, someone yelling *honey, they’ve swarmed again* and twelve opinions answering at once. It's a knot pulled tight, and every thread is humming.

And low - always low - is the seep. The weep off the black basalt, mineral-teared, braiding down the dry-stone garden walls that aren't dry at all.

That’s where I settled. That’s where I kept finding my weight.

Not up high. Down in the bottom third, where the terraces get narrowest and the air is coldest, right at the rim of the cistern-mere - that black, always-rippling eye that never stops taking the drip. Under the great cedar that has grown to be a roof over the communal tools.

If you were to find me, you’d have to crouch.

I’m not the smith, though I sit beside the stone anvil. I’m not the weaver, though the pit-loom thuds above me and sheds nettle fluff that settles on everything like snow.

I am one of the cedar ribs.

I was bent while green, seventy, eighty years ago. Maybe more. Some patient hand - a woman with bark-cord scars like white cartography on her palms - steamed me, drew me down with a withe between her teeth, lashed me to my brother cedar until we kissed and then fused. We were supposed to be an arch. A doorway. A house-post that would still put out leaves every spring right through someone’s kitchen.

And I did that. For a while.

Now I am older than that job.

My heartwood is still cedar, still growing - you can feel it if you put your palm on me in August, a slow swell, a door that rubbed clean in summer sticking by October because I’ve taken another centimeter for myself. My outer bark furrows have become shelving. My inner hollow - bored by beetles and then widened by hands and then kept politely open by smoke - is a chimney. A family’s hearth vents through me, all winter. I taste their cold smoke, their myrtle leaves that fall too close to the soup, their rendered marten fat. I exhale it up through my crown where the moss has taken my shingles completely and ferns root in my hair.

I am a landlord, the way you said. Not lumber. Landlord.

On my west side, where the seep has found me, the filmy ferns grow so thick children use me as a drinking fountain, parting the fronds to find the cold thread underneath. Tree-frogs live in my armpits - every cupped axil holding a lime-bright throat that starts up at dusk until I am thrumming. A white-collared korri hen has nested in the smoke-warm of my chimney-hollow for six seasons; she has learned to imitate the cough of the old man who sleeps in the platform slung between my ribs, and she does it just to make his wife laugh. Below me, in the runnel my roots help hold, the very old salamander lives - slate-backed, gills still feathered because why would you ever leave the water here - and when apprentices beat copper too fast she ripples her tail in annoyance and they have learned to slow to her rhythm.

People leave me things in the niche they cut without cutting me: a thimble of honey, a polished jewel-beetle wing. Not because I am a spirit. Because it is good manners to pay rent to a tree that holds your house up and breathes your fire.

This is why I am here and not up on the high bridges. The bowl doesn't need more fast feet. It needs memory that isn't in a head.

The Rememberers - those old women who nag *you took that stone, Leri, where's your moss?* - they come and press their foreheads to me when they want to know how wet this winter will be. They don't ask me. They feel how tight my bark is, how much water I'm holding. I remember the year the mere froze over despite the dripping because my sap stopped for three nights. I remember the spring wind that blew out a myrtle wall woven in the wrong braid.

I don't move. I don't need to. The whole dense, dripping, bazaar-busy knot moves *through* me. A sixty-year-old woman hauls wet tubers up three terraces on calves like river stones and steadies herself on my knee-root. A toddler wails on the platform in my chest and the neighbor sings louder to cover it and the vibration goes down my grain. Someone is always mending, someone is always harvesting, chickens roost in the bridge that ties into my shoulder, mushrooms appear overnight in boots left too close to my feet and are harvested with the little knife kept by my hearth.

You said the rule is *you do not dam, you do not clear. You redirect, you train, you borrow.*

I am that rule made wood.

I was redirected. I was trained. I was borrowed and never returned. And because of it the bowl holds.

If you come down to the knee-high walkway level, breath ghosting in the cold noon light that is just mountain light, nothing strange about it, and you look for me - you won't see a house at first. You'll see a particularly dense thicket with smoke coming out of it. Look closer. The smoke is coming out of a tree. Look closer still. The tree has a door in it that leaks warm light and the sound of an old man telling the same story again, and frogs.

Put your cold hands on me. I’m damp ten hours a day and I don’t mind cold hands. That’s a problem for the whole knot, not just for you.
