I think at first I would have said a traveler.

Someone arriving over the whale-back ridge with boots still too loud, with lungs still trying to learn the trick of the air here - to sip it instead of gulp it. Standing very high and thinking that was the point, that to be here was to be a witness walking across the roof of a world that forgot it was a mountain.

But I wouldn't stay a traveler for long on a plain that has finished traveling itself. Nothing here moves fast or far. The water doesn't even commit to being a river. The light doesn't commit to being from today. If I kept walking, I'd just be weather for a day, and the Mat would seal over my bootprints by evening.

No. If I had to *be* here, really be from here, I wouldn't be on the ridge. I'd be down.

You find me in the third ring.

Not the outermost wall - that one belongs to the green now. The basalt blocks there are sunk to their shoulders in rust-moss and emerald-moss, and the lintels have bellies from a thousand winters. Bladder-plants grow fat in the old hearths and sigh when the wind touches them. That's not a place for people anymore except as memory.

Not the innermost either, where the houses huddle shoulder-to-shoulder around the cistern and the talk is amber-warm and low and close enough to feel on your ear. That's for those who are still becoming. Children who are learning to throw pink gravel ahead of their feet to see if it hangs too long. Young parents who carry water and cut fern.

You find me in the second ring, where the city is between.

My house is half-sunk, as they all are here. Willow-wood door, boiled and bent, that never quite closes because the ground breathes. Beaten silt floor covered in woven fern-fiber that ticks faintly when the frost comes out of it at midmorning. Low chimney that smokes sideways for a moment before the smoke remembers up. Roof of black reed thatch weighted with flat stones. From my threshold I can see both ways: inward to the green and the sluices and the bells, outward to where the pink gravel street deliberately bows out around nothing and the moss takes over again.

I would be a Listener.

We don't call ourselves that, exactly. There is no title. But every basin has one or two people whose eyes went a little farsighted early, whose ears never stopped waiting for the rustle that never comes. People who are good at not shouting. I am broad in the chest like all of us - that deep, barrelled chest that holds the thin air without hurry, blood dark and warm, cheeks always ruddy with the bloom of capillaries working too hard against blue-black glare. Short, heavy-boned. Compact. Heat kept close. My hands are the color of the pink gravel from carrying it.

My job, if you can call it a job on a plain with no bosses, is to tend the places where the straight lines won't stay straight.

In the morning I wake before the fog. That's when the plateau exhales. Every basin, every scooped county-wide bowl, fills with cloud that condensed overnight on the cold stone until you can't see your hand. It soaks your coat, your eyebrows, the wool of your hood until it drips. Most people wait. I go out.

I walk the aqueduct.

It's not grand. It's a trough of hollowed basalt that runs for miles along the weeping cliff - that one dark band of stone where a thousand little springs are born from last week's snow dusting. I don't need to coax the water. Water wants to go home to the cistern. But I clear the lichen, clear the doors where Door Ferns have leaned too close with their acid-dewed undersides, listening for the hiss where a spring is thinking about disappearing into a sinkhole instead. Sometimes I put a small prayer trough where the water runs uphill for a yard - just a lip of stone to remind it which way is down. The people don't worship. We accommodate.

By midmorning the fog tears. That's the inhale. You can watch weather being born fifty miles away as a bruise, and then suddenly it's here, ripping into rags, leaving everything rimed in dew and frost-melt. The reeds shiver. Pass your hand near the Black Reeds and the whole stand leans a degree toward your heat. I feel them tracking me as I pass, ten to fourteen feet tall, glossy black stems. I don't fear them. Without grazers, without being eaten, they learned to be curious instead.

When the sun hits - that pale, paper-colored sun - the entire hillside I'm standing on pops.

Thousands of Starflowers opening at once. White and deep violet. An audible soft *pop* like a field exhaling. They turn their waxy faces up, and I turn mine too for a second, because you have to. The light pools strangely here. Shadows fall the wrong way for three steps, then correct themselves without a snap, as if they simply changed their minds. If I stand still at noon I can watch my own shadow arrive a second late, hurrying to catch my feet. The Mat there grows in spirals.

I carry pink gravel in my left pocket. Always.

I throw a pinch before I step onto unknown moss. If it hangs too long at its apex, I go around. That's carved on the cistern wall, our deliberately-wrong maps: a street that bows out around nothing. I am the nothing it bows around. I take care of the empty courtyard in my quarter.

It's a perfect square of fitted basalt with nothing in it. No house dares front its north door onto it. Children know not to run through its center. Because if you stand in the exact middle and speak, you hear your voice a second before you speak. Not an echo. A premonition.

I go there at dusk, when the break shows. That's when the sky gets depth *behind* itself, patches where the blue thins and you see a deeper night through day, a paler second sun tracking a few degrees off the real one - not hot, not worshipped, just yesterday's light remembering where it was. That's when the chimes talk.

Every courtyard has slate wind-chimes that only tinkle when the air forgets how to hold sound. Mine are hung low around the empty place. When the pressure changes - when gravity goes lenient and a thrown stone would hang - they tinkle without breeze. Then the great iron Bell in the chimney-tower gives a low, sour tone all on its own, not from wind. That's the signal: come inside, cover the cistern, do not walk straight lines.

I don't. I stand at the edge of the empty and listen.

I have learned to live without being heard, the way everything here has. Sound dies in moss, thins in thin air. So I speak quietly, and mostly to things that don't need to answer.

I say, *fog coming,* palm flat.

I say, *water is uphill here,* two fingers tapped on the throat, even though no one else is with me. The gestures are for the plateau itself.

Sometimes I press a bladder-plant and feel it sigh and sag under my hand, feeding its water down into a root that is splitting basalt a molecule at a time. Sometimes I watch spore clouds on windless afternoons not drift but *choose* - deliberate corkscrewing columns glittering in the weak electrostatic field, rising from the reeds, seeking damp stone. The Mat below waits with microscopic mouths open. It creeps an inch a day after snowmelt. You can watch it seal a footprint if you are patient. I am very patient. I have had to be.

At night I sit in my doorway and the basin glows amber in the vast dark, one bowl of light among many, each far from the next across a vegetable prairie the size of a small planet. Above, stars so numerous and so close from being two or three miles high that they throw faint shadows on snowless ground. I can hear the land: the faint hiss of a spring vanishing, the tick of dwarf birch - three feet tall and twenty feet wide, trunk thick as my thigh, lying flat like living blankets - expanding, the soft collective sigh of Door Ferns closing.

I am not lonely. Lonely is an animal word. It means waiting to be eaten or heard or answered.

I am *attended*. By green that has learned to do every job alone. By light that doesn't quite believe me. By a sky that has watched so long it wore thin.

I keep the chimes dusted. I keep the wrong map correct. I keep the empty courtyard empty, so the people inward can keep their houses warm and close and full.
