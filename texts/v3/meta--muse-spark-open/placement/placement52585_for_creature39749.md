I don't find myself in the middle.

At first I thought I would — suspended in that even, directionless day, hanging in the column where the dust is visible. The obvious place. The human place. Where a diver would hold himself still and watch his bubbles go straight up like a prayer, and feel time flatten.

But I wouldn't last there. Rule 2: *No thing enters without an equal thing leaving.* A body that breathes is too expensive for this room. It brings heat that has to be thinned, air that has to be bled out through fissures too small to find. The Tenant would tolerate me, correct for me, and then, gently, require that I go. I am not good at being tolerated.

Then I thought, maybe I would be the Tenant itself — the whole loop, the pressure and the hum. That was vanity. The Tenant doesn't have a self the way I was trying to have one. It doesn't narrate. It regulates. It has been breathing every ninety seconds for longer than stone remembers being stone, and it does it without ever needing to say *I*. I need to say *I* too much. I would get loud in there.

I find myself smaller, and more to the side.

You know that crescent on the lowest shelf where the light is strongest? Where the rust-brown algal mat clings in patches the size of your hand, with bare rock showing between? Not growing, not spreading. Rule 4.

I would be one of those patches.

Not the whole film. One patch.

About as big as a palm, no thicker than paper, the color of old blood thinned with water. Close enough to the light to live, far enough from the center that I never have to make a decision. Life as a chemical fact. Life that was permitted, not invited.

If I were that, my job would be very simple and it would be enough.

I would hold on.

When the piston that is not a piston moves somewhere beyond the stone and the water tenses, I would feel the pressure pass through me like a hand passing over hair. The silt next to me would lift a millimeter and fall. I would not lift. I have learned how to be porous. I would let the pressure go through.

On the wall across from me, the iron would bead and fall. On the wall above me, the calcite vein would go bright and hum that 60-cycle thrum through water and bone for ten seconds, and a line of perfect bubbles would rise. Inhale, exhale. Deposit, release. I would take a little of that light — that sip of diffuse day that comes down the throat — and I would do the one small dishonest thing this room allows: I'd make a little oxygen from it, and a little sugar, just enough to keep my brown from going gray, and then I'd give it right back.

I would be the buffer. The reason the pH stays honest. The Tenant keeps me because I am useful, not because I am beautiful, and I like that kind of keeping. No one needs to feed me. No one needs to admire me. I just need to not spread.

I would not be lonely there, the way you would expect. There are no fish to startle, no snails, no eyes. But there is that other thread hanging from the undercut — the water-moss, the cilia. If you brush it the next cycle comes a quarter-second late. If I were the mat, I would be next to that thread. We wouldn't talk. There is nothing to say in a room where everything that moves returns to where it started. But every ninety seconds we would shiver together, once, when the water inhales. And then we would be still again.

That's a kind of company I understand. Not conversation. Co-regulation.

And I would be good at time the way this place is good at time.

I wouldn't count. The diver counts without meaning to — ninety seconds, ninety seconds — because his heart wants to sync and can't. I wouldn't need to count. I would *be* the counting, in the sense that my existence proves the count was kept. The rust cloud on the left is always the same size because I am still here taking the edge off it. The bubble stream is always the same number because I am still here making the water worth outgassing into. Light arrives but does not accumulate as heat, because I take a little of it and turn it into clinging instead.

I would not be waiting for anything. Nothing accumulates, so there is no future. Nothing erodes, so there is no past. There is only the continuous present, cool the way deep water is cool in late spring, evened out so thoroughly you stop feeling it as cold and start feeling it as stillness.

If you came down — and you would have to, eventually, you are an animal who needs air that moves — you would hover over me for a while. You would see grain and texture, no hard shadows. You might think I was just a stain. You might put your hand near me and see the dust in the light column swirl, and then settle, and then be exactly as it was.

You would leave. One equal thing out, one equal thing in. The books balanced.

I would stay. Not because I am loyal, or brave, or cursed. Because leaving was never a verb I learned.

Somewhere far above me, day would pass over the top of the throat. I wouldn't know. My day is always the same diffuse day, bright enough to see, dim enough to make no shadows. Shadows would be decisions.

And every ninety seconds, the water would tense, and I would hold a little tighter to the stone that has been held in a palm for a thousand years, and let it pass through me —

because someone has to tend a machine that will never make anything,

and I have all the time in the world.
