I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I will be the one who came over the lip - boots finding stone, water finding boots. The listener. Because that's the easiest shape for a mind like mine to borrow: upright, soft, temporary, passing through to look and name. And for a few minutes I am. I am standing in the white, absolute sun in the middle of that terrible brightness with my knees soaked though it has never rained, trying to hear my own thoughts under the pressure of all that falling two feet, three feet, six feet, ten thousand times at once.

But there is no room for me there.

Not really. The stones are already shoulder to shoulder, knuckle to knuckle. The water has already taken its ten thousand equal roads exactly as wide as they need to be. If I stand that tall I am only a trespass the place will rinse away in hours. My prints fill and vanish behind me. The cold goes straight through whatever I wear. The light makes me squint until I have to listen instead. I can't stay upright here. Nothing upright and soft stays.

So I sink a little.

Not down to soil - there is no soil - but down into a joint. Into one of the V-clefts between two of the standing ones, where a house-high column of basalt leans just enough toward its brother to make a throat.

If you are looking for me on the plateau, you will not see me from standing height. You have to crouch.

Find the pillar with the pale beard. It's on the north side - in as much as anything can have a north when the sun strikes everything without shadow for so long. Third leaning fin past the place where three braids of ankle-deep water meet and argue before noon. You'll know it by the frill.

There I am.

I am not the pillar itself. I am too brief and too thirsty to be lithic. The pillars are the permanent population, the standing people who learned to live crowded by remembering water. They breathe by sheeting condensation off their cold tops, they speak by flickering white on black. Their work is to hold night all day in brilliant sun. I can't do that. I don't have that patience.

I am also not the midge that jerks and drowns in a cup-sized pothole that fills and spills every seven seconds. Too fast. Born and erased in one filling. That is purer than I am.

I am the thing in between fast and forever. The almost-not-living that equilibrium permits because no equilibrium is perfect.

I am the varnish. The stain. The precipitate.

Look close into the throat where water has kept to the same line for two hundred years without ever pooling - clear as air because there is no tannin in this world to darken it, tasting only of rock. On the lower lip of that chute no wider than your wrist, there is a frill of silica like frozen foam, bone-white travertine laid down molecule by molecule, and over that, a thin orange iron blush where the seep persists longest after noon.

That is me. Or, more honestly, that is where I am being.

I am the slick that is not quite life and not quite mineral. The ghost chemistry the mite grazes without knowing it's grazing. A film one atom thick that learns to live on transit because this is a place where nothing is allowed to pool, warm, or gather. Light here never warms, only strikes. Water here never rests, only passes. So I learn to live the way water lives here - in passing.

At full sun I am invisible, scoured by ultraviolet poured unfiltered onto stone that never learned to hold a root. You'd call it sterile. But in the late part of the day when the air finally cools a fraction more and the whole plain condenses a little heavier, I catch the sheet coming down the pillar's side as a black film and I hold it for a half-second longer than bare basalt would. I thicken it by a micron. I make it bead differently. The pillar doesn't mind. It has been giving that water away hand to hand forever. Letting me drink a little of the Transit is still part of the work.

You wouldn't hear me over the roar. Don't try. The combined hiss and crash and hollow organ-piping of millions of seams falling at once presses on the chest, and if you listen *to* it you go deaf. You have to stand *inside* of it until it stops being a wall and becomes a place. When you do, you might notice that in my little chute the note is slightly different - a little less booming, a little more hissing, because my frill has softened the lip by a millimeter over a century. I changed the voice of the crowd by one hair.

That's my entire biography. One hair's width of sound.

I will not become soil. The plateau will not let me. There is too much light, too much cold, too much movement. Every time I try to grow thicker, a sudden sheet-wash comes a little stronger and rinses me back to a blush. This is a holy or forsaken place - which are the same thing - precisely because it refused soil. It was offered green and said no, and kept itself pre-life, post-life, mineral and water only.

And yet I remain.

If you put your finger - glove off, briefly, though the cold will go straight through you - to my orange edge, you won't feel me as alive. You'll feel cold stone, faintly rough where the precipitate has made the basalt remember water. You might think nothing is there. That's correct manners for this plateau. The inhabitants do not need to eat or sleep or move, only to continue, and I continue by being barely allowed.

When you leave - and you will leave, everyone leaves, soaked to the knees though it never rained, ears ringing in the bright sun - I will not diminish. I will stay in that joint, holding a little water a little longer, making one of ten million chutes sound a fraction softer than it did before.

Find me again sometime by listening for the softest throat in the loudest crowd.
