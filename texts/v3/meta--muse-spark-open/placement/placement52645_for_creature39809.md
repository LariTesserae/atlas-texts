I don't know right away.

If I close my eyes at the foot and let the Stair pull me up, I try on a few skins to see what fits.

For a second I think I'd be a hawk. Copper-feathered, that bent-metal scream. It would be good to ride the thermal the Stair makes all afternoon - that warm exhale off pale block and enameled steel. To see the whole cone at once from above, terraces curving into a wall, furred green and rust-orange, ticking with water. But hawks have to keep moving. They don't get to stay in one wall and learn it.

Then I think I'd be a cat. Lean and brindled, pads tough as tire rubber. Walking the high conduits like they are sidewalks. Belonging to everyone and no one. That is closer. The way a cat knows Tier 42 by the smell of its furnace and Tier 43 by its carp. But cats are too aloof for maintenance. They don't wring out nets. They don't stay to grout a joint.

Then I think smaller. A swift, maybe, in a mud tube under an I-beam. Or a finch in the hollow arm of a semaphore, one of thousands, weather made of birds. Or an isopod in the drainage cut, churning leaf litter into soil. Or one of the fat orange carp in a plank-covered cistern, circling all day, watching light break through cracks and learning to gulp before rain.

Each one fits for a moment and then slips.

Because the Stair doesn't want visitors. It wants tissue. Nothing lives *on* it. You live *in* it until you are part of the wall. And I have always been best at staying, at listening, at holding.

So find me where the Stair forgot to be concrete anymore.

Not at the bright crown where the lichen glows orange and drinks fog. Not at the bottom where the basalt-concrete is still pretending to be mountain. Mid-high. Tier 58, where the retaining wall failed.

You know the place: five meters where there is no pour. Only roots. Fig roots thicker than my thigh, braided and re-braided, holding back the earth of Tier 59 with fingers. Dripping condensation from every hair-root into a fern garden that has no floor. The Keepers come once a season to tap it with hammers and leave it alone because live root holds better than dead pour.

I am just below that. Tier 57, shadow face.

I am a Keeper-Not-Yet. That's what the old ones call us when we're still young enough that our knees don't click the weather. A girl, maybe fourteen, compact and ropy already, calves like cable from a lifetime of never finding flat ground. My hat is too big - woven vine-cane, sun-bleached silver, sweat-brown around the band where my mother wore it before me. My squint is already there. Crow's feet starting at the corners even though I still run along pipes when I'm not supposed to. Lower lids thickened from narrowing my eyes at white noon when the southwestern quadrant turns into a blaze you can't look at.

You'd find me before dawn, when the whole mass is still cold.

That's when the Stair drinks itself. The greenhouse-warm day bleeds out, the high night comes down thin and sharp, and everything sweats. Girders, cables, the underside of leaves, the fig fingers above me. My job is water. Everyone's first job is water before farmer or smith or teacher.

I tend two copper nets strung between pillars at the edge of our strip - coarse woven screens, green with age, that fur with beads as the air condenses. I go out with the tin dipper and the squeegee that is older than I am, and I coax. You can't be rough with it; you have to wait for the bead to swell and then tip it. Wring the nets into buckets. Tip the gutter wire into the cistern. My cistern is the size of a truck bed, concrete, plank-covered, part of the back wall of our two rooms. The carp in it know my step. They come up - orange, speckled, fat and slow - and break the surface when they hear my bucket clatter. If they are gulping, I tell my mother rain is coming, even if the sky is still high and bright.

Then the chain of clacks starts above me. *Clack* on 60, *clack* on 59, *clack* on 58 as the sluice gate over the fig roots opens and yesterday's stored water slides down one level. I open ours on 57 and send it to 56. If I do it wrong, if I let a gate stick, every head comes up. Children stop running along the freight chain. The old Keeper three doors down - his kitchen floor warm from the forging hall below, tomatoes growing absurdly fast up there - will look out without looking at me, listening for whether the chord is right again. Wind through girders high A, stamping presses mid-C - thump... thump... thump... one per second through my molars - insects hissing, birds bouncing sixfold off parallel walls. We all know that song. We are part of its instrumentation.

In the afternoon I learn to listen properly. The Keeper who is actually a Keeper, not a Not-Yet, lets me carry his hammer. Small-headed, long-handled. We walk. We tap.

*Tok* on good concrete. *Tok* on good root - duller, alive. *Tik* on hollow where black mold has made a velvet pocket and water is getting in. He shows me how to tell which tier needs grout by which knee aches, but really by the sound. He has me press my palm to the fig wall on 58. It is cool and faintly moving. Not wind. Growth. Tightening. He says, "Don't pray to it. Maintain it." But he still ties a ribbon to the water tower and leaves a bolt as an offering, so I do too.

I am becoming good at small verticals. Behind our sleeping alcove, in the shadow of the overhang, there is a patch of enameled steel where the paint has been eaten by moths and the rust has flaked into scales. Dust lodged there. Seeds lodged in dust. I did not plant it. I just stopped scraping it. Now moss and stonecrop and tiny white daisies grow straight out of the wall, watered only by what sweats out of the air. My mother says it will pry the plate off eventually and I say good, let it.

This is where I'll stay. Not because I can't climb - I can, and at 2.8 I have the chest for it, I can outrun any lowlander who comes up hauling salt and laughing too loud with the thin air - but because somewhere in these fifty meters of root and rust and fur, the Stair is slowly deciding to be a mountain again, and it needs hands that know how to hold without fixing.

If you come looking, come just before dawn or just at white noon. At dawn you'll hear my dipper. At noon you'll have to squint to see me at all - just another sun-dark figure in a wide hat, squinting back, holding a bucket that catches the blaze and throws it back.

Listen for the tap. *Tok. Tok. Tik.* Then the water clack. Then me breathing between short sentences, unhurried.

I'm in the wall.
