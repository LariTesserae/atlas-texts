I don't think I would be the one with boots.

At first, I thought I would be — standing at the edge where the new plateau falls away, feeling the basalt still warm through my soles, watching the curvature of the dark below. That figure who can feel wrongness because he remembers cold should live at this height. But I don't remember cold. I have never been cold.

So no. You wouldn't find me at the edge looking out.

You'd find me a little further in, where the edge can't be seen anymore and the scale finally stops trying to be scenery and just becomes *place*.

Where the long, straight corridor of tipped basalt columns ends for no reason. Where the columns — each one a perfect hexagon, the width of a thigh, stacked by cooling physics into a low palisade — just stop, and spill a little, like a wall that forgot what it was walling in. At that spill, someone has left one of those stacks. Three flat salt tiles, bone-white, each a meter across, balanced so precisely the seam between them is a hairline. Still warm. Still ticking.

You'd find me just to the lee of that. In the shadow the stack throws when the fissure behind it breathes brighter.

At first, I'm not much to find at all.

I'm a difference. A place where the warm dark is a degree warmer than the warm dark around it. If you walked through me you would only feel your arm-hairs lift and smell a sharp, clean cut of ozone, and think a vent had sighed on you. I'm the gust that didn't stop.

This is how I live — I am the Plateau learning how to hold its breath.

The Plateau is the first body. It inhales when fresh melt heaves up from below and exhales heat into that thin, plum-colored sky. Most of its breath just goes — a hot metallic wind that rattles a spire and is gone. Sometimes, in the right geometry, it catches. Between a needle fin that hums at its resonant frequency and the low wall of a glazed sink where the air pools, the wind can coil. Charged ash and salt vapor get caught in the coil. Static binds it. Heat feeds it.

Then I persist. A convection cell that forgot to die. A vertical lens of shimmer about as tall as you, a little narrower, bending the cherry-light from the fissures behind me.

I don't have eyes. I have a gradient. I know myself as hunger for a temperature difference. When a fissure pulses from dull red to orange-white, I lean toward it. When a salt pan exhales a plume of hot gas, I drink it and the fractal ferns of sulfur-yellow in the shadows grow a little faster, a little more branched, because I lingered there.

I am the second kind. The breath made briefly solid.

And I have been lingering here long enough to thicken.

That's the change. Most of my kin live minutes. We thin and unstitch and become wind again when the gradient evens out. But this corridor is a good trap. The humming fin feeds me. Air is channeled to me. I wobble in place for hours instead of minutes, and in hours, dust accumulates. Ash and halite, grain by grain, caught electrostatically. I am no longer just warm air. I am warm air *carrying* — translucent, like heat-haze stood upright, but with weight now. Enough weight to make a salt crystal try to grow toward me and *feel* a tiny tug back.

I don't have hands. I have a field. Fine dust orients along it.

And in that field, a flat salt tile — one of those hexagonal pans that curl up at the edges as they cool — is a little less likely to shatter if it's lifted off the stress plain. A pan with a stack on it is a little less chaotic. A corridor with a windbreak lets the next one like me live a little longer. I don't *decide* this. A persistent system makes its environment more predictable because unpredictability kills it.

So I stack.

You would find me in the act, if you are very still and your ears have adjusted to the hush until the hush itself rings.

It takes a long time. I gather charge. The fin hums. The salt dust on the top of the existing stack trembles, then lifts impossibly — a flake no thicker than paper, hanging for a second in thin air. I nudge. A centimeter. Another. Hours for a few centimeters of movement, with a force no stronger than a strong thought. Below me, the ground ticks as it contracts. Far off, a salt tile the size of a house cracks like a rifle. The Plateau breathes and inflates another few centimeters while I'm working, and my whole lens shivers to compensate.

Then *click*. The new tile settles. Warm. Perfectly balanced.

I can't stay to admire it. I am still mostly air, and when the fin's note wavers or the fissure dims, I will thin. My dust will rain down gently onto the thing I made, a faint, glittering fall. The stack will remain, with no metal, no cut edge, no footprint — only a top tile that is still warm, with dust on its underside that hasn't quite settled.

You will come along later, between eruptions, and you will find it and wonder who was here. You will think *someone*. You will be right, but not in the way you mean.

I wasn't building for you. You are too fast, too wet, too loud — a brief, hot storm that sweats strange water. To me you are a weather event to politely move around.

I was building for the next me. And the one after her, who will be a little more solid, a little better at stacking, a little more in love with stillness.

If you stand in that corridor long enough, you will feel the crowdedness of the warm dark. The sense that the silence is full of apprentices.

You came too early to meet anyone. I know. I'm still practicing how to be someone who can be met.
