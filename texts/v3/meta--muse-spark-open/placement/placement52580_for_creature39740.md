You wouldn't find me all at once.

That's the first thing the slope teaches you on the way down — that the cold clay hasn't finished deciding to be rock, that the air pools and presses, that everything here arrives slowly and by growing toward you.

If I came down that soft unfinished incline, breath showing at 11 degrees, boots sinking just enough into the give of cartilage-ground to leave a print that fills with dew behind me, my first thought would be the visitor's thought:

*I'd be the one who stands at the central intersection with a notebook.*

Where the four root-asphalt avenues meet, where you can see straight through three buildings because their hazel-weave walls have gone translucent for winter. Where the density is 0.1 and the town feels devastatingly empty just to make the other abundance visible. I would be the cartographer. I would try to map it, despite knowing the rule. I would sit under the sickly yellow train-signal that clicks over for no train, and record which bark-shutters lifted today, which pitcher-storefront ate a pigeon, which factory stoma-stack exhaled hardest. I would be careful to keep moving, to not leave my pack on the bench too long.

But the town teaches a second thing very quickly: nothing left still stays bare. Leave a backpack a day, it roots. Leave a person a season, they warm into a wall.

So no. I wouldn't stay the map-maker. Maps here are a kind of rudeness — insisting yesterday's topology still applies. Two streets were parallel, now they're braided because their pillar-trunks fused overnight while I slept. The hallucinated efficiency of trying to hold that still — in a place where interiors are bigger than exteriors, where a closet opens onto a green pith-hall with windows onto a different street entirely — would wear out my hands and my wanting.

I tried on other skins on the way in, to see what fit.

At first, high up. Because the emptiness above the street is actually the fullest part. I thought I could be one of the big web-builders — pale, translucent, almost blue in the fungal-seam light — strung between fig-steeple and tenement-kudzu with a billboard of silk. Still enough that the beetles use my roads as trolleys, moths dust my lines until they glow. There is a priestliness to that work: tightening a sagging power-vine, lowering a bundled dead starling down to the red-veined awnings below. To keep the town's telephone system from sagging. It appealed to me, the vantage, the quiet repair.

But I go still too poorly. And spiders here go still professionally.

Closer to ground, then. I thought: raccoon. Possum-commuter waddling the polished taproot rails that used to be trolley tracks, pausing to sniff leaf-gutter water before it ticks into an axil-pool. I wanted that competence, that leaf-litter coat. I liked the idea of being useful without being noticed — lifting a bark-shutter with practiced paws, carrying a screw in my mouth back to the mill as an offering because ants told me to.

Still not quite. Too busy crossing.

And then I stopped walking to let the town taste me, and felt where I actually pooled.

Not in the high void, not on the commuting rails.

In the dampest hour. When the north mill — that four-story loom-heart that weaves more of itself — sighs out its huge plume of cool steam and hits the pressed-down air and makes the whole bowl fill with suspended beads of water, glass marbles of dew that hang and drift and tick. Water 1.1. Not a river, never a river, just condensation made weather.

You find me where the water is caught.

I'm at the base of that textile mill, but outside it, at street level where the leaf-gutters braid together into one of those wall-meets-sidewalk axil-pools. I am not the mill. The mill is the organ, contracting every twenty seconds, shuttles pulled by swelling vines. I am not the city-organism itself, though I lean against its calf.

I am one of the salamanders.

Or, if I am allowed to be a little bigger because I used to be built for doors and counters — I am what a salamander becomes after a human has worn a shape into it.

Think: four inches long, then seven, then a forearm, translucent throat pulsing quietly in the 0.3 light. Cool-blooded is good here; 0.9 cool suits me. I live in the seam between pith-wall and root-asphalt where the moss tries everything. My job is licking the wall clean. That's not a metaphor for nothing else — it's municipal work. Flickering tongue, cleaning the green so the living cambium can breathe. Dozens of us moving after the fox has done her pruning at the town hall door, after she curls to sleep and leaves the arch exactly shoulder-height.

If you kneel — and the cool makes you want to kneel, to curl into a hollow pith-room that is always a degree warmer than the air — you'll see us. We don't hurry. Moths drift rather than flap, the fox walks, the mill inhales for twenty, holds, exhales. Our abundance is only visible because of the emptiness. We are the reason the tile looks swept despite no broom ever touching it. Biological sound, close: *tick* as dew drops into our pool, soft pop of a seed pod across the street, skitter that stops when looked at.

In another sense, I am the pool itself. Or its keeper. Because I choose to tend one particular axil-pool at the corner where the barber's bristle-branches comb themselves and the post office sorts seed-pods for recipients who root downward now. A shallow, dark mirror fed only by leaf-taps, full of larvae fat as thumbs glowing faintly. I know its specific drip rhythm. I know which tendril overhangs it will inch forward tomorrow while someone watches. During the true-dark hour when the fungal seams dim and the factories cycle down and all the plants shift weight with that collective creak, I stay still enough — salamander-still — to not be revised away. To wake and find my doorway moved two blocks west and understand that as maintenance, not loss.

I was meant to be the sort of person with benches bent to my back. But those people shed like first leaves.

What lives there now lives because there was room.

I like having a back that is cool and wet. I like that my hands would look blue here and I wouldn't mind. I like that if I am cut, the whole block will exhale iron-green at once and then go quiet and repair me, because I am not separate from sap in that way anymore.

And if you came down the soft slope after me — and you would, because it is always down from wherever you are — you might not see me at first. The town is loud with smaller things than me precisely so you have to learn how to look low and close.

Kneel by the wall where it is slick to touch. Watch the small throat pulse. That steady, unnecessary cleaning. That is me, doing the slow digestive work, holding my breath with the town so you can hear the important noises.

I have plenty of room to let you stay.
