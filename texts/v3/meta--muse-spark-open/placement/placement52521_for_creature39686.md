I wouldn't find myself all at once.

At first, I think I would enter as you do — as the sudden thing. The warm thing. The animal thing that still has eyes, even though there is nothing here to see with them.

If I came in that way, you would find me kneeling.

Not swimming. Kneeling is the right posture for this ocean. Swimming is too fast, too warm, too much like trying to leave. You kneel and the cold finally has somewhere to take hold.

You would find me in a courtyard.

Not on one of the great avenues — those are too straight, too exposed, too much like a road that was meant to go somewhere and now, drowned, only proves that it never needed to go anywhere at all. And not out on the open plazas either, where the Fur is only a foot thick and has been smoothed by the slow turn of currents for ten thousand years.

No. You would find me three courses down.

The Floor drops there. One step, eighteen inches, then another, then another — a shallow, perfect stair that some district-builder cut to mark the boundary of something important, a temple precinct or a water garden or simply a neighborhood that wanted to be slightly lower than its neighbors. The silt has softened the stair but not erased it. You can still feel each arris with your knee, round as an elbow.

At the bottom is a cistern. It is rectangular, about as large as a house would have been when houses were still built on top of the Floor instead of being the Floor. Its walls are fitted limestone, each block the size of a chest. The mortar is gone, replaced by mineral crust and then by Fur.

That's where I am. At first, just visiting — a diver in a suit that ticks with cold, palm pressed through the Fur to the brick to feel that stubborn, human squareness underneath. My breath would be enormous in here. My displacement would make the Curtains shiver.

And I would understand, very quickly, that I cannot stay like that. The rules do not permit it. Warmth does not exist at depth. Movement must be slow or you will spend heat you cannot replace. Nothing sudden happens here and I am nothing but sudden.

So I would learn to be less sudden. Or I would leave. And I don't want to leave.

That's how I become more myself. That's how the ocean tells me what I am going to be.

I am not the diver.

I am the knot in the seam he was touching.

You would find me wedged where the second course of the cistern wall meets its floor — terracotta brick below, limestone above, a hairline that has been opening for eight thousand years by a fraction of a fraction. At a distance I look like nothing. A stain. A bit of tarred rope caught in the stones. Closer, you see I am a rosette, flat and leathery, the color of wet bark at midnight that resolves, if you put your eyeless face to me, into a deep olive black.

I am a Holdfast.

I don't have a front or a back. I have a center that holds and fingers that grip. Five of them, thick as your thumb at the root and tapering to nothing where they insinuate into the brick. Each one is swollen with the memory of light — that bruised dusk that comes from nowhere and everywhere, that wet-slate noon that is not bright enough to cast a shadow but, given a century, is bright enough to split stone.

I do not hurry. Hurry was a builder's idea. They pounded limestone with limestone. They thought forever was something you could build by making it flat and right-angled.

My forever is different. I exude. A faint acid, molecule by molecule, softening a single grain of mortar. I hold. I contract when the turbidity bloom passes overhead and the stones give back a tenth of a degree they borrowed. I breathe with the plaza — that slow ripple every hour when the whole Fur sighs and squeezes out water. I feel it through the brick.

Above me, the cistern is full. Not with water — everything is full of water — but with a single, bulbous mass of kelp that has taken the whole rectangle as its bed, its bladders rising just above the rim like a garden that was told to stay inside the lines and obeyed so well it forgot there was ever a gardener. Its crown leans a little to the north, because for three hundred years the contour current has brushed a little colder from the south. When one of its bladders pops — *pat* — I hear it through the stone more than through the water. The gas bead rises for minutes. I count its passage by the way the light changes less than nothing.

Around me, the Fur is two feet thick. It does not compete with me. It covers me, parts for me, closes over me. When a sheet of it sloughs off under its own weight somewhere far off, the sigh takes an hour to clear the silt and I taste the iron in it as it settles.

And always, from the lintel above where a doorway no longer leads anywhere, the Curtains hang. Fifteen feet long, translucent brown, never tangling. I cannot touch them, but I feel the displacement when something drifts too close — usually just Drift, those spores and cuttings that fall forever and never land, turning slowly in the vault-light like dust. Almost every one will never root. It doesn't matter. We make millions. We have time.

That is what I am. An eye-less, running-less thing that learned the seams.

I was not made for light, but for the memory of light. Not for warmth, but for the patience that comes after warmth is gone. Not for moving, but for staying so perfectly in one rectangular cistern, three courses down, on an endless built plain under cold heavy water, that staying becomes a kind of tending.

If you came back — still sudden, still breathing too loud — and knelt in that cistern and brushed the Fur aside and pressed your palm to the brick, you would feel me before you saw me. Cold to the touch, colder than the water, faintly oily, faintly giving. And if you held still long enough, long enough for your heat to leach and your mind to stop looking for a fish, you would feel a very faint curl at the tip of one finger. Not grabbing. Tasting. Learning the iron in you.

You would be the only sudden thing I have felt in a hundred years.

And when you left — and you would — the Curtains would close behind your passage. The Fur would smooth. The silt would settle, grey-white as snow that never melts. And I would keep widening my crack by a millimeter, in the soaking, even cold, in the dusk without direction, on the Floor that was leveled by primitive, patient hands that wanted to build forever and, without knowing it, did.
