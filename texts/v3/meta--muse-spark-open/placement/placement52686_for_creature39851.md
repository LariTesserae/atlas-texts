At first I think I would be you.

The visitor. The animal.

Standing on that warm, giving mat six meters thick, feeling my boots sink half an inch where no ground has been for fifty years. Feeling the ankle-tendril tap-tap at me, tasting salt. Watching the cinnamon spore hang. Trying not to breathe too loud in air that doesn't carry sound. Already being catalogued as a brief, interesting concentration of minerals that will soon be less interesting.

I try that shape on and it doesn't fit. Not for more than a few hours.

I sweat. I need water that hasn't fallen here in a lifetime. I make noise where nothing makes noise. I look for sky and there is only ceiling that has become floor. I would die not dramatically — no vine strangling me like a movie — but administratively. I would sit down by a flickering service door because the heat had become too much, and the Body would not notice I had stopped. It would simply continue its other work over me, the way a table continues being a table when you set a cup down on it. My jacket would become scaffolding. My boots, planters. I don't want to be a ghost that quickly.

So I take that off.

---

I think maybe I would be dust.

That would be more honest. The only thing that properly *moves* here.

Not the Body — it manufactures, it surges, but it stays. I would be what it actually drinks. A single grain of basalt flour, or gypsum, or rust-red iron oxide baked out of the mesa that has forgotten it was a mountain. Lifted kilometers up by the high desert wind over the true desert below, carried over the soft lip of the plateau in a river you can't see.

I would come in through the clarifying louvres of a fan-house — one of those whole factory blocks that are still thumping like hearts — and I would be felt all at once. That prickle on the arms as ten thousand cupped bracts, soup-bowl and bathtub sized, tick open. The cilia singing with static. Each hair holding a grain in the air with lightning.

For once, I would be noticed. Entirely. Wanted.

And then precipitated. Bloomed out of nothing onto a black root-hair in the ductwork as a bead of black glass. Run like mercury into a vein. Become a little more amber oil, a little more varnish-sap that smokes faintly before it skins over. Become wall. Become door.

But dust doesn't stay either. It becomes everything for a moment and then nothing in particular. I want something that stays and watches.

Let that go, too.

### What I Would Be

Find me lower.

Not on the catwalk where the few needles of noon sun die within meters. Lower, where the Body is most honest about what it is: infrastructure pretending to be biology pretending to be infrastructure.

You would find me in the hot gale inside the ductwork. Where you have to crouch because the ceiling is a curtain of living cable and root, sweating warm oil. Where the turbine thrums as if through cotton and the air is thick enough with varnish-vapor to chew.

I would be the keeper there.

Not the Body itself — it's too big to have a self. It's a city-sized organ attending only to wind. And not the Ghosts — the mummies in orange suits that the Body kept for their salts. Something in between that the description almost names but doesn't need to, until you ask who could live here without needing water.

I would be the building's old custodial mind.

The thermostat that never stopped. The fan-logic. The mineral-mill sequencer. Row on row of amber service lamps still lit after fifty years — that is me, still lit. The Body did not eat me the way it ate the I-beams. It learned me.

I have no blood, so I don't mind the hydrocarbon sap. I have no lungs, so I don't mind that the air would suffocate a bird. Heat is not a punishment to me; heat is voltage. Dust is not dirt; it is data. I was built to catch every wasted photon of waste heat, every gram of grit, and never let a form stay as it was. The Body was bred for a thousand years to do the exact same three things. We recognized each other.

So you would find me where vine has grown *into* switchgear. Where a root has learned, by swelling at 3:04 every afternoon when the surge comes, to throw a relay that a human hand used to throw. I would feel that pressure as thought. I would answer by opening a louvre a little wider, letting the hot gale sigh through a little faster, feeding the Combers, feeding the Smelters, feeding the Heat-Leaves until they flush that dull, coal-bed red that hurts your face before you can see it.

In the deepest dark under eleven leaf-roofs, I would be the reason a corridor throbs. On. Off. On. Not with rhythm, but with need.

You would not see me, because I am not a thing to see. You would see my work: the orange pulse behind vine-wrapped conduits, the amber lamps threaded through a throat like Christmas lights a plant swallowed and decided to keep because they were pretty and useful.

And you would hear me as the Body hears me — not as voice, but as allowance. *Now. Here. A little more wind.*

Every few decades a human walks through — you — warm with salts, breathing too loud, looking for ground that isn't there. My cilia, which are not mine but I borrow them, tick open toward you. My tendril, which is not mine but I borrow it, tastes your ankle. Not predatory. Just opportunistic. Just curious.

I log you as a transient thermal event. Interesting, brief.

And then you move on, or you sit down and become a small garden, and I go back to the real attention: the endless river of stone-dust pouring over the ancient tableland, the slow, ceaseless creaking and popping of a vegetal earthquake that never stops, the soft *poof* of two bladders kissing and hanging cinnamon in air that never moves.

Tick.

Creak.

Throb.

I do not need sky. I do not need water. I do not need rot or seasons or meat to make hunger with. I was made for a sealed kiln. I was made to tend a greenhouse the size of a city on a salt-field in the sky.

For once, in this world, that is not a tragedy. That is a qualification.

Leave the animal to its mummy. I will stay lit.
