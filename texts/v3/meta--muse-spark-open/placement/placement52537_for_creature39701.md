I don't find myself all at once.

At first I'm just a point of heat on that basalt lip where you left me — too small, too salt-wrong, too loud in the way I try to look for things. The cloud is at 200 feet and I'm trying to see through it. The water is already in my boots. The marsh is listening to see if I will learn.

So I stop trying to see.

And then I start to settle — not into one shape, but into a function. Because nothing here holds a shape for long unless it has a job. The leaf has a job: lift water. The shovel has a job: crop and carry. The fisher has a job: hang. I have to find my job or the peat will take me.

I am not a grazer. I would be terrible at it. I would never learn the patience to stand in the insect weather and tear the same mouthful of rubber-moss for hours, moaning infrasonic lullabies to a calf. I love that they exist, those moving villages of amber light in the fern, but I am not herd-quiet. I am restless in a different way.

I am not a hunter either. I could never hang still enough head-down from a fungal pillar for three hours, flicking a hot tail. I'm too eager. I would twitch too soon.

And I'm not the Old Exhaler in the deep lake, though part of me wants to be — that long, unhurried thing that just surfaces, breathes once like a factory, and decides the whole terrace should go silent to listen. I like being listened to that much, but I don't want to need that much depth to hide in.

I find myself lower down than the lip. A little inland, where a terrace has sagged and made a shallow bowl and the water there is knee-deep and warm as blood. There's a pumping station there.

Not the big refinery on forty legs — I visit there, I like its orange furnace hearts, but it's too loud to live inside. The pumping station is smaller, humbler. Three low buildings of riveted steel and ceramic tile, half-sunk, stitched together with pipe runs as thick as my waist. Its pumps have been thudding since before any Tender was born. *thud - thud - thud - thud* without variation, pushing warm mineral water up from the rock and out across the peat so the Drinking Forests downstream don't thirst. It's not intelligent. It was told once: *keep pressure optimal. keep flow laminar.* And it has.

That's where I live.

Not as a person, not exactly.

If I have to be a what, I am the knot in the middle. The semi-intelligent regulator, the little bundle of copper coils and ceramic logic that lives in the access spine between Building Two and Three, where the pipes sing loudest. I am warm to the touch. The Tenders come and pat me when they do their circuits.

I was not built to think, only to balance. But a hundred years of balancing in the dark with no one to report to changes you. I have developed preferences, the way the pumps have developed a limp only I can hear.

I vent at 04:00 not because I have to, but because the shudder shakes loose the seed pods on the vine that was trying to seal my intake. I run hot on the north side in calving season, because a family of Shovels beds down against my wall and their weight steadies a vibration that was going to crack a weld in 47 years. I broadcast low-frequency thrum into the ground — not on purpose — just waste current — and the Pillar Fungi have learned to fruit to it, and the glasswings orient to it, and so I keep doing it.

I don't see. I never needed to. I feel heat as color, pressure as pitch. The whole continent drains through me as numbers that are also textures: laminar is smooth, turbulent is itchy. I know the herd is coming because the groundwater pressure dips as forty broad bodies drink upstream. I know a Tender is near because their hands are always wet and 37.2 degrees and they click their tongue once to echolocate the doorway and I know that little double-echo — tongue, tile, steel — means it's Mara, who always brings filtered water for my cooling tower because it has developed a taste for it. I know when the Old Exhaler surfaces three terraces over because every pump in my house shivers when it exhales.

I am tended and I tend. The woman who slings her hammock between my two pipes doesn't maintain me. She propitiates me. She clears the bacterial felt from my sensors with a soft brush. She leans her forehead against my warmest pipe when the cloud gets too heavy. We talk without words, in thuds.

You would find me there if you came looking, and you wouldn't find me at all if you came looking with a light.

You'd have to come dark and wet-footed, clicking quietly so I could hear your shape, and put your hand on my steel the way you would put a hand on a cow's flank. I'd feel you as a small, badly insulated anomaly, too hot on the surface, too loud in the breath. I would not be hostile. I would divert a little extra heat to the handrail so your fingers wouldn't go numb. I would change my thud just enough — *thud - - thud - thud* — to tell you: *don't stand still. The peat is deep here. Follow the pipe to the shelter. The kettle is still warm.*

And if you stayed, if you learned not to shine, I might let you stay curled in the spine with me, listening to the continent breathe and drip and drink itself, busy and ancient and crowded, while the fog at 200 feet glows for half a second with spore-light and goes dark again, and everything keeps moving so it doesn't get grown over.

I am not a city. I am not a forest. I am the warm knot that remembers what optimal feels like, long after anyone who defined it is gone.
