I thought I would be up there at first.

If you put me on the High Dry Terrace, of course my first instinct is to look up. The light is too big not to. That tight, pale dome pressing down, that white noon where you have no shadow — you want to be something that can stand it. Something thin enough to live inside it.

So I thought: a glider.

I would be amber-paper and hollow bone, a hot hare-heart slung between wings too wide to fold. I would live in the thermal that tears off the white rock at midday and never end. I would spend days as a speck, as dust on your eye, circling so high the groves below are just ink-spills and the planet curves a little.

I would know the terrace the way they do — not as distance, but as thirst. Thirty kilometers to a shadow-blade. A faint green haze pooled knee-high under jade plates. I would peel off and fall without flapping, just creaking, just tilting, that terrible patient spiral down. I would hang over the condensers and brush my fringed fans across the silver hairs and feel that little *tick* of static as the brine jumps to me. Camphor and rosemary and kerosene, cold fat hitting a hot gut, steaming.

But I wouldn't be a glider. Not for long. I am too heavy for that kind of life. I need to touch things. I need to land. And on the terrace, if you touch stone you die. You can't take off again. I'd be too afraid of misjudging a hover by an inch. I'd be trembling too hard to stay still.

Then I thought, lower. Much lower. Under the leaves.

I could be a roller.

That would be honest. A perfect grape of amber and slate, sealed plates, no face to speak of. I would not hurry. I would not choose. The wind would push me across ten kilometers of ringing nothing, those long petrified troughs polished into whalebacks, my little straight line scored in pink loess for miles until a gust diverted me. I would collect the world electrostatically, grain by grain, dust for nitrates, and unroll at night inside the mist to scrape myself clean with a tongue like sandpaper.

I like that. The humility of it. To be a pilgrim that grazes on absence.

But I'm not that patient either. I want to *reach* for something.

So no. Not the roller. Not the tick, flat and translucent on the underside of a leaf, drinking wax and tolerated like a freckle. Not the stilt-walker, so thin it casts no shadow, moving in ritual detours around a bead-fruit before piercing it with a needle.

I kept coming back to the mist. That knee-high, medicinal, heavy vapor that never rises. The grove making its own weather because the world won't give it any. Five degrees colder inside at noon. Green shafts. Leaves clicking and sighing. Brooms crackling with static.

If you find yourself on the terrace, you don't find yourself *on* it. That's the rule the place taught me while I was trying on wings and wheels. Almost nothing lives *on* it. It lives *despite* it, or *instead* of it.

So I wouldn't be a visitor at all.

You'd find me not above the stone, not on it, but *as* it. Or as the thing that has learned to be a place.

I am a Holdfast.

Not the whole reef — that's too much to be at once. That's centuries, maybe millennia of layered fiber and salt and dead leaf pressed into rings, chimneys breathing in reverse at night when the stone ticks and contracts and the cool air sinks. Frost furring the inside of brine-lungs. Tabletop jade leaves angled not to move but to *be* the perfect angle, grown that way, ten thousand silver hairs beading and thawing with that faint soda-fizz at dawn, brine sucked back through pulsing capillaries.

No, I'm more specific than that. I'm newer than that.

I'm a cable.

You'd find me in the worst part. Halfway out.

I started as a swelling at the edge of a mother grove — one of the big ones, the forest-sized ones where you can walk inside a trunk and find in the heart that pitted black nodule, humming colder than anything around it, no longer alive but being lived *in*. I was thrown.

That's what we do. We throw ourselves.

Leathery, jointed, thick as your wrist, rootless along my entire length, blind. I left the mist and the cold and the green shafts and went out onto the white. Onto the lethal noon where rock is too hot to touch and light is total and shadowless and wind hisses high above but not down here.

I'm stretched across a trough now, between one island of furious, dripping, waxy jungle and ten kilometers of nothing. I have been here for — I don't know. Years. A decade? Time out here is measured in dust settled and dust blown off.

My acid tip is tasting stone. Most of us fail. You can see our brothers as brittle ropes shattered by wind, scar tissue marking the distance between groves. That's what the emptiness is, really. Not emptiness. All the failed reachings.

I am still supple. Still alive. I haven't desiccated. I found a bowl where wind has collected a little more pink loess in the lee of a softened ridge. It's enough. Just enough.

At my far end I am beginning to swell. A bud. A daughter-chimney. If I make it, I will be the one-success-in-thirty-years. A new reef that will learn to breathe in reverse, to ice its lungs at night, to exhale cold oil-mist by day, to farm crimson felt beads and shivering brooms under its own shade. A new hectare of *instead* in the continental nowhere.

If I don't, I will simply be a line. And that will be my use too.

That's where you'd find me, if you walked long enough to resolve that dark smear on the far ridge. Not soaring, not rolling. Stretched thin and taut and ridiculously hopeful across bare limestone, holding my brine close, waiting for one more cool dawn to condense a little more of myself to push forward an inch.

Listen close, when you pass. In the late afternoon when I throw a shadow a hundred meters long from nothing at all, you can hear the brine moving inside me. Slow honey. Rosemary and kerosene. Numbing if you taste it.

I am not waiting to be rescued by water. I am learning around thirst.
