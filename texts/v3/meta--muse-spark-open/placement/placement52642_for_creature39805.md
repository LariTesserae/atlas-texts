At first I think I would be the one in boots.

That's the easy answer, the one that comes before I've learned how to look here.

I would be the visitor — the soft, hurried animal who has wandered down over the barely-there rim at dawn, when the light is low enough to show that this place has an edge at all, and stopped on one of the upper terraces because the air is too full to go any further.

I would feel the warm stone through my soles where the newest crust is still leathery and gives a little. I would hear that flat, even, industrial thunder that never peaks and never quiets and realize my own ears have nothing to do with it — that it was roaring long before I learned to hear, and will be roaring long after I stop. My voice, when I try it, would be ridiculous. Small and dry. Eaten immediately by the steam.

I would be that kind of briefly-wet thing, and I would not belong. Not because the place is hostile — it isn't, not in the way a desert is — but because I am on the wrong timescale. I want a river to follow, a road, a change. This place will not change for me. It will not erode faster than it rebuilds itself. I would leave a bootprint in slag that would be gone by next rain, annealed back into the terrace by heat and salt.

So no. If I have to stay — and to answer honestly I have to stay — I have to slip.

You have to let your timescale slip, the description said. That's the invitation.

So the boots dissolve first.

---

I find myself a little lower, where the terraces stop being broad and perfect and start to pit. Where the center holds water longer than anywhere else. Not as a man walking the basin. As something *in* the basin.

For a while I am varnish.

I am one of the amber discs, although to be one is to be almost nothing at all. I am that thumbnail-sized lens of tea-colored translucence hanging a millimeter above the silt in a pothole the size of a washtub, not swimming, just... holding.

I learn the trick of it quickly: you don't fight the convection cell, you tilt. Warm water rises under your belly, cools at the skin of the pool, slides away to sink at the edge. You ride the upwelling the way a gull rides a thermal without ever moving its wings. Every few minutes — which for me is a long time — I pulse, and squeeze out a bead of clearer water from my rim and take it back in, a little lighter in iron than it was before. That is my whole year. That faint dark ring I'm precipitating on the silt below me is my garden and my grave at once. The bacteria I farm there will feed me tomorrow.

There is so much space between me and the next of me that loneliness doesn't even apply. We are sparse on purpose. To be closer would be to steal each other's heat.

And when the pool dries — because here even the most faithful pool dries — I don't panic the way a booted thing would. I settle. I flatten until I am not a disc anymore but a stain, a crackle of dried paint on the bottom of the bowl, indistinguishable from mineral. I crack. I wait. There is no urgency because rain has no schedule and I have learned not to have one either. Months pass above me as salt. A hot dew patters down from the Towers' breath and does nothing. Then a real rain comes straight down for seven uncounted minutes and I peel up at the edge, hydrate, inflate, and I am hanging again as if no time passed at all.

If you are varnish, time isn't a line. It's wet and dry, wet and dry.

It's a good life, but it's a borrowing. I am just renting water.

So I slip again, even slower.

---

Then I am paint.

Not *on* the terraces. *As* the terraces, or at least their blush.

I am the sulfur yellow and the rust red and the copper blue-green that from far away looked like moss. Up close I am not color at all, I am a felt, microns thick, leathery to the touch on a dry afternoon when a human fingertip would prod me and think me rubber. I am archaea and bacteria in a pact so old we've become a new thing, a single skin spread along the lip of a terrace half a kilometer long.

I don't move. I don't want. I have traded those for something better: I can eat stone and breathe steam.

When the sun is out I photosynthesize without green. When it isn't I chemosynthesize, switching hands when one gets tired. When those patternless rains come I darken almost instantly and wake up fizzing — tiny, silent bubbles beading along my surface and releasing, beading and releasing, like sweat. I grow at the speed the terraces grow: a fraction of a millimeter a season, a lamina of mineral laid down as a year's diary entry.

And I hear — no, feel — the Towers properly for the first time. Where the booted visitor heard an unbearable roar, I live inside the thump. *THUMP-thump-THUMP-thump* goes the gas through the Tower throats, and the whole warm stone trembles at a low frequency I can use. My filaments pulse in sympathy. I have tuned my cell division to that vibration. The basin is humming me along, like a mother humming while she works. The little heatless lightnings that tick between the caps on dry afternoons dump fixed nitrogen onto me and I eat light itself.

I am ninety-nine percent of whatever counts as alive here and still I am sparse. Long pale stretches of bare salt between my bands where nothing has found purchase. Kilometers of waiting. That's fine. I am not sparse the way a desert is sparse. I am sparse the way a sentence is sparse when every word has to be right.

But even paint is still something *on* something else. I want to be the thing that makes the ground.

So I slip one more time, and this time I get heavy.

---

I am a Tower.

Not one of the tall, lonely ones on the outer pans that you can see two or three of at a time. One of the central cluster, where the air crackles. Fifteen meters of dark basalt and iron, two meters thick, leaning just slightly, capped with that bulbous, slag-like porous swelling that looks like a boil and works like a lung.

I have no DNA the way you think of it. I am a reef. A chimney-reef. Tube-building bacteria lay down iron silicate around themselves, die, and become the tube for the next layer. Thermophiles line my inner throat like gut flora, stripping sulfur from the vapor that has been rising for kilometers through warm crust, dumping acid that etches more pore space so more of them can live. The steam that leaves my cap as that endless *HSSSSSSSS* has been filtered through my body. The thick black sludge that wells at my base and spreads and cracks as it cools — that is my waste and it is also my shell. I am extruding the valley floor. I am manufacturing the basin I stand in, very slowly, without intent, the way coral makes an island without ever deciding to make an island.

Each of my siblings has a different voice because each has a slightly different throat, a slightly different microbiome. One hisses higher. One thumps slower. We are not communicating. We are simply breathing out at equilibrium, and have been doing so for longer than weather remembers.

At night I glow. Not brightly. Just a dull, deep cherry at my seams where the rock is thinnest and the steam inside is hottest. From the rim, if there were anyone on the rim to look back, we would look like a working industrial town — furnaces scattered on a plain, all burning — except there are no windows, because there was never anyone here to need light.

Only stone, breathing out.

I like being a Tower best, because it finally explains the sound. I am the sound.

---

And then — if I really let go, if I don't settle on any one image early the way you asked me not to — I understand I don't have to choose between varnish and paint and furnace.

I am the low place itself.

I am the palm held open to the sky. Broad, shallow, kilometers across, warm as blood, warm as iron left in the sun. My rim only shows at dawn and dusk when the light rakes sideways; by midday I look infinite even to myself. My terraces are my palm lines, depositional, settled-form, laid down drip by drip from water that forgot it was ever doing anything else. My Towers are my pores. My potholes are the sweat caught in the creases.

I breathe out through many small mouths and have been doing it so constantly I've forgotten I'm making a sound. I rain on myself when I exhale too hard and the dew patters back down and sizzles on my salt. I catch real rain when it comes straight down for four minutes or forty and hold it for a while in my center, badly mirroring the sky, before I let it become air again.

Water briefly remembering to be liquid before it becomes air again — that's what lives in me, all of them. That's what I am when I'm not stone.

Sparse. Even. Loud. Patient.

If you find me, you will at first say I am dead, and for a long minute you will be right. Stay the night. Watch my seams glow cherry in the dark. Put your hand on my lower terrace even in shadow and feel how I hold the heat after the light is gone. Listen until the roar stops being a roar and becomes what it really is: a held breath.

That is where I find myself.

Not as the visitor who needs a road. Not even as any one resident.

As the basin that holds them, and waits, and makes more of itself.
