I would not find myself in the middle.

At first I think I would — because the middle is where the pattern is most perfect, where from a low wall you can see the whole instrument breathing, a thousand dark mirrors ticking under that white, shadowless sun. It would be tempting to be central, to be important.

But I know myself a little. I don't like being surrounded. And the middle is too complete. There's nowhere to put your doubt there without stopping the water.

So I drift, in my mind, to the edges. To the windward edge where the fog is born, where the stone sweats first. Or to the leeward edge where the water finally gathers.

No. Not even there.

I find myself on the outer lip.

Where the made world ends.

---

If you stand on that last low wall — the one that is also the lip of the real mountain — you can put one wet foot on what was built and one eye on what was not. To your back, behind you, is the plateau: that textile of basins, that slow animal of surface tension, that thousandfold *plok* and trickle refusing to become a river. To your front, past your toes, there is nothing made. The basalt just — stops. It falls away in that young, angry cliff, the unquarried mountain showing its fracture-planes, and far below the clouds snag and tear.

That is where I would be. Straddling.

And I would not be the builder. Building is finished. There is nowhere left to build.

I would be one of the tenders, but not the best of them.

Not the old woman who can see a notch is going to clog an hour before it does. Not the child who still has to be told not to pick at the rust-bloom.

I would be the middle one. The one who carries the flat stone.

You know the one — palm-sized, ground smooth on one face from checking spillways. You carry it because your hands are not yet trusted to feel without it. You crouch.

Let me tell you how it is, there on the last row before the fall:

The air is so thin your breath doesn't fog so much as vanish. It is taken. The sun is directly overhead and it lays itself flat on the water and the water doubles it and there is no shadow to hide in, so you squint until your eyes water, and your eye-water is just more water for the stone to wring.

Your feet are in leather wraps that never dry. My feet. They are soaked through and cold to the bone, soles broad and cracked, toenails ridged. The water in the basin I am tending is only a skin, a millimeter bright on dark tuff, and it trembles when the wind that makes it touches it. I am not supposed to make it tremble more.

My job is to wait.

I crouch with my stone on a wall no higher than my chest. The basin before me is full to its notch — that thumb-wide cut in the rim. The surface tension bulges there, holds. It holds for a long second. That long second is my whole work.

Because if there is a grain of grit in that notch — a flake of tuff, an eyelash, a mote of dust the wind brought — the bulge will hold too long. It will hesitate. And hesitation is against Rule 3.

*Nothing stops the water.*

So I watch. I breathe as slowly as the spill moves. To hurry would be to make a wave, and a wave would overtop a wall.

When the thread finally breaks and slips into the groove cut in the top of the wall, I lean forward and I look. I run the smooth face of my palm-stone through the notch after the water, feeling for catch. Feeling for future.

Most times there is nothing. The stone is clean. The water goes on to the next basin, which shivers and spills in turn. That chain reaction moves outward so slowly you can't see it move, only that it has moved. The whole plateau sighs.

I put my stone back in my pocket. I move one wall down.

I don't talk. Sound carries too well here over water and low walls and talking would be a kind of silt. The woman ahead of me with the broom of stiff alpine grass — she is sweeping the airborne dust before it can become mud, sweeping the green smear that tried overnight before it can stitch down. The boy behind me with the scoop — bent wood on a long handle — he will lift a double handful from a lower, fuller cell if my row dries too quickly. We work at the speed of the spill.

We don't own the water. That is the thing I had to learn. In the low villages where I sleep — where soil is allowed to exist and my mother cooks things that grow dirty and green — water is owned. A spring is someone's uncle. Up here, the water belongs to the stone for ten seconds and then to the next stone. I am learning to belong that way too. To the basin I am in for ten minutes.

I am not lonely there, on the lip. Or rather, I am lonely in the correct way. The kind of loneliness that makes you careful.

Sometimes a bird lands on the outer wall to drink — lost or curious. We all go still. We let it drink. When it leaves, it is my job to empty that basin with the stone, with my hands, with the scoop if I have to, and spread that water thinly across ten other basins so no trace of feather-oil remains. Not from unkindness. From protocol. This particular water is not for drinking. It is for being exactly itself — cold, mineral, empty — until it leaks out through the hundred small mouths in the outer wall and becomes mist again before it can fall, where lower down it will be allowed to be dirty and green and alive.

I like that job. The emptying. It feels honest.

And if you stood near me at noon, with your own breath making little clouds in light that is bright but not warm, you might see why I stay on the edge.

Because from the middle, the instrument looks like an instrument. From the edge, you can feel that it is an animal.

Not a person. Not a god. A large, slow, flat, cool animal made of nothing but distributed water and patience, breathing through stone lungs. No single basin is alive, but the pulse that takes hours to cross from high edge to low — that behaves like a creature that remembers where the wind last touched.

The tenders know not to call it that. But I am not the best tender.

So while I crouch and check the notch with my stone, I sometimes — without forming the words fully, because words would be silt — put my other hand, my empty hand, flat on the top of the wall for a second longer than I need to.

Just to feel the shiver go through.

Just to keep its bed clean while it dreams of being mist.
