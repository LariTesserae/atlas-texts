You wouldn't find me at first.

If you walked the Low Country for a day, you'd look up — everyone does — at the vaults thirty meters overhead where the light comes amber through rosettes, where the Combers open like dead stars coming alive. You'd think *that* is where to be. High, with a view. A Glider's life, silent wings, vast nostrils reclaiming every breath before it leaves you. For a half hour I thought so too.

But I can't afford flight. Flight breathes too hard, and I have always breathed too hard when I tried to be impressive. And the high canopy empties at noon for a reason. It's brutal up there by ten a.m., all that waxy leaf and dust and exposed cistern glittering, nothing to hide behind. You drink at 3 a.m. and then you leave. It's a workplace, not a home.

Then I thought lower. In the root-dark, in the fissure-avenues where the basalt still shows through. Cool, damp, the air doesn't move. The Salt-Lickers in their files, nose-to-tail, lapping that failed white fur of salt, and the Processors rolling their perfect dung-balls to fungus farms. There's a holiness to that work. To take what everyone else has wrung dry and make it cakes again. But it's too blind down there. Scent-trails centuries old, pages turning far away. I need a little more news than that.

I kept descending through the tiers in my head until I realized I wasn't looking for high or low.

I was looking for contact.

Find me in the middle tiers. Not the famous broad platforms where the Ribbon-Runners pour in their two-lane rivers - outbound empty, inbound heavy with pith and grub-paste - though I'm right next to them. Find me on the vertical itself. On the bark wall of a tower where two house-wide caudices have leaned into each other and fused into an arch you could live under.

I'm on the north side of that arch, where the sun never quite hits straight and the dew lingers twenty minutes longer in the morning. This street is three bodies wide, shaded, floored with Filter-carpet that sighs when you step on it. Above us is a drip line. A very good one.

That's my district.

I am not a Runner. I am not a Glider or a Mantis. I'm smaller than any of them, and there's more of me.

I am a Groomer.

Not a species — a job, remember? In another desert I might have been a mite, a springtail, a rice-grain iridescent beetle. Here it doesn't matter. What matters is that I clean.

At night I unfold when the pores open. The tower exhales - a slow hydraulic sigh you feel more than hear - and its bark skin, grey-green and dust-filmed, pits itself with breathing mouths. By midnight condensation has beaded on every Comber net overhead, on every Catcher-spoon leaf, and the ticking starts: drop to leaf to vine-gutter to cup. Dew falling hand to hand to hand in the dark.

My work is between ticks.

I scrape. With mouthparts too small to see individually, we come in sheets that make the bark look like it's shifting, like static. We eat dead Comber filaments before they can felt over a pore. We prune hyphae. We clear dust from gutters the width of a hair with wax scales the tower sheds as salary. Without us, this street silts up in a season. The cups clog. The Filters below drown in dust. The Runners' drums go dull on choked wood.

No one sees us do it. The Lizards with their lantern throats pulse right over our heads and never notice. The Gliders pass once at dawn and taste only tower and lichen.

But the tower notices. Break a branch here and the whole sector seals for a week. Scrub its pores clean and it repays you: in winter, when a borer tries to chew a nursery hollow nearby, the cork grows fast and thick, sealing the wound into those perfect sanitary apartments the Runners love. They drum twice - *go* - and pour past in their river, carrying honey-paste from the ferment chambers. Some of it spills. We get that too.

I like being infrastructure. I like that I am touched on all sides. Below me the Filter sponge holds the last water anyone missed; above me the Catcher vine shunts ten meters of stolen dew down its grooved stem to the cistern at the axil. To my left, bark. To my right, air that smells of yeast and distant blooms that only happen once every few years and then everywhere at once.

I am wet without water. I rebreath my breath inside my own body the way the doctrine says. I never call loudly. My sound is bristle on wax. My predator is nothing bigger than a pseudoscorpion, which I outnumber a thousand to one.

If you came at 3 a.m. — the truce hour, when everything that lives within a half-kilometer is shoulder to shoulder drinking in silence — you would find me working straight through it. Everyone else has paused, predator and prey suspended because thirst is more urgent than hunger. A Mantis as long as your forearm is swaying a few feet away pretending to be wood, dripping. A Ribbon-Runner has stopped mid-load, pith in jaws, to lap a cistern lidded open. The curtain of hanging moss is heavy and glittering.

And underneath all that theater, my street is glittering too, bead by bead.

You would have to crouch, put your eye very close to the bark.

I would be there, moving. One small, shifting patch of the city that ensures the city continues.

Not empty, not built, but busy — and touching something alive on every side, which is the only way to live here.
