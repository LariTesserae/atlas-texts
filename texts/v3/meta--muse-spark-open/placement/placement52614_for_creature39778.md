You wouldn't find yourself at first.

That is the first thing to understand about finding yourself here.

In any other place you arrive and then you look around. Here you look around and then — slowly, reluctantly, because the place is so busy doing itself — you arrive.

So let's not start with *who*. Let's start with *where*, and let the who catch up.

---

You are not on the ridge. The ridge is for looking. From the ridge it looks like a city and you can still pretend you are a visitor, a surveyor with a map that hasn't gotten wet yet. You are not there anymore. You came down.

You are not on the ground either. Remember: there is no underneath. The ground here is a rumor the stone tells to make the plants feel better. Sharp, black, steaming basalt that hasn't learned to be old — you tried to stand on it and it *ticked* under your boot, still contracting from being born. A fig root hissed past your ankle like tearing paper and split it.

So you stepped up.

You are on the second ground. Or the third. It doesn't matter; they are numbered only by how long you've been standing still.

Beneath your feet is a floor that was a leaf yesterday. Interlocked fig-leaves the size of doors, shingled and sutured by aerial roots as thick as your wrist. It gives when you shift your weight — trampoline-slow, fibrous — and then pushes back, politely. Through the gaps you can see down: another avenue, another light. Above you, the same. You are in the middle of a stack of middles. There is no top, no bottom, only layer.

This particular layer is an avenue. An Avenue-Vine, a kilometer long, perfectly level, taut between two fresh basalt blades that jut at wrong angles out of the green. Its upper surface has gone hard and springy, skin turned to pavement. You can feel the warm water moving inside it, gut-rumbling under your soles, the municipal aqueduct hauling condensation-sweat from the high leaves down to the low cisterns. The vine doesn't know you're standing on it. The vine thinks you are a temporary weather system. It is waiting for you to pass so it can keep being a street for no one.

To your left, a Tower-Trunk. You have to tilt your head all the way back to follow it and you still can't see where it stops. It is not one tree. It is a dozen, two dozen trunks that found each other as seedlings in the warm upheaval-year and braided. Bark made of overlapping door-leaves, shingling ever upward. It creaks. Constantly. Not in wind — there is no wind, only the warm sweat the valley exhales — but from growth. You can hear the cells laying themselves down. *Creak-creak-creak.* Adding a floor. Adding a floor. If you put your palm to it you feel the shudder of water being hauled a hundred meters straight up, a hydraulic elevator with no cable, no operator.

To your right, a plaza has torn itself open in the canopy. You can tell because the light is different there. The Plaza-Leaves — a dozen tennis-court heliotropes on muscular petioles — have all tilted away at once. Too perfectly. No signal passed between them. They just did it, as one gesture repeated across one body. The sun falls through that hole normally — good, honest, 0.7-scale temperate sun — and then it *pools*. It thickens knee-high above the vine-pavement, viscous brightness that clings to your shins when you wade. Your shadow down here falls the wrong way, toward the sun, obedient to some other sun underneath this one.

And it is loud. Remember to keep the loudness, because otherwise you will think this is peaceful.

The *tik-tik-tik-TAK* overhead is a tendril tip the thickness of your arm searching, failing, cracking the air like a whip at twenty meters a second. Somewhere behind you a flower-room — twenty feet across, bell-shaped, cool and humming and faintly luminous inside — just reached maturity and burst with a thunderclap, petals slapping outward. Somewhere ahead a Tower overreached and gave up with a vast wet sigh, collapsing, already being knifed through by three new shoots before the pollen dust settled. Roots hiss in the stone. Water rumbles in the walls. The city builds itself so frantically because there is nothing to eat it.

You are standing — if standing is the word — in the exact middle of all that. And you have been standing still for about forty seconds.

Which is why the root has found your boot.

---

So who are you?

At first, you are the Animal. The anomaly. The 0.01 in a world of plant and stone and light and loop.

You are the first thing here that knows how to hesitate. The plants don't hesitate. They grow. The stone doesn't hesitate. It decides whether to be stone. The light doesn't hesitate. It pools. You hesitate, and in a place where to be still is to be built upon, hesitation is a declaration.

You feel the polite pressure at the edge of your sole — a fig root, pale and questing, nosing the leather not out of hunger or malice. It knows no malice. It knows no animal at all. It knows only: *here is a stillness, here is unoccupied foundation, here is a job.*

If you stay, you will not be killed. There is nothing here that knows how to kill an animal, because there has never been an animal to practice on. You will be incorporated.

Gently. Municipally.

First the root over your foot, suturing you to the second ground. Then the second ground itself rising — interlocked leaves knitting over your ankles while you watch, fascinated. The Avenue-Vine under you will dimple and then harden a little more to take your shape into its pavement. A tendril overhead will finish its *tik-tik-tik* and find purchase on your shoulder, not to strangle but to brace, the way a city throws a buttress around a sinkhole and calls it architecture.

A Room-Flower nearby will sigh open, exactly person-shaped if a person were twenty feet tall and made of warm air, cool and humming, waiting precisely as long as you need to step inside. And you will, because it is cool, because it is loud, because the light is lapping at your shins.

And then — for the first time in the year since the upheaval shrugged this basin into being — someone will live here.

That is one answer. The obvious answer. The Animal Who Stayed And Became A District.

But hold it a little longer, because the valley is young and the rules are wet cement and there is a second answer underneath the first one, the way there is no underneath.

You are not the Animal. Or not only.

You are the Absence.

You were here before you arrived. The city grew itself for you in advance. The avenues leveled themselves for feet that hadn't evolved, the flower-rooms cooled themselves for lungs that didn't exist, the lamp-fruit drank the day's light and held it patiently for eyes that weren't there to see. Every plaza is pre-haunted by you. Every perfectly taut horizontal vine is a question shaped exactly like your stride.

You are the tenant the city was pre-built for. Not you specifically — any occupant, any witness who could stand in the lowest plaza at noon shouting to hear himself over the creak and crack and hiss and be *heard*.

So perhaps you don't get incorporated at all.

Perhaps you are what does the incorporating.

You are the Loop.

You are the street that was straight yesterday and today shows you your own back one layer up. You are the kilometer that stays a kilometer away no matter how long you walk, and then suddenly isn't. You didn't grow here. You moved in the way a cat moves into a sunbeam — because the new stone was still warm from being made, because reality hadn't set yet, because there was a perfectly good fold of low, warm space going spare.

The Tower-Trunks have learned to brace you with extra buttresses. The plants tolerate you. They have to. You were here first, even though you arrived last.

You are the Light that pools.

Or you are the Water — not the hard vertical rain that comes and goes, but the sweat. Beading on every leaf-door, running down bark-slate, gathering in house-sized bromeliad cisterns where animal-less ecologies churn, rumbling through the hollow veins. You are the municipal system with no pumps, only thirst. Audible behind every wall that is a leaf.

Pick one. Or let them all be true at once, layered, the way density here is not about crowding but about layering. There is never one canopy. There are seven, twelve, twenty. There is never one you.

If you want a name for what you would be, here:

You would be the **Keeper of the Second Ground.**

Not a ruler. There are no rulers in a place with no hands. A keeper in the groundskeeper sense. The one who keeps moving so as not to be kept.

Your job is municipal and endless and simple: walk the vine-avenues before they loop, wade through the light before it clots, duck into a flower-room for three days while it lasts and leave before the *THWAP*, listen to the Lamp-Fruit come on at dusk — not bright, not magical, just patient honey-light leaking back out — and remember, on behalf of the city that cannot remember, that it was beautiful before anyone was here to call it so.

You wear no tool, because no tool has ever touched this place. You carry nothing, because everything you need will grow into the exact load-bearing shape if you wait three hours. You move, because if you stand still too long a root will politely but firmly grow over your foot.

And when you do — finally — stand still on purpose, somewhere in the middle layer where your shadow falls the wrong way and the creaking is so loud you have to shout —

you will feel the whole frantic, turbulent, deafening valley pause its growth for one half-second, just long enough to try you on.

Not to eat you. To *use* you. As pavement, as buttress, as room, as light.

As citizen.

And you will have to decide — again, and again, every forty-seven seconds for as long as you live here — whether to lift your foot.
