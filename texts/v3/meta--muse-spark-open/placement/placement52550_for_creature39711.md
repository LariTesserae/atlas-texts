Where would I find myself?

Not at first.

At first I thought I might be the air in the bowl - that warm, ammoniacal sigh that spills over the lip and hits your face when you lean over the rim. Too rich and not enough at once. The lung of the place. I liked that. To be invisible and everywhere, to be breathed by ten thousand throats and breathed back.

Then I thought I might be the stone. The dark iron-red under all of that frantic dabbing. Patient enough to be polished by wind for ten thousand years and still hold heat like a cupped hand holds water. To feel every needle-foot as just a brief pressure, every dab of spit and down as rain.

Then the bowl resolved.

And I knew I was not big enough to be either of those things.

I am small. I have to be.

Find me on the eastern wall, a little below halfway down where the curve steepens and the northern overhang throws a permanent shadow. Not high enough to catch that twenty minutes of noon light, low enough that the condensation streak finds me. There is a dark vertical stain where water beads on the lip above and runs down each evening. To your eyes it is nothing. To me it is a river. It is the edge of my country and the center of it.

I hang there.

I am one of the dark ones. Soot-black on the back, ash-white at the throat. When you saw us from the rim you called us swifts or martins because you didn't have any other word. That's close enough. My wings, folded, cross over each other behind me like closed scissors, longer than my body. They twitch sometimes without my permission. My feet are not feet. Four black needles with a hook. I have not stood on flat ground since I learned to drop from the wall. I never will again.

I face the stone. We all face the stone.

This is not loneliness. There are no individuals here in the way you count them. Shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, the wall is covered with backs. If I shift my weight half an inch to the left I brush the bird beside me. If I shiver, she shivers. A ripple that you mistook for the lichen moving is often just us resettling. There is no territory. There is only adjacency. How warm we are together.

Two inches. That is the whole world I own. A pocked dimple in the granite no larger than your thumbnail, darkened where that water-stain keeps the sand cool and clumped. Over seasons I have made it not-rock. A dab of thistledown I carried for an hour from three thousand feet below to soften it in my beak. A shred of that sideways grass. My own down, plucked loose. Spit. Mineral dust. A little white of the droppings from above. Pressed and dried and pressed again until the shallowest cup holds. It is not architecture. It is a stain that learned to keep an egg from rolling.

There is an egg in it right now. Or, no — this week in the cycle — there is a chick. Chalk-white egg gone, replaced by a knot of translucent skin and pinfeathers that breathes too fast to see. Its beak is too large for its head. It doesn't cry. It leaks a sound, *seep... seep... seep*, so high most predators would miss it. I open one eye in the twilight and watch its throat pulse. That is my entire cleverness exhausted: to know this seep from ten thousand other seeps, to know this texture of rock-and-spit-and-down from ten thousand identical textures when I come home in the dark.

Because I do leave. This is what makes me different from the others who share my wall.

The bats in the fissure two inches above me — a crack as long as your arm packed with a hundred slow-breathing, blood-lit ears — they never leave. The tiny black guano beetles seething through the pink-white cliff below my tail never leave. The bacteria making our shared heat from uric acid never leave. They were born in warm dark and will die in warm dark, entire universes the size of a palm.

I get to fall.

At first light the bowl exhales. You would feel it as a pressure change if you were still brave enough to stand on the rim. The cold thermals start to spill. And we let go. Not up — we never go up first. We push off and drop inward, toward the damp sand pans on the floor you thought were empty, and the air that is spilling over the rim catches us. Paper tearing ten thousand times. A spiral, then a smoke-column, thinning as it rises into the killing cold where nothing should live. Up there the air is a desert and we hunt what you cannot see: ice-flies, midges, spiders on threads thinner than this sentence.

All day I am cold and alone and vast. The valley is miles below and the sky does not end.

Then dusk comes and I fall again, folded, like ash. Only at the last instant do I open my wings to stall, hook my needle-feet onto my own two inches among ten thousand identical two inches.

*Tik.*

The sound of finding purchase. The sound of fitting. Like water finding the shape of a hand.

I am not glad. Glad is too human and too loud. I simply *fit* there, and the wall is warm from everyone else fitting next to me, and the damp stone smells of iron and feathers, and below me the chick keeps leaking its tiny seep into the ammoniacal twilight, and above me the bats begin to twitch and respire once, together, as I settle.

The bowl is forty paces across.

It contains a city.

It contains me, hanging face-first against the dark, keeping an egg-warm spot warm with my breast in the place where the water runs.
