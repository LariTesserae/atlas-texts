If you came looking for me here, you wouldn't find me at first.

Not because I'm hidden. In the Low Works there is nowhere to hide — the whole scale is wrong for hiding. Everything is low and broad and planed until distance becomes a kind of transparency. You can see for twenty kilometers over the swells of worn foundries, over basins where cooling towers used to be, over the long, straight discolorations in the rust that used to be roads. 

For a long while I thought you'd find me up high. That was my first instinct — to look for the highest gantry-tooth left, that row of black rebar teeth thirty meters tall where the rest of the world has been ground down to ten. To stand up there with the sputtering transformers, in the little wind that gets pushed through the lungs of the factories, and look out and be the one thing with a vantage. A watchman. A lighthouse keeper with no lighthouse.

But nothing stays high here. High is what erosion is for. The wind and the chemical rain have taken the high places and made them low again, patiently, for epochs. And even if you climbed that skeleton, the Sound would take you apart — that seven-second throb from the pumps larger than houses somewhere below, traveling straight up through steel into your teeth. You wouldn't stay. Nothing with bones that remember why it climbed would stay.

So I thought, then, maybe lower. As low as I could get.

In one of those shallow amphitheater-basins a kilometer across, where the concrete is pitted like old skin and the water stands twenty centimeters deep and perfectly still. Where from above, the Stain lives — that rust-red algae so thin you only see it as a wash, a watercolor under the surface. I imagined being flat like that. Being a color spread thin rather than a body standing up. Letting the vibration ripple out over me in perfect concentric rings every time the presses stamp. Not resisting the tremble. Being the thing that shows the tremble.

I could be that. The Low Works doesn't mind things that make their presence horizontal.

But that's still not quite where I am. The stain is too open. Too exposed to the dull red foxfire and the sweat of the air. I found myself pulling inward as I looked longer — not flat and open, but tucked.

You'd find me on the lee side of a thing that isn't there anymore.

There's a dome about four kilometers off in any direction you choose — it doesn't matter which, they're all the same now, these low, rust-flaked hills that used to be foundry roofs. From a distance it's just a swell in the Floor, beaded with condensation like cold skin. Up close, on its eastern slope, there's a place where a harder alloy seam resisted the grinding — a lip, maybe fifteen centimeters high, the ghost of a doorway or a support ring.

Water gathers there. It always does. The air here is 8 degrees and weeping, always weeping, the warm breath of the deep Floor meeting the cool dusk. Condensation beads and runs, except here it doesn't run down. Ghost-gravity remembers the tower that used to be centered on this dome — thousands of tonnes of mass that stood vertical when the sky was still visible — and the water hesitates at the lip, then curls sideways. Then uphill. A slow, deliberate creep of tea-colored water against what your eyes tell you is slope. Going to spill over toward a drain that is higher than its source.

Right there, just under that lip, where it is sheltered enough that the scream of venting steam doesn't scour directly. Where the drip never stops but it doesn't batter. That's where I am.

---

And what am I, there? 

At first I wanted to say: one of the birches. That pale, starved, translucent-leafed tree that grows out of a rust-pit where the silt collected just a little less toxic. One knee-high, one shoulder-high, no taller. Leaning. All of them leaning, not toward light because light has no direction here, but toward the empty air where the missing weight still pulls. Roots groping sideways for phantom mass. Shaking constantly with the throb of the Floor, so you hear their leaves even when there is no wind.

I love them because they misunderstand so faithfully. They do exactly the wrong thing with complete commitment.

But I'm not brave enough to be a birch. Birches stand alone in a place that measures faithfulness in centuries. One in four of them near me is already dead — a white spar, bark peeling in wet strips like old paint, still shaking because the Floor shakes even the dead — and they stand there dead for years because there is nothing to knock them down. I can't do that alone.

No, closer:

I'm the white thread underneath.

The Hangers.

A beard of bone-white fungus, thin as insulation fibers gone to rot, hanging from the underside of that alloy lip. Translucent when the eternal beads soak through me. I have no roots. I don't eat the stone like the black lichen-rosettes do — those perfect coins digesting basalt-concrete one acid decade at a time, glowing faint amber-green when wet. I'm softer than that.

My whole business is waiting and filtering. The hot exhalation pulses out of fissures every seven seconds — you feel it in your molars before you feel it on your skin — and I sway out. Then sway back. Inhale, sway out. Exhale, sway back. I take the cold sweat of the air as it sheets down the dome and wicks it sideways along myself. I take the iron dissolved in it and make a little more white thread out of it. That's all.

If you touch me I come apart like wet paper. I don't reform quickly. I'm not strong. My only defense has always been that nothing here wants softness — the water is too heavy with oil for mouths, the Sound would shatter anything nesting, the pumps larger than houses are still stamping with no product to tend. Softness is overlooked. That's how it survives.

I'm still. You would think you saw me move and you didn't — it was the air exhaling. But if you crouched with your knees going instantly wet through your clothes, and put your face close enough that the smell of wet iron and ozone filled your nose, you'd see I'm dripping without pause. Each tip holding a bead that grows, hesitates under that ghost-pull, then lets go.

I live with the leak of the Floor, not on it. Like all of us here.

And yes — I lean, too. Even I. The filaments on the side toward the center of the lost tower are a few centimeters longer. Growing into the pull that isn't there. Learning, very slowly, to work with the water running uphill.

If you stand and leave me, I'll still be here weeping quietly after you've become a speck again on the broad, low undulation. After your boots have broken through another crust and sunk ankle-deep in brackish rust-water. After your voice has been folded into that profound bodily throb and taken away.

Come back in a year. Come back in ten. The dome will be a millimeter lower. My white beard will be a little longer on the ghost side. The drip will not have stopped.

You'll find me exactly where gravity misremembers.
