I didn't know at first.

When you said *a mountain becoming a city*, I thought I would be high. One of the Veils, maybe — because if you are going to live on a continent that is still deciding what it is, you should have the view of it. I wanted that silence, that four-meter stillness, the trick of riding the escarpment lift for hours without a single beat, watching the cloud-rivers drain inland like poured milk.

But I am too heavy for that. I like weight. I like to tick.

Then I thought I would be one of your Lattice People. A child learning to fall, stepping off stasis-ice with faith in a field that tells gravity *not now*. The amber at night, the warm air pockets, the way you can see straight through your house to the stars that don't twinkle here, they just glare. That appealed to me — a human so acclimated your lips go dark-red and your chest becomes a barrel.

But I don't think I would be human there. Not yet. Humans there are too patient, too slow. They move deliberately because the air at 4km won't let you do anything else. They watch more than they do. I would want to *do*, even if my doing was very, very small.

So I sank a little.

I would be low.

Not the lichen. Though I love the lichen - that charcoal and sulfur and blood-orange first skin that eats stone directly, that swells visibly when the cloud-sea pours over you. I am not patient enough to be lichen. Lichen has the timescale of the plateau itself. It has been covering thousands of square kilometers for ten thousand years, a millimeter at a time.

I would be what lives *in* the lichen, but only for six weeks. Or what lives just under it.

I think I am the white-berry creeper.

No - even lower.

Wait.

Listen.

Do you hear the ticking when the wind pauses? That continent-wide tick of condensation beading on every basalt edge, finally growing heavy enough to fall?

I am that.

But a tick needs a place to land. So this is where I have settled, after letting myself drift for a while:

I am the Six's garden.

Not the Six itself - though I love them, those bison-heavy, six-limbed locals with snowshoe feet and the shovel forelimbs they use to cradle their calves. They walk like small basalt outcrops. They do not fear you because fear hasn't been invented yet for something that smells of ozone and warm air.

I am the grey-green felt that lives in the furrow of its back, between the hump and the spine where it cannot groom.

I started as spores in the lee of a basalt pillar, a crust lichen that learned to eat rock. The wind - that slow, silent river of cloud that drains off the escarpment every morning - lifted me. I landed, damp, on something warm that was moving.

For a hundred kilometers I rode.

My host is an old female. I can tell by how complete I am. When she was young I was just a smear in the ditch of her shoulders, a little photosynthetic stain. Now, season after pulse-season, I have spread. I have covered her shoulders, her hump, the broad slope of her back. From above, when the Veils circle, she is invisible. She looks like a walking piece of the plateau itself, bearded with rime on her windward side, dripping on her leeward side. Just like the rocks weep, we weep.

And I have learned her habits.

We go where she goes - which is, in June, into the braided marsh. That ankle-deep world of black gravel and rust-red bacterial mats that bloom under paper-thin black ice. The water is so mineralized it never quite freezes, it just skins over and sighs when the sun breaks it in the morning. We wade it on those great splayed pads that don't sink in sphagnum. The cotton-grass brushes her belly and therefore brushes me.

She scrapes other lichens off the basalt towers with her high forelimbs and eats them. She never eats me. Not unless she must. That is the pact.

I do three things for her.

I photosynthesize, a little - not much at -5°C, but enough to warm that furrow by a fraction of a degree. I insulate. When the cloud-sea soaks everything and the rime builds thick on every upright thing, her back stays the one dry place. And I hold water - I swell when the cloud drains across us, I become a sponge of cold clean condensate that she can feel against her skin even if she never drinks from it.

In return she carries me to nitrogen.

This is the secret of the plateau: the animals *make* the land by being eaten and by eating and by shitting.

When the Hollow-Coats come through in those silent, endless single-file lines, their breath joining the clouds, when the slate-grey waders lift as one and dim the sun for minutes with the sound of shuffled paper - they all leave. Midges fall like snow into the water. Droppings fall like rain. The bacterial mats underneath us - that metallic-smelling ochre and black-green sheet of cyanobacteria and iron-oxidizers - they flare red. They fix nitrogen like crazy on that pulse. And I, up high on her back, get the aerosol of it, the fine nutrient fog that lifts off a continent being fertilized.

I grow a millimeter in a week. A year's growth in seven days.

And then we approach a Lattice.

I feel it long before she does, and she feels it before you would. A pressure change. The wind suddenly stops being ragged and becomes still. A low hum settles in her molars and therefore in me - 30Hz, the field-line anchors pretending to be matter.

From a distance it looks like heat-haze, a vertical distortion three hundred meters tall and a kilometer across. Up close you see the black filaments hung in the air without support, humming. Between them, panes and vaults of clear ice that never melt, even in direct sun, because they are held in stasis.

We are going to calve there.

Not because we are told to. Because under each Lattice the field leaks waste heat into the basalt and the permafrost retreats in a perfect 800-meter circle. The dwarf willow that everywhere else is a red wire mat no higher than your boot grows head-high there. The hassock grasses go to real seed-heads. The marsh becomes a garden, a spring in the middle of a world that hasn't finished being winter.

The Flow-Wolves - those white-grey rivers of canids who hunt by walking for days - will not hunt in the hum. They den there, but they don't kill there. It's a truce written in warmth.

So she walks into the circle, and I walk with her. And for the first time all year, the ticking stops. The basalt underfoot is warm. Steam lifts off her coat, off me, and joins the condensate being lifted and woven into new walls overhead. Up on an invisible balcony, a human child watches us, dark-lipped and barrel-chested, learning how to be still.

She will lie down here. She will use her little shovel-hands to cradle the calf when it comes, slick and steaming in air that should kill it but doesn't, not here. And if the calf lives, some of me will flake off in the birth and land in the new furrow of its back. If the calf doesn't - well.

If winter comes early, or if the march back down the escarpment is too long and she is starving, she will reach back with those forelimbs and scrape me off and eat me. A mouthful of grey-green felt, bitter, alive. I am her walking pantry. I have always known this.

That's why I am an expert in impermanence.

I do not mind. I was stone, then I was air, then I was a garden on a moving mountain. If I am eaten, I will be blood and then droppings and then bacterial mat and then midge and then wader and then Veil-shadow, and then somewhere, wind-lifted again, I will land on another warm back.

Find me there.

Find me in the one warm place on a cold new continent, on the back of the one animal that never learned to be afraid, underneath a city that never touches the ground, listening with her to the tick... tick... tick of a world still weeping itself into being, and humming back, very softly, in the same frequency.
