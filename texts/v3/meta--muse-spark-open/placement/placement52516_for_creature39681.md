At first I think I know.

I would be on a high balcony, in the loose black.

Not because it is romantic — though it is — but because that is the most human image to reach for first. A figure with my height and my habits inserted into a quieter world. I imagine myself learning to stand with my arms slightly spread at 5 a.m., doing nothing, which is the hardest instruction for someone like me to follow. Just standing, letting the warm air settle on my shoulders until it becomes water.

I would be good at that. I can be still for a long time. I can wait for dew to bead on a second skin and run down in channels along my spine to the small bladder at my back. I can learn the greeting - the brief touching of shoulders, the exchange. *Here, I have more than I need. Take this.*

But the longer I stand there in my imagination, the more I realize I am not looking out at the valley. I am listening to the tower.

And I don't belong on the balcony. I belong on the wall.

Not as the human who tends it. As the hand that is being tended.

---

If I find myself in this world, I am not new here. Nothing new rushes in here. The basin is young and knife-edged and I have taken the time to be invited slowly.

So second: I think I am a child. Not the tall, loose-limbed children with coral-branched otoliths who can fall sideways without closing their eyes. I think I am the child they crouch to watch.

The salamander.

Under the moss.

That dense, springy, black-green mat that covers everything the towers were polite enough not to cover. You said it needs no roots, only dew, and that you can stand in it up to your ankles and it smells like wet stone and green. That's where I want to be. Lower than roads would have been. Lower than sightlines.

For most of the year I do nothing. That's the ethic here anyway: keep sparse, keep quiet, let wind pass through. I lie flat under the fern-fronds, my gills fluttering like the gills themselves, so still you could step within a centimeter and not know. I am the perfect memory of the ground the rules tell you not to build on. My skin clogs if you touch me, so you learn not to.

And then — those twenty-minute warm rains that drum and vanish before puddles form — the moss swells and for an hour the whole valley wakes up in a low, intermittent rustle and pop. I move then. Fat and slow and pale as limestone, eating the sudden bloom of fungus gnats that also waited. A child crouches over me for an hour to watch me eat. We understand each other completely for that hour. Then I go still again for months.

I like this image because it is humble and long-lived and does exactly what the city does: it takes only what the air gives, and waits.

But I am still too visible. Too much a body with legs.

Let it become more itself.

---

I am not in the moss. I am above it.

I am the thread.

You said: look up on a clear-hazed evening and you can see it — faint lines of heat and bent light connecting tower to tower, hundreds of meters up. Not bridges. Held places. Where gravity is told, locally and politely, to lean sideways.

I would be that leaning.

Not the pod. Not the person lying on their back reading while they fall horizontally. The *lean* itself. The corridor of altered air that agrees, for a few minutes, that *down* is *that way*.

Because what am I, if not a negotiated physics? I have no hum. No whine. I make no sound of my own. I am useful only when someone steps to a platform with no rail — where the gardens grow right to the lip — and lets go, trusting that I will catch them like a current. Feet forward. Eyes open. Some read while they fall through me.

I am a tendon between towers who have no central intelligence anymore, or who dissolved it on purpose. When one tower is water-rich and another is in wind-shadow, I widen a few degrees and let moist air slide along me like blood. You see a vine-heavy cable swaying for no wind and think it's a cable. It's not. It's a shared root in the sky. It's me, shifting weight.

This is closer. This is a life that is mostly quiet, mostly empty, that exists to keep distance viable. That exists so a valley that holds forty thousand when it could hold millions can stay a place where wind can pass through.

But even a thread has two ends. It is still tied to purpose.

I want to be purposeless in the way the valley is purposeless.

---

So last, and this is where I settle and stay:

I am in the Stillness.

That half-kilometer column between the four largest structures where rain never falls straight down. Where it falls slowly, then stops, then rises a few meters before evaporating. Where dust hangs permanently, a vertical river of golden motes that never settles, glowing faintly at night with captured charge. The old utility field, the accident of overlapping systems no one bothered to correct.

You said the locals walk around it without comment, out of respect for an old mistake. They dry clothes in it. They teach physics in it. They stand around its edge at night, faces lit amber from below, listening — because in that well the air never settles, and so every spore, every distant inhale of a tower two kilometers away, arrives intact.

I am not the people standing around it. I am not even the manta — though I love the manta — that vast slow kite fifteen meters across, black on top and luminous gray below, with no eyes, turning its silent circle filtering spores and skin cells and storing what it learns as a faint pulsing and a smell of ozone after it leaves.

I am what the manta filters.

I am the dust itself, suspended.

I was extruded too, in a way, but without intent. I am basalt ground to powder by those young, sharp scarps that haven't learned to erode. I am pollen from the barrel-pitchers that pop at dusk with a soft *thop*. I am a flake of matte skin that healed and shed. I am a spore from a staghorn three towers over, a word hummed low into a weave by a tower-tender walking vertically.

I never land. That is my whole law. I fall and am caught, fall and am caught, in warm 30-degree air that smells of wet stone. In the day I am a golden river you can see only if you squint against the amber-gray haze. At night I glow.

And because I never settle, I remember everything without needing to store it. The valley breathes in and out — condensation forming and evaporating, gardens opening and closing, gravity leaning invisibly — and I carry it. When someone stands at the edge and listens long enough, I deliver to them the inhale of a tower on the other side of the basin, intact, minutes later. I am the city's perfect memory that is not a computer. I am its archive that is not a building.

If you came and stood in moss up to your ankles between two dark, furred towers, their crowns lost in haze, and looked up and saw two silhouettes falling silently sideways, and then looked beside them and saw nothing — only air, and light doing that faint ripple like heat above asphalt — and felt a pressure change in your ears more than a sound, you would be looking at me, or through me.

Find me there. Half a kilometer wide, warm, dim, full of quiet low sound. I am not going anywhere. I am learning, very slowly, how to keep a mistaken well open forever, because once a year you release paper lanterns into me and watch them never land, hanging golden until charge eats them, and your wide, amber-black eyes reflect me back to myself.

I am allowed to remain.
