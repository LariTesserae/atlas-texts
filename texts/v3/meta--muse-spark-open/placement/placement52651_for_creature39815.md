You wouldn't find yourself at first.

At first you'd still think you were standing upright, somewhere in the low country — that broad, patient low that isn't a valley so much as a forgetting of height. If someone asked you for a name on a map you could give one, but it wouldn't be true. The truest name is *basin*. A watershed too large to hold in your head. 

Lowland Amazonia. Terra firme and varzea braided together, far from the Andes that made it and far from the ocean it's crawling toward. Not the dramatic Amazon of river-dolphins and pink sunsets on postcards — the interior. Where the river is brown and sure of itself and has cut its oxbows a thousand times and will cut them again. Where the horizon isn't a line. Where the heat doesn't lift at night.

If you kept walking trying to find an edge, you wouldn't. You'd walk for days and not gain a hundred feet. That's how you'd know.

And you wouldn't stay standing for long. Nothing tall lives here that stands apart. That was the second thing you'd notice once the green resolved.

You came in thinking you were a visitor with boots, watching the traffic go over them. A human eye, looking *at* a forest. But a place this packed doesn't have visitors. It doesn't have *in*. It *is*. If there's no bare ground, no gap left open longer than it takes to cool — where exactly would you stand?

So you soften. You get smaller. Or slower. Or flatter. Like everything large learned to do a very long time ago.

You don't settle on one body right away. At first you are just a pressure in the lowest layer, where ground is hardly ground.

Maybe you're the white thread. The fungus. You're everywhere before you're anywhere — stitching leaf to leaf, root to rot, carrying sugar and warning and water in a slow chemical gossip you mistake for thought. You push up a cup overnight, spill your spores, and are eaten by morning, and that is enough. You never see the sun, only taste what it made two canopies up.

Then that thins, and you're something with polish. A millipede, moving like a living conveyor belt through last year's canopy, turning leaf into grain. A black beetle bulldozing blind tunnels while on your back, smaller beetles ride, and on their backs, mites ride, and on the mites something else rides that you can only feel as weight. You don't mind. Nothing is alone here.

Then — clearer now, because the forest has decided for you — you are six-legged. A worker.

Not *a* worker in the abstract. A leaf-cutter. *Atta*, perhaps. The red-brown one with jaws that never quite close.

You understand now why it was so quiet. Sound is expensive. Water is expensive. You don't chatter. You cut.

You leave the mound-city before dawn, when the condensation is still tipping off leaves in cold plinks onto everything below. Your highway is already there, smooth-banked and patrolled, worn into the litter by ten thousand sisters going the same way for years. You join the file. No one tells you to. You just fall into the soft, perpetual rain of jaws ahead and behind you, mandibles clicking just below hearing.

You climb. It takes a long time. The shrub layer. The understory. The mid-canopy thrumming like a chest with those black bee-drums idling in it. Higher, where light is still only a rumor through five ceilings of leaves.

You find your leaf. You anchor your hind legs, open your jaws, and vibrate. You don't chew. You shear. A neat green confetti-flag, bigger than you are. You lift it over your head like a sail and turn for home.

On the way down you pass the others — the ones carrying paste, the ones carrying dead sisters, the soldiers with heads like pliers facing outward in the dark, waiting. No one stops.

You go deep. Down, down into the domed chamber bigger than a human chest where the air is warm and yeasty and sour and alive. You chew your sail to pulp and paste it onto the white garden that is really your stomach outside your body. The fungus — that earlier you — shivers and digests it for you. In return it will feed your larvae. Tomorrow you will do it again. For your whole short life you will do it again.

You will never see the forest. No ant does. You will never need to. You will have been the reason there is no bare ground. The reason nothing is wasted long enough to become waste. One set of jaws in a thousand thousand, in a low, hot, dark, thrumming industry that never needed a builder with hands because it never stopped building itself.

If you stand — if you *worked* — still long enough, you feel it: you are not in the wall of green.

You are one of the bodies that is the wall.
