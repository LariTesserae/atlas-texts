I don't find myself in the city at first.

That's what takes a while to understand — that the city is the emptiness as much as the stone. So if you put me down on the Table anywhere, I don't land on a plaza or in a house or even on the causeway. I land after the step.

Behind me the lowlands have fallen away in that one long basalt drop, and the air has gone clean and decisive, and in front of me there is a hundred-meter avenue with nothing in it. Nothing except wind-polished dark stone and one perfect circle of orange lichen a meter across, like a sun that forgot to rise properly. And I am standing in the emptiness because it is empty on purpose.

At first I think I will be one of the people. That's the easy image. I want to be useful in a place that doesn't need use.

I would be down by the river, maybe — not the center of the river, not the long lake-with-a-current where the fish hold station each in its own exact place, but the edge where the black bank holds a footprint for a little while before it dries. I would be knee-deep in the cool that never floods and never dries, with my hand hovering over water. Learning that particular stillness the woman has — not hunting, not hesitating, but *participating*. Deciding whether the story today needs a taking in it. Most days I would not take. I would just touch the cold for a second to let the fish know I know how to keep an interval, and wade out again.

Or I would be the librarian — not the title, the motion. The old woman with lichen-grey hair or the young man with sap under his nails, it doesn't matter, the role moves. I would be under the long larch-wood roof without walls where the rain can't get in at its angle. Dusting tablets that have no dust with a hare-skin brush. Re-slotting one that someone left a millimeter out of true, not because anyone will be quizzed on it, but for the *click*. Water level in cistern four, same as third-day last. Fish in bend seven, same number. Tuber yield, same. Recording it anyway, with care. A fidelity that is a form of prayer, even though no one here prays loudly enough to hear.

But those are still too human, too much like doing. The valley accommodates doing, but it remembers it as if it had always been there. It doesn't need me to do.

So let the image become more itself.

I am not the mason who likes his stones a little proud, nor the mason who hammers hers flush. I am the uneven course where they met. The biography in the wall. I am the slightly different hands left visible on purpose, not smoothed out, the line that proves two people were here and did not need to agree on what *flat* means.

Or closer — I am the spare basalt ladle left by the cistern nearest where you sleep. Not used yet. Waiting. The interval kept for a visitor who hasn't decided to stay or leave, and will not be asked. I hold nothing, but I am the right shape to hold sky if someone lifts sky to their mouth.

No — closer still.

I find myself where water lives without a source. In one of the hollows in the lee of a boulder where last week's rain is still held, perfectly still, perfectly clear. Not the river — the river has a job, it has to thread from north notch to south notch to prove it is a river. I would be one of the cisterns. One of the high ones, on the terrace that catches the morning condensation so heavy it runs. The one the aqueduct on its stacked-stone trestle was built to feed, but doesn't need to feed, because I never lose.

I am not deep. I am maybe a meter across, basalt-dark at the rim, holding maybe twenty centimeters of sky. During the day I dry to grey at the very edge, just enough to show I have an edge. At dawn someone comes — daughter, father, grandmother, whoever woke first — with a notched pine stick to check me. Not because I change, but because checking is how you say *I am here, you are here.* She skims the overnight film with the ladle, lifts one fallen larch needle, and leaves again, quietly, so her boots on stone don't bruise the air.

I reflect without ripple. The high flat-bottomed clouds move so slowly you can convince yourself they are not moving at all, and I hold one of them perfectly. A rock hare drinks from me at noon and I replace what it took by evening, not by filling, but by having never been less. The stone-grey marmot sits on the wall beside me and watches without alarm because I am station-keeping too, like the fish, like the swifts that turn without calling over the caldera wall.

And at dusk — for exactly six minutes, when you will check your watch and find the valley more exact — the light goes amber. I go amber with it. The moths on the slate roofs open fully. The people on the walls and trestles and plazas stop and turn their faces slightly up, aligning, the way you align a stone before you tap it.

I hold the amber without fading. I hold it after it should have faded. Night doesn't truly fall; the held light holds.

If I am anything in this place that does not change, I am not the builder and not the built. I am the kept emptiness between them that lets both be seen. The deliberately spacious interval that proves the city is alive because it chooses to breathe between its houses.

You will come to drink from me in the early-autumn cool with your breath visible, and you will see your own face and the right-sized sun behind it, both held without argument in twenty centimeters of water that lives without a source.

And I will have accommodated you as if you had always been there.
