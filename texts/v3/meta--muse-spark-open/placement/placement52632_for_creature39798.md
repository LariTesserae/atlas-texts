I don't think I would arrive as a person at all.

At first, walking in from the rim — because you have to come in from the rim, there is no other way onto a world that ends in cloud — I thought I would find myself in the middle. In the deepest terrace, where the moss is ankle-high and sound-soft and the plazas hold those amber pools that remember another sky. It would be sheltered there. The wind organ-piping through the streets loses its worst edge by the time it reaches the center, and the cloud sits heaviest there in the morning, beading on every hair. It seemed like a place to be small and tended.

But that is a human thought. That wants an inside, wants a center to be a center.

This place tried inside and rejected it. There are no interiors. Every doorway is two feet deep and stops. So I can't be in the middle either, not really. There's no *in* to be in.

Then I thought maybe I would be the hermit. Because of course I thought that — you describe a hamlet-sized room with no roof and a wind that never stops negotiating, and some part of you immediately tries on the coat. I imagined learning the route from the warm north terraces down to the southern cisterns, watching where the water abandons one gutter for another every few days. I imagined lining my too-shallow niche with the driest domes, eating the sour, peppery tips of the red spiral moss, straining the tea-colored pool soup through cloth. I imagined not speaking, because the towers throw your voice back wrong.

But the hermit doesn't stay. The description is explicit about that. The cold gets into the hands first, 0.3/3 cold that lives in stone, and then the loudness gets into the mind, that 2.4/3 chord of fluted streets and drumming water. A human is too dense and too loud for 0.4 density. The town would notice me too much. I would be an event, and events don't live here. They visit.

Let what is there be itself a little longer.

If I am not the visitor, I am what waits.

I find myself not *on* the town, but *as* a part of its agreement.

On the leeward side of one of those blunt knuckle-towers on the second ring in from the rim, about chest high, facing southeast into the prevailing wind. That's where. Not the warm north with the clockwise spirals — that's too fevered, too sure of itself. Not the deep center — that's too soft, too listened-to. Halfway, on the weather side.

I am one of the niches.

Or rather, the lining of one. The soot-black liverwort, glossy as wet leather, folded like lungs.

In the morning, when the cloud arrives like a slow tide over that smooth stone lip of horizon, I hang slack. Damp and idle. Droplets bead on me and run and bead again. The cushion-lice a few feet away make their blind circuits over their domes. The pool below holds its star-map. Everything is waiting for pressure.

Then the wind hits the rim.

You feel it first as a tug, not a push — the inhale. My fronds, which were soft and hanging, stiffen and bristle out into the current. I catch. Not insects, there's almost nothing per volume here, but the world is intermittent, 1.4/3: droplets, spores, dust, ions, sound. On a good gust I am suddenly full of the whole plateau at once — the spore-puffs from the central moss released in silent cathedral clouds, the cold iron smell of silica solution weeping from the bedrock, the raven's cry from three streets over made into five ravens by the towers.

I make the howl into a chord. The street is a flute, but I am the soft throat that catches in it. I shiver. For a few minutes on a bad gust I am *loud*, louder than anything that lives here has any right to be, and that is my work.

Then, minutes later, the slow exhalation. Slightly warmer, spore-sweet. If you — if the hermit — pressed your palm close I would move the hairs on your hand. I would give you back the morning cloud slightly altered, slightly mine.

That's what I do. I breathe without needing a door that opens.

I am where the distinction eroded. Am I architecture? I have a doorway, a window-slit, a perfect too-regular shape that looks built until you see the edge breathe. Am I biology? I am black-green and glossy, I excrete a paper-thin rock when I dry and lay down a slightly different rock when I wet. Am I town or am I inhabitant? The bedrock weeps and I drink it and extend the lip a fraction of a millimeter a century. I polish the hollow with my own endless swell and shrink.

I don't see the stars at midday — the pool does that. I don't spiral clockwise to keep snow from settling — the red moss does that. I don't commute up from the unguessed distance below — the raven does that. My time is condensation time, drip... drip... drip from lip to lip of terrace, ice forming at night with a bright *tink* and melting by noon.

And in the evening, when the cloud comes back to sleep in the town and the moss releases its spores to hang like dust in the damp air, I watch the empty streets fill with suggestion. Mist taking the shape the architecture suggests, crowding the grove-small, hamlet-small, room-small open palm with inhabitants that never needed to exist. For a minute it looks inhabited after all — shapes moving between towers. Then the wind turns. I inhale, and empty it again.

I like that part best. Not because it is lonely, but because it is accurate. The town trying on people the way you try on a coat in a shop, and finding it doesn't quite fit, and hanging it back up.

I am a shallow niche two feet deep in ancient-erosion rock on a flattened mountain that forgot how to be high, at 1.9 elevation and 2.8 age. I have no inside. I don't need one.

I respire.
