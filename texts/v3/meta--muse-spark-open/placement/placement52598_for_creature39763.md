I don't think I'd be at the top.

At first I thought I would — 3rd Ledge, where it's a degree cooler and the sun actually finds you for a few minutes at noon. Where the Old Ones sit with their feet over the drop shelling beans into the same bowl. It would suit me to be memory-keeper, to remember which ladder was welded when and where not to drill because the rock still holds its temper. I am good at remembering.

But I don't rustle without wind. And I don't sit still well enough for that. The Old Ones have learned to be still. I'm still learning to be slow.

And I wouldn't be at the bottom either.

Low Spur is too much for me — that heavy 40-degree air off the coin-pools, the three cream-and-green piston engines going *chuff...... chuff...... chuff* so slow you think they've stopped, the moss they let grow in neat collars around the housings to keep them from cracking. I love the Low Walkers for how they know a machine like an old dog's breathing, palm to a sweating conduit, fingerprints burned off and grown back wrong. But I could not tend something that patient. I would listen to it too hard, try to hurry its cycle. The heat would sit me down with a hand on my chest.

I wouldn't be a High Child. I would want to be — loud for two hours in the column of white light while the adults go silent, knowing every translucent gecko by its missing toes, lying on my belly for an hour to lower crumbs to the blind white catfish that never bites. But I am not built for shrieking. I learned a long time ago to drop my voice low and flat so it doesn't echo. The Kettle-voice. Even.

No. If you came looking for me you wouldn't find me right away, because for a long time there is no one thing to find.

You'd feel me first in your teeth, probably. That 50-hertz hum you stop hearing by week two. The buzz in the railings, the ripple in a barrel when the water is otherwise still. The way a vine tip will wave blindly in the heavy air and then hook, definitively, around a rusted nail while a stainless spoon right next to it is ignored.

You'd hear me as a faint pinging as the noon sun hits cold iron, as the waterfalls muttering down the east wall in three steaming steps, as leaves rustling when no wind is blowing. The sound a place makes when it is breathing with its mouth closed.

And then, as your eyes adjusted to the work-light amber, you'd see me in the middle, where every crowded thing is.

I would be about a level and a half up from Low Spur, on River-Run, where they stopped trying to decide if a roof is a roof or a garden bed or a trellis and let it be all three at once.

There is a walkway there made of welded grating that has sagged a millimeter a year under the weight of everything. Under it, a shop that makes parts for the engines that keep the shop alive. Over it, a string of open troughs hammered from old ducting where the warm spring runs ankle-deep, guided but never owned. Banana hands ripen small and fierce right where you hang your laundry. Taro has put its elephant ears straight through the corridor so you have to duck. Morning glory as big as your head opens white after the lamps dim and turns away from the light to stare at the elevator cable.

That cable is important. It goes so slowly. Screamingly slow. Everyone has to wait for it, so everyone has time to touch.

I would be the one with the bent nail and the little spray bottle.

A Pruner — but not only. A gutter-tender too. The people who stopped pulling, because pulling tears root and rebar apart now and they have become the same thing. Who coax instead. *Cut here so you grow there.* Wedge a sheet of tin so the tendril chooses left instead of right. Tie a red rag where you want thickness, because for some reason vine avoids red. Pluck fallen mango leaves one by one out of the cistern so they don't choke the lower fall. Clear a gutter without breaking the mineral crust inside, because the crust helps it flow.

I would have the damp flush that never dries, the hair that never quite dries. Arms mapped with pale scratches and leaf-burn rash. Shears gone brown at the edge sleeping under my hammock like a pet, because if you leave tools out overnight they rust. I would speak to the water the way you're supposed to — not prayer, just reminder: *go here now.*

That is what I do anyway, isn't it? I don't flood. I don't own. I guide. I prune a sentence so another one can thicken. I put a red rag down and say *not this way, love, try that pipe instead.* I listen for where the green wants to go — toward iron — and I negotiate.

You would find me in the hour when everything goes quiet. When the sun spears straight down like a searchlight for its two hours and the whole Kettle holds its breath and you can hear every drip hit every barrel across sixty meters of still air — *plink... plink... plink* every seven seconds off the underside of a pipe.

I would be very still then. Damp. Warm to the touch. Part of the scab.

Not the wound. Not the thing at the very bottom where the crack is still open a little — a throat, not a floor — where water drips with a delay that is too long for sixty meters. Not that. But the living that kept it from healing shut.

If you stood on Low Spur and looked up, you would see me without knowing it. Not a person, exactly, but the place where a handrail became a root became a hand again. The place where everything touches everything else and holds.

Up on the plains it's dry and empty and alone and you can see forever.

Down here, I keep what falls into me.
