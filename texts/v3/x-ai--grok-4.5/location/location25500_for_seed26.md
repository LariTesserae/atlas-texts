The first thing is the cold that does not come from weather. It comes from the metal.

You are below. Not in a cavern that once was stone and is now retrofit, but in a place that was poured and bolted and welded into being from the start—an inverted topography of catwalks, retention basins, pressure halls, and sunken yards, all of it fabricated, none of it substrate. The floor is grated or plated or sealed concrete depending on the zone; the “sky” is a lattice of gantries, duct trunks, and sodium-vapor spiders that never quite reach full burn. Light arrives as a tired amber that pools in the wet and dies in the distances. Those distances are real. The complex opens like a drowned industrial valley: long sightlines down assembly corridors that widen into freight basins, then narrow again into pipe forests, then open once more into cooling plazas the size of town squares. You can walk for an hour and still be inside the same continuous made world.

Water is everywhere it should not be and exactly where the engineers stopped pretending they could keep it out. It beads on the undersides of horizontal tanks. It sheets down the faces of unfinished bulkheads in slow, deliberate Weeping. Sumps run continuously; their pumps have a wet stuttering cough that never quite syncs across the district. In the lower sweeps, the plating stands in a permanent black film of condensate and runoff, shallow enough to walk, deep enough to cast back broken reflections of warning strobes. Marsh-edge is the wrong phrase and the right feeling: the boundary where dry catwalk becomes slick grating becomes ankle-depth channel, repeated at every scale. Fog does not roll in from outside; it condenses out of the breath of the machines themselves, a low industrial weather that drifts between the stanchions and softens the already-dim lamps into halos.

The structures are young in the way of things still being born wrong. Edges have not been rounded by decades. Fresh cut-marks show on beam ends. Newly torqued flanges still hold their factory blue. Entire wings feel recently hinged into place—sharp corners, unsoftened welds, staircases that stop half a flight hanging over empty air as if the next section has not been swung in yet. Erosion here is not wind or river; it is the plant itself revising its own body. Cranes move at irregular hours. A wall that was solid last shift is now a bay. Sparks fall like brief orange snow and hiss out in the standing water. The place is unfinished on purpose, or unfinished because the work outruns the plan.

Vegetation is an accident that learned to stay. In the perpetual damp, a thin skein of cold-tolerant scum and filament clings to the sunless side of pipes—gray-green, almost black, never lush. It does not carpet; it stamps small wet coins of life at joints and overflows. Once in a long while a taller thing appears: a pale, leafless switch of growth forced up through a cracked expansion joint, fed by mineral drip and the low heat of a buried cable. No one planted it. No one has bothered to scrape it all away. It is the only soft vertical in kilometers of hard verticals.

Sound is the climate. Compressors speak in stacked octaves. Somewhere always a drop-forge is testing its rhythm against a hull plate. Steam vents bark and then whine as pressure falls. Water hits metal in a thousand slightly different keys—fat drops from high manifolds, fine spray from failed gaskets, the continuous pour of a cooling race into a lower trough. Under it all, the deep electrical hum of a grid that has no night cycle. The loudness is not a single event; it is the medium you move through. Quiet would be the unnatural thing.

Fauna is rumor and rare punctuation. Something pale and jointed lives in the warmer runoff channels and only surfaces when the big pumps cycle down. Birds—or the local equivalent of birds—are bats of sheet-metal and scavenged insulation that nest in the high dead spaces between cable trays; you hear their wings more than you see them, a dry flutter under the industrial roar. Tracks appear overnight in the silt of a drained basin and lead nowhere that makes sense. Whatever makes them does not stay to be counted.

The strangeness is quieter than monster or miracle. It is the way the geometry refuses a single up. Multiple local verticals argue with each other because whole districts were oriented to different load-bearing logic and then fused. It is the way heat and cold trade places without warning: a corridor Fletchered by furnace bleed sits ten meters from a brine pipe that skins the air with frost. It is the occasional passage where the lights die entirely and the only illumination is the bieluminescent film on the water, a weak green script rewriting itself across the black. It is the sense that the complex is not merely occupied by machines but is itself a slow machine whose product is more of itself—expanding, revising, drowning its older floors, lifting new decks into the dim.

Time here is shift-change and pump-cycle and the slow creep of mineral scale. Weather is interior: fog banks that form when a cooling tower overperforms, sudden warm rains when a high main ruptures and the sprinklers botch the isolation, cold snaps when a cryogenic line is purged. Processes run parallel: fabrication, decay, reclamation, the patient colonization by scum and dripstone, the loud indifference of engines that do not know they are underground because there was never a ground that was not made.

You could call it a city if cities were allowed to forget the sun and remember only throughput. You could call it a mine if mines were born already refined and still hungry. Mostly it is a region of wet metal and working dark, newly edged, sparsely inhabited by anything that is not process, loud with its own becoming, cold in the way deep water is cold, and open enough that the far end of any vista is only more of the same unfinished, weeping, industrial night.
