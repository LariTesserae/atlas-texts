The lowlands hold a city that never learned dryness.

It sits in a shallow basin where the land forgot how to rise, a broad, open reach of wet stone and drowned foundations stretching farther than sight would normally allow—if sight were the right word here. There is almost no light. What passes for day is a thick, brownish gloom that never quite becomes black and never quite becomes anything else, as if the air itself has been steeped too long. Heat presses from below and from within the masonry, a slow, constant fever. The ground sweats. The walls weep. Water does not fall so much as exude: it beads on every surface, gathers in the carved channels of steps, threads down the faces of towers that lean without quite falling, and stands in still, skin-warm pools across what used to be plazas. The air is heavy with it—condensing, beading on skin that isn’t there, tasting faintly of mineral and old mortar.

Nothing green thrives in any ordinary sense. Vegetation is a rumor of itself: thin, pale threads of something algae-like clinging to the wetter joints, a few brittle stalks of reed or rush that look more mineral than plant, rooted in cracks where the stone has softened. They do not sway; there is little wind. They simply occupy the sparse intervals between one mass of built matter and the next. The city is dense with structure and sparse with presence. Streets open into courts that open into further streets, all of it settled into its own exhaustion—arches slumped, colonnades weathered into ribbed, organic profiles, stairways that dissolve halfway into shallow cascades of warm water. Everything is established. Everything has been wearing itself against the wet and the heat for so long that the erosion has become a kind of balance, a final shape the place intends to keep.

There are no animals. No insects skim the pools. No birds thread the dark. The quiet is nearly total, broken only by the small, persistent sounds water makes when it has nowhere else to go: a drip from a high lintel into a basin three storeys down; the soft collapse of a film of moisture deciding, at last, to run; occasionally a deeper groan as some submerged course of stone shifts a finger’s width and settles again. These sounds do not carry far. The heat and the wet air drink them.

What moves here moves according to rules that have begun to fray.

The city’s plan is almost legible if you do not look too long: wards of low, thick-walled buildings, broader avenues sunk into reflective channels, taller masses that might once have been civic or sacred, their upper reaches lost in the gloom. But the geometry does not stay. Walk a straight colonnade and the intervals between columns lengthen without your having noticed; turn a corner you have turned before and the street has acquired an extra junction, or lost one, or now slopes gently upward into a cul-de-sac of warm standing water that was not there. Interiors leak into exteriors. A doorway opens onto another street that should be a room; a window frames, for a while, a view of the same window from outside, slightly wrong in its proportions. Distances breathe. What feels like a region—town-sized, valley-spanning, multi-featured—can contract, when the attention slips, into a single flooded square that contains, somehow, all the rest.

The heat is not only thermal. It is a pressure of unfinished change. Water climbs walls against any sane gradient, gathers on ceilings, and falls upward in slow, belated drops that reverse themselves halfway and become mist. Stone softens in places into a clay-like yield, then hardens again into forms that remember arches and cornices and the vague suggestion of faces. In the deeper channels, where the water is clearer and hotter, the reflections do not always match what stands above them. Sometimes the reflected towers are taller. Sometimes they are absent. Sometimes they are occupied by drifting pale shapes that have no counterparts in the air—and then the water stills, and the shapes are only lightlessness again.

There is no technology here, no metal that still holds an edge, no tool left that has not become another damp stone among stones. Whatever built this place did so with patience and weight and the logic of load-bearing masses; whatever maintains it now is only the long conversation between water, heat, and time. The processes continue without witnesses: moisture migrating through capillary networks in the masonry; slow chemical softening and re-hardening; the faint, tidal breathing of the entire basin as unseen pressures rise and fall far below. At intervals the gloom thickens further and the water seems to glow with a dull internal sheen, as if remembering light it has never properly owned. Then the sheen passes, and the city resumes its quiet dripping, its almost-imperceptible rearrangements, its settled, feverish patience.

You could walk it for a long time without meeting another living thing. You could stand in one place and feel the street under your feet drift, degree by degree, toward some neighboring alignment it has been approaching for centuries. The place is not ruined so much as completed into a different state of being-city: hot, wet, dark, empty of breath, still organized, still shifting, held in a balance that includes the slow unmaking of ordinary sequence and edge. It does not need you. It has been becoming itself in the dark for longer than the idea of visitors, and it will go on doing so—water rising through stone, distance folding, heat blooming from nowhere—long after any particular description of it has peeled away and run down some leaning wall into the warm black pools.
