The first thing that reaches you is the light. It is hard and white and nearly shadowless for long stretches of the day, pouring out of a sky that feels closer than it should. At this height the air has been thinned on purpose and then thickened again with vapor, so the brightness arrives already pearled, already carrying a faint milkiness. Surfaces dazzle and then soften. Metal catches fire for a moment and goes dull. Leaves shine as if oiled. Everything built has been built to accept this light and return it in slower forms: frosted glass vaults, whitewashed terraces, the pale undersides of enormous louvers that track the sun with the patience of old machinery.

There is no ground that was not poured, riveted, woven, or grown into place. What you walk on is a succession of decks and fill-slabs and compacted artificial soils held in deep iron cribs. The cribs themselves rest on older cribs, and those on cavernous undercrofts where the true mass of the place accumulates—ballast tanks, condensate reservoirs, flywheel halls, the slow-turning roots of elevators. Elevation here is not a feature of landscape; it is the entire premise. The structure rises in long, weathered steps and hanging shelves until the lower world is only a rumor of haze. On the highest working terraces the wind has a thin, continuous voice, and the horizon curves more than flatland eyes expect.

Water moves through the place the way blood moves through an old animal: not abundant, but always present in small necessary quantities. Mornings begin with fog that condenses on every rail and cable, running in bright beads down into gutters no wider than a finger. These gutters feed cisterns shaped like elongated lungs, half-buried among the root floors of the gardens. At irregular intervals the sky opens and a brief, intense rain scours the decks; the rain is sometimes natural weather forced upward by the mass of the structure, sometimes released from suspended collars of netting and pipe that the inhabitants treat as clouds they negotiate with rather than control. Between rains the air stays damp. You taste metal and greenness. Mosses and film algae keep the shaded joints black and slick. In the deeper arcades, droplets fall with the regularity of a slow clock from seams that have been leaking for generations and will leak for generations more. No one seals them. The leaks are part of the hydraulic balance now.

Vegetation has been given almost free rein inside the constructed envelope, and it has answered with density. Trees stand in staggered orchards whose planters are the size of buildings, their roots plunging through grated floors into nutrient galleries below. Vines knit the vertical faces so thoroughly that whole cliffs of riveted plate have become soft, breathing walls of leaf. There are hanging meadows turned on their sides, fern galleries lit by mirrored shafts, fruiting corridors where the air is thick with pollen and the low thunder of bees. The green is not decorative. It is load-bearing in the long accountancy of the place: it holds soil against wind, wrings moisture from fog, feeds the animals, quiets certain frequencies of the engines, and slowly, patiently, digests the older layers of concrete and slag into darker earth. Where iron has rusted for a century, the plants have learned the taste of it; their leaves come out a little redder there, and the insects that graze them carry a faint metallic iridescence.

The industrial skeleton never stops. Deep in the undercrofts, compound engines turn with the unhurried authority of things that have outlived their designers. Some burn residual gases tapped from the composting vaults; others run on pressure stored in mountain-sized accumulators that are charged during the bright months. Belts move. Governors click. Heat is exchanged between water jackets and greenhouse flues so that almost nothing is wasted and almost nothing hurries. The sound is constant and layered: a low chorus of revolving mass, the periodic cough of relief valves, the clatter of sorting works where bulk materials are still separated by shaking screens and magnetic drums, the higher ringing of mill wheels that reduce last decade’s broken decking into aggregate for next decade’s repairs. Above this mechanical bed the living sounds ride—bird shriek, the abrasive cry of some adapted goat-thing moving along a ledge, human voices bounced and clarified by all the hard surfaces, wind picking at every loose edge. Together they make a loudness that is not chaotic. It has settled, over long years, into a single complex tone that residents no longer hear as noise and visitors eventually sleep inside.

Fauna is everywhere and unafraid. Flocks of dark, short-winged birds treat the cable arrays as forest canopy, nesting in the tarred junctions and feeding on the insect bloom that follows every mist. Something like a marmot, but with denser fur and tooling scars on its claws, keeps the drainage slots clear and is considered as much infrastructure as animal. Larger creatures move along the mid-level terraces: pale deer with front limbs elongated for climbing, their antlers grown in deliberate corkscrew forms that do not tangle in the guy-wires; slow armored browsers that graze the vertical meadows and whose shells are colonized by the same mosses that colonize the rails. In the warm condensate tunnels there are pale fish and hearing-blind amphibians that have never known another sky. None of this is wilderness and none of it is captivity. The animals are simply further inhabitants of a made ecology that has had time to become intricately itself. They die and are composted. They are occasionally eaten. They are repaired when the repairs are obvious and left alone when they are not.

The scale is regional rather than infinite. You can walk from the windward escarpments to the leeward settling bowls in a long day if the elevators are kind. Distances are marked by changes in the grain of construction: older brick-and-tie-rod districts give way to poured-basalt cantilevers, then to lighter lattice spans added when the passion for height outstripped the early materials. Whole neighborhoods hang over empty air, tethered and counterweighted, their undersides dripping gardens. In the middle distance you always see more of the same—terraces, stacks, green seams, the pale stroke of a distant rain collar—so the place feels open even while every cubic measure of it is occupied. Density here is vertical and interleaved. Workshops sit under orchards sit under dormitories sit under evaporation fields. People brush past vines, machines, animals, and each other without much room to spare, yet the routes are so thoroughly worn that motion has the quality of circulation inside a living body rather than collision.

Everything shows its age without apology. Handrails are polished to a buttery shine in the centers and rough as coral at the edges. Boltheads have lost their corners. Painted numerals that once numbered construction bays are now half-effaced ghosts beneath lichen. The weathering is not decay; it is the surface expression of a long equilibrium. Loads have found their settled paths. Joints have worked themselves into the exact amounts of play they need. When something finally fails, the failure is usually slow enough that moss has already begun the patch and a maintenance crew arrives more as midwives than surgeons. The prevailing pace is seasonal, yearly, decadal. Crops turn over. Bearing grease darkens and is renewed. A generation of tensioned cables is moused and retired. The bright days lengthen and shorten. Fog seasons come. Nothing is sudden.

There is a mild strangeness that never quite rises to rupture. Shadows sometimes fall in directions that imply a second, lower sun produced by a bank of heliostats no longer on any map. Certain stairwells are warmer than the rooms they serve because an old steam main still passes behind the wall and no one has found a reason to cut it. At the very highest gauges the air tastes faintly of ozone and apricot, an accidental perfume born from the interaction of high-altitude light, lubricating oils, and the night-blooming vines. These are accepted facts, like the loudness and the damp and the way the whole edifice sways a hand’s breadth in the long wind—measurable, survivable, already priced into the way life is lived.

Stay long enough and the place stopped being a structure you occupy and becomes a climate you inhabit. Your clothes take on its permanent humidity. Your sleep aligns to the night cycle of the heavier engines. You learn which railings are safe to trust with your full weight and which ledges the pale deer prefer at dusk. You begin to hear, underneath the obvious industrial chorus, the softer continuous sounds: water finding its next cistern, leaves scissoring against leaves, the almost subsonic flex of kilometer-long spans as they breathe with temperature. The landscape does not ask to be admired. It asks only to be kept in the same slow good repair that it has already, for longer than memory, been keeping itself.
