The depression sits no higher than a deep cellar, a shallow bowl pressed into the plain as if the world has not yet decided whether to heap itself up or hollow itself out. The stone here is still learning its own weight. Fresh basalt ribs push through the surface in slow, unfinished arcs; some are warm to the touch from residual formation heat, others already glazed with the thin skin of frost that forms every night and retreats only partially by day. Between these embryonic ridges the ground is damp rather than wet—condensation that never quite becomes rain, beads that gather on every cold edge and then slide, stop, gather again. Occasional thin mists lift from the cracks and hang without drifting far.

Into this unfinished bowl someone—or something—has packed a city the size of a large churchyard. Structures crowd so tightly that walls share membranes. They rise only three or four human stories, yet they layer inward and sideways until the open air is reduced to narrow vertical shafts and slit courtyards. The architecture does not obey ordinary load-bearing logic. Struts terminate in mid-air and continue as faintly luminous threads that tug at nearby surfaces, pulling stone into temporary cantilevers or folding a corridor through itself so that its far end opens two meters to the left of where geometry insists it should. These are not decorations; they are load-paths written in a physics that the local rock is still trying to accommodate. Where the threads fail, the stone simply refuses to finish crystallizing, remaining a granular slurry that glows a dull porcelain blue and slowly decants itself into new, errant forms.

Light arrives from no single source. It is ordinary daylight strength—no brighter, no dimmer—but it arrives filtered through the density of the place, bouncing off planes that do not quite exist, so that shadows sometimes fall upward or stack three-deep on the same wall. The air itself carries a faint particulate shimmer, nano-scale servants that once maintained the structures and now wander half-forgotten, occasionally stitching a cracked walkway back together or, more often, unstitching a patch of street into a brief, silent aperture that looks onto an identical street two meters lower. These openings seal again after a few minutes, or a few hours; the cycle is patient.

Vegetation is almost absent. In the cold damp, only a thin gray lichen colonizes the rawer stone, and even that grows in reluctant patches. Here and there a single stalk of something like glass reed forces itself between plates of pavement; its Eula is translucent and filled with a slow-moving dark fluid that never freezes. No trees. No moss carpets. The city does not spare the room.

Sound stays near silence. Footfalls—if any—are absorbed by the layered density. The only regular noise is the faint, irregular tick of the stone still settling, and the occasional sigh of moisture releasing from a seam. Once every long while something larger shifts: a wall reconsiders its angle, a cantilever unhooks and rehooks a meter farther on. These events are felt more than heard.

Fauna is rarer still. A single pale arthropod the size of a hand may be seen picking its way along a frost-rimmed ledge, antennae tasting the condensation. It vanishes into a joint between two non-adjacent walls and is not observed again for days. Nothing else moves of its own will.

The entire enclave changes the way glaciers do—too slowly for any one glance to catch, yet unmistakably. Over weeks the raw basalt ribs lengthen by a finger’s width; over months a courtyard slowly rotates a few degrees, carrying its small cargo of lichen and abandoned maintenance threads with it. Reality here is not broken so much as unfinished and over-written at once: the emerging geology and the old hyper-engineering keep trying to finish each other’s sentences, producing a place that is dense, quiet, cold, and continuously, almost politely, coming apart at the seams while remaining perfectly still.

Somewhere near the center, moist air collects against a wall that is half stone and half luminous equation. The condensation runs down, freezes into a narrow hanging blade of ice, and waits.
