The plateau does not end. From any tower or gantry or open gallery you can look out and the layered mass of the city continues until the air itself thickens into haze and the farther districts become only heavier weather. It sits high—high enough that the sky feels closer than the lowlands ever were—and the stone beneath it has been weeping for longer than the maps remember. Water does not fall so much as arrive: it beads on every cold surface, sheets down the flanks of cooling towers, pools in the long-settled depressions between foundries, and rises again as a fine, warm mist that never quite burns off. The light is ordinary daylight, thin and even, diffused by that perpetual damp so that shadows have soft edges and metal gleams without sharp glare.

There are no plants. Nothing green has taken here in ages, if it ever did. The world is mineral and made and animal. Surfaces are basalt, poured slag, riveted plate, brick kiln-fired so many times it has fusion-glazed into dark glass. Where water runs longest the rock has grown soft mineral beards—calcite, rust-bloom, pale sulfur crusts that flake underfoot like old paint. In the deepest sumps a simpler film sometimes spreads: dull, greasy, neither plant nor quite fungus, feeding on warmth and dissolved metal. It is the only softness that is not flesh.

The city is industrial in the old, patient sense. Engines turn on schedules measured in seasons. Pistons the size of houses rise and fall with a sound like distant geology. Rail viaducts braid through the districts at every height, their beds wet and shining, carrying ore, scrap, cooled glass, barreled chemicals, cages. Smokestacks do not so much smoke as exhale: slow, heavy breaths that add to the mist and leave a faint metallic taste. Power is everywhere—open substations humming, cable looms thick as tree trunks (though no one here has seen a tree), dynamos whose housings are warm enough to sleep against. Construction is layered rather than replaced. Newer gantries stand on the cemented ruins of older ones; stairwells spiral around the bones of forgotten presses; entire neighborhoods occupy the cooled interiors of what were once single machines. Everything has been used long enough to look inevitable.

And the animals are everywhere.

They outweigh the people, outnumber the windows, fill every interval the engineers left and many the engineers did not. Great slow herds of hide-bound grazers move along the lowergantry meadows of drip-algae and mineral film, their many-jointed legs clicking on gratework, their breath adding to the wet. Flocks of angular birds (if birds is the word) wheel in the steam above the cooling ponds, diving for the pale crabs that swarm the outfalls. In the walls and the cable races and the hollow legs of towers there are torrents of smaller life: soft-bodied things that sing at dusk, armored millers that click in time with the nearer engines, translucent gliders that ride the updrafts from forge halls, and everywhere the scavenging packs—lean, clever, many-eyed—that know the shift changes better than most workers. At night their eyes turn the mid-level bridges into slow-moving constellations. The air is never empty of wings or calls or the wet slap of something climbing. Ecologies have folded themselves around the machinery until it is no longer clear which is infrastructure and which is habitat. A cooling pipe is also a migratory route. A slag heap is a nesting cliff. The deep warm cisterns under the oldest foundries have become calving grounds for things that never surface, only send their booming, subsonic answers up through the floorplates when the weather turns.

Sound is constant and layered. Under everything runs the industrial bass: turbines, trip-hammers on their weekly cycle, the long groan of ore-lifts. Above it the wet sounds—water sheet-falling from a thousand lips of stone and steel, gutter spill, the soft roar of vapor released. Then the fauna: a dawn screaming of the high flocks, the midday click-and-rustle of the wall-horns, the evening choruses that seem to answer the factory whistles and then continue long after the whistles stop. Voices and bootsteps and the occasional klaxon are only bright threads in a fabric that never rests, though it does change key with the slow weather.

Because everything here is slow. The plateau’s climate belongs to itself: vast enough for its own rotating banks of fog, its own weak pressure systems that take days to cross a single sector. Rust advances by decades. Populations of the larger beasts rise and ebb in generations measured against the maintenance schedules of the great engines. Even catastrophe is patient—an overtopped reservoir will take a month to find its new path through the districts; a failed reactor dome cools over years while the scavenger clades carefully recolonize its gradient of warmth. People move at human speed, but the place they move through does not hurry. Streets are worn into soft troughs. Handrails have the polish of centuries of shoulders. The oldest mafic outcrops, still shedding water, show the same rounded shoulders they did when the first kilns were lit.

At the dripping edge of a mid-level overlook, where a row of loaders stands idle between shifts, you can feel the whole of it at once: the warm wet weight of the air, the low tremble of a distant press-hall through the stone, the near chatter of small claws inside the railing, the white noise of falling water, the sense that past the next array of stacks and the next and the next the same density continues—buildings, beasts, vapor, iron—until distance itself becomes just another saturated layer. The plateau city does not end. It only becomes weather, and the weather is alive.
