The high places drowned long ago, and what remains is neither seafloor nor summit but something that has forgotten the difference.

You float in warm water the color of old iron and bruised peach, pressure steady against the skin like a held breath that never needs releasing. Above is only more of the same: column after column of dim medium, thinning by degrees toward a surface that may or may not still exist. Light arrives as rumor—diffuse, undecided, the memory of day filtered through altitudes of liquid. Below, the world drops away into blue-black gradients that never quite resolve into bottom. Everything here is high. The drowned ridge-lines, the hanging meadows of pale fronds, the slow towers of mineral growth—all of it occupies the altitude once claimed by the tallest ranges, only those ranges have been worn to their oldest, final geometries and then filled.

The rock itself has finished speaking. Erosion wrought its last meaningful change so many cycles ago that the shapes feel less geological than architectural, as if the planet settled deliberate into this posture and has no further amendments. Broad, flattened tables tilt at gentle angles. Spines of basalt run for continental distances, smoothed into continuous curves that catch the dimness and hold it. Canyons have become long trenches of clearer water where denser cold drifts downward in sluggish rivers. Everything has been rounded, thinned, patiently emptied of sharp edges until only silhouette and mass remain. The place feels post-cellular, as though even the mountains have reached senescence and are content simply to endure.

Through this final topography the vegetation thrives without urgency. Forests of translucent crystal-leaf rise from every shoulder and saddle, their stalks thick as ancient trunks, branching into lattices that interlock overhead until the water itself becomes a second canopy. Softer mats of velvet-green and copper filament carpet the gentler slopes, dense enough that swimming through them is like pushing aside living drapery. Some growths drip strings of pearlescent bladders that slowly inflate and rise, carrying dustings of mineral spore toward the higher gloom. Others pulse with barely perceptible peristalsis, churning nutrients through capillary networks thick enough to feel like weather when you pass near. The denseness is constant: every cubic body of water is threaded, layered, almost saturated with living material. You rarely touch rock without first brushing frond or membrane. Yet true animals remain rare. Once in a long while a shadow larger than architecture glides at the edge of vision—some leviathan of the upper strata, gelatinous and slow, trailing curtains of its own internal light that extinguish before they can be named. Smaller things exist as suggestions: a flicker of silver needle through a thicket, the brief eclipse of one frond by another that should not move. Mostly the world is alone with its plants and its water.

Silence is the default medium. Currents speak in low, continuous hushes that register more in the bones than the ears. When a great frond-forest sways it produces a sound like cloth drawn across distant stone, almost apologetic. Bubbles—rare, large, slow—ascend with a single soft syllable and vanish into the overhead dusk. Nothing cries out. Nothing rings or clatters. The place has no harsh edges left with which to make louder noises.

And yet it is profoundly active. Thermal lenses of warmer water bloom and collapse on timescales of hours, reshaping visibility the way weather fronts once rearranged skies. Forests cycle through phases of expansion and retraction; one decade a ridge may be entirely screened in rose-gold curtains, the next the same stone stands nearly bare while the growth has migrated along a slow chemical gradient miles distant. Mineral towers lengthen by basalt breath, shedding dust that the plants drink. Somewhere far off, pressure shelves fail and entire plateaus tilt a fraction of a degree, sending long, almost solid waves rolling through the drowned highlands—waves that take days to pass, bending every stem in the same direction before the water remembers stillness again. The ecology is not frantic; it is tectonic in patience and continental in consequence.

Scattered through all of this, almost erased by the same eons, are the faint marks of something that once built. A ring of fitted stones on a level saddle, mortar long dissolved, the circle now a perfect home for a single immense crystal-leaf that has grown through its center. A stair cut into a cliff face that continues down into darkness and up into gloom, its treads softened until they feel accidental. An arch of fused glass half-sunk in a hanging meadow, still faintly prismatic when the dimness catches it just so. Nothing more. No machines, no tools, no lingering spark of artifice. Whatever intelligence made these things has been fully composted back into the water and the stone. The marks survive only as the mountains survive: final, worn smooth of purpose, allowed to remain because removal would require more effort than the world still possesses.

Reality, here, is no longer reliable. The weirdness is not decorative; it is structural. Distances refuse to add correctly. Swim toward a recognizable ridge for what feels like an afternoon and find yourself suddenly above it, looking down through clear league-deep water at the place you meant to reach, while the ridge itself has elongated into a different range entirely. Sometimes the water darkens into vertical walls that have thickness and grain, like sheets of packed rain frozen mid-fall; pass through one and the temperature shifts a full degree, the vegetation changes species, and the faint light arrives from a new direction. Memory leaks. You may surface—if surfacing is still a concept—into an air that tastes of altitude and find yourself still submerged, lungs working liquid without complaint. Time pools in the deeper trenches; an hour spent drifting there returns you to forests that have advanced a full season. Occasionally the entire medium flickers, as though the idea of “water” is briefly withdrawn and something older, more viscous, more aware, presses in its place before the ordinary liquid reasserts itself with a feeling like shame. The oldest stones sometimes show dry faces for minutes at a time—sunlight that cannot exist, wind that has nowhere to come from—then the dream collapses and the warm dark closes again.

This is the place after the last shaping has finished and the ocean decided height was better occupied than abandoned. A vast, warm, dim solemnity of drowned summits still busy with their quiet hungers, their slow rearrangements, their occasional failures of continuity. The water holds everything, including the possibility that holding is only temporary still.
