The valley floor has not finished deciding what it is. It lies low and broad, a shallow dish of land recently pressed flat by forces that left the rock still angular and raw. Basalt shoulders rise in broken steps, their edges unsoftened, weeping thin sheets of meltwater that never quite freeze. The cold is persistent and wet: a dense, luminous chill that soaks through everything and never lifts. Rain falls more often than not—fine, tonal rain that turns to mist the moment it meets the ground. Where the water gathers, the soil gives way to black peat and standing pools skinned with ice so thin it rings like glass underfoot.

Vegetation thrives in the cold damp with a kind of restrained fury. Mosses carpet every horizontal surface in layered greens so deep they look black in the gloom. Ferns uncurl in wet crowns the size of wagons. Stands of pale, dense-wooded trees crowd together, their trunks furred with lichen that drips continuously. Between them the understory is a saturated tangle of horsetails, pitcher-leaved plants, and mats of spongy red sphagnum that rise and fall with the water table like breathing. Nothing here is sparse. Growth presses against growth until the air itself feels filtered through chlorophyll and rot.

Light arrives late and leaves early, or perhaps never fully arrives. Cloud sits on the valley like a low ceiling, thick enough to turn noon into a dim blue-gray. Most of what can be seen is seen by spill and reflection: the dull gleam of wet leaves, the colder gleam of standing water, occasional pulses of pale light from things that are not vegetation.

The sound never drops. Water moves everywhere—seeping, sheet-flowing, dropping from ledges in sudden white braids that smash into basins and keep smashing. Wind funnels between the sharp young ridges and tears at the canopy in long, rolling howls. Under and through it all runs a deeper vibration, almost tactile: a continuous low roar that seems to come from the ground itself, punctuated by sharper tearings and concussions as something large shifts or collapses or is remade. Birds and other voices add their own overlapping layers—harsh metallic cries, wet coughs, and long sliding calls that carry surprisingly far in the dense air. Standing still for more than a few moments makes the mix feel physical, a pressure against the eardrums and sternum.

Animals are present in numbers that match the plants. Dark-furred forms thread the moss at speed, never quite visible as whole bodies. Clouds of midges and larger glittering insects rise in vertical columns wherever the rain thins for a minute. Amphibian shapes—some the size of dogs—hang half-submerged in the colder pools, throats working. Higher up, between the pale trunks, things with too many joints move in brief, decisive bursts and then freeze again. Their collective motion keeps the undergrowth in a state of constant low-level thrashing. Nothing here is sparse.

Here and there the living density parts around manufactured things. They are few and incomplete. A rib of matte black alloy juts from a peat bank at an angle that suggests it was driven rather than placed; thin films of ice continually form and shatter across its surface. Further on, a cluster of open frames stands knee-deep in a marsh, each frame filled with a slow-turning lattice of light that does not illuminate anything around it. The lattices reconfigure without visible joints, folding through geometries that leave brief afterimages in the eye. Once every few hours one of the frames unfolds entirely into a temporary pavilion of translucent planes, holds that shape against the wind for a short time, then collapses again into a humming triangle no taller than a person. Elsewhere, hair-thin filaments hang between trees like abandoned spider silk; when the wind loads them they sing at the upper edge of hearing and the nearest pools spontaneously climb their banks in thin, obedient sheets before slumping back. None of these objects appear maintained. They simply continue, indifferent to the moss taking slow hold on their cooler surfaces.

The valley is large enough that its far walls are only suggestions in the murk—soft horizontal bands that might be cliffs or merely thicker weather. Between here and there the same saturated, shouting landscape repeats and varies: new channels cutting themselves overnight through the peat, whole thickets collapsing under sudden water weight, ridges calving small landslides of fresh angular stone that the moss begins to claim before the stone has finished settling. The advanced remnants do not dominate. They punctuate. Their changes are simply another layer in the larger turbulence, no stranger than a flash flood or a fouled nesting colony, just operating by rules the cold wet dark does not bother to explain.

Everything here is overcrowded, lit poorly, and in motion. The air tastes of iron, leaf-melt, and the faint ozone tang that follows thoroughly unearthly discharges. If you stay long enough the deafening resolution of water, wind, and machine begins to stratify into separate weathers you can walk between. None of them are quiet.
