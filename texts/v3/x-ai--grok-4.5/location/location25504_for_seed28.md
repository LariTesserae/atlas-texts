The pocket opens only when you are already inside it.

From the mineral flats that stretch out forever under a white, unrelenting sky, there is nothing to mark the place—an almost imperceptible sag in the salt crust, a faint change in the way heat shimmers. One more step and the ground drops away in steps so shallow they feel accidental. You descend through a throat of weathered stone and find yourself standing on the floor of something that was never meant to be found: a city folded into a depression smaller than a lake bed, every surface the color of old bone and dried clay.

There is no water here. There has not been water in living memory, nor in the slower memory of the stones. The air tastes of lime and superheated dust. Yet the city is lush. Between the buildings grow forests of tall, ribbed columns the color of tarnished copper and young pistachios—plants that have long since abandoned any need for liquid. Their skins are thick as leather, segmented, faintly translucent when the light hits them hard. They drink the heat itself, and the mineral vapor that rises from the salt in the afternoons. In places they stand so densely that the streets become green-gold tunnels, fronds overlapping, rubbing, shedding a fine dry pollen that never settles. Elsewhere they thin to solitary giants rooted in the cracked plazas, their crowns crowding the sky like a second architecture.

The buildings are few and far apart. This is a city of emptiness measured carefully. Wide courts open between free-standing walls; single towers stand alone like markers left for some vanished procession. Everything is hand-made, ancient, fitted without metal: blocks of the local pale conglomerate, dressed with stone tools and set with a mortar of burned shell and dust that has since grown harder than the stone. Doorways are low and lintels heavy. Staircases climb exterior faces and end in platforms that overlook nothing but more emptiness. The whole settlement feels both intimate and hollow—rooms the size of a body, then sudden voids big enough to lose sound in, then another quiet chamber full of leaf-shadow.

Light never softens. It comes straight down and bounces off every plane of salt and worked stone until the air itself seems luminous. Shadows exist only as thin, vibrating edges. You squint even under the densest fronds. The heat is steady, thick, the temperature of a body that has been running. Nothing cools. Nothing freezes. The place simply holds its warmth the way a kiln holds heat long after the fire is gone.

And the sound. The sound is the one true weather.

Wind funnels into the pocket from the dead flats above and becomes something else—something continuous and physical. It tears through the blade-gaps of the vegetation with a high, tearing shriek. It races along the empty avenues and finds every arch, every unfinished arcade, every freestanding wall and sets them resonating like enormous dry instruments. The combined roar is absolute. You feel it in the teeth and the joints. Conversation is impossible; thought itself arrives already half-shredded. Sometimes the wind drops for three heartbeats and the silence that replaces it is worse—an abrupt absence that makes the ears ring as if they have been struck. Then the howl returns, louder for having been away.

There is almost no animal life. Once in a long while a pale, hard-shelled something the size of a thumb scuttles from one cracked foundation to another and vanishes. Wings are rarer still: a single dark shape turning high in the white glare, then gone. The ecology that matters here is slower. Dust settles into the grooves of carvings and is scraped clean again by the next gale. The coppery forests lean a fraction more westward each season. Walls lose a grain at a time and yet keep their shape—an old equilibrium of erosion and stubborn mass. The city has been finished, abandoned by whatever built it, and then lived in again by the wind and the heat-drinking plants and the rare, secretive fauna until all four parties reached an understanding. Nothing is ruined. Nothing is new. Everything is simply in its long middle.

At the very center of the depression stands a low circular structure open to the sky, its floor a perfect mosaic of dark and light stone laid in spirals. No one knows what it was for. The wind plays it differently from everything else—less a howl than a deep, fluctuating chord that you feel more than hear. Around its rim the strange vegetation grows in a perfect ring, denser and taller, as if listening.

You can walk the entire city in an hour if the wind allows. You can also spend days moving from one shaded hollow to the next, never seeing another person, never finding a well or a cistern or even a stain of damp, yet never doubting that this was, and somehow still is, a place of organized dwelling. The scale is pocket. The ambition was urban. The contradiction is held open by brightness, by heat, by the deafening continuous voice of air moving through settled form.

The flats above remain empty and silent. Down here the world insists.
