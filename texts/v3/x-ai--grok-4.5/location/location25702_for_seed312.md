The plateau does not so much rise as simply refuse to end. From any vantage—perched on a knife-edge of basalt that still remembers the uplift, or standing in the narrow throat of a street—the eye travels until perspective itself grows tired. Continents of stone have been folded upward into a single cold tableland, its edges scalloped by fretted ranges that look unfinished, their arêtes sharp enough to cut cloud, their cirques retaining the blue ghosts of glaciers that have only lately begun to recede. Everything feels recent: talus slopes still angular, fault scarps unsoftened, the very air tasting of crushed mineral.

Cold is the medium. It does not arrive in fronts; it simply is, a permanent low register that keeps water in a state of intermittent mercy. Moisture appears as rime on the windward faces of walls, as thin veins of ice that weep and refreeze daily in the shadowed joints of masonry, as sudden local fogs that condense out of clear air when a column of breath or a herd’s exhalation meets the right temperature. True rain is rare and ceremonial—brief silver hours that darken the stone and send meltwater stuttering down steep channels into cisterns older than memory. Between these events the land is merely damp: a film, a seepage, a promise kept only in pockets.

Vegetation has learned sparseness as a morality. Lichens map the rock in slow metallics—gunmetal, rust, pale sulfur. In sheltered crevices, cushion plants form tight green fists no larger than a fist; higher still, only dry filamentous algae blacken the lee of boulders. Trees are rumors told by traders from lower escarpments. What grows does so by patience measured in centuries, and the people treat every green scrap as both sacrament and scarce timber.

Yet the plateau is dense beyond ordinary reckoning. Cities do not sit upon the land so much as constitute it. For hundreds of leagues the stone has been worked into continuous habitation: stepped terraces stacked until they become artificial mountains, alley-canyons plunging ten and twenty stories between blank walls, bridges of single shell-limestone slabs thrown across gulfs of air. The architecture is cyclopean and hand-hewn—megaliths levered into place with timber cribs and raw muscle, surfaces still bearing the quarry scars and the shallow dish-marks of stone hammers. Doorways are low, lintels megalithic, interiors lit by seal-oil lamps and the cold vertical light that pours through unglazed slits. There is no metal beyond occasional cold-hammered native copper used for ritual edges; tools are flint, bone, hardened wood, and the patient abrasion of sand. Everything smells of cold rock dust, tallow, and the clean mineral tang of uncounted generations of foot traffic polishing the same thresholds.

Fauna thrives in the interstices. The streets and ledges throng with life that has never learned to fear the builders. Thick-coated caprids pick their way along cornices, browsing lichen. Packs of low, pale canids trot through the lower markets as if they own the right-of-way, which in a sense they do. Above, immense slow birds with frost-colored primaries ride the weak thermals between towers, occasionally folding and dropping to snatch a scrap or a careless pup. In the deeper galleries, bioluminescent insects—kept in woven cages—serve as living lamps; outside, clouds of midges rise in the brief midday warming and are harvested with sticky poles. Domesticated herds of shaggy bovids are driven along spiral ramps from byre to high pasture and back again; their breath adds to the city’s perpetual low fog. No one finds this crowding remarkable. Space is vertical and shared; the animals are citizens of a slower polity.

Sound is discouraged by the cold and by custom. Voices carry only a few paces before the dense air and the endless stone swallow them. What remains is a textured quiet: the tick of cooling rock, the distant clack of a flint knapper, the soft thunder of a thousand hooves far below on a switchback, the almost subsonic groan of a megalith settling another fraction of a degree after centuries. Wind is the loudest citizen, and even wind speaks mostly in whispers along the serrated ridgelines, rising to a hollow flute only when it finds a perfect alignment of towers.

Time itself moves like cold honey. Seasons are debates rather than revolutions; a “year” is counted by the slow creep of a particular ice veneer across a sacred pavement, or by the incremental lean of a leaning menhir that three generations have watched. Buildings are never finished, only paused. A wall begun by a great-grandmother may receive its capstone from a great-granddaughter. Erosion, though geologically young and still spectacular in the outer ranges, has been drafted into the civic aesthetic: fresh rockfalls become foundations overnight, scarps are anderscored newly and claimed as grand staircases. The city grows by accretion and patience, the same way a glacier advances—imperceptible except in the long memory of stone.

At certain hours the normal light turns architectural. Low sun rakes across the plateau and every raised edge, every hammer-dressing mark, every caprid path worn into a ledge, throws a blue shadow so precise it feels drawn by a god with a ruling pen. In that light the entire vast urban lithosphere looks less like a place people built and more like a single organism of rock and furs and quiet breathing that has been rising, folding, and densifying since the world was sharp and new.
