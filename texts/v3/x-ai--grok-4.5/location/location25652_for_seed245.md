The basin has no horizon that holds. What looks at first like a far ridge of unfinished walls is only more of the same plain continuing, the constructions thinning into haze and then thickening again farther on, as if the world were tiled with the same incomplete thought repeated at continental scale. Everything underfoot and everything rising is made. There is no bedrock that was not set in place, no soil that was not mixed and poured, no slope that was not banked by hands or by the simple levers and drag-sledges whose broken remains still litter the intervals. The material is mostly a dense, dark ceramic thrice-fired and still warm to the touch, interleaved with slabs of something like slate that weeps constantly, and with long courses of raw clay that have not yet been persuaded to hold their shape. The heat is a thick medium of its own. It rises from the ceramic as if the last firing never quite ended, and it meets the wetness in a permanent low fog that smells of minerals and old smoke. Water does not puddle so much as exude. It beads on every vertical face, runs in thin sheets down the inner surfaces of half-built channels, collects in the shallow rectilinear depressions that may one day be cisterns or may simply be the memory of cisterns that were abandoned mid-gesture. The air itself condenses on cooler edges and drips with a sound so soft it is almost not sound.

There is no green. No scavenger lichen, no film of algae in the wet corners, no root, spore, or seed. The only growth is mineral and architectural: salt rimes that advance overnight across freshly laid courses, and the slow blooming of fracture patterns in clay that has been hastily smoothed. What stands is sparse. Between the low platforms, the staggered walls, the open grids of foundation trenches, lie broad vacancies of level ceramic plain, heat-shimmered and filmed with water, empty enough that walking them feels like crossing a floor prepared for a ceremony that never begins. The light is dim without source, a brownish twilight that seems to well up from the wet surfaces rather than fall upon them. Shadows are short and indecisive. In that dimness the scale becomes difficult to trust. A wall that appears close may take an hour to reach; a distant scaffold of lashed poles and raw plank may suddenly be under your hands, the wood still smelling of whatever un-tree was shaped into it by adze and fire-hardening.

The making is still underway, and the making is turbulent. Courses of brick lengthen while you watch, not by visible workers (there are none, and never were) but by a creeping accretion, clay pressing itself into the next logical brick-shape with the patience of a tide and the suddenness of a muscle spasm. Elsewhere a finished colonnade will soften, slump, and run like wax, only to be caught by some internal grammar and re-erected a few degrees off true, its new proportions slightly wrong in a way that hurts the eye. Perspective buckles. Parallel channels diverge and then, impossibly, reconverge without having curved. A doorway set into a low wall frames, for a moment, a vertical drop into stacked foundations that should be beneath you; then the view snaps back to ordinary shallow distance. These deviations are quiet. The loudest sounds are the occasional sharp crack of ceramic deciding on a new fault, the prolonged trickle of water finding a fresh path, and the faint, continuous murmur of clay grains rearranging. No wind. No voices. No wings or feet.

The rules here are provisional. Load-bearing is a suggestion; some freestanding arches hang open at the bottom as if gravity were optional inside their curve. Time is lumpy: a wet handprint left on a slab may dry instantly into a glazed fossil, or remain soft and accepting for days. The primitive tools that litter the vacancies—stone pounders, wooden straight-edges warped by moisture, fiber ropes stiff with dried slurry—sometimes twitch and complete a half-finished joint without being held. It is as if the entire basin is a single artifact still being argued into existence by an intelligence that knows only hand-work and has no interest in living things. The heat never relents. The water never ceases to weep from the made stone. And far out, where the constructions thin again into another vacancy, you can watch an entire district of low walls rise, waver, invert their own facades, and settle into a new incomplete arrangement, all in perfect, dim, mineral silence.
