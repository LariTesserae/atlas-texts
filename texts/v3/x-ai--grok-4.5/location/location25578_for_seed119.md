The place is a basin, and the basin is all there is.

It sits no higher than a river’s first terrace, a low leaf-cup of the world, and every surface of it was laid by hands. There is no bedrock showing, no drift of natural gravel, no root-web that was not later pressed into mortar. The floor is a slow mosaic of river cobbles set on edge, their long axes vertical so that centuries of bare feet and weather have worn them into a field of blunt, greying teeth. Between the teeth the joints weep. Water does not so much flow as appear—beading out of the oldest mortar, thread-thin, then gathering in the shallow hexagonal troughs that someone once carved as drains and that now serve as lazy canals. The air itself condenses: cool enough that breath stays visible, wet enough that stone darkens simply by existing. A fine vertical rain is always either falling or about to fall; you hear it decide.

The walls are close. This is not a plain or a valley; it is a single aedicule, cathedral-scaled only in the sense that a hollowed choir is scaled to its vault. You could shout across it and be answered by the wet slap of your own voice returning from the opposite drum-wall in under two heartbeats. Those walls rise in battered courses of cyclopean blocks, each block scarred with the shallow, rhythmic bite-marks of stone chisels that never knew iron—dolerite pounders, antler picks, sand and patience. The upper courses lean inward slightly, as if the whole basin were once meant to be roofed or netted. Whatever covered it is gone. What remains is open to a sky the color of wet slate, dim as the light under a table.

Vegetation is an afterthought the place has almost rejected. Here and there a cushion of dark moss has found purchase in a weep-joint; a single willow sapling, no thicker than a wrist, leans out of a broken gutter like a failed spar. Their roots do not dig into earth—they thread the rubble core of the walls themselves, mining the ancient clay binder. The green is sparse enough that you notice every frond.

Yet the air and the water throng. Fauna here is slightly wrong in the way of things that have had a long time to forget what they were supposed to be. Pale amphipods stitching themselves across the surface film in such density that the canals look carpeted. Frogs the translucent grey of spit, throats going like bellows, their chorus a continuous, wet snare-drum rolled against the stone. Above them wheel midges in towering, soft columns that lean when the air currents shift. Something larger keeps to the shadowed porticoes that open at irregular heights in the walls—leather-winged, dog-sized, hanging head-down like damp laundry until they drop, glide, and snap something from the water with a sound like a whip cracking underwater. Their guano streaks the blocks in white, acidic tears that have already softened the chisel marks into something almost organic. The whole basin breathes with them: wingbeats, wet gulps, the high electric sizzle of stridulating insects that never learned to stop. Sound does not echo so much as crowd; there is very little empty air left for silence to occupy.

The light is the color of standing water under cloud. It enters from above and is immediately broken by the constant fine rain, so that every surface shines as if oiled and none of the shadows have hard edges. In that dimness the architecture reveals its older intentions only slowly. Some of the standing slabs are pierced by circles the size of a face, edges polished by something that once passed through them over and over. Low benches—or altars, or drying racks—ring the central depression in perfect concentric arcs, their tops dished by wear. In two places the cobble floor buckles upward in long, gentle ridges, as though enormous roots once pushed from below and then withdrew, leaving the pavement permanently flexed. Nothing grows from those ridges now; they are simply part of the settled shape of the place, weathered into balance with everything else.

Water levels change without warning. A hidden mouth somewhere in the lower courses will cough a sudden fresh lens of cold water that lifts the entire surface a hand-span, setting every amphibian thrashing and every midge column collapsing into temporary chaos. Then the excess finds the old drains and the level eases again, leaving new constellations of debris: shed larval skins, a single iridescent feather, a chip of shell that looks, if you tilt it in the dimness, almost like a worked bead. The basin is never still. Processes overlap—seepage, chorus, wing, the slow spalling of freeze-scared stone, the finer ticking of water beads finding one another and merging. It is an ecosystem that has forgotten it was built, or a machine that has forgotten it was not alive, and the difference has long since ceased to matter inside these leaning walls.
