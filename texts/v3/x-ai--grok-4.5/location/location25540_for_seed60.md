The place is a hollow no wider than a long hall and no longer than the nave of an old church, sunken a few meters below the surrounding flats. You step down into it and the world contracts. Walls of living tissue close in from every side, not stone or earth but a continuous thickness of leaf, stem, root, and aerial rhizome packed so tightly that air itself seems to have to shoulder through. There is almost no bare ground. What used to be soil has long since been replaced by a compressed matte of rhizomes and fungal bras urged into load-bearing have been coaxed into load-bearing arches and vaults by centuries of patient pressure. The whole basin is plant architecture: trunks fused into buttresses, curtains of aerial roots hanging like cables, massive interlocking canopies that form a low, irregular roof. Light arrives as ordinary daylight, filtered and greenish, never dim enough to feel subterranean and never bright enough to cast hard edges.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a river. It beads on every surface, wells from pores in the bark, sheets slowly down the insides of hollow stems, and pools in the cupped bases of enormous leaves that serve as cisterns. The air is permanently post-rain: warm, heavy, tasting of wet iron and sap. Rocks—scarce, low outcrops of something dark and fine-grained—weep steadily, their faces glazed with mineral crusts that plants have colonized into vertical gardens. Moisture condenses on the cooler undersides of metal remnants and drips in slow, synchronous rhythms. The entire hollow sits near the water table; press a hand into the living floor and it answers with a soft, delayed surge.

Scattered through this green density are the industrial ghosts. A single vertical boiler, brick half-swallowed by cambium, still faintly warm. A run of cast-iron pipe that has become the central rib of a corkscrewing liana. Gear trains immobilized mid-mesh, their teeth packed with moss that has taken the exact negative shape of the cogs. A small turbine housing lies on its side like a fallen drum; vines have entered its intake and exited its outlet, turning the machine into a living conduit. Nothing here looks recently abandoned. The metal is deeply weathered, pitted, friable in places, yet held in permanent tension by the plants that both crush and cradle it. The relationship is old enough to have reached a kind of equilibrium: the machinery no longer functions as intended, but neither has it been allowed to vanish. Slow hydraulic cycles inside the living mass still turn a few free wheels at a barely perceptible rate. Once every few hours a valve somewhere coughs a gout of warm, mineral-stained water that cascades down a leaf-stair and is immediately reabsorbed.

Sound is louder than the scale should allow. Water never stops moving: it drums on taut leaf membranes, gurgles through tubular stems of different diameters (each pitch distinct), and occasionally finds an old pressure line and produces a brief, shocking clang that resonates through the metal-and-wood skeleton of the place. The vegetation itself is far from silent. Growth is audible if you stand still—fibers creaking as they thicken by microscopic increments, aerial roots scraping as they adjust their grip, a low continuous susurrus of leaves adjusting to the slow redistribution of weight. When wind finds the hollow it does not so much blow as press; the packed canopy transmits the pressure as a deep, organ-like note that builds and fades over minutes. Underneath everything runs a patient mechanical undertone, the last residual rotation of shafts and the soft chuffing of some partially biological engine that has long since forgotten what it was meant to drive.

Fauna is almost absent. Once in a long while something small and dark—part insect, part wet leaf—scuttles across a stem and vanishes. No birds. No persistent mammals. The ecological space is already fully claimed by the vegetal mass; there is simply no unoccupied niche left for larger creatures to occupy for long.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It accumulates. Stems that terminate in perfectly circular flanges as if flanged for bolting. Leaves whose veins have arranged themselves into the ghost pattern of old electrical schematics. A cluster of seed pods that open and close with the same slow period as a nearby piston that no longer moves. Time feels viscous. Change happens, but only across durations measured in seasons or decades: a pipe gradually lifted a handspan higher by the tree that has adopted it; a new vault forming where three trunks have decided, over half a century, to lean together. The hollow does not expand or contract. It simply continues, dense, wet, warm, and quietly loud, an established pocket of plant-industrial life that has forgotten any purpose beyond its own saturated persistence.

Nothing here hurries. The place has already arrived at the only form it intends to keep.
