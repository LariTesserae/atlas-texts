The pocket sits in a shallow bowl of land so slight you could miss it from a hundred paces, a depression worn smooth by something older than weather. From outside it is only a rise of pale stone shoulders and a thickening of green that does not belong to the colder, barer country around it. Step between the shoulders and the world collapses inward to room-scale: a contained settlement no larger than a generous courtyard, yet so densely stacked and terraced and undercut that it feels like a town folded into a single breath.

Everything here is cold the way stone after frost is cold, a deep mineral chill that never quite leaves the fingertips. Moisture does not fall so much as cling. It beads on every surface, threads down the gutters cut into the rock steps, gathers in shallow stone basins that have been scooped and rescooped until their rims are soft as bone. There is no river, only a slow numbers of seeps that darken the lower walls and keep the air tasting of wet iron and leaf mold. On the brightest days a fine condensation fogs the narrowest alleys and then thins again, never quite rain, never quite dry.

The light is relentless and shadowless, a high white clarity that seems to come from the stone itself as much as from any sky. The buildings are cut from the same pale, close-grained rock that forms the bowl; over ages they have been worn into one another so that wall becomes stair becomes roof becomes wall again. Floors are polished by use into shallow concavities. Lintels sag in long, graceful arcs. Every edge has forgotten it was ever sharp. This is not ruin. It is finishedness. The place has reached the shape it intends to keep, and the damp and the cold and the slow traffic of feet have only deepened the polish.

Vegetation occupies every unclaimed plane with the frantic calm of things that know they will never be cleared. Thick mats of moss in black-green and bronze spill from ledges and cushion the steps. Ferns of a dozen tiny species pack the vertical joints between stones, their fronds permanently beaded. Pale lichens map the higher walls in fractal continents. In the coldest angles, translucent ice-leaf plants hold their own small reservoirs of water that never freeze solid, only thicken. Roots works into mortar that was never more than packed clay and grit; the buildings and the green have become a single load-bearing tissue. There is almost no bare stone left at walking height. Where a handrail was once carved, moss has made a softer, rounder rail. Where a doorway narrows, the living frame narrows with it.

The density is total. Rooms open into other rooms with no corridor to spare. Ladders of lashed wood and cut footholds stitch the levels together so tightly that moving through the settlement is less like walking a street than like circulating inside a single intricate lung. Storage niches, sleeping shelves, work-platforms, cistern lips—all of it abuts. People here (and they are few, quiet, long-practiced) move with the economy of those who have never known empty space. Their tools are hand-scale and older than memory: antler wedges, greenstone adzes still painstakingly resharpened, bone needles, cordage of beaten inner bark, simple copper needles and fishhooks no larger than a fingernail that are mended rather than replaced. Every object has the dull gleam of infinite reuse. Nothing is discarded; there is nowhere to discard it to.

Animals treat the architecture as just another cliff face of unusually regular ledges. Small cold-climate rodents with frost-tipped fur run the moss highways. Bright-eyed birds the size of thumbs nest in the densest fern pockets and make almost no sound beyond the dry tick of claws on stone. Slow, narrowly flat frogs occupy the permanent damp at the lowest basins, their skin the exact mottled grey-green of wet rock. In warmer crevices (warmer only by comparison) beetles with translucent wing-cases graze the algae films. Nothing large. Nothing loud. The whole fauna has edited itself down to fit the available seams, and the seams are full.

Sound itself seems reluctant. A single drip falling into a basin three levels down is an event that carries. Cloth shifts. A foot finds a familiar wear-hollow. Once in a long while someone draws a blade of grass across a stone to test its edge, and the note is pure and brief and immediately absorbed. The bright air holds no wind. The temperature does not swing. Even the condensation beads form and slide and reform in the same slow rhythms they have always used. Time here is not stopped so much as already completed; the settlement has arrived at an equilibrium so exact that change would require more force than the place contains.

There is a mild strangeness under the clarity. The light never angles. Shadows, when they appear at all, are only denser greens. Paths that should lead outward curve gently back into the same crowded center, as if the bowl itself rejects the idea of elsewhere. You can stand in the highest moss terrace, look up, and see ordinary cold sky; yet the longer you remain, the less that sky seems connected to any larger weather. The pocket has its own closed meteorology of damp and chill and brilliance, self-sustaining, politely indifferent to whatever season is occurring beyond the rim.

At the exact lowest point sits a circular basin no wider than an outstretched arm, cut so smoothly it might be natural. It is always half-full of water so clear it is invisible except by the meniscus and the occasional drifting frond. People habitually touch two fingers to it when they pass—greeting, measurement, or simply the confirmation that the level remains what it has always been. Around the basin the moss is worn to a dark, resilient felt by centuries of kneeling. Everything in the pocket slopes, ultimately, here. Everything returns.

This is the whole of it: a cold green city the size of a secret, saturated with quiet life, weathered into perfect fit with itself, brightly lit by a light that does not travel, damp without flood, ancient without decay, and sealed inside its own unchanging hour.
