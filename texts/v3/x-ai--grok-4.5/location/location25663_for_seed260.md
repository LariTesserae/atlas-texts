The high places do not finish themselves.

You stand on a spine of raw basalt that has not yet decided whether it is a ridge or a wall. The stone is still warm in places from its own birth, cool only where the dim light has been touching it for centuries. Elevation here is not a measurement but a condition: the ground simply continues upward in slow, monumental shoulders until the air thins into something like memory. There is no true summit. The land keeps offering higher shelves, each one sparser than the last, each one still shedding flakes of itself as if the mountain is learning how to stand.

Water is a rumor. The rock is dry enough to drink the spit from your tongue. What little moisture exists has been stolen and locked into the vegetation, which grows anyway—against chemistry, against reason. In the broad, open basins between the rising spines, forests of something like glass-bamboo and felted blue-black fronds rise in scattered stands. They are lush where they stand: thick-boled, heavy-crowned, dripping with secondary growths that look like frozen smoke or crystallized lichen. Between the stands lie long empty reaches of mineral grit and salt-rime pans that crack underfoot with a sound like distant bones. The plants do not drink. They seem to exhale a faint blue vapor that never quite becomes mist, and the vapor is what the fronds feed on. Their roots grip unfinished stone and pull it into new shapes over decades. You can watch a root system slowly drag a boulder across a slope if you are patient enough; the movement is the same speed as grief.

Light arrives already tired. It seeps rather than falls—a low, pewter radiance that never declares a source. Shadows have soft edges and sometimes point slightly wrong. In that dimness the glass-bamboo catches what there is and returns it as a dull internal glow, so each stand looks like a drowned lantern forest. The air is cool enough to keep breath visible, a pale ribbon that frays quickly into the general silence that is not silence.

The sound is absolute.

It is not wind, though wind exists here as a slow, thick river of cold. It is not stonefall, though the mountains sometimes shrug whole flanks of themselves loose in recipes of collapse that take years to complete. The sound is the grinding of the place against its own possibility. Reality here is thin enough to rub. Edges do not stay edges. A ridge you walked an hour ago may now fold behind itself like cloth. Distances breathe. You hear the mountains rehearsing different versions of their own silhouettes—deep subsonic chords that rise into a continuous, bone-vibrating roar. It never stops. It is deafening in the way a waterfall is deafening, except the water is absent and the fall is conceptual, endless, occurring in every direction at once. After a time the roar becomes a kind of pressure behind the eyes, a second heartbeat made of crumbling axioms.

There are no animals. Nothing runs, crawls, or calls. The ecology is only rock, thin air, the stubborn glass-and-felt forests, and the slow architectural suggestions left by whoever comes here and then leaves again. Scattered across the broad shelves and the shoulders of the unformed peaks are marks of early hands: cairns of deliberately stacked raw plates, low dry-stone windbreaks no higher than the waist, spiral paths scraped into grit that seem to lead both uphill and inward at the same time. Some of the cairns have tools nested in them—blanks of greenish metal that might be copper, refrozen glassy blades broken from the vegetation itself, cords braided from the blue-black frond-fibers. The work is careful and pre-elegant. No one has stayed long enough to build a proper dwelling. The structures feel like attempts to argue with the noise, little correctives set against the roar of unfinishing world.

Time moves the way the roots move. Days (if they are days) are long gray plateaus. A boulder detaches, drifts a hand-span, settles. A stand of glass-bamboo leans a fraction toward a new waterless spring that is only a denser patch of blue vapor. The mountains raise another incomplete terrace. Reality frays a little further at the corners of vision: a shelf appears that was not there, then softens into nearly was. You can walk for what feels like weeks across open scree flats and never leave the same incomplete valley, or turn once and find the entire range rearranged into a different argument of height.

The rules are local and provisional. Stone remembers being liquid longer than it should. Sound has weight. Growing things convert impossibility into mass. Nothing here is finished, and the unfinished state is loud enough to crush ordinary thought. If you stay, you begin to suspect the forests are not vegetation so much as the land’s attempt to grow a nervous system, something that can hear the roar and eventually answer it. The answer, when it comes, will be geological. It will take geologic time. Until then the high places remain embryonic, sparse, cool, thundering with the effort of becoming, and green in all the wrong ways.
