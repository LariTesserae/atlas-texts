The pocket does not announce itself. From the outer approaches—razor spines of young orogeny, plates still ringing faintly from the lift—you would climb past it without noticing. The massif rose within living memory, or the memory of the stone, which is shorter than it pretends. Its faces are still bright where the fracture planes sheared clean: no lichen varnish, no softening, only the chalky glitter of fresh feldspar and the dark, glassy teeth of unweathered andesite. Wind scours the heights in long, mute strokes. There is almost no snow and less melt. What moisture exists is locked in sealed inclusions or thin, bitter rime that sublimates before it can pool.

The settlement occupies a vertical lens inside the peak—a crushed geode of air, a fault pocket that opened when the mountain tore itself taller and then failed to close. You enter by a fissure no wider than a body, marked only by a dull brass plate sweated into the rock and a faint, residual heat that never quite matches the cool. Beyond that throat the space blooms: not large, never large, but densely occupied in every direction. Walls, floor, and ceiling are continuous city. Scaffold and cladding and pressed-metal walkways cling to the raw mineral in layers, some load-bearing into the living rock, some hung from earlier generations of hung things. The architecture is industrial without apology—ducts, gear housings, stamped catwalks, riveted cisterns that hold nothing wet, only powdered reagents and compressed dry air. Factories occupy whole shelves of the pocket: belt lines that turn with a soft, insulated clatter, stamp-presses muffled under felt and ash, dynamos that hum rather than roar. Light is low and directional—yellowish filaments behind wire cages, the occasional blue-white arc sealed in thick glass, long shadows that double every ladder and every protruding eave. The ambient temperature stays cool; heat is vented carefully into sink strata or bled as low-grade work into the mineral itself, which accepts it without glowing.

There is no true street. Movement happens along the dense mesh of stairs, ladders, conveyor underbellies, and the narrow galleries where two people cannot pass without turning sideways. Everything is near-touching. Housing is stacked in the interstices—pressed cubes and curved bulkheads wedged between machine halls, their windows small and thick. The air smells of hot metal, dry lubricant, and the faint ozone of old insulation. Water is not present as water. Processes run on non-aqueous cycles: molten salts in closed loops, powdered fluxes, ultra-dry pneumatic transfer. Any exterior sweat that forms on cold pipes is wicked into capture cloths and evaporated again before it can bead. The rock itself is desiccated; even the deepest drill cores come up dusty.

Vegetation is absent. What green or soft life once dreamed here never arrived, or arrived and failed. In its place the fauna thrives without apology. They are abundant to the point of saturation: pale, hard-bodied millers that graze the mineral dust from every surface and leave behind faint polished tracks; soft-footed cliff-hounds the size of cats that nest in warm ductwork and click their teeth when dynamos change pitch; clouds of singer-mites that ride the low thermal currents and whose wings make almost no sound, only a granular pressure in the ear; larger, slower things like living slag that migrate along the basal ledges and slowly sort ore scraps into neat, rejected piles. They do not wilt in the dryness. Their chemistry is sealed or hygroscopic in ways that do not require free liquid. At peak shift the air is thick with their passage—bodies sliding over bodies, claws finding purchase on the same stanchions the workers use—yet the overall sound remains subdued. The pocket swallows noise. Felt lagging, baffled vents, and the strange acoustic deadness of fresh fracture faces keep the industrial thunder down to a continuous private murmur. You hear your own breath more clearly than the stamp hall three shelves away.

The turbulence is bureaucratic and geological at once. The mountain is still settling; millimeter slips happen without warning, dropping a catwalk half a degree or pinching a conduit until the pressure gauges climb and gangs must reroute under lamps. Production quotas shift just as abruptly. Entire machine suites are unbolted and walked crablike to new shelves overnight. Scaffolding is temporary by doctrine. Buildings acquire scars of previous attachments, bolt-holes peened shut, weld beads left like lichen. Work crews and fauna rearrange the same volumes in overlapping tides. In the dim, cool air the motion never quite stops: a slow, dense boiling of metal, flesh, and powder. Shift bells are felt as vibration in the railings more than heard. Conversations happen close, lips nearly at ears.

There is a mild strangeness that never tips into spectacle. Compass needles prefer certain bulkheads. Dust suspended in the few shafts of borrowed daylight hangs a fraction too long before falling. Some of the larger millers arrange their leavings into faint concentric rings that the maintenance crews leave undisturbed, less from reverence than from a quiet calculation that the pattern is doing something useful no one has named. The brass entry plate is always slightly warmer than it should be. No one writes this down as doctrine. It is simply part of the pocket’s weather.

At the highest loft, where the fault lens pinches toward a crystal-lined blind end, a bank of sealed observation ports looks outward through meters of young stone. If the exterior shutters are opened (rarely, and only under sealed procedure), you see the upper world: the knife-edged ridges still shedding their birth-scree, the thin sky, the absolute absence of green. Then the shutters close again and the dense, quiet, turbulent interior reasserts itself—cool air, soft machine-song, the constant nearness of lives that have made a city out of a wound that has not yet finished opening.
