The world is all underwater, and has been for so long that the distinction between “flooded” and “always wet” has worn away. You move through a cold, dim volume that was once a continent of craft and is now a single, quiet, liquid room of uncountable chambers.

Everything under you, around you, above you is made. There is no bedrock, only fitted work. Cyclopean courses of stone—some the size of houses, some no larger than a hand—lock into one another without mortar, their joints so exact that even after ages of current they still drink the water rather than let it pry them apart. Where the blocks meet edges have been rounded by the slowest abrasion possible: not waves, not storms, just the patient rub of cold water and the microscopic drag of silt that never quite settles. The whole fabric has the look of something that finished becoming what it is and then simply stayed. Pillars rise by the tens of thousands, some broken midway and left as truncated stems, others still carrying lintels that vanish into vegetable murk. Staircases spiral down into deeper courts and then reverse themselves, climbing again toward faintly lighter water that is still not light. Bridges of single slabs span gaps that feel measured in forgotten units. The scale is continental; you can travel for days along a single terrace and the stonework never gives out, only gradually changes its grammar of joints and rises as if different hands, or the same hands across centuries, refined the same patient idea.

It is dense. Passages open into halls that open into smaller courts that telescopically nest; walls carry niches that hold miniature versions of the same architecture; freestanding stelae stand so close their shadows (such as shadows are here) overlap. Every surface has been touched by intention and then by time until intention and time became the same texture.

Over all of it grows the green. Not the thin opportunism of late colonization, but a lush, continuous pelt of aquatic vegetation that treats the built world as its proper soil. Long ribbons of dark kelp stream from every edge, their holdfasts locked into the worn joints like they were always meant to be there. Soft mosses and feathery red and brown algae cushion the treads of drowned stairways. In sheltered re-entrants, pale leafy things unfold in slow fans, catching whatever weak nutrients drift past. Where light fails entirely—which is most places—the growth becomes darker, almost black-green, and more felt than seen: a living nap that thickens every angle and softens every fall. The vegetation does not fight the stone; it completes it. In many plazas the original paving is no longer visible, only guessed at beneath meters of layered, slow-growing mat. The cold keeps the growth deliberate rather than frantic. Everything lengthens by fingers over decades.

There is almost no light. What reaches down from whatever surface still exists far above arrives as a memory of pewter, a direction more than an illumination. Deeper courts sit in true darkness. The eye learns to read by pressure and by the faint differential glow of pale algal films that store the last scraps of photons. Sound is likewise reduced. Water moves, but only as a continuous low note, a pressure-hum more felt in the chest than heard. Once in a long while a hanging curtain of kelp shifts and releases a soft, wet applause of fronds, then settles. Silt turns over with a sound like distant fabric. Nothing calls. Nothing thrives that needs to announce itself. The fauna is gone, or never arrived, or finished its cycle so long ago that even the calcium of its bones has been reincorporated into the joints of the masonry. Only plants, water, stone, and the lingering grammar of the makers.

Those makers worked with hands and levers and the patient geometry of weight. No metals bright enough to survive, no machines that would leave more than the sheer scale of the cutting as evidence. The technology is the placement itself: the ability to quarry, to float, to settle, to true an edge by eye and cord in an age when dry land still mattered. That age is over. The water closed over the work and has never receded. What remains is the final settled form—an artificial abyssal plain of halls and galleries and processional ways, now the substrate for a cold garden that has no need of keepers.

Time here is almost still. Currents circulate on century-long loops. The vegetation thickens and thins in rhythms too long for a single life to map. Blocks settle another hair’s breadth every hundred years. The darkness does not change. You can float in a narrow slot-canyon of perfect ashlar, listening to the quiet, and feel that the place has already arrived at the only shape it will ever need: total craft, total water, cold, green, ancient, and finished.
