The air down here does not so much move as settle and re-settle, thick with the slow wet of a place that never fully dries. You stand on a shallow ledge of unfinished stone—dark basalt still cooling in places, still weeping mineral water from hairline fractures that have not yet decided whether they will become seams or vanish. The ledge is no wider than a narrow street. Beyond its lip the floor drops three meters into a black shallow pool that covers most of the chamber floor. The water is hot. Not scalding, but body-hot and rising in lazy sheets of steam that never quite make it to the ceiling before they collapse again into condensed droplets. Those droplets strike metal and stone with a constant, soft percussion, like rain remembered by a room that has never seen sky.

This is not a natural cavern in the finished sense. The walls are still arriving. Great rounded bosses of rock push inward from the dark periphery, their surfaces glazed with fresh silica and streaked with orange iron seep. In places the stone is soft enough that a boot heel leaves a temporary print which the mineral deposition begins to fill within hours. The chamber itself is only a hundred meters across at its widest, vaulted like a half-collapsed basilica, the ceiling lost in dimness twenty meters up. Westward a low arch leads to a twin chamber of similar size; eastward the rock pinches into a corridor already half-choked with new deposition. That is the entire known settlement: two wet rooms and the short passage between them. Everything else is still becoming rock.

They built into it anyway.

Iron walkways, already freckled with rust blooms, span the hot pools on squat pilings driven straight into the embryonic floor. The walkways do not form a grid so much as a sparse constellation—platforms and catwalks spaced far enough apart that you can always see water or raw stone between them. On the largest platform sits the pump-house: a single-story brick-and-plate structure whose walls sweat continuously. Inside, three slow reciprocating engines thump with the patience of geological time, drawing mineral-rich water up from deeper seams and forcing it through a lattice of cooling pipes that snake overhead like industrial ivy. The pipes drip. Everything drips. The sound is low and constant, a wet industrial heartbeat underwritten by the deeper gurgle of water finding new paths through stone that has not finished hardening.

Stacked against the south wall of the main chamber are the living cubes—prefabricated metal boxes raised on stilts above the vapor, connected by narrow grated bridges. There are perhaps thirty of them, widely spaced so that each has its own small radius of empty air and dripping dark. Laundry hangs in the steamy intervals and never fully dries. Electric bulbs, caged and infrequent, throw a yellowish half-light that dies quickly in the moisture; most of the illumination comes from the dull red glow of the engine housings and from the bioluminescent film that coats every permanently wet surface. The film is not plant life. There is almost no plant life. A few translucent ferns the color of old ivory cling to vertical seeps where the water is slightly cooler, their fronds no larger than a hand. That is the entire flora.

The fauna more than compensates.

The air and the water throng. Pale shrimp-like things, each the length of a finger, swarm in glittering curtains just beneath the surface of every pool; their collective motion makes the water look grainy, like static. Above the water, clouds of midges with glassy wings orbit the warm updrafts from the engines, ordinary enough until you notice they never land. Larger shapes move in the deeper channels: long, flattened fish the color of wet concrete that surface only to take insects, their eyes milk-white and useless. Crabs with bodies no bigger than coins carpet the lower walkway supports in slow-motion migrations that take days to cross a single piling; their shells rasp faintly against the iron. Higher up, in the permanent dusk of the upper vaults, something webbed and membranous glides in lazy figures-of-eight. You hear the soft leather flap of its passage more often than you see it. At night—meaning whenever the bulbs are switched to their dimmer circuit—the entire chamber fills with a low, continuous susurrus of chitin and breath and the wet click of too many small mouths. It is not hostile. It is simply total. The settlers have learned to walk carefully, to sleep under fine mesh, to treat every surface as already occupied.

There are rules here, quiet ones that have emerged as slowly as the rock itself. Do not drill deeper than three meters; the stone at that depth is still plastic and will close around a bit like soft clay. Waste heat is currency—any process that cools the engines is paid for in favors. The bioluminescent film is left untouched; scrub it away and the next tide of midges becomes unbearable. Children are taught early to listen for the change in the engines’ cadence that means a new vent has opened beneath the floor. When that happens everyone stands still for a few minutes while the crabs and the fish and the floating membranes rearrange themselves around the fresh heat. Then life resumes its patient grind.

Time feels thick. The engines cycle once every forty seconds. Mineral crusts thicken on the walkway railings at a visible rate if you watch long enough—one millimeter every few weeks. People speak softly not from reverence but because the chamber already supplies its own loudness: the thump of pistons, the eternal drip, the wet white noise of thousands of small bodies in motion. Conversations stretch, pause, resume hours later without anyone noting the gap. A courtship may last a season. A grudge may outlast the current shape of the eastern corridor. The settlement has no name that is not temporary; they call it the Rooms, or sometimes simply Below. Outside—far above through successive damp throats of stone—there is surface weather and daylight and wind. Down here those concepts feel theoretical, like stories about a country that may or may not still exist.

Stand long enough on the central catwalk and you will feel the entire place breathing: the slow expansion of heated rock, the almost imperceptible rise of the water table, the collective pulse of the uncounted lives that treat the industrial iron as just another kind of reef. The heat never relents. The wet never ceases. The stone continues its unfinished argument with gravity. And through it all the engines keep their unhurried rhythm, as if they, too, understand that nothing down here is finished, and that finishing is not the point.
