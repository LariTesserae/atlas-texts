The highland does not end. From any vantage it runs outward until the eye invents a horizon that later walking always disproves. What looks like sky-line is only another fold of the same raw plateau, another low ridge of stone that has not yet decided whether it will become mountain or sink again into the marshy saddles between. The rock itself is young—pale, angular, still tasting of the pressures that forced it upward. Surfaces weep. Thin sheets of cold water slide constantly from every face, gathering in hairline rivulets that never quite become streams before they vanish into the next porous ledge. The air holds the water; it condenses on skin and metal alike, a fine perpetual damp that never quite freezes and never quite falls as proper rain.

Temperature stays low. Breath hangs. In the long, normal daylight the light itself feels thinned, as if it has traveled farther than light should to reach here. Shadows are clean but soft-edged from the moisture. Where the plateau cups into shallow basins the water stands in dark mirrors among black grit and the few plants that endure: low cushions of gray-green that look more like lichen than grass, brittle stems that snap underfoot with a sound like frozen silk. Vegetation is a minority presence. Most of the ground is bare emergent geology—scree that has not yet rounded, slabs still bearing the feathers and chatter-marks of whatever force cracked them loose.

Across this immensity the settlements appear at intervals measured in days of travel. They are cities, unmistakably, but spaced so that each sits inside its own weather and its own silence. Approaches are long: first a glint on a distant rise, then the slow revelation of towers and terraces grown or extruded from the same pale stone, surfaces that drink the damp and exhale it again as controlled mist. The architecture does not fight the cold or the wet; it incorporates them. Walls are laminated with materials that current understanding only half-names—lattices that shift thermal gradients without moving parts, membranes that pull water vapor into storage and release it as clear limbs of ice or sheets of pure liquid according to need. Bridges span empty air on cables thinner than reason, yet birds roost on them without fear. Inside the cities the same quiet prevails. Sounds are few and local: the low continuous hush of water moving through capillary architecture, the occasional soft chime of a load-adjusting spar, footsteps on crowed terraces that somehow never accumulate into crowd noise. Conversation carries only a few paces before the damp air takes it.

Between the cities the plateau belongs to the animals. They are abundant and largely unafraid. Herds of long-legged browsers move in loose constellations, their coats beaded with the same condensate that sheets the rocks; they crop the sparse cushions and leave neat dark droppings that steam briefly in the cold. Overhead, wide-winged forms ride the weak thermals that rise off sun-warmed stone, banking in near silence. Smaller lives thread the scree: pale arthropods that drink from the weeping faces, burrowers that embroider the wet gravel with perfect round holes, things like hares that freeze into stone-colored stillness when a shadow passes. Predators exist but hunt without spectacle—sudden, efficient, then gone. The whole ecology runs on a muted clock of fog advance and retreat, of slow seasonal pulses that never quite become the dramatic seasons of lower lands. Everything is active, yet the activity seldom rises above a whisper.

Faint rules govern the place. The stone is still rising in places; a traveler may sleep on level ground and wake to find a new low scarp a hand-span high, edged with fresh mineral shine. The cities know this and flex. Foundations are less poured than negotiated, layers of responsive lattice that lengthen or shorten by microscopic degrees, keeping streets true while the planet rearranges itself beneath. Water is both enemy and medium: every surface sheds it, yet every structure drinks it, cycles it, uses its phase changes for power so subtle the generators make no sound beyond a deeper quiet. Light remains ordinary—no enhanced spectra, no permanent aurora—yet the cities store and release it with thrift, so that at true night the distant settlements glow like banks of cool coals rather than electric glare. Sound rarely travels far; the damp and the sheer scale eat it. What remains is the sense of a world still under construction at continental size, its cities already ancient in craft, its animals already perfectly fitted, its geology still deciding the final shape of every ridge and every marshy saddle.

Walk long enough and the pattern repeats without ever feeling like repetition: bare wet stone, sparse life, a city like a frost-flower of impossible engineering, then emptiness again under the same thin cold light. The highland simply continues, vast enough to hold its own slow weather systems, quiet enough that the loudest thing most days is the soft drip of water from a ledge onto stone that did not exist a century earlier.
