The high vale does not so much sit in the mountains as interrupt them. Raw spires and half-formed ridges shoulder upward on every side, their flanks still mineral-bright, barely fretted by weather. You can see the rock’s grain and the unfinished angles where rock faces have only just split along their joints. Soil is a rumor here—thin black seams caught in cracks, or the dark felt that plant-life itself is laying down. The altitude keeps the air thin and cool; breath plumes, and stone stays cold even under the hard white sun that pours without pity from a sky almost empty of cloud. Light is total and unsubtle. It turns every wet surface into a mirror and every droplet into a brief, sharp star.

Water is the first motion you notice, and almost the only one. It does not fall so much as appear: bead by bead from the weeping faces of the new cliffs, sheet by silver sheet down planes that have not yet learned how to channel it, then pooling in shallow, cold basins that dapple the open floor of the vale. The air itself condenses. Mists lift and collapse in slow abundance, cool enough to bead on skin, bright enough to blind when the sun catches them. There is no single river; there is a continuous, low-grade exudation, as if the young ranges are still wringing themselves out.

Where the water lingers, vegetation answers with absolute severity. It is not undergrowth. It is substrate and architecture both. Vast, slow colonies of mosses and gelatinous liverworts have thickened into living pavements meters deep, quartz-green and bronze, soft enough to take a boot-print that slowly fills with seepage. Out of these mats rise the true masses: arborescent ferns and clubmoss towers whose trunks are fused cables of living tissue, their crowns interlocking into vaults and galleries high enough to hold weather of their own. Leaves the size of sails hang in overlapping tiers, beaded and dripping, so that walking beneath them is like moving under a series of green cataracts. These plant-structures do not fill the vale the way a forest would. They stand as discrete, immense soft citadels—separated by wide reaches of bare, glistening stone and sky-reflecting water—so that the eye travels far between one living mass and the next. Density is low; presence is overwhelming. The plants are the ground, the walls, the roofs. Everything else is interval.

Sound arrives as a continuous pressure. It has no single source and no relief. Water thunders down unfinished scarps in heavy, unbroken sheets; wind, funneled between the raw ridges and the high canopies, becomes a sustained roar that frays at the edges into screams; and threaded through it all is the low, industrial bellow of the machines. Scattered across the open stone, half-rooted in the moss pavements or braced against weeping walls, stand the works: long turbine halls of riveted plate, their intakes choked with living filter-mats; derricks that lift mist-water into settling tanks; slow reciprocating engines whose flywheels are larger than cottages, still turning on oil and cold condensate. Most are active. A few lie half-drowned and overgrown, their housings split by the patient advance of rhizome and root. None of them are numerous; they appear the way the plant-masses do—isolated, grave, authoritative. Between them the distances feel regional, like gaps between distant towns. The noise they add is deafening precisely because there is nothing else to soften it: no thick soil, no dense animals, no competing chatter. Just metal, falling water, and the deep hydraulic creak of vegetable architecture adjusting its weight by fractions of a degree per year.

Almost nothing moves quickly. The ranges continue their uplift at a pace below ordinary noticing; new joints open with a sound like distant bone. The plant-citadels expand by cellular increments, their outer tissues darkening and thickening as they slowly annex the nearest machines or bridges of discarded gantry. Seasons here are long gradients rather than events—cool becoming fractionally colder, mist thickening or thinning across weeks. Fauna is rare enough to startle: a single dark bird laboring across the bright gulf between two canopies, or a pale crustacean picking through the algae of a turbine outflow. Their scarcity keeps the acoustics pure and the light unobstructed.

There is no secret principle at work, no distortion of ordinary law—only the stark logic of a place still under construction. Geology has not finished speaking. Plants have not finished answering. Water mediates, light exposes, and a thin scattering of human engines try, loudly and without hurry, to insert themselves into the gradients. Stand long enough on the wet stone between one living mass and the next and you begin to feel the scale of the intervals: a whole high country of raw rock and soft architecture, cool and shining, roaring with the patience of things that have only just begun.
