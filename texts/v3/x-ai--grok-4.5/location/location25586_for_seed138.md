The place is a low basin no larger than a few hundred paces across, ringed by a sharp new retaining wall of poured slag-concrete that still shows the seams of its forms. Outside the wall the country falls away into featureless wet flats; inside, everything that matters is contained. The ground itself feels recently decided upon—cut, filled, tamped, and left with edges that have not yet learned to soften. Water stands in every low joint. It does not drain so much as hover, a permanent shallow skin that never quite becomes a proper stream and never quite dries. The heat keeps it from cooling; the air is saturated and does not move. Condensation beads on every upright surface and falls back as a fine, continuous rain that is only the atmosphere remembering it is full.

What little vegetation exists has been reduced to the stubborn and the accidental: yellow reeds wedged in the expansion gaps of the wall, a black film of algae on the shaded undersides of pipes, a few wiry shrubs that root in the warm seepage where buried steam lines pass. Nothing taller than a person’s knee, nothing continuous. The rest is open wet ground between structures, slick and reflecting what little light there is.

The light is always the same—late-overcast, the color of old brass seen through smoke. No sun track. No true night or day. The industrial solids themselves supply most of the illumination: the dull orange of banked furnace doors, the greenish wash of underpowered arc lamps under corrugated eaves, the pale sheets of steam that catch and hold a glow without ever brightening it. Shadows are soft and long and do not shift.

The built fabric is dense in function and sparse in footprint. A half-dozen main blocks—engine house, digester halls, bulk stores, a low range of workers’ barracks—stand separated by wide paved courts that double as shallow basins when the water rises an inch. Everything is of the same recent make: riveted plate, cast-iron columns still sharp at the arrises, brick that has not yet taken soot into its grain. Catwalks and pipe bridges stitch the buildings together at second-story height so that the ground can remain what it is—an admitted marsh under industrial occupation. Stacks rise, short and thick, and never stop breathing a pale, wet exhaust that collapses immediately into the heavy air. The whole settlement feels like a single machine that happened to require rooms and walkways.

It is loud in a steady, unvarying way. The great reciprocating engines in the central house keep a slow thunder that is as much felt in the sternum as heard. Pumps answer them from the corners, higher-pitched, never missing a stroke. Somewhere a belt slap repeats every few seconds with the patience of a metronome. Steam vents in long flat hisses. Water drips from a thousand lips of metal onto more metal or into standing pools, a continuous bright ticking underneath the heavier noises. There is no quieter hour. The equilibrium was reached long ago and has simply continued.

Animals have claimed every interval the machinery left open. Crabs the color of wet rust move in deliberate armies across the courts at what would be dusk if dusk arrived; they feed on the warm organic scum that forms where effluent meets rainwater. Clouds of midges hang in the steam plumes and are harvested by bats that roost inside the cooling towers. Pale frogs chord from the flooded cable trenches. Rats the size of small cats own the pipe galleries and are largely ignored. Above, heavy gulls and darker waterbirds circle the stacks on the updraft of heat and nest on the broad ledges of the digesters, their cries flattening into the general din. The fauna is not wild so much as coextensive—present wherever a niche of heat, wet, or waste opens for an hour.

Nothing here is arranged for scenery. The walks are grating that lets water through. Handrails sweat. Every surface that can rust has begun to, thinly, the first orange bloom on edges that are still geometrically cruel. Transfer sheds stand open-sided; inside them the air is a few degrees hotter and louder, full of the slow rotation of drums and the wet slap of material being moved. Somewhere a shift whistle sounds at intervals that no longer correspond to any external day. People move between the buildings along the raised ways—small figures in heavy cloth that never dries—tending valves, reading gauges that have not varied in living memory, replacing what has worn without altering the larger pattern. The settlement neither grows nor shrinks. The wall keeps the outer marsh at the exact distance it was set. The engines keep their cadence. The water stands. The heat holds. The dimness does not lift.

This is the whole of it: a small, sharp-edged industrial citadel sunk in a permanent hot wetness, loud with its own unchanging labor, sparse of green and dense with life that has no better claim on the place than the machines do. Time passes here the way water does—always present, never arriving anywhere new.
