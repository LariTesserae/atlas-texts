The place is a narrow rock space no wider than a long stone’s throw, cupped into the knife-edge of a ridgeline that itself dies into empty air. It sits so high that the world below is only a rumor of weather. The walls are basalt gone soft with age: fluted, polished, undercut into shallow galleries and hanging lips, every surface carrying the slow memory of water and wind that have worked here for longer than ice ages. Nothing is sharp anymore. The stone has settled into its final grammar of curves and ledges, balanced, unhurried, already finished.

Light almost never arrives. What little does is a dilute blue bruise that leaks under the massive overhang for a few hours and then fails. The rest is darkness thick enough to feel on the skin—warm darkness, for the rock itself holds a deep geothermal breath. Heat rises in patient waves from hairline vents along the floor, keeping the air at a constant low fever. Moisture forms without rain. It beads on every cooler face, slides in threads, gathers in shallow scoops worn into the basalt, then evaporates again into the next slow cycle. The place is always damp the way a living mouth is damp: never dry, never flooded.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. Pale lichens map the higher walls in ghost continents. In the wettest seeps, a few iron-colored mosses and one species of black fern uncurl with extreme caution, their fronds no longer than a finger. Nothing taller. Nothing greener. The economy of life here is spent elsewhere.

It is spent on bodies.

The hollow is saturated with animals. They occupy every ledge, every crevice, every hanging pocket the stone has made. The densest are the night-fold bats—soft, mouse-sized, millions of them hanging in layered curtains so thick the rock disappears. Their wings make a continuous dry rustle like rain on parchment. Below them sprawl the heavy ground colonies of basalt voles, blind and pink-eyed, whose tunnels braid through the thin soil and under one another until the floor itself seems to breathe. Above both, nesting in the higher flutes, live the screamers: long-legged birds the color of wet ash, throats permanently open. Their call is a single rising note that never quite finishes; when thousands sound at once the hollow becomes a single vibrating throat. The sound does not stop. It only changes register with the slow turning of the day-that-is-not-day—louder when the faint light dies, softer when the geothermal heat peaks and the bats shift in their sleep. There is no silence, only the patient modulation of noise.
