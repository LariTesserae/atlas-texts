The high plateaus do not end. They fold into one another under a sky that has forgotten how to brighten, a slow-boiling darkness the color of wet iron. What little illumination there is leaks upward from the city itself: faint violet seams along the edges of towers, pulses travelling the undersides of bridges, the occasional pale flare when a pressure membrane ruptures and reseals. The light never reaches far. It is swallowed by the saturated air long before it can climb the flanking ridges.

Everything weeps. The rock is new—barely cooled, still flexing—and it sweats mineral water in continuous sheets. The water does not fall so much as crawl: thick films sliding down facades, gathering in the inverted gutters that line every walkway, dripping in slow curtains from one engineered ledge to the next. In the deeper cuts between districts the runoff becomes a sounding. Not a river exactly; more a arterial murmuring that never quite finds a single channel. It fills the air with a wet white noise that never drops below a roar.

There are no plants. There never were, or they have been edited out so thoroughly that even the memory of chlorophyll feels foreign. The surfaces are mineral and manufactured only: basalt stillshows the glassy skin of its last flow, fused to carbon lattices and self-healing ceramics. Fungal analogues exist, but they are not organic in any familiar sense—thin conductive mats that harvest thermal differentials, growing in fractal sheets across cooling vents, feeding on the city's low-grade waste heat. They glisten black-purple and make no soft sounds of their own.

The city is dense the way a freezing fog is dense. Structures pack against one another without the courtesy of streets wide enough for distance. Towers lean in clusters, connected at many heights by translucent tubes and vibration-absorbing spars. Some buildings are still being extruded from the living rock by machines that move at the pace of geology; you can watch a new facade lengthen across decades if you care to return. The tech does not announce itself with chrome or neon. It sinks into matter. Walls remember pressure patterns and adjust porosity so condensation is guided rather than fought. Entire districts breathe—slow diaphragmatic flexing that equalizes the crushing humidity. Gravity is locally renegotiated in the taller spires so that water can be lifed without pumps; you feel the shift as a thickening in the joints when you step across certain thresholds.

And the fauna. They are everywhere and they are loud. Not animals in the old taxonomic sense. Something closer to mobile ecology that the city either manufactured or failed to prevent. Swarms of lens-bodied things the size of fists thrash through the air in disciplined rivers, their hard carapaces clicking against guidance filaments strung between towers. Larger forms—six-limbed, matt-black, heat-shimmering—haul themselves along exterior walls on suction pads, scraping mineral skin with a sound like distant stone being sawn. In the flooded undercrofts, vast soft bodies press against grated openings, exhaling plumes of warmer mist that immediately recondense. Their calls are infrastructural: low seismic chords that resonate through the bedrock, ultrasonic needle-storms that make the water films dance, wet percussive drumming when colonies shift en masse from one cooling fin to another. The sound never peaks into chaos and never rests. It is a continuous, patient saturation, as though the entire plateau is speaking with one multi-throated voice that takes years to finish a sentence.

Movement itself is slow. Weather systems crawl. The city's orthogenesis proceeds on schedules measured in centuries. Even the fauna's overwhelming presence has a glacial quality: individuals dart and thrash, but the great migrations of densiy—the way a swarm will occupy a neighborhood for a generation then abandon it—happen across spans longer than most recorded lives. Erosion is only beginning. Fresh scarps still show the tool-marks of their making. New fingers of stone push up through plazas and are simply built around, or incorporated, their weeping surfaces turned into public basins. The place feels embryonic and finished at once—raw geology forced into urban density before it had time to weather into anything gentle.

Stand on any high gantry and the scale asserts itself. District after district recedes into the dark wet, lights and bodies and dripping architectures repeating until they become texture. Somewhere far out the plateau finally drops, but the drop is only rumored. Here, under the iron dark, amid the constant water-sound and the clicking rivers of hard-bodied life, the city continues its unhurried construction of itself, a mineral organism teaching water and darkness how to dwell.
