The high country begins without announcement. One simply finds the air thinner, the light a fraction sharper, and the horizon farther away than it has any right to be. What rises here is not a finished range but the idea of one—an embryonic highland still deciding its own contours. Ridges stand half-exhaled from the planet’s crust, their flanks raw and faceted like unfinished crystal, edges unsoftened by time. Between them the land stretches in long, vacant shelves of pale mineral, nearly featureless, a salt-bright emptiness that holds almost nothing per cubic mile. No rivers have ever cut here. No rain has ever agreed to fall. The chemistry of the place rejects water the way a sealed flask rejects air; what little moisture once existed was baked out or locked into dry lattices long before the present stillness settled.

Yet the shelves are not barren. At irregular intervals, sometimes two miles apart, sometimes twenty, dense thickets rise with a sudden, almost architectural lushness. These are not plants in any ordinary sense. They are vascular lattices of dull brass, verdigris, and smoked glass, trunked like mangroves but branching into broad, translucent fronds that catch the normal daylight and refract it into slow internal pulses of amber and copper. The fronds overlap in stratified canopies so thick that the interior of each thicket is a perpetual green-gold dusk. From a distance the clusters look soft, even inviting—billows of metallic foliage swaying without wind. Up close they reveal themselves as slow industrial organisms. Along every stem run fine channels of circulating reagent, clear and oil-slick, carrying out reductions and condensations that require no aqueous solvent. At the joints, silent micro-forges bloom and fade: tiny tidal zones of heat where metal is annealed, glass is drawn, and catalytic dust is precipitated onto waiting surfaces. The entire thicket is a factory without walls, a self-assembling production line grown rather than built. Nothing has been manufactured by hands; the structures simply continue the planet’s own metallurgy at a more deliberate pace.

Because the processes reached equilibrium ages ago, nothing ever finishes and nothing ever starts. The same leaf-scales are re-forged and re-hung in endless quiet cycles. No waste accumulates. No raw stockpile dwindles. The system is thermodynamically closed and aesthetically complete. Sound does not travel far inside the thickets; the only noises are faint ticks of contracting metal at the shift of day into night and the occasional dry whisper of one frond settling against another. Outside, across the empty mineral flats, silence is absolute.

Elevation keeps the temperature uniformly warm. Heat rises gently from deep fractures still knitting themselves shut, a residual industrial warmth that never spikes into violence and never cools into chill. The light is ordinary, high-altitude light—unfiltered, slightly polarized, free of haze because there is neither dust nor vapor to scatter it. Shadows fall knife-clean across the raw stone. In that light the thickets glitter like abandoned foundries given permission to become forests.

No animals move here. The chemistry is wrong for flesh; the air itself is a thin preservative. What grows does so without pollination, without predation, without the least tremor of hunger. The landscape has no wooden structures, no roads, no markers, no ruins. Whatever intelligence once initiated the industrial vegetation (if intelligence was ever required) has left no further signature. The place simply continues, high and dry and half-born, its geology still emerging in slow crystalline drafts while its silent machine-jungles keep perfect, waterless time.

Walk far enough and the pattern repeats: empty shelf, abrupt lushness of living metal, empty shelf again. The scale is regional; one could spend days crossing a single basin and never see the same thicket twice, yet the logic never changes. Everything is already what it will remain. The ridges may still be rising by micrometers per century, but they rise without drama, without avalanche, without the usual vocabulary of erosion. They simply become more themselves, cooler at the core, sharper at the crest, while the brass-and-glass canopies overhead continue their quiet, perpetual manufacture of exactly what they already are.
