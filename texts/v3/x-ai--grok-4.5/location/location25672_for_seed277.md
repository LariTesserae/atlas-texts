The high dark has no edge that can be walked to in a lifetime. It is a single continuous upland, continental in reach, raised so far above the forgotten lowlands that the air thins into a dry warmth that never quite cools, even in the long seasons without sun. What light there is arrives only as a faint, directionless gray seepage from somewhere beyond the sky’s skin—never enough to cast a shadow, never enough to name a horizon. The world here lives by other illuminations.

Stone has had ages to settle into itself. The bedrock is not jagged; it has been worn into long, rolling tables and shallow concave basins, their edges softened like bone left in weather. Wind and sparse rain have not conquered the rock so much as negotiated with it, leaving a surface of interlocking plates and muted escarpments that feel finished, balanced, complete. Between these plates the ground dips into countless dry swales and blind valleys. Most of the year they hold nothing. Then, without warning, the air cools a few degrees and moisture blooms out of nowhere: beads of condensation thick enough to run, brief curtains of warm rain that last less than an hour, thin sheets of water that slide across stone and vanish into fractures. In the lowest folds, temporary black pools form, mirror-still, already shrinking before the next breath of dryness returns. These pockets are the only rivers the upland knows—riverine for a day, then memory.

Vegetation keeps to the same thin economy. Pale crustose lichens map the cooler faces of stone in gray-green lacquer. In the deeper basinsto where water once lingered, scattered tufts of wiry, heat-tolerant grass hold brittle seedheads that rattle only when something larger passes. Once in a great while a single columnar plant rises—part fungus, part succulent—its fluted trunk the height of a person and no thicker, crowned by a dull phosphorescent cap that glows the soft orange of banked embers. These appear kilometers apart, never in groves. The eye learns to treat them as solitary events rather than a landscape feature.

What fills the distances is animal. Life here is abundant yet spaced with almost ceremonial spareness, as though the dark itself rationed proximity. Herds of low, many-legged grazers move like slow weather fronts across the stone tables, their padded feet silent on the rock. They scrape lichen with keratinous plates and drink from the vanishing pools by pressing spongy snouts flat to the wetness. Above them, on thermals that rise from hidden geothermal seams, glide membranes the size of small clouds—creatures more kite than beast, steering by shifting the tension in living spars. Their undersides carry faint bioluminescent veins that pulse in slow code; from far away the signals look like distant, soundless lightning.

Closer to the ground the true density of life reveals itself in motion rather than numbers. Packs of angular predators the color of cooled slag hunt by vibration, belly-low, pausing for minutes at a time until the dark seems empty again. Swarms of hard-shelled scavengers materialize after every rain, clattering softly as they work the brief mud, then bury themselves once more. In the fractures and under the plate edges, entire climates of smaller things thrive: soft phosphorescent worms that braid themselves into temporary ropes to cross dry gaps, gelatinous grazers that cling to the undersides of stones and drop away when warmth increases, and filigree flyers no larger than thumbnails that ride the faintest convection currents like ash.

All of it happens inside a hush that is nearly complete. Sound carries strangely in the warm thin air and then dies. The great gliders make no call. The herds produce only the occasional dry click of plate against plate. Even the predators prefer stillness so absolute it becomes another kind of camouflage. What noise exists is low and infrastructural: a continuous sub-audible throb that seems to come from the bedrock itself, the slow flexing of the upland’s internal heat. Now and then a deeper note rolls through—far off, like a single enormous engine turning over once and falling back to idle. These are the geothermal seams and pressure ridges doing their ancient work: water flashing to steam in sealed chambers, stone swelling and settling, mineral-rich fluids forced upward through capillary faults to crystallize in new layers or to exhale as brief, scalding vents. The land manufactures and re-manufactures itself without hurry and without residue that looks made. There are no ruins, no casings, no geometric scars. Only the quiet industrial metabolism of a crust that has been hot and active for so long that its processes have worn smooth as the rest of the terrain.

At irregular intervals the sky thickens and a dark front builds—our own weather, generated by the upland’s sheer scale. Warm rain sheets down harder than usual. The temporary rivers connect for a night. Fauna converge from every direction, drinking, breeding, quarreling in near silence. Bioluminescence flares denser than at any other time: blue-green constellations moving across black water, orange beacons on the tall plants answering one another across kilometers. Then the moisture lifts, the pools contract, the animals disperse into their wide private spaces again, and the long quiet resumes—stone ticking as it cools a fraction, wind combing the empty distances, the deep engines turning underfoot.

Nothing here was built. Everything here works. The high dark continues, warm and barely damp, its distances unpointed by any structure save the ones the rock and the creatures keep making and unmaking in the same breath.
