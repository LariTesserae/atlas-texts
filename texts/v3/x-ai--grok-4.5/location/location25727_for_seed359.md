The place is called, by those who still use words for it, the Potting.

It is not large. You could walk its longest axis in the time it takes a thick candle to gutter halfway, if walking were simple here and if the paths agreed to stay paths. It sits in a shallow natural depression that has been entirely overwritten: every cup of soil scraped out and replaced, every stone reset, every contour of the ground rebuilt by hand until the original earth is a rumor under the work. What remains is a low, warm bowl of made ground, open to ordinary daylight that arrives flat and honest and then loses its nerve among the denseness.

There is almost no water. Rain, when it remembers this place, beads on surfaces and vanishes into joints rather than pooling. Yet the vegetation is lush in the cramped, overstuffed way of a room that has been planted too many times. The green is not born of aquifers. It drinks the slow bleed that comes through the seams—of intention, of leftover naming, of the fact that too many hands have insisted this ground should live. Leaves are thick, matte, and slightly warm to the touch, like skin that has been in the sun. Stems are jointed with the same logic as the walls: lapped, pegged, bound with fiber that was once plant and is now something that remembers being plant. Roots do not seek moisture so much as they seek joins. They force themselves into mortar lines, into the hairline gaps between fired tablets, into the drill-holes left by older construction, and there they swell and lock, becoming architecture that photosynthesizes.

Everything is built. There is no substrate that has not been handled. The floor of the Potting is a tight mosaic of hand-sized tiles—clay, beaten bark pressed into hardness, slates of a pale mineral that rings when struck, plates of layered horn. They are set without level, so the surface frets and rises in low ridges, young work that has not yet been worn soft. Edges are still sharp enough to catch a sandal. The walls that enclose the bowl are not walls in the defensive sense; they are retaining structures for density. They lean inward slightly, made of stacked cribwork: timber that was shaped with adze and fire-hardening, lashed with twisted gut and vine, then infilled with packed tiles, broken vessels, and the compressed ash of earlier attempts. You can read the layers like growth rings of failure and repair. Near the top, where the cribwork thins, living screens begin—trained espalier that has forgotten it was ever separate from the frame. The whole circumference is only a few dozen arm-spans across. From the outside, if there is still a trustworthy outside, the Potting looks like a low kiln-yard or a forgotten orchard corral. From inside, scale behaves badly.

Density is the governing law. Nothing stands alone. Vines web the air at shoulder height in slow curtains. Between them hang the smaller crafts: coils of unfired clay left to cure into permanent spirals, bundles of reeds that have begun to leaf from their bindings, charms of bone and seed that tick when the warm air moves. Platforms and shelves jut at irregular heights, all load-bearing packages of the same handmade logic—mortise, lash, wedge, weight. Creatures use every surface. The fauna here is abundant and mostly quiet, which makes their presence heavier. They are not one kind. There are the tile-foxes, low and angular, whose coats are patterns of dust the exact color of the mosaic; they sleep in the undercut ridges and climb by hooking claws into the same joints the roots love. There are the hinge-birds, small, with beaks like worn awls, that nest inside the hollow knuckles of the cribwork and emerge only when the light shifts a true degree. There are soft, many-legged browsers that graze the mineral bloom off old pottery and leave behind a faint polish. There are the long ones that are mostly throat and patience, draped through the upper screens like discarded rope until they decide to drop a loop and listen. None of them drink. They lick seams. They chew the dried decoctions that seep, very slowly, from certain overloaded bindings where too many successive repairs have stacked meaning on meaning.

The temperature is a held warmth, the warmth of a place that has been in the sun and then cupped by its own walls. It does not spike. It endures. Air moves in patient verticals—up the inner faces of the cribwork, across the suspended green, down again through the central clearings that are never quite clear. Sound is scarce by design and by habit. You hear the tick of cooling tile after the light has stood too long on one face. You hear the dry brush of a hinge-bird settling. You hear, sometimes, the almost sub-audible creak of a root taking up slack in a joint, the sound of the Potting continuing to tighten itself. Voices, when they happen, do not carry; the density eats them into local weather.

The craft is primitive and total. No metal that has not been scavenged and then humiliated into softness—thin beaten straps used as hinges, nails clenched over and over until they are more like staples of dark gum. Tools here are hand and tooth and the inherited shapes of earlier tools. Firing is done in squatter ovens set into the base of the outer crib, ovens that double as shrines because heat, in a waterless lushness, is the nearest thing to a spring. People—when people are present—move like additional fauna: slow, close, touching everything they pass as if confirming the inventory. They patch. They re-bind. They plant another cutting into a crack that has begun to show pale, dry light from nowhere in particular.

That light from nowhere is the first ordinary symptom of the weirdness. The Potting is young in the geological sense and old in the insistence sense. It has not had time to erode into forgiveness; its corners still cut, its decisions still show. But it has been remade so many times in the same footprint that the footprint has begun to argue with location. Reality does not break here with spectacle. It thins. In the deepest inner pocket, where the mosaic rises into a low stepped nest of platforms and the vegetation braids itself into a single fat column of leaf and lashing, the air acquires a faint double edge. Surfaces admit cursory second surfaces. A tile that shows a spiral on one glance shows a small open hand on the next, and neither is paint. Looking up through the green, you may see the ordinary sky and also a low ceiling of the same cribwork you are standing inside, as if the Potting has begun to stack a copy of itself a few body-lengths overhead and has not finished deciding which layer is occupied. Shadows fall in slightly wrong lengths. The abundant quiet creatures do not seem troubled; they use the inconsistencies as extra rooms. A tile-fox will slip into a joint that is only present when you are not trying to measure it and reappear a shelf higher with dust on its whiskers that belongs to a firing several repairs ago.

Time leans the same way the walls do. Change is real but grudging. A binding will take a season to stretch. A new screen of leaves will darken from the pale of fresh craft to the matte green of acceptance over many warm days. The fauna breed in low numbers and replace themselves without drama. Even the thinning of the world is a patient process: each successive re-making adds a little more pressure to the idea of the place, and the idea, having nowhere else to go in this sealed bowl of total construction, begins to precipitate as extra geometry, as vegetation that owes nothing to water, as animals that are half craft and half appetite. The Potting does not flood, freeze, or spar. It densifies. It sharpens. It keeps its heat. It holds its quiet like a held breath that has forgotten the need to end.

If you stay long enough to be claimed by the inventory—if your hands learn the automatic check of lashed joints, if your tongue stops expecting water and starts expecting the mineral rime on the oldest pottery—you will notice that the outer cribwork is no longer clearly the boundary. The made ground continues, in thinning confidence, a few steps beyond where the walls should stop. Outside and inside have begun to share a seam. The lush dry green pushes through. The quiet things commute. And somewhere under the youngest, sharpest tiles, the original depression still exists as a shape the Potting cannot quite forget and cannot reverse: a low place, warm, completely spoken for, growing denser by slow degrees while the daylight goes on behaving as if nothing, anywhere, were wrong.
