The High Nave hangs where the air thins to a warm, metallic breath. It is not a mountain summit colonised by machines, nor a factory sunk into living rock. There is no living rock. Every surface—floor, vault, buttress, and the slow-curving ribs that hold the whole place against the sky—was poured, riveted, cast, or plated long ago. The substrate itself is artefact: layered decking of pitted iron and fused slag glass, worn to a soft lustre by centuries of patient weather. You stand inside a single architectural thought roughly the size of a cathedral nave, open on three sides to the drop. The fourth side rises into a clerestory of greened copper louvers that never quite close.

Elevation here is absolute. Below the grated galleries the world simply ceases for a very long time; cloud decks form and unform in silence far underneath, and the only for ground is the suggestion of other, similar structures—faint geometric ghosts on the horizon, too distant to matter. The Nave does not sway. Its mass is settled, its joints fretted by wind into shapes that look almost geological, as if the builders had intended the erosion from the start. Everything has the unhurried dignity of something that finished becoming itself ages ago and has been quietly continuing ever since.

Moisture arrives as condensation more often than rain. Warm air rising from deep thermal stacks meets the thin cold at this height and blooms into fog that beads on every pipe flange and leaf. Soft drips fall in irregular rhythms, gathered by shallow runnels cast into the decking, led toward root-masses and cisterns. Occasionally a true squall climbs the updrafts and lashes the open sides; then the entire Nave hisses, and the lushness brightens as if voltage has been applied.

Vegetation is rampant yet never chaotic. Thick aerial roots of copper-leaved strangler figs have braided themselves through lattice girders, their trunks fused in places with the iron so that it is no longer clear which upholds which. Mosses the colour of verdigris and old blood cushion every horizontal surface an inch thick. Ferns uncurl from the throats of mute speaker-horns. Flowering vines with translucent bell-shaped blossoms weight the tension cables, opening and closing on a schedule slower than any clock still running here. The greenery is dense where it occurs, almost suffocatingly alive, yet the overall volume of the Nave remains empty: great shafts of warm air and pale light stand unoccupied between the major supports. You can walk a hundred paces across riveted plating and meet only the occasional fallen petal or the glimmer of a snail whose shell has grown iridescent from mineral exhaust.

Sound is the true architecture. It never drops below a physical pressure against the skin. Far below the visible decks, slow turbines of impossible diameter turn in baths of heavy oil, each rotation marked by a sub-audible thud that travels up through bone and iron alike. Steam valves speak at long intervals with the voice of bronze giants. Chains the thickness of tree trunks pay out or draw in across months, their links ringing like tempered bells. The cumulative effect is deafening, a continuous cathedral chord made of labor and heat, against which every closer sound—water drip, leaf rustle, footfall—must fight to be noticed. The machinery is not frantic; it is geological in pace. Gears advance one tooth at dawn and another at dusk. Pressure builds across seasons. Nothing here is in a hurry except the vines, and even they seem to climb with ceremonial patience.

Fauna thrives in the interstices. Flocks of soot-terns wheel through the upper vaults, their wings ticking against the residual heat shimmer; they nest inside the throats of cooling stacks and feed on the midges that rise from every damp cushion of moss. Larger shapes move at the edges of vision: grey, long-limbed browsers that pick iron-rich lichens from the outer cladding with cast-ceramic teeth, their joints whispering faintly as if ball-bearing grease still circulates inside living bone. Insects with abdomens like spent cartridge casings light the dusk with weak chemical lamps. None of them crowd. Presence is spaced as carefully as the columns. You can stand for an hour and see only two birds, one slow ungulate crossing a distant catwalk, the glint of many small eyes in a single patch of shadow.

Light is ordinary and therefore strange. Day enters through the open sides and the louvers in clean, dustless shafts that travel exaggerated distances before striking anything. At night the Nave makes its own modest illumination: residual furnace glow leaking through floored grilles, the bio-lights of the insects, and a few surviving arc lamps that ignite on no schedule anyone remembers, burning with the white patience of stars. Shadows are long and precisely edged. In that light the marriage of leaf and machine looks inevitable, as if industrial forms had always been a kind of slow botany.

There are rules, though no plaque states them. Do not force the valves; they will reverse on their own in eleven days or eleven years. Do not cut living metal that still conducts sap—the distinction has blurred. Walk the marked gratings when the great chain is due to move. Leave the browsers their lichen; there is enough. The place does not forbid presence, but it has never required it. People have lived here, briefly, in hammocks strung between conduit bundles, harvesting the sweet drip from certain flowers, repair-welding a seam that had begun to weep rust like blood. They leave again. The Nave continues its single, sustained note, warm and damp and green and roaring, suspended at the edge of the breathable sky, complete unto itself.
