The place is called the Hollow Reaches by those who still mark names on its walls, though the walls themselves have long since forgotten how to hold a name for long. You descend through a ragged throat of stone—no true tunnel, only a sequence of collapsed galleries worn into one another by pressures that finished their work before memory began—and emerge into openness that should not exist this far below.

The cavern does not feel like a cavern. It feels like a valley that decided, ages ago, to close its sky and keep going. The ceiling is so distant it registers only as a pale, continuous brightness, a sourceless daylight the color of weak tea left in a cool room. That light never shifts, never dims. It simply is, soaking every surface until shadows become thin, polite things that cling close to the bases of structures and then give up. The air is cool enough to keep skin raised, cool enough that breath ghosts faintly, yet never cold enough to bite. Moisture lives here as a constant low presence: a film on every face of stone, sudden beads that gather and run in threads, brief mists that bloom in the middle distance and then thin again. Somewhere, always, an unseen seam is weeping. Pools form in ancient depressions, mirror-still for hours, then drain through fractures you never quite locate. Rain, of a sort, happens—fine mineral condensation that sighs down from the height when the upper air currents change—but it is never weather so much as the cavern remembering it once knew rivers.

There are no plants. There have been none for so long that the idea of green has become a kind of myth half-carved into door lintels and then rubbed illegible. What grows is slower and stranger: sheets of translucent mineral skin that creep a finger’s width in a century, carpeting the flatter reaches in colors of milk-glass and bruised pearl. In the damper seams, soft mineral gels bloom overnight and dissolve by the next bright hour, leaving only a faint sour scent and a slickness underfoot. Crystalline whiskers nail themselves into every joint of stonework, then shear off when the ground shrugs. The world here is finished with the soft experiments of life. It has settled into mineral finality—yet that finality is not rest.

Because the Reaches will not stay still.

The turbulence is almost silent. That is the first wrongness you notice, and then the last you ever stop noticing. Whole districts rearrange themselves with the patience and indifference of weather. A plaza that stood empty at one hour will, after a long low tremor you feel more in the teeth than the ears, have gained a new ridge of collapsed wall, or lost a row of standing stones to a sudden sink. Streets—broad, shallow troughs worn by nothing but time and the passage of bodies—lengthen, kink, or simply cease. The stone itself seems to flow on timescales just faster than comfort: a polished ramp that was gentle yesterday has steepened; a gateway has narrowed until only the smallest walkers pass. Dust rises in slow vertical veils, hangs, and settles into new dunes that the next shift will abolish. It is not earthquake. It is older. The Hollow Reaches are still deciding their shape the way a cooling slag field decides, or a salt pan under wind, except the wind here is pressure and the memory of water and the constant small labors of the things that live in the cracks.

Those things are many.

Fauna thrives in the cool damp brightness with a quiet abundance that feels almost polite until you realize how completely it owns the place. Pale, long-legged crawlers move in dispersed constellations across the open floors, their jointed bodies clicking so softly the sound is mostly imagined. Flocks of something like boneless kites—thin membranes stretched on flexible spines—ride the weak updrafts near the light-soaked ceiling, banking in slow unison and never calling. In the wetter trenches, soft segmented ropes thick as a wrist pull themselves through silt, leaving shallow canals that other creatures immediately begin to use as roads. There are burrowers the size of fists that excavate perfect spiral pits overnight, and things like stone-colored disks that attach to vertical faces and graze the mineral gels with rasping tongues you cannot hear. Swarms pass. Herds of low, many-eyed grazers drift between the sparse clusters of structures, their hides the same worn gray as the oldest walls, so that at a distance a group can look like a neighborhood that has chosen to stand up and walk. They do not fear the built places. They simply move through them, over them, into them when the stone opens a new seam.

The city—if the word still applies—occupies this restless openness in scattered constellations rather than a single mass. Density is low; emptiness is the larger citizen. You can walk a long time between clusters, boots soft on the damp grit, and hear almost nothing but the occasional drip or the faint fretting of a crawler colony under a slab. Yet when you do reach a settlement it is unmistakably urban in intent: streets that once meant to meet at right angles, drainage channels cut by hand and then re-cut after every major shrug of the ground, plazas whose margins still show the ghosts of market lines. The construction is entirely of the local stone, shaped with patience and primitive craft. Surfaces bear the long scrape-marks of sanding stones, the dotted bruises of hammer-picks, the smoother facets left by bronze or hammered copper tools that have themselves long since oxidized into green ghosts in forgotten caches. Doorways are megalithic or corbelled. Upper stories are rare; when they exist they lean inward under the eternal light like people sharing a secret. Some walls are cyclopean, fitted so long ago that the joints have begun to flow. Others are stacked rubble refined by generations of hands into something almost elegant. Everywhere the wear of extreme age has rounded every corner, emptied every sharp incision, turned bas-reliefs into suggestions. The city feels less built than precipitated, then slowly edited by the same forces that edit the cavern.

No one remembers the first makers. The current inhabitants—sparse, careful people who move like the fauna, with long pauses—maintain what they can with the same simple kit: Lever, wedge, abrasive pad, fire that is coaxed from sulphur traces and dried gel-fuel, rope twisted from the fibrous sheaths the membrane-kites shed. They carve new steps when the old ones tilt too far. They prop lintels. They sweep the mineral skin from the meeting floors before it can cement. Their work is endless and nearly silent. Conversations happen in gestures or in words pitched below the level of echo. Children learn early not to shout; the cavern prefers quiet, and large sounds sometimes wake larger shifts in the stone.

There is a mild wrongness underscoring everything, the kind that never quite becomes fear and never quite becomes normal. Directions drift. A street you have walked a hundred times may deliver you to a familiar plaza by an unfamiliar angle, as though the map itself has been gently crumpled and smoothed again overnight. Reflections in the intermittent pools sometimes show the ceiling light as a pure white disc, sometimes as a soft rectangular glow, sometimes as nothing at all while the real air remains bright. Certain corners collect a faint humming in the bones though the ear reports silence. And once in a long while the fauna pause—all of them, crawlers and kites and grazers together—and face the same empty point in the air for the space of twenty heartbeats before resuming, as if an order had been given in a frequency no human body owns.

You can stand on a high worn plinth at the edge of one of the larger clusters and look out across the Reches and feel the scale of it: not infinite, but large enough that the farthest settlements are only suggestions of geometry under the even light. Between them the land (the floor, the drifted dust, the low rolling waves of ancient collapse) lies mostly open, sparsely stitched with the slow traffic of creatures and the occasional thin thread of a channel carrying seepage from one dark mouth to another. The air smells of wet stone, of faint metallic tang, of the clean neutrality of a place that has outlived its own history. Every surface glistens faintly. Every silence is textured by damp and by the nearly imperceptible working of uncounted small lives.

The Hollow Reaches do not feel abandoned. They feel completed, then reopened by forces that have no interest in completion. The city clings to its scattered upright habits. The bright cool dimness (bright, yet somehow never harsh) continues without source or schedule. The stone keeps rewriting the ground plan in long, quiet revisions. And somewhere always, a bead of water gathers on an overhang, grows heavy, and falls—one soft tick in a world that has learned how to change almost without sound.
