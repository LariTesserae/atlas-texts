The plain begins nowhere and continues past every horizon that tries to contain it. What first appears as soil is not soil: it is a compressed mat of former leaves, bark-plates, and root-cables pressed so long and so evenly that they have become a single continuous floor—gray-green, faintly luminous, cool to any touch that still remembers skin. Nothing here has ever been wet. The chemistry runs on light, on mineral dust scoured from deeper strata, and on the slow oxidation of metallic lignin that the plants themselves secrete and reabsorb. Moisture is a rumor from other epochs; the air tastes of chalk and static.

Everything that stands is grown and everything that grows is built. Trunks rise in deliberate ranks, not as trees but as load-bearing columns whose heartwood has been lathed, in living increments, into helical gears and pressure chambers. Their bark is plated with overlapping scales of oxidized brass-green, each scale a photovoltaic lamina that drinks the white, shadowless glare pouring from above. The light has no single source. It is emitted by vast ceiling-vaults of translucent canopy hundreds of meters overhead—canopies that are themselves industrial: leaves fused into continuous skylights, veins thickened into bus-bars conducting bio-electric current. Between the columns hang curtains of monofilament vine, motionless yet never still; microscopic barbs along their length snag airborne particulates and sort them, molecule by molecule, into feedstock. The sound of this sorting is almost nothing—an occasional dry tick, like a seed falling inside a sealed gourd.

The density is close. Walk any path and the next structure is already brushing your shoulder: buttressed walls of woven stem, crawlspaces of interlocking pneumatophores, gantries that are simply horizontal boughs drilled with bearing races and still alive enough to thicken if load increases. Catwalks of braided aerial root lead to platforms where the real machines open. Here the vegetation has been trained into factories without ever ceasing to be vegetation. Petal-plates peel back on circadian timers to reveal spinning armatures of xylem and extruded silicate. Pollen is not reproductive; it is powdered catalyst, released in timed bursts that drift like ochre fog through the lower galleries and settle on reaction beds of spongy cambium. The beds darken, swell, and slowly extrude finished components—cog-leaves, circuit-berries, flexible pipe-stems—that are carried away by silent, wheeled galls along grooves worn smooth over centuries. The entire production cycle makes less noise than wind through dry grass, yet it never stops. Shift changes are marked only by a faint brightening or dimming of the interior cellular phosphors.

Because the plane is continental in reach and low in elevation, weather develops inside it. Slow pressure collisions between districts of differing photosynthetic intensity create broad, cool fronts that move like invisible tides. They carry no rain. Instead they roll banks of reflective seed-down and metallic loft that catch the omnipresent light and turn whole regions into temporary white-outs of glittering hush. When the loft storms pass, the settled dust is already being reclaimed by surface mycelial conveyor felts that drag it toward the nearest processing thicket. These cycles have repeated so long that every surface is rounded, soft-edged, established. Nothing sharp remains; even the jagged memory of original construction has been grown over and worn into equilibrium. The place feels ancient in the way a coral reef is ancient, or a river delta—except the river here is dry current and the coral is alloyed cellulose.

Fauna is almost absent. Once in a great while something small and many-legged, half-insect half-tool, scuttles from one maintenance fissure to another and vanishes. Larger animals never took. The architecture simply left no niche. What moves instead are the systems themselves: elevators of coiled tendril that rise and fall without motors, floodgates of stomatal plates that dilate to vent waste heat as shimmering dry aurora, neighborhoods of tower-trees that slowly reconfigure their internal plumbing according to demand signals traveling as chemical polarity waves. The whole vast colony is in continuous low-grade reconstruction of itself.

And then the real irregularity arrives. At certain densities—where too many living conduits cross, or where light reflects from too many polished interior surfaces at once—the geometry loses patience with three dimensions. A corridor lengthens while you watch it, not by deceit of perspective but by actual insertion of new meters of grown floor that did not exist a breath earlier. Staircases of compressed leaf-litter spiral into themselves and open onto the same landing inverted. Sound itself can be subtracted: whole districts go preternaturally mute as if the air has decided to stop carrying vibration, then resume a moment later with a soft pop of restored physics. Shadows detach and hang in mid-air like dark, frayed banners before slowly dissolving back into the bright vegetated glare. These breakdowns are not disasters; they are weather of another order, long accommodated. Markers of living metal have been grown at their edges—spiral scars in the bark that warn or invite, depending on how the light hits them. Travelers who understand the language of those scars simply wait, or take the longer route through less saturated volumes, or step through and accept that they may arrive somewhere angle-shifted, carrying a faint after-image of leaves that never quite existed.

Standing anywhere inside it, you feel the coolness first—the steady 10 or 12 degrees that never warms because the canopy-ceiling radiates heat upward into an empty black stratosphere the plants long ago sealed away. Then the brightness, shadowless and permanent, filtered through living glass. Then the quiet density of processes that never need to raise their voice. The floor is dry. The air is dry. The machines are green and breathing. The horizon keeps its promise of more of the same forever, only larger, only weirder at the junctions, only more completely itself.
