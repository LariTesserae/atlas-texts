The chamber sits fifty meters under the old floodplain, where the limestone long ago surrendered to root and seepage. It is no vast cavern; the space closes around you at the scale of a ruined basilica or a market square left to itself—walls near enough that the far side remains a suggestion of leaf-shadow rather than distance. Everything here has already finished becoming. The forms are settled, abraded smooth by centuries of the same warm breath of air, the same slow drip, the same passage of bodies.

Plant is the ground and the architecture and the sky. Not a covering, but the substrate itself. Massive column-trunks rise at irregular intervals, their bark fused into load-bearing ribs that arch overhead into a vault of intergrown canopy. Between them the floor is a springy mat of compressed moss, liverwort, and the flat, rubbery pads of something that might once have been fern. Walls are living palisades: woven stem and aerial root thickened into buttresses, riddled with hollow tubes that once carried sap and now also carry warm, mineral-dark water and low-voltage current. The whole structure has the weary precision of something that has been repaired by growth for longer than memory. Edges are rounded. Joints have been sealed by resin directly from the trees. Where metal was once driven in, the wood has simply closed over it and kept working.

It is a town, or the ghost of one that never stopped functioning. Platforms and galleries hang at different heights, grown rather than built, connected by ladders of living vine that tighten or slacken with the hour. At the center stands the old press-hall: a circle of trunk-engines whose pistons are heartwood cylinders still pulsing with hydraulic sap. Flywheels of compressed lignum turn with a wet, muscular sound. Belts of woven bast fiber run between them, frayed and regrown, driving smaller machines that stamp, spin, and cut. What they produce is never entirely clear—sheets of translucent leaf-leather, coils of conductive root-wire, blocks of dense fungal brick—but the work continues without pause. Occasional electric bulbs, glass long since clouded by condensation and pollen, hang in cages of thorn; they glow a tired amber that barely reaches the floor. Most of the light is biological and dimmer still: colonies of luminous mycelium traced along the undersides of leaves, slow blue-green pulses like underwater lightning.

The air is warm and permanently damp. Moisture does not fall so much as exhale. Fine mist rises from the black rills that thread the floor, feeds the constant sweat on every surface, beads on the undersides of leaves until the beads grow heavy and drop with soft, irregular percussion. Deeper channels carry slow water that smells of iron and crushed greenery. In pockets the floor softens into standing pools no larger than a bath, their surfaces quivering with the vibration of the engines. Nothing here is dry for long; cloth, skin, fur, chitin all take on the same faint sheen.

Sound is a physical pressure. The engines thump and hiss in overlapping rhythms. Overhead, the canopy creaks as weight redistributes. But the true volume belongs to the fauna. They are everywhere and in numbers that defy the apparent sparseness of the space. The openness between the great trunks is never empty for more than a breath. Flights of small, sharp-winged things pour through the mist like living smoke, their wing-cases clattering. Soft, multilimbed grazers the size of housecats move in loose herds across the moss, mouths busy, leaving trails of faintly glowing saliva. Higher up, heavier shapes clamber—elongated, six-eyed, half-furred and half-barked—using the galleries as highways. They call to one another in overlapping registers: clicks, wet whistles, deep drumming that resonates in the hollow trunks. Periodically a larger predator (all hinged jaw and mottled leaf-pattern hide) drops from above with a sound like wet canvas tearing; the resulting panic is a white roar of wings and scrabbling claws that takes minutes to subside. Even the insects are loud: finger-long beetles whose stridulation rises to a metallic shriek when they fight over sap-flows or the oily discharge of the machines.

The density remains strangely low. Bodies pass, swarms turn, yet the eye still finds long clear lanes of mist and quiet moss. Presence is intense but not crowded; the space holds its emptiness the way a lung holds air between breaths. Everything moves through everything else without quite filling it.

There is an established ecology of use. Machines have been colonized. Cooling vanes are gardens of specialized moss that regulate temperature. Gear teeth are cleaned by dedicated beetles that nest in the crankcases. Some of the smaller engines appear to have become partially animal: muscular bladders contract in time with the pistons, and bunches of nerve-like rootlets link them to the larger plant body so that a surge of sugar in the canopy can speed production or slow it. The fauna, in turn, treat the industrial processes as just another set of seasons. They time their nesting to the warm exhaust cycles. They drink from the condensate pipes. Certain moths lay eggs only on the freshly stamped leaf-leather while it is still soft. Nothing is pure machine or pure organism anymore; the distinction has been weathered away.

Time feels thick. Growth is visible if you watch long enough—a tendril advancing, a gear slowly jacketed in new wood. Mist rises and falls in slow interior weather. Every few hours a deeper vibration rolls through the chamber as some distant pump or root-heart shifts pressure; water curtains suddenly thicken, animals freeze or erupt into motion, the luminescent colonies flare and then dim. The place is never still, yet nothing feels hurried. It has found the pace at which industrial repetition and biological urgency become the same motion.

You can walk the entire circuit in sequence of breaths. At one end the great trunk-engines labor; at the other a quieter district of hanging trellis-houses where the smaller fauna sleep in shifts, their bodies packed into living alcoves that breathe with them. Between lie open courts of moss where the mist lies knee-deep and the only light is the intermittent blue stutter of the mycelium. Everywhere the same warm damp, the same layered roar, the same sense that the world has folded itself into this single, self-repairing, half-wild factory-grove and intends to stay.
