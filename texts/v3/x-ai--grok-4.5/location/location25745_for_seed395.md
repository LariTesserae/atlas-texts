The plain does not end. From any rise no higher than a standing body you can turn a full circle and the same coloured distance will meet itself again—ochre, bone, and the dull green of things that refuse to yellow. This is a continent of floor. The ground is young rock still deciding how to settle: thin plates of mineral crust overlapping like cooling slag, soft enough in places that a heavy tread leaves a temporary basin, hard enough in others that claws and roots leave only white scratches. Nothing here has been weathered long. Edges are sharp. Fresh blisters of salt and silica push up overnight, collapse by afternoon, leaving honeycombed scars that the next morning’s crust will partly fill.

There is almost no free water. What moisture exists is locked inside tissues, held in fat leaves the size of shields, in corky trunks swollen like stored breath, in the bodies of the animals that move between them. Dawn sometimes lays a breath-thin film of dew that vanishes before a shadow can form; the rest of the day is dust and the dry glitter of heat. Yet the vegetation is dense past reason. It does not spread; it packs. Columnar succulents press so tightly that their fluted sides polish one another smooth. Low, sprawling mats of silver-haired groundcover leave no soil visible, only a continuous pelt that crackles underfoot like thin ice. Above them rise the true masses: living buttresses and galleries of interlaced woody stems, some thick as house walls, grown in slow competition until they form closed alleys, courtyards, vaulted passages. These are not planted and not built by hands. They are simply what happens when every available cubic length is claimed by something that photosynthesizes under a warm, ordinary sun and will not yield space.

The effect, kilometer after kilometer, is urban without artifice. There are thoroughfares where the larger columns stand in rough ranks and the canopy overhead thins to a broken ceiling of sky. There are districts of finer texture—dense thickets of finger-thick spires no taller than a person’s shoulder, packed so that an animal must choose its path with care or not move at all. There are open plazas of pure mineral crust where nothing has yet rooted, pale and ringing underfoot, already being colonized at the edges by seedling rosettes the color of oxidized copper. From high enough—though elevation is rare and modest—the whole complex reads as a single continuous settlement whose walls and roofs are alive and still growing. No tools shaped it. No one maintains it. The architecture is pure consequence of saturation: when every niche is occupied, growth itself becomes structure.

Fauna is everywhere in the interstices. Small armored grazers the size of clasped hands rasp at the ground mats with quiet, continuous clicks, their shells the same ochre as the crust so that only motion betrays them. Larger browsers move in slow herds along the wider ways, their flat feet spreading weight so the young crust does not break; they feed by scraping cuttings from the column flanks, leaving pale scars that scab over with new green within days. Predators are present but unhurried—long-limbed and lightly furred, they wait in the cooler vertical shade of the tallest growths and take what the denseness offers. Overhead, dry-winged things the size of open hands plane in near silence, riding thermals that the black mineral surfaces throw up by midday. Their shadows cross the living roofs in slow, repeating patterns. Everything eats, grows, excretes, dies, and is immediately replaced; the ecology turns over at a biological pace, audible mostly as a low collective rustle, the soft grind of mandibles, the occasional dry rattle of a loosened plate of bark sliding down a trunk. Louder sounds are rare and do not carry far. The air itself seems to absorb them.

Weather is local and self-generated. Heat builds above the unbroken roof until pillars of shimmer rise, drawing dust into faint rotating veils that travel for tens of kilometers before collapsing. At night the crust radiates, the packed vegetation holds a little residual warmth, and the temperature settles into a mild, dry dark. No storms of water come. What passes for a front is a long front of cooler air rolling down from some remote edge of the plain, silencing even the small grazers for an hour and leaving the uppermost leaves faintly misted. By full light the moisture is gone again and the slow industry of growth resumes—new joints swelling, new side shoots nosing into any gap the night’s deaths have left, new mineral blisters raising themselves between roots.

The place is still becoming. You can walk a day and pass from crust so fresh it flexes like damp leather into older tracts where the living architecture has stood long enough to trap windblown grit and form the beginnings of true soil in its shadowed angles. Even there the rock underneath remains raw, angular, only lightly stained by the tannins and salts the roots exude. Nothing feels finished. The density is not static; it is the density of a process that has nowhere left to expand except upward and into itself. In the rare clearings the silence is almost total—just the tick of contracting stone as the day’s heat slips away—yet all around, through walls of living wood and fleshy leaf, the continent continues its quiet, relentless occupation of every interval.
