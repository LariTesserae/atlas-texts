The high basin has no floor that was ever stone. Whatever bedrock once formed the bowl between the three ridges has long since disappeared under the continuous labor of bodies. What remains is a single, continent-sized nest of compacted dung, saliva-hardened mud, interlaced root, shed antler, broken chitin, and the slow lignin of trees that were never allowed to stand alone. Every surface is load-bearing. Every surface was placed.

You move through warm dusk. The canopy is not foliage in the ordinary sense; it is a stitched ceiling of living branches forced horizontally by generations of climbers, then roofed again by mats of epiphyte and the paper nests of social wasps the size of fists. Light arrives only as a green-black seep, a moisture that has already forgotten the sky. Condensation beads on every overhang and falls in slow, irregular ticks. Occasional rain does not strike the ground; it is caught, siphoned, re-routed through grooves worn smooth by centuries of the same dripping path, until it pools in the shallow, hoof-polished basins that serve as both cistern and wallow.

The air is thick with breath and fur and the sweet rot of crushed leaves. Temperature never drops; the mass of living and once-living and packed organic matter holds the day’s thin highland warmth the way a compost heap does, exhaling it slowly through the nights that are only slightly darker than the days. Everything here is damp to the touch—not wet, but permanently humid, the way the inside of a closed mouth is humid.

Sound is total. It is not one cry but the layered continuum of millions: the wet click of mandibles pruning new growth, the thunderous lowing of the heavy grazers that move in slow processions along the ramparts, the continuous silk-tearing noise of web-spinners repairing the suspension bridges that stitch one gallery to the next, the wing-roar of the night-jars that never truly night, the scraping of claws on the polished ramps, the soft avalanche of fresh pellets being Very carefully patted into a weakened buttress. There is no quiet interval. The sound has the density of water; it presses the eardrums until thought itself takes on a pulsing rhythm.

The architecture is patient. No single creature understands the whole. Regions of wall fourteen body-lengths thick have been under repair for longer than any living memory, the outer skin weathered into soft concavities where rain and flanks and wind have agreed on a shape. Elsewhere the structure flares into hanging amphitheaters whose tiers are lined with soft moss cultivated for nesting. Tunnels spiral downward through older strata, their walls glossy with the passage of countless shoulders, opening into chambers where the air is warmer still and the darkness complete. In these deeper rooms the great soft-bodied excavators move like living bellows, packing fresh material into any seam that has begun to weep moisture. Their work is almost invisible on the scale of a season; on the scale of centuries the entire basin breathes, settles, thickens.

Vegetation is not separate from construction. The trees that still manage vertical ambition do so only because their trunks have been incorporated as load-bearing columns, their bark scarred into staircases by climbing claws. Vines are trained—by habit, by appetite, by the simple repeated weight of bodies—into braided hawsers. Lushness here means saturation: every horizontal surface furred with mosses and liverworts, every vertical face stitched with fern and the pale suckers of parasitic flowers that open in the permanent gloom and are pollinated by the same midges that feed on the dung. There is no bare mineral left to erode. Erosion itself has become a kind of slow editing performed by tongues, by rain channeled through old tooth-grooves, by the gentle abrasions of fur.

From any high rampart—if you climb the living ladders of root and rib—the basin reveals itself as a single continuous artifact stretching to the haze of the far ridges: a brown-green city without streets, only gradients of density, galleries opening into galleries, the whole mass gently terraced by the forgotten logic of drainage and herd movement. Nothing is ruined; nothing is new. The place has simply reached the state in which further building and further wearing cancel each other into permanence. The animals do not leave because there is nowhere else that is not already this. Their generations are the mortar.

At the very center the oldest cores have compacted into something almost stone-like, dark and faintly warm to the palm. Even there the sound still arrives, transmitted through the mass itself, a deafening, velvet vibration that never ceases. The basin does not sleep. It only continues, thickly, warmly, in the dark.
