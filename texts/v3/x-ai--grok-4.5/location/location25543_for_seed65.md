The plateau does not so much rise as simply continue, long after the last trees have quit and the last reasonable elevations have been left behind. You have been climbing through thinning air for days, and then the pitch gentles and the world opens into a bright, cold country of stone that has already finished becoming what it is. Nothing here is still in the process of forming. The ridges, the soft-edged mesas, the long parallel flutes cut by older water—all of it has the settled patience of something that reached its shape a very long time ago and has been weathering in place ever since, losing only dust.

The light is relentless and nearly shadowless at midday, a high white pressure that flattens depth and makes every pale surface glare. The sky is a hard, thinned blue that feels closer than it should. Temperature never quite leaves the realm of cool; even in full sun the stone stays chill under the hand, and the wind that never stops carries a mineral cold into every fold of cloth and skin. That wind is the first fact of the place. It does not gust. It does not pause. It pours steadily out of the northwest across hundreds of open kilometers of bare upland, finding every gap between the old erosional forms and singing there—an enormous, continuous chord of friction and pressure that sits in the bones of the skull after an hour and becomes, after a day, simply the medium in which thought occurs. Conversations, if anyone still tried them, would have to be shouted mouth-to-ear. Most living things here have stopped trying.

Water exists as a series of discreet, temporary mercies: thin seeps that darken the base of a crag for a few weeks after the rare high storm; knife-narrow rills that appear in the mornings from rime and vanish by afternoon; isolated tarns no larger than a room, cupped in old scour hollows and skimmed with ice even in what passes for summer. Around these damp pockets the only vegetation clusters—low, glaucous cushions, black-green mats of something like moss that is not moss, and wiry shrubs no higher than the ankle that lock themselves into cracks and wait. Between the pockets stretch long reaches of pale grit, wind-burnished pebbles, and the exposed ribbing of bedrock. The eye travels far before it finds another green mark.

And yet the country is not empty of life. It is empty of clutter, of close horizons, of anything that would interrupt the long sightlines—but it teems. Herds of low, dense-bodied grazers move in slow tides across the grit, their coats the same mineral colors as the stone so that a group of forty can vanish while you watch. Above them the air is stitched with wings: endless traffic of harsh-voiced birds that ride the permanent wind without apparent effort, and darker, heavier flyers that drop onto carrion or onto the unwary almost before the mind has registered their stoop. In the rubble fields and the shadowed underside of every ledge there are smaller existences—clicking, armored things; soft-footed nocturnal rummagers that leave neat print-constellations in the rare damp sand; pale spiders the size of hands that string their silk in the lee of boulders where the wind’s note drops half a tone. The abundance is lateral and patient rather than lush. Life here has had geological time to fit itself into the unchanging regime of cold light and moving air, and it has done so thoroughly. There are hours when the combined voices of wind, wings, and the dry percussion of countless small feet and shells rise into something like a single deafening fabric, a sound so total it erases the idea of silence.

No roads. No walls. No lasting marks of manufacture except, very rarely, a line of purposefully set stones that someone once raised to shoulder height as a windbreak or a marker and then abandoned to the same slow abrasion that shapes everything else. Whoever still crosses this country travels light and leaves almost nothing: a scraped hide, a bifacial blade of the local chert, a cairn added to by strangers over generations until it becomes another weathered outcrop indistinguishable from the rest. The technology that can survive here is the technology that can be carried in two hands and repaired with what the plateau itself drops—flake, cordage of tough root, bone needle. Nothing more complex endures the wind for long.

There is a mild wrongness that never quite resolves into threat. Distances misbehave in the uniform light; a ridge that looks an hour away may take half a day, or you may crest it in twenty minutes and feel mildly cheated. The wind’s pitch sometimes seems to carry fragmentary harmonics, as if somewhere far out on the plateau the air were being forced through shapes more deliberate than stone. At the edges of vision the flocks occasionally slant in ways that suggest a shared decision rather than simple aerodynamics. None of it announces itself as impossible. It is only that the place has been left alone with its own rules for so long that those rules have grown slightly private.

Night, when it comes, is relative. The sky darkens to a deep, glowing indigo rather than black, and the wind’s note does not change. The animals shift their shifts. The seeps freeze into thin lenses that will glaze and thin again under tomorrow’s identical sun. Nothing is building toward a future different from this. The plateau has already arrived at the condition it means to keep, and every living and non-living thing upon it has long since adjusted its clocks accordingly. You walk in a bright, cold, roaring equilibrium that has no intention of becoming anything else.
