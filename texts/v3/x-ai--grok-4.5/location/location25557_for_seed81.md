The plateau does not so much rise as insist. From lower valleys you climb through thinning air and thickening heat until the world levels into a wide basalt saucer several days across, its rim mountains softened by ages of steam into rounded black shoulders. Elevation here is not cold. The rock itself is warm underfoot, scored with older flows and newer cracks that weep mineral water the color of weak tea. Heat comes from below in a steady geothermal breath, so the air stays thick and wet even as it thins. A permanent low ceiling of condensed vapor hangs fifty meters up, dimming everything to the gray-orange of banked forge light. Day and night differ mostly by how the steam glows.

No green thing holds. Whatever seeds once blew in long ago simply cooked and mineralized; the ground rejects chlorophyll. Instead the wet places grow skins of iridescent bacteria and slow crystal gardens—selenite blades, clusters of yellow sulfur roses, dendritic manganese that looks like fossilized lightning. These spread in the shallow, steaming meres and along the weeping cliffs, then stop. Between them stretches open black mud and shattered pahoehoe, empty enough that a walker can travel an hour without brushing anything taller than a boot.

Yet the place is urban. Across this open basin stand the works: vast, sparse, industrial skeletons spaced like chess pieces on a board too large for the game. Each complex is a town unto itself—foundries, pump halls, cooling towers, rail viaducts—built of basalt block and riveted iron that has long since gone to deep ochre rust and white mineral scale. They do not cluster. One great assembly might occupy a low ridge; the next waits four kilometers away across steaming flats, linked only by elevated pipe-runs and monorail spines that cross the emptiness on spider-leg piers. Inside the buildings the volumes are cathedral-huge and nearly vacant. Catwalks span darkness. Miles of ductwork still thrum with geothermal pressure redirected through turbines that have no living supervisors. The machinery is not abandoned so much as indifferent; it cycles because the heat and the water keep arriving, and the valves were set generations ago into patient equilibria.

Sound rarely rises above a hush. There is the constant soft exhalation of vents, the drip of condensate from overhead pipes, the occasional deep clunk as some thermal expansion joint shifts. Once in a long while a distant rail crawler grinds along its elevated track, empty cars traveling from one silent complex to another according to schedules no one updates. Rain comes often and sideways, warm as blood, drilling the mud into new temporary channels that the next day’s heat partially erases. The erosion here is mature: everything has already found the shape it will keep. Edges are rounded, foundations half-swallowed by mineral crust, iron fretted into lace that somehow still bears weight.

Fauna is a rumor. Pale, heat-tolerant amphipods flick through the darker pools. Something larger—shell like a kiln lid, too many jointed legs—sometimes drags itself between cooling ponds at the edge of vision and is gone. Birds do not come. The air is too thin of insects, too thick of sulfur. Yet the emptiness feels watched in the way old turbine halls feel watched: not by eyes but by residual attention left in the wiring.

The strangeness is quieter still. Walk the long pipe bridges at the dim hours and the steam sometimes coheres into vertical sheets that hold the silhouettes of workers who are not there—gestures of checking gauges, of calling to one another—then tear without sound. Certain plazas between the works collect rain that refuses to reflect the sky; instead the water shows slow-moving constellations or the interiors of rooms that do not exist in any standing building. Pumps that have had no fuel or maintenance for decades continue to lift black water into cooling towers, and the water leaves those towers cleaner than physics should allow, as if something unseen is still being refined out of the landscape and discarded into vapor. The whole plateau feels like an engine whose purpose was forgotten after the engine proved more durable than the purpose.

At the center, where several pipe-runs converge, a single freestanding cooling tower larger than the rest bleeds white steam into the permanent cloud-roof. Around its base the ground is crazed with stress fractures that glow dull red at night. The tower’s interior is a spiral of dry, hot dark. No one lives here. No one needs to. The processes continue—water lifted and dropped, heat exchanged, metal tick-tick-ticking through its expansion cycles—across a city scaled for tens of thousands and occupied by almost no one. The emptiness is not absence; it is the final product.
