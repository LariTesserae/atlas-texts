The place is called the Underflat by those who still remember names for it, though the remembering itself has slowed to a geological pace. It lies far below any surface weather, a regional open of linked chambers and galleries whose ceilings have mostly collapsed inward over spans of time no calendar measures. What remains is a single vast, near-level plain of pale mineral—salt fused with powdered gypsum and older silicates—stretched under a dim, ochre half-light that never brightens and never fully fails. The light seeps from slow-growing veins of luminous lichen that have colonized the last high remnants of the old vaults; it is cool light, the color of old bone, and it leaves long soft shadows that barely move.

There is almost no free water. The air tastes of dry salt and powdered stone. What moisture exists is locked inside the tissues of the living things or bound as faint hydration shells around dust motes that take centuries to settle. The ground itself is the remnant of an ocean that died so long ago its memory has become the terrain: a final, stabilized crust of evaporite, smoothed by patient abrasion into gentle swells and shallow basins kilometers across. Nothing sharp remains. Edges have been rounded into shoulders. Columns that once held ceilings have slumped into low, soft-sided mounds. The whole expanse has settled into its oldest stable shape—an eroded city that is no longer quite architecture and not yet pure geology.

That city is still legible. Low walls of fitted stone, now half-melted by deep time, trace out blocks and avenues. Broad plazas open between them, empty enough that a walker can go half a day without brushing a surface. The construction is dense in plan yet sparse in residual bulk: foundations everywhere, standing bulk rare. No metal, no glass, no worked wood survives. Whatever hands or processes raised the place used only the local mineral, stacked dry or fused by long pressure. The result is urban in scale and layout—districts, thoroughfares, graduated sizes of enclosure—yet wholly without device. It is pre-tool permanence, or post-tool forgetting; the distinction has worn away.

Across and through this skeletal grid grows the lushness. It is not the lushness of rainforests. It is a dry lushness of pale, fleshy rosettes and brittle silver fans that root directly into the salt. They form open woodlands of low, multi-stemmed structures whose branches never tangle because the spaces between them are kept clear by the slow resuspension of dust. In the basins the growth thickens into layered mats—cool, rubbery, faintly phosphorescent—that look almost aquatic until the foot discovers they crunch like thin ice. These mats are the greening of an end-state desert: every structure maximized for surface area and mineral scavenging, every color bleached to ash-green, lavender-gray, dull gold. They do not sway. They enlarge by macroscopic increments across seasons that last decades, adding a millimeter of tissue while the air stays motionless around them.

Fauna is everywhere and nowhere dense. Herds of low, many-legged grazers move in loose constellations across the avenues, their plated bodies the same pale mineral hue as the ground; they scrape the mats with rasping mouthparts and leave behind broad, polished trails that take years to dull again. Above them, in the residual height of half-fallen galleries, hang colonies of membrane-winged things that never truly fly so much as sled on the faintest density currents, clicking in enormous numbers. Smaller hard-shelled scavengers lace the wall bases in slow rivers of carapace. Nothing here is hurried. Birth, grazing, molting, and collapse into new substrate all unfold on timescales of quiet accumulation. Yet the sound is deafening.

It is a continuous, layered roar without source or peak. Part of it is the dry friction of countless limbs and plates shifting against salt. Part is the deep, subsonic settling of the Underflat itself—ancient voids compacting one grain at a time, transmitting through the floor as a permanent thunder felt in the bones. Part is the collective stridulation of the membrane-wings, a sound like distant rockfall that never ceases. The ear finds no quiet pocket. Even the sparse intervals between herds are filled with the same oceanic noise, only slightly thinned. Over long listening the roar resolves into slow pulses, almost tidal, though there is no tide and no sea—only the great population breathing and scraping in unison across geologic afternoons.

Temperature holds steady and cool, the residual chill of deep rock that no longer remembers sun. Dust hangs in the dimness for years before choosing a surface. Occasional dry falls of loosened ceiling material descend in lazy curtains that take a day to reach the floor; they make no change that cannot be walked around. The ecology has closed its loops so tightly that nothing is wasted and almost nothing is new. Vegetation digests stone and shed carapace. Fauna digests vegetation and one another at the measured pace of necessity. The ruined avenues serve as migration paths that have been the same paths since the walls were taller than memory. Everything has already become what it was going to be.

In the farthest basins, where the mats grow thickest and the herds thin to individuals, the black openings of still-deeper shafts puncture the plain. From them rises a slightly fuller version of the same roar, as if the Underflat continues in stacked iterations below, each level more completely flattened than the last, each still carrying its open city of salt and its pale, patient green, its abundant lives spaced wide under the dim, unending noise.
