The high plateau does not begin with stone. Stone is only a rumor far below, a pressure and a memory. What holds the elevation is plant: a single continuous body of living mass that has had geological time to settle into itself, to weather and re-weather, to forget the difference between trunk and terrain. From a distance the place looks like a deeply folded valley system carved from dull green-black jade and then left out in the cold for a thousand centuries. Up close the folds resolve into boles wider than houses, root flanges that have become cliffs, and horizontal beam-branches that have anastomosed into long silent causeways. Everything is load-bearing vegetation. There is no bare ground.

It is cold enough that breath would show, if anyone were here to breathe. Frost never quite finishes its work; it forms, melts into beads under the slight geothermal warmth that still rises through the living mass, then re-forms as rime in the next hour. The moisture regime is almost entirely this slow internal weather. True rain is rare and brief—an hour of needle-fine cold drops that the uppermost canopy catches and redistributes as cold sweats and sudden interior drips. Elsewhere water lives as films, as blisters of condensation inside hollow stems, as dark pockets of melt caught in crotches fifty meters deep. These pockets are the closest thing to rivers: slow vertical seep-lines that migrate over years, leaving mineral stains like old industrial runoff.

Light arrives normally, a high-altitude clarity that should feel sharp, yet the density turns it pearlescent within a few meters. Canopy layers do not so much block light as delay and re-color it. You walk (or rather pick your way along the great branch roads) in a perpetual late-afternoon green-gold even at noon. Deeper still, in the under-architecture where old collapsed trunks have been tunnelled and braced by newer growth, the light becomes a cave dusk that never quite goes black.

The density is saturating without being chaotic in space; it is chaotic in time. Every surface is already occupied by some living thickness—mosses that have become peat cliffs, epiphytic shrubs whose root-mats function as soil, curtain lichens thick as felt. Vacant volume is temporary and suspect. Yet the whole expanse remains open at the regional scale. Ridgelines of fused canopy run for kilometers; amphitheaters open where an ancient super-trunk sheared and the gap was colonised by regular, almost coppiced ranks of pale-barked giants. You can see weather moving on the next massif of forest. The place has the spatial grammar of a worn-in mountain range, only the mountains are alive and slowly locomoting through growth and collapse.

Sound is nearly absent. Wind is fractured into harmless eddying by the layered crowns long before it reaches anything that could moan. The occasional drip is absorbed by moss. Even the rare fauna—thin-legged, felt-covered browsers the size of deer that appear once every few days and then vanish—move without footfall, their pads adapted to the perpetual give of living bark. What remains is a pressure in the ears, the sense that enormous work is being done just below the threshold of hearing.

That work is the turbulence. The forest is not calm; it is only quiet. Entire sectors enter spasm-growth without warning. A buttress root can thicken by a meter overnight, shouldering lesser trunks aside with the slow inevitability of a glacier. Elsewhere a venerable beam-branch loses some internal negotiation of sap pressure and suddenly unloads, dropping its outer two hundred meters of self in a silent green avalanche that is already being re-colonised before the dust of shredded leaves settles. These events have the scale of industrial accidents and the frequency of weather. New structural solutions appear—lattices, counterweights of water-filled bladders, tension cables of living vine extruded at astonishing speed—then are abandoned when the local stress geometry changes again. The plateau is forever redesigning its own load paths. It has been doing so long enough that the failures and reinforcements have weathered into a kind of settled savagery: scarred, overbuilt, elegant in the way of something that has survived its own over-engineering.

There are no constructed objects. No cut timber, no metal, no paths that were not grown. Yet the industrial quality is inescapable. Vascular bundles run like pipework, some of them under enough pressure that a wound sprays cold, sweet sap for hours. Certain hollow trunks touch subterranean warmth and act as low-grade boilers; their interiors cycle air with a slow piston rhythm you feel rather than hear. Massive mycelial mats underlying whole districts function as distributed chemical plants, breaking down the continuous rain of self-litter into usable sugars and belching faint, clean clouds of waste vapor at dusk. The forest has the metabolism of a factory complex that has forgotten why it began manufacturing and now manufactures only more factory.

At the weird edge of this, some of the larger boles have developed what can only be called workshops: chambers where temperature, humidity, and nutrient drip are held within narrow tolerances, and where the living walls extrude precise, replaceable parts—new growth tips, hardened spine-leaves, root-caps already shaped for future fretting. These chambers open and close over weeks. No animal tends them. They seem to be the forest tending itself, tool-using without minds as we understand them. The parts are used or reabsorbed. Nothing is stored. Nothing is stockpiled. The only product is the continued, turbulent competence of the plateau.

Stand long enough on any high run of fused limbs and you feel the entire region as a single slow engine whose cylinders are centuries and whose fuel is light and thin air and the damp ghosts of rain. It is neither paradise nor hostile. It is simply finished with the idea of unfinishedness. Every collapse is already budgeted. Every silence is working. The cold keeps the vat from spoiling. The rare animals pass through like half-remembered thoughts. And the living mass, established beyond any founding date, continues its open, dense, nearly soundless industrial eternity of becoming and unbecoming itself.
