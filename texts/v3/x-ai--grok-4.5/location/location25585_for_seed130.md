The ground is not ground. It never was. What holds you is a single continuous pour that once covered every horizon—an artificial basalt of sintered slag, rebar-veined concrete, and cooled furnace glass, laid down in an age when the world still believed in foundations. Time has not cracked it so much as polished it into finality. The surface is an endless low plain of soft undulations, each swell the collapsed lung of some vanished workshop, each shallow trough a canal that no longer remembers which factory it was meant to feed. Elevation never rises more than a few car-heights; the whole region has settled into the same tired horizontal, as if the planet itself grew weary of upright things.

Water is everywhere and nowhere ambitious. It does not fall so much as exhale. The air is a cool, permanent damp—breathing-temperature, the chill of metal left overnight. Condensation beads on every vertical surface and runs in slow sheets. Ancient cooling towers, their flues long fused shut by mineral scale, still weep from a thousand hairline pores. Between the widely spaced structures the plain becomes a black marsh of standing seepage: ankle-deep, mirror-still except where submerged pipes pulse. The water tastes of rust and old voltage. It has the patience of something that has already dissolved every readable name.

What little grows does so without permission and without abundance. Long brushstrokes of iron-black algae follow the weep-lines down the flanks of the great housings. In the marshier sinks, sparse stands of hollow, jointed reeds—more chitin than cellulose—click faintly when the low wind moves them. They are the color of wet ash and never densen into thicket. No root penetrates far; the forged substrate refuses true soil. The greenery is a rumor the moisture keeps telling itself.

Light has mostly given up. What remains is a oyster-colored gloom, the sky a single low lid of cloud so heavy with suspended particulates that noon and midnight share the same bruised continuum. Occasional pilot flames still burn at forgotten stacks, but they are a deep orange that fails to travel, swallowed within a dozen meters by the wet dark. Most illumination is accidental: corona leakage from insulation so ancient it has become a semiconductor, or the brief blue spit of a short somewhere deep in the understructure. Shadows are not cast; they are the default.

Sound is the true weather. It is total and directional all at once. From far across the open plain comes the oceanic roar of induction furnaces that have not been charged in geologic time yet refuse to cool, their magnetic fields still thrashing ghost charges through empty crucibles. Closer, the deafening is more intimate: the piston-hammer of pumps the size of basilicas forcing water through circuits that loop only into themselves, the scream of turbines spun by steam that has nowhere to go, the sub-bass throb of generators feeding a grid whose last lighted socket fused shut before memory. The noise does not echo; the saturated air and the soft, eroded geometries drink the high frequencies and leave only pressure. Speech is a private fiction. After an hour the bones themselves seem to vibrate at industrial frequency.

There are no animals. Not a bird-shape against the murk, not a insect skitter across the wet alloy. The ecology is purely processual—water cycling through dead heat-exchangers, minerals precipitating into slow stalactites inside conduit, the reeds collating heavy metals into their brittle stems until they grow too dense and snap under their own slight weight. Everything that moves is either fluid, mechanical, or the slow chemistry of corrosion that has long since reached equilibrium and now simply continues out of habit.

The structures themselves are few and far between, as if the builders finally understood sparseness as a kind of mercy. Each is a citadel of blackened plate and riveted drum, rounded by eons of acid mist into soft, almost organic silhouettes. They squat on the plain like exhausted gods. Catwalks hang in mid-collapse yet never fall further, locked by rust into their last possible angles. Conveyor skeins loop through open air and return into the same darkened maw. Some still carry nothing but a shining ribbon of water that has found the old belt paths the easiest way to travel. Inside (if the word still applies) the spaces are cathedral-vast and almost empty, lit by the intermittent arc of equipment performing ritual motions for vanished products. Pistons rise and fall. Gears the diameter of plazas advance one tooth per minute, polished to mirror by their own eternal meshing. Purpose has eroded into pure activity.

The strangeness is quiet and structural. Distances lie. Walk toward a cooling tower and the plain subtly lengthens so that you arrive no closer than when you began, as though the geometry were tired of arrivals. Reflections in the standing water sometimes show the understructures in daylight—bright, busy, crowded with workers who cast no reverse reflection upward. Certain rivets are warm to the touch in patterns that spell nothing yet feel like almost-words under the fingertips. The deafening noise occasionally drops, for the length of a held breath, into perfect silence; in those gaps you hear, impossibly far below the poured crust, something like a second set of machines answering in perfect antiphase. Then the roar slams back and the moment is erased.

This is a landscape that has finished becoming. It will not collapse further, will not reclaim, will not heal. It simply continues: wet, dark, thundering, sparse, and entirely made, cycling its cool water and its purposeless power across an open plain that has forgotten every name except the ones stamped into the original pour forms—names now worn as smooth and illegible as river stones.
