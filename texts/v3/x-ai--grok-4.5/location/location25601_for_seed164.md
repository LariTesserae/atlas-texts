The high plateau does not end. It has weather systems the way lesser ranges have valleys—self-contained cells of mist and pressure that have forgotten how to migrate. From any ridge you see only the next three weather-cells, each a fixed bruise of cloud, before the curvature or the haze swallows the rest. The stone itself is young: knife-cut escarpments, freshets of scree that have not yet learned to round, whole fist-ranges punched upward so recently their faces still shine with unweathered mineral. Nothing here has been given time to soften.

It is cold enough that breath hangs and does not leave. Yet the air is thick with water. Every surface weeps. Basalt sweat beads and runs in slow sheets; the undersides of ledges grow transparent curtains of drip-ice that never quite freeze solid and never quite fall. In the hollows the ground is marsh without heat—black peat packed with the roots of things that should not survive this altitude, yet do. The vegetation is a cold lushness: vast, silent carpets of bronze and blue-green moss deep enough to bury a walker to the thigh; forests of jointed, leafless wands that click faintly when the eternal wind threads them; shelf-fungi the size of terraces stacked up the wet cliffs so that entire escarpments look armored in soft plating. Nothing browns. Nothing advances. The green is locked at crown.

Between these soft masses the emptiness is almost absolute. Kilometers of bare, sweating stone. Open galleries of air so wide that sound arrives delayed and flattened. You can walk half a day and meet only the drip and the low mechanical thunder that seems to come from below the world.

That thunder belongs to the cities.

They are sparse—settlements dropped like industrial seeds across a continental board, far enough apart that each keeps its own weather-cell and its own horizon. They do not sprawl so much as clamp. Foundries and turbine halls are socketed directly into the young cliffs; cooling towers rise from marsh like the trunks of a second, metallic flora. Everything runs. Has always been running. Belts the color of old ice move without variation. Pressure stacks exhale the same pale steam in the same unbroken plumes. Electric arcs jump the same gaps with the same blue stutter. The noise is total and unchanging: a stacked chord of riveters, teething gears, descending cage-elevators, safety valves lifting on their exact schedule. In the empty tracts between cities the sound thins to a single deep note you feel in the ribs, as if the plateau itself were an engine idling forever.

There are no animals. No birds cut the mist. No insects tick in the moss. The only motion that is not mechanical is the slow cold slide of water down stone and the almost imperceptible nodding of the jointed wands in the locked wind. Walking the streets (catwalks, mostly—grates over black canals that never rise or fall) you meet the occasional sealed tram or the rhythmic stamp of a press-gang of maintenance frames, but nothing that breathes for its own sake. The vegetation stops at the outer blast walls as though by agreement; inside, only oil-shine and wet metal.

Light is ordinary and shadowless in the mist—neither the hard raking light of altitude nor the gloom of deep cloud, just a level, shadowless gray-white that might be ten in the morning or four in the afternoon on some other world where time still gradients. Here it does not. The same shift-whistle has been screaming in the same key since the plateau finished rising. The same freight of ore has been rolling toward the same smelter. Even the weirdness has settled into equilibrium: sometimes the condensation freezes mid-drip into long glass organ-pipes that hum with the factories’ note and then, without thawing, simply resume dripping. Sometimes a whole weather-cell dims for the space of a held breath and the moss gives off a single synchronized pulse of blue bioluminescence before the light returns to normal and everything continues exactly as before. No one records these events. There is nothing to compare them against.

Stand on an outer gantry long enough and the scale reasserts itself. The city becomes a bright, loud island of organized heat in a cold ocean of stone and wet green. Beyond the next ridge another island answers on the same unvarying frequency. Beyond that, more. The plateau has room for weather of its own, for silences the size of former nations, for industrial choruses that will still be sounding when the young rock finally remembers how to erode. Nothing is coming. Nothing is leaving. The water weeps. The gears mesh. The moss holds its perfectly unchanging crown.
