The high plateau city stretches past any single horizon, a weathered continent of stone that has long since stopped arguing with the sky. Cool air hangs heavy and wet, not quite rain and not quite fog—more a perpetual condensing, as if the atmosphere itself is always deciding to become water an inch above the surfaces it touches. Every wall weeps. Every cornice and lintel beads with slow, clear runoff that finds the old channels carved centuries ago by the same patient process. The water does not hurry. It simply continues.

What stands here was built without metal, without wheels, without anything that later ages would call a tool. Only stacked and fitted stone, tremendous blocks of pale gray and iron-stained ochre, laid in courses so dense that the streets between them are little more than vertical slits open to the dim sky. The city is dense in the way a coral reef is dense: every cubic measure packed with wall, stair, arch, terrace, cistern, until walking becomes a negotiation with nearness. You move from chamber to stair to gallery and the next mass of masonry is always at your shoulder. Yet the whole arrangement is vast enough to generate its own weather. Clouds form inside the larger courts. Wind finds long fetch down empty avenues and arrives chilled and damp at the far end, hours later, carrying the faint mineral scent of wet rock and nothing else.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. Lichens map the sunward faces in muted cartographies of olive and rust. In the deeper cracks where water lingers longest, thin blades of grass the color of old copper lean outward, never tall, never many. No trees. No canopy. The light that reaches the lower ways is ordinary daylight filtered through mist and the narrow geometry of the architecture itself—normal in quality, reduced in quantity, constantly sliding across wet stone as the moisture moves.

The silence is nearly complete. Water makes the only consistent sound: a soft ticking where droplets strike already-wet ledges, a hush of thin sheets sliding down facades, sometimes the deeper note of a cistern somewhere below accepting another slow increment. Footsteps would carry, but almost nothing walks here. Fauna is rare—an occasional gray bird riding the updrafts between towers, a quick dark shape (salamander? something smaller) vanishing into a weep-hole. The processes that remain active are older than hunting or fleeing: the weather cycling through the stacked voids, the water finding and abandoning routes, the very slight settling of stones that have already settled for geological time. The city is in balance with its own erosion. Edges are softened, corners blunted, surfaces fretted into shallow honeycomb by freeze and thaw and the constant damp. Nothing looks new. Nothing looks unfinished. It simply is, fully itself at this altitude, under this cool wet light, across distances that turn districts into climates.

In the broadest open squares—rare interruptions in the density—the mist thins enough to show the true scale. Row upon row of stepped masses climb toward a higher cloud deck. Beyond them, more of the same, fading into blue-gray haze that might be weather or simply more city. The rules here are quiet and physical: water runs down, cold sinks, stone endures by yielding microscopically each season. There are no devices to contradict those rules, no lights that are not daylight, no voices to fill the acoustics of the stone. Only the vast, established, weeping continuum of a place that was organized long ago and has been weather ever since.
