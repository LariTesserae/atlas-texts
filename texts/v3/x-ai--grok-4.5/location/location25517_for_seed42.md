The highland lake sits in a basined caldera that long ago stopped boiling and simply kept filling. From the ridges it looks like any cold alpine water—slate-blue, wind-riffled—but the surface is a skin. Everything that matters lives under it.

You drop through the thermocline and the light settles into a normal green-gold, the kind that would fall through high-canopy forest at mid-morning. There is no true bottom in the ordinary sense. What holds the place together is root.

Millennia of aquatic growth have turned the crater into a single, continuous rhizome mass. Thick, pale cables twist through black water like slow lightning, binding old volcanic rubble into a suspended architecture. Where the rhizomes thicken and braid they form platforms, vaults, vertical shafts. Some of these load-bearing knots were shaped by hand; most simply happened and were later accepted. The settlement occupies only a few dozen of these natural chambers and bridges, spaced far apart so that between one cluster and the next there is only silent water and the occasional drifting leaf-scale of something larger farther down. The whole inhabited volume is not much wider than a cathedral nave stood on end, and not much taller.

The people here work in living timber. Their tools are bone, fire-hardened reed, and the abrasive edges of certain mussel shells. They prune and freeze-graft more than they cut. A house is a hollowed swelling in a vertical rhizome trunk, its door a valve of flexible epidermis that seals when pressure changes. Floors are tissues allowed to lignify just enough to bear weight. Smoke is impossible; light comes from cultured phosphorescent films and from the slow, patient work of polished quartz lenses set into the upper surfaces to catch whatever daylight the surface still offers. Everything is cool to the touch. The water never warms past the temperature of deep spring shade.

Because the habitat is sparse, sound travels like rumor. Currents thread the open galleries and set whole hanging forests vibrating; the result is a constant low organ-note that never quite resolves into a chord. Over that foundation the fauna keep up their own cascade. Schools of silver, nearly transparent fish clatter through the kelp-lanes in sudden metallic bursts. Larger grazers—slow, armored things the size of calves—scrape the older rhizomes with rasp-tongues, a noise like distant stone-cutting. Every few hours a pair of long-necked divers surface with a sound like wet canvas tearing, then plunge again trailing chains of silver bubbles that ring against the plant-walls all the way down. Nothing here is hurried. Colonies of filter-feeders open and close on multi-day rhythms. Sap moves. Structures lean a finger’s width over decades and are corrected with a single wedge or a redirected graft.

At the edges of the settled chambers the rhizome thins into freer forest. Here the weirdness is quiet but consistent: certain terminals of the plant-mass flower underwater, releasing not pollen but slow balloons of trapped air that rise like sloughed skins. When they break the surface they sound like soft bells. The inhabitants treat these as weather. Children are taught to read which way the air-flowers lean before they choose a path between the far platforms. No one remembers a time before the water. The old volcanic stone is only encountered as dark inclusion inside living wood, a hardness the knives restart against, a memory the plants themselves refuse to forget.
