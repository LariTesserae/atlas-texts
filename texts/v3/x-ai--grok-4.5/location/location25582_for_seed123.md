The hollow is no larger than a back room and sits so tight against the surrounding flats that you can miss it until your boot heel catches the lip. The lip itself is new: a clean fracture in pale, low-country mudstone, edges still sharp enough to cut skin, the break no older than a season of rain. Whatever pressure flexed the ground upward and then let it sag has not yet been sanded by weather. Inside the drop—three body-lengths across, two deep—the air holds steady warmth, the kind that never quite becomes heat but never cools, like the breath of something large sleeping just out of sight.

Moisture lives only in pockets. Condensation beads on the underside of a half-buried pipe that crosses the hollow like a rusted rib; the beads fatten and drop with soft irregular ticks onto a skin of clay that never fully dries. After rare night rains a thin film sheets across the lowest bowl and vanishes by mid-morning, leaving dark cups between stones where midges and something longer-legged gather to drink. There is no stream, only this intermittent damp that keeps the sphere of life from blowing away.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought: three pale, jointed stalks of a fern-like plant jacked into a crack where the pipe sweats, their fronds permanently furled as if flinching; a patch of cushion moss the color of old brass that thrives on the constant breath of steam leaking from a hairline seam in a valve housing; nothing else. The rest of the surfaces—broken scree, concreted spoil, the flanks of machinery—are bare.

What fills the space instead is body. The hollow is packed until empty air feels like a luxury. Carpet beetles with iridescent wing-cases click over every plane in slow rivers. Soft, translucent isopods the size of thumbs stack themselves in the damper crevices, pulsing in shared rhythm. Above them hang the quiet flyers: moth-things with powdered gears for eyes, wings folded like folded letters, so numerous the ceiling of the hollow appears upholstered in dusty gray. Between the stones, jointed runners—part millipede, part cable—thread and rethread, tasting metal and each other. Nothing here is sparse except the green. Life has saturated the volume; every shadow has an occupant, every surface a claim.

Threaded through this crush are the industrial remnants, never dominant, always present like bones in a single corpse. A single upright boiler, no taller than a person, leans against the fresh rock wall, its rivets sweating orange. A truncated driveshaft juts from a collar of concrete, still able to turn a half-degree when temperature shifts, greased by the secretions of the translucent isopods that farm its heat. Flexible conduit, black and cracked, snakes between stones and disappears into holes too neat to be natural; every so often a low blue tick of residual charge jumps a gap and the nearest beetles freeze, antennae trembling, then resume. There is no factory, only these scattered afterimages of scaled work—enough to suggest the hollow itself might once have been a foundation pit, abandoned when the ground failed and the machines were left where they stood.

Light arrives normally: a disc of ordinary sky overhead, warm and colorless at noon, lengthening into amber that slides down the eastern fracture wall in late day. No dramatic shafts or gloom; just the same daylight the flats receive, slightly pooled by the depth. Under it the activity never ceases, yet almost nothing rises above a hush. The soundscape is a dense quiet: the papery shuffle of moth wings adjusting, the tiny ratchet of beetle feet, the occasional drip, the almost subsonic tick of cooling metal. You hear the ecosystem the way you hear blood in your own ears—continuous, intimate, easy to miss until you stop moving.

The strangeness is not theatrical; it is structural. The animals do not merely inhabit the machinery; they complete circuits. A runner will bridge two exposed wires and hold itself rigid while current—feeble, leftover—travels its length and a cluster of eggs glued nearby twitches into faster development. The soft isopods arrange their bodies into living gaskets around leaking joints, sealed and fed by the minerals they filter. When a larger beetle dies, the others do not eat it so much as install it: nestling the carcass into a gear-gap where its hardening shell becomes a new tooth, the whole assembly turning fractionally smoother afterward. The hollow is young, still deciding its rules, and the rule it seems to be writing is that flesh and fabricated metal are negotiable substances, interchangeable under enough pressure and quiet time.

Everything here cycles circulating. Steam ghosts rise from the valve and collapse. Beetle columns reverse direction on schedules no clock would recognize. The moth-things open and close their wings in overlapping waves that pass around the hollow like weather. Predator and prey are both present and temporary: a long, glassy centipede will seize a moth, only to be defended by three runners that taste of copper and left alone again. The entire pocket is in motion the way a lung is in motion—never frantic, never still, denser than any outside landscape has a right to be, sealed by its own sharp new rim from the dry, emptier ground that surrounds it. 

Stay long enough and the scale begins to feel less like smallness and more like focus: as if the rest of the world is the afterthought, and this heated, damp-packed, softly ticking chamber is the place where the actual work of remaining alive is being tried out in every available combination.
