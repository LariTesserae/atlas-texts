The high basin has no floor that was not poured or bolted. Whatever bedrock once cupped this valley long ago was jacketed, trussed, overbuilt until the original contours exist only as load-bearing memory inside kilometers of stained ferrocrete, riveted plate, and sintered slag-brick. The whole expanse sits at that cool altitude where breath hangs visible and the air never quite dries. Mist is the permanent weather: not weather so much as the permanent exhalation of the place itself—condensation sheeting down every vertical surface, weeping from seams, gathering in the low spots of every gantry and catwalk until the water finds the next grate or downpipe and continues its slow vertical migration.

From any open platform the view does not end; it merely loses definition in the dim. Terraces and stacked machine-halls, cooling towers furred with moss, elevated railbeds and pipe racks braid outward and upward until the farther structures become gray silhouettes inside the perpetual low cloud. Scale here is regional rather than intimate: a person can walk for hours along the same clanking maintenance level and still be inside the same continuous built body, only the serial numbers on the bulkheads changing. Everything is occupied. There is no empty lot, no spare margin. Where a walkway widens, someone long ago installed a compressor house or a lattice of culture trays. Where a roof is flat, it carries another roof, or a shallow basin of black water thick with reed and filter-weed. Complexity has reached saturation; every cubic meter already contains its claim.

The lushness is not decorative. It is structural weatherproofing that escaped control and then was quietly accepted. Thick cushions of dark moss drink the constant drip and keep the older iron from bleeding out entirely. Curtains of liverwort and fine fern collar every weeping flange. In the broader catch-basins—some the size of small lakes, their bottoms nothing but layered grating and settled solids—rafts of water hyacinth and engineered cattail move in slow gyres driven by submerged agitators. The vegetation is cool to the touch, always wet, and it softens every edge until the industrial skeleton beneath feels older than it is, settled into itself the way cliff faces settle. Erosion here is not ruin; it is the agreed surface of things. Paint has become patina, patina has become substrate for root and holdfast. The forms are finished. They no longer pretend to newness.

Light stays dim regardless of hour. The cloud ceiling is low and permanent, stained by the same fine particulate that greases every handrail. Bioluminescent strips and sodium work-lamps burn in recessed cages, but they map only the immediate neighborhood of valves and gauges; beyond twenty meters the world falls back into wet charcoal and the faint green of chlorophyll under gloom. Sound is correspondingly subdued. One expects factory roar, yet the prevailing impression is hush. Water does most of the talking: the soft continuous pour from a thousand weeps, the occasional hollow drumming where a larger outflow hits a cistern, the quiet suck of filter beds. Deep mechanical life continues—turbines behind thick housings, conveyor chains in sealed tunnels, the slow rotation of enormous screw-lifts drying sludge—but the designers (or the successive generations of modifiers) armored and baffled everything. What reaches the open air is mostly vibration felt through boot soles rather than noise. The turbulence is almost silent.

That turbulence is constant. Nothing here is static for long, even though the overall shape of the basin has been stable for generations. Whole sections of grating are designed to be withdrawn and rearranged; gantry cranes idle only briefly before swinging another prefabricated module into a gap that did not exist yesterday. Steam vents open without schedule, blanketing three terraces in whiteout and then collapsing again. Nutrient sluices reverse direction. Bridge segments articulate to follow the slow creep of thermal expansion across the artificial canyon. At mid-levels the atmosphere itself feels busy: curtains of fog suddenly thicker, threaded with the metallic smell of ozone or the sweet-rot of overgrown settling tanks, then thinning to reveal a different arrangement of lights than the one you walked through an hour earlier. The hyperactivity is infrastructural rather than human. People move through it carefully, by maintenance routes that are themselves subject to temporary closure and rerouting, but the human figures are sparse compared to the density of pipe and leaf and moving platform. Animals are rarer still—an occasional pale moth battering at a lamp cage, a wide-toed lizard motionless on a warm conduit, once in a long while the low flight of some dark water-bird between basins. Their scarcity makes every appearance feel like a minor event.

The air is cool enough that prolonged stillness lowers the temperature of skin. Where the mist thins for a moment you can look across a broader trough and see the stacked silhouettes continuing without signature change all the way to the far ridge—itself only another constructed cliff of retaining walls and turbine halls. No natural substrate shows. Even the “waterfalls” that drop from upper levels are engineered outfalls, their lip of poured stone already rounded by decades of flow and colonized by the same dripping greenery that covers everything else. The place has finished becoming what it is: a single, valley-sized machine-ecosystem, wet and dim and quietly roaring with rearrangement, so densely woven that leaf, rust, condensate, and cable can no longer be cleanly separated. It does not feel unfinished. It feels like it has been running exactly this way long enough for the running itself to have worn smooth.
