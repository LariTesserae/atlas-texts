The pocket is no larger than a ruined amphitheatre, yet it contains a city.

You do not enter by any gate. You become aware that the dark has edges, that the cool mineral air is bounded by something that was once bark and is now denser than stone. The floor underfoot is a single continuous cambium, black, matte, crazed with hairline fractures that took ten thousand seasons to open a finger’s width. It has been walked smooth in places—subtle channels where feet once passed—but the feet are gone and the channels hold nothing, not even dust. There is no dust here. Everything that can fall has already fallen and been reabsorbed or exhaled as dry scent centuries ago.

The architecture is load-bearing vegetation settled into its final argument with gravity. Columns rise at wide intervals—thirty paces, fifty—each a fused fascicle of trunks that braided while living and then lignified into fluted pillars wider than a house. Between them the space is almost empty. Early builders (hands, adzes of chert and greenstone, fibre cordage, patience measured in generations) trained lateral limbs into vaults and mezzanines; those limbs are now beams the colour of old blood and old soot. Walls are screens of compressed leaf-litter and aerial root, pressed flat by the slow weight of everything above until they read as contrasting strata. Doorways are simple negative spaces where growth was discouraged. Stairs are spiral knees of wood that forgot they were once climbing toward light.

There is no light. Never has been, not in the memory of the place. Whatever dome or overburden sealed this pocket did so before the last free water left. The plants that became the city were already masters of dryness—succulent only in their deepest sealed tissues, surfaces varnished against loss, chemistry running on residual sugars and slow mineral oxide breaths. They photosynthesised once, long ago, in some upper world that no longer connects. Down here they merely persist, cooler than the body, faintly hygroscopic, tasting permanently of iron and tannin and extinguished green.

Sound is the city’s remaining climate. Because the volumes are large and the material continuous, every micro-adjustment of the ancient mass travels. A fibre parts somewhere in the upper galleries and the report arrives as a rifle-crack followed by a long wooden groan that rolls the length of an avenue. Pressure equalises through a hollow buttress and the whole quarter resonates like a struck hull. These events are infrequent on a human scale—hours or days apart—yet each is enormous, and the after-echoes never quite die; they stack into a permanent low thunder, a hard weather of settling. Stand still long enough and you feel the floor itself tick, tick, tick as cells somewhere finally surrender their last molecular water and collapse a fraction of a millimetre. The city is loud the way a glacier is loud: patient, total, indifferent.

Nothing animal lives here. Nothing has for an age. The absence is clean. No droppings, no nests, no chewed edges. The only motion is the vegetable motion too slow to see: a beam continuing its century-long sag, a screen wall thickening by the width of a hair as residual cambium dreams it is still tasked with enclosure. In deep niches you can find the last tools—stone blades polished by handling, needles of thorn, hanks of cord gone rigid as wire. They lie where they were set down. The makers shaped the living wood while it could still be persuaded, then stayed until the persuasion hardened permanently around them, then left or ended. Their pathways remain as slight polish, their rooms as intentional hollows in the biomass.

Elevation is negligible; you are already at the bottom of whatever basin or sealed sink this pocket occupies. The larger world above (salt pan, basalt lid, root-continent, whatever roofs the dark) exerts only the steady compression that finished the city’s form. Erosion here is finished. Edges have lost their sharpness not through abrasion but through simple deep time. Every surface has decided what it will be and has become it. The urban layout—radiating concourses, nested courts, stacked galleries—reads clearly if you move by touch and echo, yet it feels less planned than inevitable, the way crystal lattices or dried river deltas feel inevitable once the last solvent is gone.

There is a strangeness that does not announce itself with spectacle. It is the quiet fact that hierarchy has inverted: the buildings are the organisms, the streets are digestive spaces between them, and whatever intelligence once organised growth into civic pattern has withdrawn so completely that the pattern continues alone, self-maintaining by inertia and residual turgor. Occasionally a vault will release a single unfamiliar scent—something like rain on iron, or crushed scale, or the ghost of chlorophyll—and then the ordinary darkness and its loud, slow breathing reassert.

You can cross the entire city in an hour of careful walking, hand to column, ear to the rolling cracks. At the far margin the cambium floor curves up and meets the ceiling in a seamless cusp. Beyond that cusp the material continues, undifferentiated, forever. The pocket ends. The plant did not.
