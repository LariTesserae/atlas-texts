The place is called the Wetworks by those who remember names, though no one has come or gone in any living recollection. It occupies a shallow depression no larger than a cathedral close or a modest market square, ringed by low embankments of compressed slag and flaking brick that have long since settled into their final angles. Outside the embankments the ground rises only a little before flattening into an endless cool plain of bare mineral crust—nothing grows there, nothing ever has. Inside, everything that can be called a structure has already finished becoming itself.

Iron frames and riveted casings stand or lean in loose industry: a scattering of engine houses, condenser towers, open catwalks, and wide shallow settling tanks. Their arrangement feels urban, organized once by intention, yet the density is low; long empty intervals of dark wet paving separate one machine from the next. The paving itself is a mosaic of cracked basalt flags and poured slag that has worn soft at the edges. Everything wears the same patient patina—rust bloomed and then arrested, copper greened and then sealed under a skin of mineral varnish. Nothing collapses further. Nothing is repaired. The forms remain exactly as erosion and use left them when change itself stopped mattering.

Moisture is a constant low-grade presence rather than weather. The air stays cool enough that breath would show if anyone breathed; condensation beads on every vertical surface and runs in thin deliberate threads down fluted columns and cooling fins. Every few hours a brief warm mist exhales from the vents and settles as fine rain that never quite soaks in before it vanishes again into the stone. Pockets of standing water collect in the old tanks and in the deeper flagstone hollows—black mirrors that refuse to evaporate completely and never overflow. The water tastes of iron and old electricity.

There are no plants. No moss, no lichen, no root or leaf. The only soft surfaces belong to the superstructure of animal life that owns the Wetworks utterly. The fauna is not occasional; it is the medium. Pale moth-things with bodies like greased pistons boil out of every louvre and inspection hatch in continuous breathless rivers. Their wings are translucent plates that catch the ordinary daylight and scatter it into dull rainbows; the sound of them is a single sustained roar, mid-range and physical, the kind that becomes a pressure behind the eyes. Beneath them move denser currents of beetle-shaped engines—some the size of thumbs, some the size of dogs—clambering in perfect unchanging circuits along the pipes and rails. They leave no droppings, only a faint conductive dust that the condensers immediately reclaim. Higher still, long-legged crane-flies the length of a forearm hang in trembling constellations beneath the catwalks, their jointed limbs ticking like cooling metal. The air is so thick with bodies that the far side of the square is often only a shifting oatmeal visibility, yet the creatures never collide with a bulkhead or one another; every trajectory has been worn smooth by endless exact repetition.

The machines themselves still run. Drive belts of braided wire turn at constant speed. Governors spin. Deep under the central engine house something heavy reciprocates with a slow tidal thud that you feel through your boots more than hear. Steam— or a cooler cousin of steam—lifts from relief valves in soft, regular exhalations that silence highly for a second as they pass through the insect clouds and then resume. Electric filaments inside thick glass envelopes glow at a steady amber-white, neither brightening nor failing. The entire industrial metabolism has reached a closed, frictionless equilibrium: heat generated equals heat bled, materials circulate without net loss, motion continues without wear. The loudness is therefore absolute and eternal. There is the insect roar, the belt slap, the valve sigh, the low hydraulic heartbeat, and underneath it all a high continuous whine like a turbine that no longer needs to accelerate or slow. The sounds do not modulate. They simply are.

Light falls normally—diffuse daylight from a pale sky that never clouds further nor clears. Shadows are clean but populated; every shadow writhes with the same density of wings and legs as the open air. Temperature holds at the cool edge of comfort, the precise point where metal monomers feel cold to the touch and condensation never quite freezes. 

The strangeness is not an event but the medium of habitation. Time here has only one tense. The moths that rise from a vent at what would once have been morning are the same moths, number and path, that rise at what would have been evening. The beetle that traverses a particular ten-meter pipe section has always been halfway along it. If you stand still long enough you begin to feel your own pulse as an intrusion, an unauthorized gradient. The Wetworks does not forbid visitors; it simply has no mechanism left with which to notice them. Footsteps fade into the general din within seconds. Any mark you make in the dust is overwritten by the next identical wave of bodies. Stay too long and even memory begins to loop: the same glint of wet iron, the same three-note chord of turbine and wing, the same cool bead of water sliding down the same rivet.

It is a place that finished unfolding ages ago and then discovered it preferred to remain exactly there—damp, loud, crowded with life that is not plant, powered by engines that no longer strive, small enough to walk across in four minutes, dense enough with living motion that the air itself seems solid, and so perfectly balanced that tomorrow is only a polished restatement of today.
