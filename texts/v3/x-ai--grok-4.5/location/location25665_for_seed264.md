The land lies low and wide, a shallow basin that might once have been a sea-floor or the floor of a caldera, now neither. It stretches past any single sightline in a slow roll of mineral surfaces—basalt planed smooth by ages of wind that no longer scrapes hard enough to change anything, sheets of dark glass that caught the light the same way yesterday and will catch it the same way tomorrow, scattered low mesas of compressed ash and iron oxide that rise like forgotten machine housings without ever having been housings. Everything has already been worn to its final shape. The edges are rounded. The hollows are exact. Nothing is becoming; the place simply is.

Warmth rises through the soles if you stand still long enough. Not heat that bakes or sears—just a steady internal warmth, as if the ground itself runs slightly above body temperature and has done so since before memory. In the shallow depressions that freckle the plain, moisture gathers without hurry: thin skins of water that appear as condensation on the cooler underside of the glass sheets, or as slow seep from hairline cracks that never widen and never close. The water is intermittent only in the sense that it stats temporarily elsewhere and returns; the total volume never shifts. A shallow braid of damp channels threads the lowest contour, never flooding, never drying past a dark stain. When rain comes—rare, soft, always the same grey quantity—it finds the same paths and leaves the same soft gleam before the warmth lifts it again into the air as haze.

There is no green. No lichen even. The world is mineral and simpler. Here and there the condensation freezes mid-bead into translucent nodules that look almost organic until you touch them and feel only silica and salt. In sheltered cracks a black, greasy film grows in perfect stillness—fungal only by courtesy, more like a slow chemical bloom that has long since finished bloomimg. The film neither spreads nor dies back. It simply occupies its appointed millimeters.

Sound fills the openness. Not the sound of wind or insects (there are almost none of either), but a deep, continuous industrial chord: a low mechanical thrum that seems to rise from underneath the basalt itself, punctuated at irregular but unchanging intervals by the heavier report of pressure vents far out across the plain. Steam or something like steam issues in short white plumes, always from the same vents, always for the same number of seconds, then vanishes. The air carries the scent of hot metal and wet stone and something faintly oily. The noise is loud enough that soft speech disappears inside it, yet the land is so open that the sound never becomes chaos; it settles into a single vast engine-note that the ear eventually accepts as silence’s louder cousin. Somewhere under the crust, processes that once required factories now require only the planet itself. Gears of pressure and mineral flow turn without casings. Production continues: new glass cools in exact hexagonal plates in one distant basin; iron-tinted sinter builds and collapses in another at a rate too slow to watch. Nothing is built. No walls stand. No roads cut. Whatever infrastructure once existed has been ground back into the same polished plain that now holds its abandoned functions in geothermal permanence.

Light is ordinary daylight, neither harsh nor dim, arriving from a sky the exact pale of thin milk. Shadows are short and soft. In that light the sparse scatter of forms becomes clear: a lone upright fin of black glass half a kilometer off; two rust-red tablets leaning together farther still; a shallow circular depression where water always sits black and still, ringed by a thin collar of the same greasy film. Between them, emptiness—enough space that the features feel deliberate rather than random. The eye travels, rests, travels again.

Fauna is almost theoretical. Once in a long while a pale, many-legged thing the size of a hand scuttles from one heat-vent to another, jointed like a piece of discarded mechanism that learned to walk. It does not forage; it simply relocates. Bird-shapes—if they are birds—pass at great altitude on fixed routes, silent against the engine-drone. Their shadows slide over the glass and are gone. Nothing nests. Nothing multiplies. The rare living presence feels like an after-image of something that finished its story long ago and left only these looping residuals.

The whole basin is locked in equilibrium so perfect it borders on the uncanny. Temperature does not climb or fall. Water neither advances nor retreats. The vents never weaken or strengthen. Erosion has concluded its arguments and signed the treaty. Time here is not frozen so much as satisfied. Stand in one place long enough and the conviction grows that the next century will look exactly like this one, that the loud thrum will still be sounding when every witness is mineral again. The place does not threaten. It simply continues, warm and damp in its pockets, empty of green, full of the everlasting noise of processes that no longer need anyone to run them.
