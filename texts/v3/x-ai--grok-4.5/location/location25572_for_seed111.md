The place is a single shattered lancet of stone, no wider across than a longhouse floor and scarcely longer than a cathedral nave, thrust nearly vertical into a sky that never quite arrives. It stands at an elevation where the air thins to a cool, metallic film against the tongue; the surrounding ranges drop away so abruptly that the eye loses them in a gradient of blue-black that might be distance or simply the permanent half-light. Nothing built has ever clung here. The rock itself is young—edges still razor, faces still bright where they have not yet darkened under the slow breath of frost and the rarer touch of water. It feels freshly broken from something larger, still ringing with the memory of the fracture.

Moisture arrives only as condensate. At irregular intervals a thin skin of water beads across the lee faces, drawn out of the cold air by the stone’s own chill, then freezes into translucent feathers before the next pulse of slightly warmer vapor can release it again. Occasional curtains of fine rain drift past without ever quite wetting the summit; they leave the air smelling of wet iron and distant snow. In the deeper clefts small pockets of meltwater collect, black and still, reflecting nothing.

Vegetation is a rumor. Here and there a rosette of black lichen grips a micro-ledge, its surface cracked like old glaze. A few stalks of something that might once have been grass lean out of sheltered seams, bleached and stiff, more mineral now than plant. Their presence only sharpens the barrenness.

What fills the space is flesh.

The lancet is saturated with bodies. They press so closely that limbs overlap, carapaces click against one another, soft underbellies slide across the cold stone in slow, continuous adjustment. The creatures are not uniform. Some are low and many-legged, plated in overlapping scales the color of old ice; others rise on single columnar legs that end in splayed, adhesive pads; still others are all membrane and joint, folding and unfolding like living bellows. Their density is absolute: every crevice, every overhang, every infinitesimal terrace carries weight. They do not jostle so much as breathe as one slow organism, a living corrosion that never quite erodes the rock because their movements are glacial. Hours pass between noticeable shifts. A limb withdraws. Another settles. The entire population rearranges by fractions of a degree across a day.

They make sound constantly. It is not language, or not any language that resolves into words. It is a layered thunder of chitin knocking chitin, of air forced through spiracles, of low pelvic drums beaten against the stone itself. The noise has no peak and no trough; it simply occupies the narrow column of air above the lancet the way the bodies occupy the stone. In the rare moments when the wind rises, the sound sharpens into a single blended scream that seems to come from the mountain rather than the things upon it. When the wind drops, the cry softens back into its patient, interlocking percussion.

Light reaches the place only as a residual bruise. The higher peaks stand forever between this splinter and any true sun. What illumination exists is the secondary glow of icefields far below and the faint bioluminescence that seeps from the seams of the denser creatures—cold green, cold blue, pulsing in the same unhurried rhythm as their breathing. Shadows are not cast; they are the default state. Depth is felt rather than seen, a pressure of nearness.

There is strangeness in the way the bodies do not quite match the physics of the stone. Sometimes a creature will pause halfway through a step and simply hang, unsupported, for half an hour before gravity remembers it. Sometimes the condensate freezes into shapes that briefly resemble the creatures themselves, then sublimates with a soft hiss. Occasionally one of the larger forms will pry a flake of rock free with a simple, patient levering motion—using nothing more advanced than a jointed limb and time—and will carry the flake a short distance before stopping, as if the act of moving matter were an end in itself. No tools remain afterward. No structures rise. The gesture is repeated across years, decades, the slow cultivation of an intention too gradual to name.

The dynamic of the place is patience. Storms climb the surrounding walls and pass without altering the angle of a single limb. Seasons are measured by which species emits a slightly brighter light, by which undertone dominates the ceaseless noise. The rock weathers almost not at all; the bodies rearrange almost not at all. Yet nothing is static. Everything is becoming, at a rate just below the threshold of ordinary attention. Stand here long enough and the boundary between the young stone and the overwhelming life that coats it begins to blur. The lancet is not a perch that happens to be occupied. It is a single, slow inhalation shared by rock and flesh and the thin, cold moisture that binds them, continuing in darkness, in noise, at the edge of the breathable world.
