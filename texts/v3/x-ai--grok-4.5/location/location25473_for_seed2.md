The place is called the Underskip by those who still chart it, though the name is a habit more than a claim of ownership. It has no true floor and no true ceiling that anyone has ever reached. What it has instead is depth: a constructed ocean the size of a world, poured into a shell that was never a natural planet at all.

Everything here was built. The “bedrock” is a continuous lattice of polyhedral cells, kilometers on a side, pocked with access trunks and ballast tunnels. The cells are not solid stone; they are stacked voids framed in ribbed ferrocrete and black alloy struts, their faces perforated so water can pass freely. From a distance the lattice looks almost crystalline—sharp, unfinished edges, new welds still bright under the lamps. Erosion has barely begun. Nothing has had time to soften. Corners remain knife-straight. Rivet lines stand proud. The whole scaffold feels recently extruded, as if the builders walked away only a few centuries ago and left the pumps running.

Water fills every corridor, every shaft, every open bay. It is cold: a steady nine or ten degrees, the temperature of deep current rather than surface weather. Visibility is surprisingly good. Strings of industrial luminaires (thick glass bulbs in wire cages, spaced every forty meters along the major spines) pour out a flat, white-daylight spectrum that the water carries without much scattering. At mid-range the light looks almost natural, the kind of even glow you might find under high overcast. Farther out it simply fails, swallowed by distance. There is no up-welling of sunlight from any surface; there is no surface. You are always below.

The lattice is mostly empty. Density is low by design or by abandonment. Between the great struts you can travel for minutes without seeing another solid object larger than a drifting cable. The water itself becomes the architecture: slow horizontal drifts, sudden vertical shear zones where pump stations still force circulation, gentle gyres that turn for days inside a single cell. Weather exists here—internal weather. Pressure fronts slide between tiers. Cold fingers of denser water sink and drag mist-like plumes of micro-bubbles with them. Occasionally a whole district of the lattice resonates as a standing wave passes through, a deep subsonic flex that you feel in the ribs before you hear it.

Sound is constant and layered. The primary note is mechanical: the long, staggered thudding of circulation engines, some still perfectly timed, others limping. Secondary harmonics come from the water itself—groans as the structure flexes, the fretting of cables against fairleads, the occasional metallic shriek when a seized turbine spins just enough to catch. Over that lies the alive layer. Fauna is abundant even though vegetation is not. Forests of filter-feeding tubes (pale, rubbery, sparse) cling to the undersides of beams, but they are thin and widely spaced. What thrives are the swimmers and the crawlers. Schools of silver fusiforms move like weather fronts of their own, tens of thousands strong, turning in unison when a shadow passes. Larger things hunt them: torpedo-shaped predators with industrial camouflage patterning (matte greys and safety-yellow stripes, as if they once belonged to the machinery). Crustacean analogues the size of ground vehicles graze the bacterial films that coat every surface, their leg-tips clicking like loose fasteners. Once in a while something truly large moves in the far dark—an eel-form longer than a freight keel, or a colonial jellyfish whose bells have fused into a single pulsing membrane fifty meters across. Their passage is loud: displacement roar, the slap of water against alloy, the startled scatter of entire districts of lesser life.

The industrial heart is still beating in places. Whole manufactory nodules hang in the lattice like organs. Conveyor tunnels cough out clouds of metallic silt. Arc lights inside half-flooded assembly bays still strobe whenever a dying capacitor bank surges. Automated arms, encrusted but not yet seized, continue their last programmed gestures in empty water, welding phantoms. Heat exchangers bleed warm effluent that draws both scavengers and the sparse white weeds that can tolerate the copper taste. Where the machinery is dead, the fauna has taken the housings for dens. Luminous shrimp nest in breaker boxes. A predators’ nursery occupies the barrel of a silent turbine.

There is no natural ground. If you stop swimming you do not settle onto soil; you drift until a current claims you or you snag on a spar. Orientation is local and provisional. “Down” is simply the direction the heavier silt falls. Maps are recursive: each cell contains smaller cells, and the largest navigable voids are themselves only chambers inside still larger frames that no living survey has finished tracing. The scale is such that weather systems form, live, and dissipate without ever touching a boundary. A storm of sediment and panicked fish can rage for a week inside one sector while the next remains calm and clear.

Everything feels temporary and permanent at once. The construction is raw—young metal, sharp geometry, incomplete spans that end in mid-water as if the budget ended mid-sentence—yet the volume is planetary. You can travel for a lifetime and still be inside the same continuous artifact. The water never ends. The lamps never fully darken. The engines never all fall silent together. Life keeps pouring through the gaps. The Underskip does not care that it is unfinished. It simply continues, cold and loud and full of motion, an ocean that was manufactured and then left to become its own weather.
