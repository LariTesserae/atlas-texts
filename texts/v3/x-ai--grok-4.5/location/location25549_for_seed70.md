The high places are underwater.

Not drowned late and reluctantly, but born that way: a young cordillera still lifting out of the warm basalt plain of the seafloor, its ridges and knife-edged cols only a few geological moments old. You feel the youth in every surface. Basalt and pale pillow lava still wear their extrusion skins; freshets of mineral-white fracture push through the dark rock like new scars. Nothing has had time to round itself. Edges stay edges. The tallest summits rise through two hundred meters of clear, yeast-warm water and then stop, just shy of air, so that their tablelands and hanging valleys exist in a perpetual, brilliant shallows. Light arrives almost unbroken—normal daylight thinned only by depth and by the green filtering of what grows here.

Because everything grows.

The vegetation is not decoration on the stone; it is a second geology. Dense, layered, near-touching, it occupies every cubic manspace the water will allow. Giant kelps with trunks as thick as old-growth firs rise from talus slopes and braid themselves into vaulted canopies thirty meters overhead. Between them stand slower architects: calcified arborescent algae that have learned the trick of verticality, their branches plated in rose and cream, jointed like bamboo, ringing faintly when a rare current shoulders past. Understory is a saturated textile of seagrasses in ground-covering sheets, feather-duster crowns, and sticky translucent veils that trap the slow rain of organic glitter. Epiphytes do not wait for a trunk to die; they colonize living stipes mid-growth, stacking leaf upon filter upon holdfast until the original plant is only a rumor inside its own garden. In the narrow slots between ridges the growth becomes almost solid—walled corridors of green and amber tissue you would have to shoulder through, if anything large ever tried.

Almost nothing large does.

Fauna is a rumor and a rarity. Sometimes a single silver firearm of a fish hangs motionless in a shaft of light, then decides against the effort and eases whiptail-first into a crevice. Sometimes a soft, fist-sized jelly pulses once along a thermocline and is gone for a season. The ecology here is patient and vegetal; it has not yet invited the quick metabolisms. Decomposition itself is gentle, almost ceremonial—Fallen fronds take months to ghost away into the fine white sludge that fills every hollow like untrampled snow.

Sound is accordingly scarce. The water holds a low, continuous hush that is less noise than the memory of noise, the way thick snow remembers wind. Occasional soft collisions—frond against frond, a loosened chip of basalt turning over once—reach you attenuated and polite. No surf; the surface far above is a distant, wrinkled skylight rather than a breaker zone. No voices. The place keeps the volume of a held breath.

It is warm the way blood is warm. Currents, when they move at all, move like thought during fever: slow lateral drifts that take hours to cross a cirque, gentle upwellings that polish the sharper ridges without ever scouring them. Change itself is attenuated. A holdfast may spend a decade deciding to ration calcium to a new branch. A talus slope advances grain by grain across centuries. The mountains continue their centimeter-by-century rise, and the forests simply ride them upward toward the light, thickening as they go.

There is a strangeness that never quite names itself. In certain hanging basins the water clarifies beyond ordinary optics; you can see a ridge three kilometers off as if it occupied the same arm’s length, every detail of its young spires and their clinging gardens crisp, while intervening distance feels optional. Elsewhere, curtains of microscopic life orient to magnetic lines no instrument (had any existed) would detect, forming slow vertical banners of gold and indigo that part around you without turbulence and reseal behind. At the highest tablelands, just beneath the wrinkled surface light, bubbles of atmosphere are sometimes trapped for days in the upper canopy—shimmering lenses in which a few adventitious leaves unfurl into temporary air, taste it, and then are reclaimed when the lens migrates or collapses. The plants do not seem surprised. Nothing here is in a hurry to decide what medium it prefers.

No path, no cairn, no tool-mark. The only architecture is the negotiation between rising stone and the saturation of green that refuses to leave any volume empty. You could swim a long regional arc—across valley after drowned valley, over razor cols still shedding glassy fragments, through galleries where the canopy knits so tight that noon becomes a green dusk—and never once encounter a straight line that was not grown or erupted. The place is entire unto its own slow grammar: young, high, drowned, warm, and densely alive in near silence. It does not need you to finish it. It is still finishing itself.
