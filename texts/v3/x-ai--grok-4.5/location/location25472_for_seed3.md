The plateau does not remember being lowland. Whatever forces punched this slab of world upward did so recently enough that the rock still sheds grit in slow, continuous rain—not dust storms, not cascades, just a patient isolation of particles that drift and settle and rise again whenever the wind invents itself. The elevation is absolute: the horizon is a hard blue-black curve empty of anything that could be called ground. Clouds form below more often than above. The air tastes of metal and wet stone.

What stands here calls itself a city only out of habit. It sprawls without packing tight—towers and factory halls and arterial viaducts scattered across kilometers of raw basalt and new-cut scree, each district separated by stretches of emptiness where the stone still looks freshly torn. Between the built masses the vegetation has taken opportunistic hold: thick-leafed shrubs the color of oxidized copper, vines that cling to cooling towers and drip condensation in slow metronomic ticks, mosses that glow a dull mineral green under the perpetual dim. The light never quite arrives. It filters through a high, suspended haze of coal smoke, geothermal steam, and something finer that might be powdered mountain or might be the residue of rules that no longer apply. Everything exists in permanent late dusk the color of wet iron.

Moisture is nowhere abundant and everywhere present. It beads on every surface. It condenses inside the lungs. Occasional curtains of cold rain sweep through without warning, thin enough to walk through, heavy enough to darken the brick and make the ironwork steam. In the deeper cuttings, black water collects in long rectangular basins that used to be foundations or ore pits; the water never freezes, never fully evaporates, merely sits and reflects the wrong number of moons. Rivulets thread between paving stones that themselves seem only half-decided on remaining flat.

Sound is industrial and continuous. Great reciprocating engines buried in the basements of the larger blocks keep up a low, overlapping thunder—each piston stroke a half-second late or early, never quite synchronizing. Steam valves exhale in long wet hisses. Chains clank on open elevators that rise and fall without passengers. Over it all the fauna insists on being heard. Birds the size of dogs nest in the gantries and cry like torn sheet metal. Multi-legged things the color of wet soot move in herds across the open plazas at a glacial pace, their jointed legs ticking against stone like loose clockwork. Smaller species throng the vine-choked rooftops and the undersides of bridges: luminous moths, hard-shelled crawlers that leave trails of phosphorescent slime, something almost mammalian that sings in chords hanging just below the threshold of language. They do not fear the machines. They treat the entire settlement as another kind of geology.

Reality itself frays at the edges of attention. Walk long enough down a straight boulevard and the vanishing point begins to breathe. Doorways open onto rooms that continue farther than the exterior walls allow. Occasionally a factory chimney will reverse its smoke for an hour, drawing the haze downward into itself while the workers continue their slow circuits as if nothing has changed. Shadows detach and drift a few meters before remembering to reattach. Time softens: a single work shift can last three days of clock time or collapse into forty minutes of felt duration, and no one bothers recording the discrepancy anymore. The erosion of the mountain and the erosion of cause-and-effect have begun to rhyme.

The processes that govern this place are almost pathologically slow. Buildings settle another centimeter every decade. Forests of the copper-leaved shrubs advance a meter per generation across the open scree. Engines designed a century ago still run on slightly modified cycles; the modifications themselves arriving as anonymous chalk diagrams left on boiler room floors overnight. Even the breakdowns of physics feel gradual—like ice melting under uniform pressure. You can watch a streetlamp’s light pool and spread like liquid for an entire afternoon before it finally drops off the edge of its own beam and puddles on the pavement, still glowing.

At the outer margins the city simply thins rather than ends. Scaffolding gives way to bare rock still bearing the tool marks of whatever lifted it. Vegetation grows sparser, the damp turns to cold mist, animals become rarer but larger, more deliberate. The engines fall silent behind you one by one until the only sound left is the almost inaudible grinding of new stone learning how to be mountain. Beyond that the dim thickens into true dark, and the plateau continues, unfinished, still becoming the height it already claims to be.
