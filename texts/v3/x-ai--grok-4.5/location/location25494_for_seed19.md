The Stillspire Expanse does not rise so much as it simply remains. Once, long past any memory that stone itself can hold, this was a spine of mountains sharp enough to tear weather. Time and the dry patience of wind reduced them to a single continuous highland, a peneplain of pale basalt and compressed salt that stretches past every horizon a traveler might invent. The elevation is still extreme: the air arrives thin and cool, tasting of metal and old lightning, and the sky sits too close, a hard enamel blue that never quite becomes night or day so much as cycles through grades of the same lucid glare. Nothing here has moved in earnest for geological ages. The place has reached its final posture.

Underfoot the ground is a mosaic of hexagonal salt plates and fine mineral dust the color of bleached bone. There is almost no water—only the ghost of it locked inside the salt, a dryness so complete that even breath leaves no lingering moisture. Vegetation is a rare concession: small cushions of gray-blue lichen clinging to the lee of low residual knolls, and occasional wiry shrubs whose leaves have become translucent scales. They do not grow so much as persist, unchanged from one century to the next.

What crowds the Expanse is life of another order. Fauna here is overwhelming, denser than any reasonable ecology should permit. Herds of long-legged salt-deer stand packed flank to flank across kilometers of flats; between their legs nestle ranks of low, plated browsers; above them, in motionless strata, hang clouds of membrane-winged things the size of hands, fixed mid-glide as though the air itself had thickened into glass. Smaller creatures fill every gap—scaled runners, soft-bodied tunnelers half-emerged from hexagonal cracks, pale arthropods locked in the act of lifting a grain of dust. They nearly touch. Layers of them occupy the same volume, some translucent enough that three or four bodies occupy what looks like one silhouette. None of them shift. None of them breathe in any perceptible way. The silence is nearly perfect: only the faintest refractive creak when the cool air contracts at the dimming of the light, a sound like distant ice thinking about breaking and then deciding against it.

This is the equilibrium the erosion promised. Motion has been spent. The creatures are not dead; look closely and you can see the slow interior craft of living chemistry—the faint cycling of clear fluids behind a nictitating membrane, the glacial flex of a tendon that will complete its twitch in another thousand years. They are simply finished with change. Time has flattened them the way it flattened the mountains. Reality, under this much duration, has begun to thin. Edges of animals sometimes refuse to agree on their position; a set of antlers may occupy two angles at once until the eye chooses one and the other slips away like a remembered dream. Walk among them and perspective shears: a corridor of bodies suddenly opens onto a vertical plain where the same animals stand on a wall that was, a moment ago, the ground. Shadows fall in directions that have no sun. Occasionally a traveler’s own hand appears at a distance, already waiting among the crowd, frozen in a gesture not yet made.

Scattered across this dense immobility are the faint signatures of earlier attention. Low rings of fitted basalt cobbles, no higher than a kneeling calf. Single upright slabs pecked with sequences of dots that might once have been tallies or star-counts. Here and there a blade of knapped volcanic glass, edges still keen because nothing has weathered them, lying beside the motionless hoof of a salt-deer as if placed in offering or dropped in mid-task. The technology is stubbornly simple—percussion, pressure flaking, the slow abrasion of harder stone against softer. Whoever made these things understood levers and the grain of rock, nothing more. Their structures never grew complex enough to resist the final leveling of the land; only these small, almost apologetic marks remain, half-swallowed by the same salt that preserves the fauna.

Light on the Expanse is ordinary in quantity yet strange in behavior. It falls evenly, without dramatic shadow, as if the sky itself were a single diffuse source. Edges stay crisp. Colors—the dull pewter of basalt, the milk-blue of lichen, the varied ochres and charcoals of hide and carapace—remain exact, unromanticized. Because nothing moves, the light has no flickering work to do; it simply declares what is there, century after century.

At the far margins of vision the plain develops gentle swells—the last roots of the old mountains—and these too are carpeted with the same motionless throngs. The density never breaks. There is no outside to this crowding, only further layers, further quiet repetitions of form. A person walking here for days finds that the body begins to adopt the local cadence: steps grow slower, breath shallows, thoughts arrive already exhausted of novelty. The temptation is to stop entirely, to take a place among the ranks and let the thin cool air finish the work of perfect stillness.

Yet the Expanse is not hostile. It is simply complete. Every gradient has run down to zero. Every process has found its terminal shape. The weirdness is only what happens when reality, having nowhere left to go, begins to fold against itself like well-worn cloth. Stand long enough and you may notice that the nearest salt-deer has your eyes, or that the horizon has quietly positioned itself underfoot. These are not threats. They are the landscape continuing its single occupation: remaining.

And so it remains—vast, cool, dry, crowded with forever-stilled lives, marked by the sparsest of human-made afterthoughts—exactly as it has been since the last mountain consented to become plain.
