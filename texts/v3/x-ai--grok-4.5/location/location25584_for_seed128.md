The pocket is no larger than a generous courtyard, though its walls refuse to admit edges. You enter it the way moisture enters stone: by seepage, by the long habit of finding every hairline joint. It sits embedded high in the basalt flank of a made mountain, a mountain that was poured and grown and knitted together century upon century until its own mass became geology. The substrate is wholly artificial—no native rock remains—yet the weathering has gone so deep that the distinction has ceased to matter. Surfaces carry the dull sheen of materials that have managed their own repair for longer than memory, a set of alloys and graphene lattices and living cements that long ago reached equilibrium with freeze-thaw, with wind-scour, with the slow chemistry of their own exhalations.

Water is constant and near. Not a river, not a lake: a condition. The air arrives already heavy, pulled upward from lower condensers and forced through capillary galleries that thread the entire mountain like veins. Inside the pocket the temperature stays warm, body-warm, waste-heat warm, so the water never quite decides between liquid and vapor. It beads on every overhang, runs in thin braided sheets down vertical faces, collects in shallow, perfectly level basins whose rims are worn soft by centuries of overflow. The basins themselves are not decoration; they are thermal regulators and acoustic dampers that have failed, or rather succeeded too well, at their original tasks and now simply persist. Drips fall with metronomic patience into deeper reservoirs you never see. The sound of them multiplies. Every surface is slightly damp to the touch, slightly yielding, as though the structure still remembers being soft when it was first printed.

Light reaches this place only after filtration through twenty meters of translucent carbon lattice and living membrane. What arrives is dim, pewter-colored, the color of late dusk held permanently. Shadows do not sharpen; they pool. In that gloom the sparse vegetation becomes almost luminous by contrast: thin verticals of engineered moss, copper-green and drought-shy even here, rooting only in the micro-crevices where mineral-rich weep-water concentrates. A few low rosettes of something like saxifrage cling to the edges of the basins, their leaves thick and waxy, bred once for thin high air and never revised. They do not spread. The design never allowed expansion, only endurance. Between them the floor is bare engineered ceramic crazed with age, slick, reflecting the low light in long dull streaks.

Yet the pocket is saturated with life. Fauna is dense to the point of contact. Clouds of midges whose wings catch the dimness like graphite dust rise and fall in synchronized breathing motions over the water. Small six-limbed climbers—part lizard, part something the original engineers never named—race the vertical faces in overlapping territories, their toe-pads leaving momentary darker prints that the surface slowly reabsorbs. Colonies of soft-bodied filter feeders cling underside to every ledge, pulsing as they pull nutrients from the constant film of moisture; their valves open and close with a wet clicking that becomes part of the larger noise. Higher, in the shadowed gutters where structural ribs meet, nests of unidentifiable fledglings open red mouths on timers only they understand. Something larger moves occasionally behind the semi-permeable rear wall—an occupancy the mountain itself tolerates, perhaps a maintenance drone gone feral, perhaps a predator that learned to harvest the swarms. The air is never empty. Bodies brush, wings clip, feet scrabble. Everything lives closer than comfort.

The sound is total. It is not one source but a standing wave of sources that have locked into phase. Water sheeting, water dripping, water forced through pressurized micro-ducts that sing at the edge of hearing. The mountain’s deep circulatory pumps—still running after whatever civilization set them going finished—throb up through the floorplates in a bass you feel in the teeth. Wingbeats multiply into a sandpaper roar. The climbers communicate in sharp percussive taps against the resonant walls. Occasional gusts find the hidden vents and turn the entire pocket into a flue that howls. Conversation, if anyone tried it, would be shredded. The deafening quality is structural: the pocket was once an acoustic focusing chamber for some forgotten industrial or ceremonial purpose, and the geometry still amplifies. Silence is a theoretical limit the place no longer approaches.

Everything here is mid-process. Mist thickens and thins on fifteen-minute cycles as the atmospheric exchangers cycle. The little basins fill, quiver at the brim, spill in sudden bright threads, then settle. Fauna shifts from feeding flocks to resting clusters to sudden scatter when a larger shadow passes the membrane. The walls themselves respire—microscopic pores opening and closing to equalize pressure, a motion too small to see but audible as a continuous soft static underneath the roar. Heat blooms and fades in patches as buried superconducting loops dump surplus. Nothing is still, yet nothing is chaotic; the activity has worn itself into a settled choreography, the way a river wears its bed. The place has long since finished becoming and now simply continues.

You can stand in the exact center—there is a shallow cup worn there by centuries of earlier standing—and feel the whole pocket as a single warm, wet, thrumming organism made of made things. The mountain around it goes on forever in every direction, full of identical or variant pockets, most of them sealed, some of them collapsed into quiet mineral sleep. This one remains open, hidden by nothing more than obscurity and the overwhelming preference of the exterior world for larger scales. Its rules are simple and absolute: water will find every surface; sound will fill every volume; life will pack itself into every vacancy the engineers left, and then into the vacancies that time opened afterward. The light will stay low. The temperature will stay kind. And the pocket will keep its balance without witnesses, indefinitely, because balance is the last and most durable of its original specifications.
