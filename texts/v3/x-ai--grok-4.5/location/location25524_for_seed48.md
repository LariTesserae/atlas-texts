The low flats begin where the ground forgets how to rise. What passes for a horizon is a long, diluted smear of heat and moisture, broken at irregular intervals by the silhouettes of cooling towers, lattice derricks, and the blunt shoulders of long sheds. Everything sits only a meter or two above the water table. The water itself is not a dramatic presence—no rivers in flood, no lakes—but a constant nearness: it stands in shallow depressions that never empty, beads on every vertical surface, and hangs in the air so thickly that breathing feels like drinking. After the frequent clammy rains the concrete exhales for hours. Walls weep. Rust runs in thin copper and orange threads down corrugated siding. The ground is a skin of wet silt, cracked concrete slabs, and dark mineral crusts that crunch faintly underfoot before giving way to mud again.

There is no green. Whatever once tried to root here has long since failed; the soil and the runoff are wrong for it. What remains is mineral and manufactured: expanses of stained pavement, gravel yards going soft with saturation, piles of clinker and spent catalyst, the pale bloom of salt and alkali on brick. In the deepest shade of collapsed loading bays a few slick films of bacterial mat or colorless slime occupy the wet corners, but they do not alter the essential bareness. The place reads as post-vegetal, an industrial terminal that outlasted its own ecology.

Heat is the other constant. It is not the dry heat of deserts but a heavy, enclosing warmth that never fully lifts even when the light fades. Metal wrapper sheets tick and expand. The air above the wider yards shimmers. Standing water steams almost invisibly. Workers—if any still move through the outer districts—shed layers and still glisten; most of the time the yards stand empty of them. The emptiness is structural. Buildings are spaced as though land were once cheap and abundant: long gaps between fabrication halls, open marshalling planes, canals cut straight and now half-choked with sediment. You can look a kilometer and see only isolated blocks of architecture, the rest a wet open field of low forms. Density is measured in absence. Vast interior volumes of the old plants remain hollow—cathedral spaces of rusted gantries and silent conveyors, floored in pools that reflect the dim roof lights still burning on emergency circuits.

Those lights, and the sky, keep everything in a permanent half-dusk. The cloud cover is low and fine-grained, a perpetual condensation layer that the local heat never quite burns through. Smokestacks—some cold, some still dribbling pale vapor—add their own soft gray. Edges blur. Depth is hard to judge. The few operating concerns keep to a reduced rhythm: a compressor station cycling every few minutes, a distant pump house, the occasional slow traverse of an automated flatbed along reinforced causeways. Engines exist, factories exist, but they are no longer a roar; they are a subdued industrial metabolism, more felt in vibration through the wet ground than heard.

Sound overall stays low. The humidity empties the air of sharp edges. What carries are small things: the drip from eaves into standing water, the soft clack of cooling metal, wind moving across guy wires with a subdued moan. Against this quiet the fauna is overwhelming in sheer number and motion, yet rarely loud. The place belongs to them now. Clouds of small flies and midges hang in the lee of every structure, so dense they look like smoke that fails to rise. Along every canal edge and flooded basement, pale crabs and amphipods move in continuous soft scrabbling armies, feeding on whatever film the water brings. Higher up, in the hollow lattices of towers and the dark rafters of sheds, bow-winged birds and leathery bats turn and settle in shifts; their wings make only papery sounds. In the warmer outfalls, frogs of several sizes maintain slow choruses that rise and fall like tired machinery. Snakes—dark, thick-bodied—hold the sun-warmed rails and the tops of pipe racks, motionless until something smaller passes. The overall impression is of a completely colonized ruin whose new inhabitants have no interest in filling the air with noise. They simply occupy every niche the heat and wetness and emptiness provide, moving in tides of their own: dawn and dusk roost exchanges, feeding pulses after each rain, the steady sideways traffic of crustaceans whenever the shallow water shifts.

The city—or what remains organized enough to still be called one—spreads at the scale of a broad shallow valley or coastal industrial belt. Districts repeat with variations: tank farms whose vessels now stand half-reflected in their own leaked rings of water, rail spurs ending in submerged buffers, administration blocks with every window open to the weather and every sill whitened by mineral deposits. Walkways and capstans and valve wheels have been polished not by recent hands but by years of weather and the passage of small bodies. Everything has the look of having arrived at a stable late condition. Rust is deep but no longer accelerating; concrete spalls in rounded, almost gentle flares; metal stairs are thin yet still bear weight. The processes that shape the place—capillary rise of water, slow chemical attack, thermal cycling, the endless light traffic of animals—have settled into balance with the remaining structures. Nothing is new, nothing is dramatically collapsing. It simply continues.

At certain hours the dimness deepens and the heat grows more intimate. Mist lifts from the canals in low sheets. The insects thicken until distance vanishes. Somewhere a residual generator changes pitch. A thousand small claws continue their quiet transit across the wet mineral world. The city remains itself: open, emptied, industrial, thoroughly inhabited by everything except the people who built it, and held in the long, warm, unremarkable damp of its own endurance.
