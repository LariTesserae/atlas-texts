The Skyreefs rise where the world thins. What were once continental spines of stone have been planed by ages into a single, slow gesture: broad mesas and hanging shelves stacked across thousands of vertical meters, their edges crumbling into pale scarps that drop through cloud. Time has dulled every angle. The rock itself is nearly finished with change; it endures now mostly as memory beneath the living mass that claimed it.

Everything here is plant. Not forest in any ordinary sense, but a continuous body of lignified tissue, root-fusion, and aerial architecture that has overwritten the old geology. Trunks the girth of hills rise and braid, their bark corrugated into canyons and terraces. Where they meet they melt against one another, sealing the joins with resin that hardens into amber buttresses. Between these titans the space is cathedral-hollow—vast vertical voids where a single shaft of filtered daylight can fall half a kilometer before touching living surface again. The “ground” underfoot is never soil. It is layered cambium, thick moss-mats, and the slow coil of cable-thick lianas that serve as both path and taut structural guy-wire. You walk on the shoulder of something still growing.

Moisture is scarce and precious. Rain reaches these heights only as brief, hard veils that hiss through the upper fringe and then vanish. What remains is dampness held in tissue: beads of condensation swelling on leaf-flanks at dawn, cool seeps where root-pressure forces water out of living wood, and shallow basin-ponds cupped in the crooks of ancient branch-collars. The air itself carries a warm wetness that never quite becomes humidity field or drought; it is the breath of the reef, exhaled and reabsorbed in a closed private cycle. In the deeper hollows the smell is of spice and slow rot, sweet cellar-dark.

Light is ordinary sunlight, but ordinary sunlight rearranged. It arrives thinned by altitude and then further broken by successive canopies until it becomes a green-gold twilight even at midday. In the open shelves higher up the glare can be fierce, bleaching the pale upper bark almost white. Shadows move with the slow internal weather of the plants themselves: a branch shifts over decades and an entire gallery that has known dusk for a century abruptly blooms into day.

The fauna is the true weather. Every surface crawls, thrums, or clings. Clouds of iridescent midges turn the light into stuttering galaxies. Columns of leaf-cutter things the size of hands march in rivers along the living bark, their trail a constant dry rustle like paper being shredded. Flocks of gliders—some membranous, some feathered, some pure membranous bag—launch from vent-holes and spiral for minutes on the updrafts that climb the reef faces. The noise is continuous and total. It is not one sound but the layered detonation of millions of stridulations, wingbeats, territorial screams, and the low gut-drum of larger browsers chewing through wood. At certain hours the volume spikes until conversation becomes lip-reading and the bones in your skull vibrate. Then, without warning, whole coasts of the reef fall into simultaneous silence for a few heartbeats—an ecological inhale—before the roar rebuilds from the edges inward.

Movement never ceases. Sap tides rise and fall inside the mega-trunks, audible as distant surf if you press an ear to bark. Epiphyte gardens open and close their flowers on schedules tuned to the shadow of something largercutting across the sun. Predators the color of wet lichen wait motionless for days, then strike in spasms that send cascades of smaller lives tumbling into the voids. The whole structure sways, almost imperceptibly, under the massed takeoff of winged things at dusk; you feel it first in the sole of the foot, a long horizontal shiver.

Human presence is slight and provisional. Here and there a woven platform clings like a swallow’s nest to the lee of a trunk, lashed together from stripped lianas and floored with springy bark-shingles. Smoke from small resin fires stains the air above a few of these perches. The tools leaning against the walls are bone, fire-hardened wood, and the occasional trade flake of glassy stone. No metal outlasts the damp and the acids that weep from cut plant-flesh. The people who keep these places move lightly, harvesting nectar bladders, trapping the fat grubs that bore deep galleries, and listening—always listening—for shifts in the great noise that might mean a branch is about to fail or a swarm is turning predatory. Their paths are polished by bare feet into the living wood, shallow grooves that the plant slowly tries to heal.

At the outermost edge of a high shelf the world simply stops. Wind, thinner and colder than the interior damp, pours across the lip. Far below, through torn gaps in cloud, other reefs appear as distant green islands suspended in pale air, each one an isolated nation of wood and hunger and sound. Between them stretch only emptiness and the slow glitter of rivers that no longer remember how to reach so high.

Nothing here feels newly made. The forms have the patience of things that have already survived their own apocalypses—fire, freeze, the long starvation of earlier climates—and settled into this loud, warm, half-hollow forever. The reef does not wait for anything. It simply continues, leaf by leaf and scream by scream, under the ordinary light of a sky that has forgotten it was ever otherwise.
