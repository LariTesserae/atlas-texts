The high plateaus do not end. They only change their angle of light, their grain of mineral, the particular way heat rises from them in sheets that never quite become wind. From any standing place the land stretches in long, patient folds of ochre, iron-black, and chalk-white salt-pan, broken by low ridges of fused basalt that look poured rather than risen. The air is thin and dry enough to crack the inside of a throat in minutes; what moisture once lived here finished its leaving geological ages ago and left behind only the mineral ghosts of vanished lakes—hard, glittering skins that ring underfoot like cracked porcelain.

Nothing green has ever held here. The ground is pure chemistry: sulphate crusts, sintered glass from ancient lightning strikes, veins of native copper that spear the surface like cooled lightning. Erosion has had so long to work that every edge has found its settled form. Hoodoos lean into one another with the patience of pillars that have already decided they will never fall. Spines of rock ride the skyline in balanced arrays that rearrange only across centuries. The whole region feels finished with itself, locked into a slow equilibrium of heat-expansion and contraction, grain by grain.

Distance is the first authority. Features stand far apart: a black mesa, then kilometres of empty salt glare, then a lone fumarole ridge whispering heat. Between them the eye finds almost nothing—just the faint, slow shimmer where air density changes and the occasional upright needle of metal that is not ore. Those needles are the first signs that industry is present without ever having been built.

They grow. Not upward in a season, but outward and denser across decades: lattices of self-assembling pipe and cooling fin that hang in the thin air like vertical reefs. No foundation, no wall, no roof. Just engine-logic crystallised into landscape. Pistons the size of hills cycle once every few years. Flywheels of pure basalt turn under convection that no one set in motion. Somewhere inside the lattices, ore is smelted by ambient sunlight focused through natural lenses of volcanic glass; somewhere else, slag is extruded in slow black ribbons that harden into new walkways no foot will ever use. The processes are industrial in every measurable way—scale, heat signature, electrochemical complexity—and yet they answer to no maker. They are simply what this substrate does when left hot and empty long enough. Reality here has a taste for manufacture.

Then the fauna arrive, and the emptiness becomes theoretical.

They come first as a vibration in the chest, a subsonic pressure that makes the salt-crust hum. Sound rises after: a grinding, layered roar of chitin against metal, of wing-cases the size of roofs slamming open and shut, of internal combustions that have no fuel except the dry air itself. The swarms do not fill the sky so much as replace it. Bodies the colour of soot and fresh rust move in slow rivers kilometres wide, each creature a hybrid of beetle and turbine, of lung and bellows. Some drag chains of half-formed machine parts behind them; others carry glowing cores that pulselike slow hearts. They do not hurry. Their migrations follow thermal gradients that take months to shift, and they leave the ground scraped clean of every loose grain down to the welded bedrock. When two swarms meet they do not collide; they interpenetrate, trading components, rewriting one another’s geometries in mid-air until the noise becomes a single sustained scream of negotiation. Afterwards the air is full of discarded silver dust that never settles, only hangs and refracts the normal daylight into wrong angles.

The breakdown begins at the edges of vision. Distances stretch and contract without warning: a mesa that should be a day’s walk is suddenly underfoot, radiating heat that smells of ozone and burnt copper. Shadows fall in directions the sun does not authorise. Sound arrives before its source—engine-roar first, then the swarm that should have made it. In places the mineral crust has grown transparent; look down and you see residual versions of the same landscape stacked beneath, each running its own slower industry, each crowded with fauna that have already passed or have not yet arrived. Causality loosens. A piston that finished its stroke a century ago still ejects heat into the present. A creature dies and keeps flying, its body becoming another self-assembling lattice that will, in two hundred years, smelt its own replacement.

No structures stand that were ever planned. No road, no furnace door, no marker. Only the open, weathered, roaring expanse and the slow industrial thunder of a world that grew factories the way other worlds grow mountains—patiently, emptily, and without ever asking permission of physics. The light remains ordinary: hard white day, long violet dusk. Under it the plateaus continue, the swarms continue, the needles keep assembling themselves into louder and stranger shapes, and the heat never relents.
