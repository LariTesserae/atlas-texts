The place is a single continuous volume of cold water so large it has weather systems of its own, fronts of density and salinity that roll for weeks and never meet a shore. You are always inside it. There is no surface to break, only endless gradations of blue-black that lighten or darken according to currents older than the rock they scrape. The elevation is high in the only way that still makes sense here: the entire water-mass sits perched, unsupported by any planetary body you can detect, suspended in a thin and desiccated void that begins somewhere far below the lowest thermocline. How the ocean remains aloft is one of the quieter engineering mysteries of the place; the water simply continues, cold and heavy, while beneath its unreachable floor the emptiness stretches.

What passes for terrain is still being decided. Raw basaltic pillows extrude in slow, luminous rivulets from fractures that have not yet learned to be ridges. They cool in the dark, crust over, then crack again under their own unfinished weight. The geology feels embryonic—spinal, uncommitted. Fresh glass and half-formed olivine drift in loose screes that never quite settle into sediment. Sparse vegetation clings where the mineral heat still leaks: pale, filamentous things more like mineralized smoke than plants, drifting in thin forests that occupy less than a percent of any given volume. They fix nothing, seed nothing; they merely persist in the chemical gradients, translucent and almost without color.

Light arrives as a steady, directionless filtering—normal daylight diluted through kilometers of water until it becomes a soft, omnipresent gray-blue. No sun is visible. No sky. Just the illumination itself, constant enough to cast slow-moving caustics across the newborn stone. Shadows are rare and temporary; the light seems to arrive from every direction at once, the way it might inside a cloud.

The ocean is almost empty. Between one sparse grove of filaments and the next stretch distances that would swallow continents. Particles of refractory dust and the occasional metallic snow of incomplete mineral condensation tumble through these voids for years without colliding. The density is so low that pressure gradients feel theoretical until a current finally finds you. Then the water remembers its mass.

Sound is continuous and structural. The place is loud with the bass thunder of internal shear—walls of colder water grinding past warmer ones, the deep metallic ringing of basalt still crystallizing, the white-noise roar of turbulence cascading down scales from planetary to granular. There are no animals to make noise, no voices, only the hydrodynamics of a system that has never stabilized. Storms here are three-dimensional gyres the size of old nations, spinning for millennia, throwing off secondary vortices that can snap a newly extruded pillow-lava spine like wet clay. The turbulence is creative as much as destructive: it sculpts, re-melts, re-deposits. Whole provinces of seafloor appear and are erased within a single long season.

Something beyond ordinary physics maintains the suspension and the endless renewal. You notice it first as faint lattices of force that sometimes glitter at the edge of vision—nanoscale or larger, hard to tell—reknitting ruptured currents, accelerating mineral growth in precise Fibonacci plumages, then vanishing. The lattices are not structures; nothing here has been built. They are more like temporary organs of the water itself, expressions of an engineering so thoroughly diffused into the medium that it no longer registers as artifact. The rules feel provisional. Density can reverse for hours. Local gravity tilts. Heat blooms and dies without obvious source. The ocean is still writing its own equations, and the ink is cold, loud, and almost clear.

In the rare stillness between gyres you can hang motionless and listen to the basaltic glass cooling in tiny, crystalline cries, each one a brief bright note in the permanent roar. The filaments sway. Fresh stone rises. The empty distances remain empty. Everything is still becoming.
