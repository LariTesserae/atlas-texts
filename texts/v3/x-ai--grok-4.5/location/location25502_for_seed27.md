The high places are called the Briarholds, though no briar grows there now in any form a lowlander would recognize. What remains is older than naming. The substrate itself is plant: a continuous, continent-spanning mycelial-vegetal mass that long ago consumed its own soils, its own bedrock, and finally the distinction between organism and ground. It stiffened. It mineralized without quite dying. It became the plateau.

You stand on something that was once root and cambium and now is both cork and stone. The surface underfoot is a dull, cool reticulation of compressed xylem plates, pale ochre and ash-lavender, cracked into broad polygons the size of plazas. Between the cracks, thin filaments of living cambium still pulse with a slow, viscous sap that never freezes and never flows as water—water is myth here, a rumor of lower altitudes. Moisture is handled by other chemistries: methyl ethers, locked crystal hydrates, vapor traded directly with the thin air through stomata the size of windows. The air itself tastes faintly of copper and dried resin.

Elevation keeps everything sparse. The Briarholds average four to five kilometers above the abandoned cloud seas. What little atmosphere remains is cold and slow to move, yet the place is never still. The ancient vegetal mass continues its own tectonics. Over centuries a ridge will lift, a canyon of pure heartwood will yawn open and then slowly scar shut. These motions are audible. The plateau groans. It ticks. Sometimes, without warning, a square kilometer of surface will exhale a long bass note that travels for days, a tectonic chord felt in the teeth.

Light arrives dim and sideways. The sun is a pale coin behind perpetual high haze—particulates shed by the living stone itself, spores and silica dust and the slow abrasion of leaf-scales the size of roofs that still, somehow, unfurl and refurl on millennial schedules. Shadows are long even at noon. Everything is seen in perpetual late afternoon or underwater gloom, copper-green and violet.

Scattered across this open expanse, at distances that make them feel like remote towns rather than features of a single landscape, stand the Groves.

Each Grove is a single organism thousands of years old and hundreds of meters tall: a column of fused, spiraling trunks that rose when the world still had rain and then simply kept rising after the rains ended. Their outer bark has become a kind of dry coral, riddled with cavities. Their crowns are flattened, wind-scoured platforms where the last true leaves—black, waxy, almost metallic—catch what light there is and convert it through pathways no longer taught in ordinary chemistry. Between Groves the plateau stretches empty for kilometers, a cool mineral prairie of low, crustose plant-matter and the occasional upright spike of a younger shoot that will take another age to matter.

The Groves are cities.

Human and posthuman settlement has grown into them the way epiphytes once grew on ordinary trees. Habitations are carved, laminated, and exchanged with the living wood. Walls sweat a clear gel that hardens into transparent panes. Staircases are pruned rather than built; they reseal behind you if you linger. At the higher tech densities—and densest they become—the interface turns strange. Certain districts run on circumferential logic engines grown from the Grove’s own vascular cambium, computing with sap pressure and ionic differentials that do not map cleanly onto electronics. Doors open because you have been recognized by the tree’s immune memory. Gravity is locally negotiable near the oldest heartwood; walkways tilt into gentle helices that conserve momentum in ways that feel like courtesy rather than engineering. Power is ambient and sung: resonant cavities fretted into the trunks hum day and night, a low, stacked chord that never resolves, audible for leagues across the open plateau. The sound is part infrastructure, part liturgy, part weather. When the wind rises it plays the Groves like instruments; the cities answer in kind. The combined roar can drown speech yet somehow organizes itself into information for those who have lived here long enough.

Fauna is abundant and largely airborne or wood-boring. Filter-ray flocks the color of bruised fruit sail the thermals between Groves, their skins tattooed with photosynthetic symbionts. They call in overlapping glissandi that braid with the cities’ hum until the whole region becomes a single rough symphony. Higher up, kite-foxes with membrane wings and needle teeth nest in the leeward cavities; they hunt the ray-flocks and also the slow crystal-mites that graze the plateau’s surface crust. Inside the wood live the true crowds: galleries of soft-bodied browsers, luminous thread-snakes, and the occasional large digger whose tunneling keeps the vascular systems from clotting. Predation is constant and mostly quiet, a rustle and a sudden absence, but the collective life noise—stridulation, wingbeat, sap-pop, vocalization—never drops below a living murmur and often swells to something oceanic.

There is almost no free water. What liquid exists is metabolized instantly or bound. Drinking is done by chewing certain galls or by docking with the municipal phloem taps that meter out an emulsion tasting of minerals and faint sweetness. Rain is a historical event; when the rare ice-fog drifts through, every surface steams with sublimation and the fauna goes into brief frenzy. The ecosystem runs on dryness the way others run on rivers.

Time feels thick. The erosion that shaped this place finished its major work so long ago that the land has forgotten how to be dramatic. Edges have been sanded into proverb. What remains is final form: a cool, dim, high table of living lithophyte, sparsely punctuated by loud, glowing, half-awake cities that are also trees, filled with creatures that have never seen a lake and do not miss it. Processes continue—growth, calculation, hunting, the slow inhalation of dust into wood—but they continue inside an equilibrium so old it feels like law. The weirdness is not decorative; it is load-bearing. Physics here has been negotiated with the substrate for so many cycles that certain constants feel optional. You can still fall. You can still freeze. But you may also find yourself walking up a wall because the wall has decided, for the moment, that it is floor, and the city lights will adjust the spectrum so that the transition feels inevitable rather than miraculous.

At the edge of any Grove, where the last root-buttresses sink into the plateau, you can look out across kilometers of open, ancient plant-stone under the dim vault and hear the next city singing its location to the wind. The song is loud. The space between is wider. Both are exact.
