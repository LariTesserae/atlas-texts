The dark arrives before any sense of bottom. You are already inside it—warm water the color of closed eyes, pressure a steady hand on every surface of the body. Light here is not absent so much as spent; what remains is a soft, particulate glow that seems to rise from the soft architecture itself, the way heat sometimes seems to rise from stone at dusk. You hang in it. The city hangs with you.

It was never built for air. That much is clear in the way the streets curve and braid, in the openings that face every direction at once, in the complete lack of roofs or doors. The structures are traffic and destination both: towers of pale, established stone whose bases have grown into each other over centuries until whole districts share a single foundation mass, weathered into rounded shoulders and long horizontal flanges. Between them, narrower corridors of water thread like arteries. Everything has the settled look of something that finished becoming a long time ago and has only been deepening its own habits since. Edges are soft. Corners have forgotten how to be sharp. The stone is pitted and fine-grained, halfway to bone in color, and every surface is gently scalloped by the same slow current that still moves through the avenues like breath.

Above a certain height the towers thin and stop. There is no skyline in the ordinary sense—only a gradual surrender to the open dark, a regional expanse of pillars and platforms and hanging galleries that fades, at the edge of perception, into more of the same. The scale is town-sized and then valley-sized; you can feel other districts elsewhere in the murk the way you feel other rooms in a large house. Distance here is measured in layered silence and the occasional string of bioluminescent signals drifting between far towers like slow punctuation.

Vegetation is almost a rumor. Here and there a thin banner of something kelp-dark unfurls from a crevice, or a mat of felted growth softens a ledge, but mostly the hardscape is bare in the way deep places are bare—claimed instead by animals and their constructions. The density is animal. Every stay and arch is occupied. Filter-feeders carpet the undersides of bridges in overlapping plates. Schools of small, flattened swimmers turn as single thoughts through the wider channels, their flanks catching what little light there is and throwing it away again. Larger shapes hold station in the lee of towers: long-bodied things with slow fins, armored grazers working the sparse films of growth, predators that look like folded knives until they unfold. They do not startle easily. This is their weather, their hour, their established order. The whole place pulses with small motions—gills, cilia, the minute revision of position against current—yet the sound stays low, a near-quiet of water over stone and the soft click and hush of living bodies adjusting. Only occasionally does something larger announce itself: a distant boom of displaced water, a chain of pops as a colony of something contracts, then stillness again.

There is no metal. No glass. No tool-mark sharper than the long negotiation between creature and current and mineral. Whatever minds first raised these forms—or grew them, or sang them out of the substrate—left no devices behind, only the forms. In the warm dark the city continues as biology continues: shelter becoming body becoming shelter. Anemone-like gardens have claimed the older plazas, their tentacles feathering the same rhythm as the current. Shell spirals the size of rooms have been incorporated into walls as if the stone learned the habit of coiling. In places the architecture itself seems slightly alive, or once-alive—surfaces that sweat a faint mineral milk, joints that have sealed with pearlescent scar. The weirdness is quiet and local, never theatrical: a corridor that ends in a perfect sphere of still water denser than the rest; a tower whose windows open onto other windows in recursive tight spirals; a district where every surface is etched with the healed tracks of countless feeding radiolars, so the stone reads like worn script no one living remembers how to voice.

Time moves in layered tides. Warm currents arrive from somewhere lower and farther, lifting silt in slow curtains that dim the already-dim, then clear again. Spawning happens in simultaneous clouds that briefly make the water thick as smoke before the soft rain of eggs drifts into every crevice. Predators follow the clouds; cleaners follow the predators; the stone accepts the residue and grows a little more opaque, a little more itself. Nothing here feels new. Nothing feels finished. The city has the equilibrium of a reef that learned streets, or a street-plan that surrendered to reef pressure until the difference stopped mattering.

If you rest against a wall, you feel the faint transmission of other lives through the mineral—scrapes, settlings, the distant thunder of a heavy body turning in its sleep far below. The water holds you. The dark holds the water. The established, low, abundant place continues, dense with near-touching presence, almost silent, almost lightless, entirely underwater, and in no particular hurry to be anything else.
