The plain is not ground. Nothing here ever was. What holds you is a continuous deck of fitted basalt blocks, each face still bearing the clean scars of antler wedges and polished river-stone hammers, the joints so tight that water has nowhere to go except the deliberate channels cut between them. The blocks have never weathered. Their edges remain razor-true, as if the last tool struck only moments ago and then time simply refused to continue. Heat trembles above them in thick, unmoving sheets.

Water stands everywhere yet never advances. It wells from hairline seams that should not exist in stone this dense, filling shallow rectangular pools no deeper than a forearm, a lattice of black mirrors that covers half the plain. Between the pools the blocks rise only a hand-span, still perfectly level, still dry on top. The air itself sweats; moisture beads on every vertical face and slides down in slow, silent runs that never quite reach the next joint before vanishing. There is no rain and no drying. The wetness is permanent, the temperature a constant bright fever.

Vegetation occupies the illuminated gaps between the channels with deliberate sparsity. Tall reeds of a braided, glassy green thrust up in tight circular groves, each grove spaced far enough from the next that you can walk the empty stone avenues without brushing a stem. Their roots do not pierce the basalt; they bulb against it in dense, pale knots, drinking the surface film. Between the reeds, sprawling mats of low, rubbery leaf cover the block tops like disciplined carpets, thick enough to walk on without sound. Flowers open and stay open—wide white cups that hold a single drop of condensate forever. Nothing wilts. Nothing seeds beyond its allotted circle. The lushness is absolute inside its boundaries and strictly absent outside them.

Light is total and shadowless. It has no source and no direction; it simply *is*, a hard white presence that bleaches color to its edges and makes every droplet throw a miniature rainbow that hangs without moving. The heat does not fluctuate. The air does not stir. Under this light the entire construction—blocks, channels, reed circles—extends to a low, sharp horizon that never softens. Features repeat at mid-range intervals: another raised platform of woven cane lashed with sinew, another sunken court whose walls still show the chisel marks in perfect parallel, another grove. The scale is large enough that distant platforms appear as dark teeth against the brightness, yet never so large that the pattern dissolves.

Fauna is everywhere and almost silent. Pale crabs the size of fists click once, then freeze for hours along the water seams. Long-legged insects with translucent wings glide between reeds and land without vibration. Schools of thumb-sized silver fish hang motionless just beneath the surface of every pool, their gills working so slowly the water does not ripple. Higher up, something like herons stands one-legged on the cane platforms; their feathers are the same pale green as the reeds and they do not shift weight. Occasionally a low, velvety thrum passes through the stone—perhaps many throats at once—then ceases. The quiet is not empty; it is held.

Everything artificial is primitive in technique and absolute in extent. There is no unworked substrate, no soil that was not slaked and poured, no stone that was not carried and dressed by hand. Rope bridges of braided vine link raised walks; the knots are simple, endless, and never fray. Where walls rise they are dry-stacked, slightly battering inward, their tops unfinished yet perfectly even. Tools lie where they were set down: bone levers, wooden mallets swollen with permanent damp, flint adzes still sharp enough to shave hair. No metal. No wheel. The builders—if they still move among the groves—have left only these and the faint smell of crushed resin.

The weirdness is not spectacle; it is structural. Stand long enough and the perfectly straight channels begin to refuse single vanishing points. Walk a right-angle corner and arrive facing the direction you came from, the same grove waiting, the same crab still frozen mid-step. Reflections in the pools sometimes show the underside of the blocks instead of the sky. The heat makes the air thick enough to hold a handprint for several breaths before the print slowly rises and dissipates without a breeze. Time feels optional. The young edges never dull; the water never stagnates or clears; the flowers never close. The place has reached an equilibrium so complete that change itself has been constructed out of existence, replaced by a quiet, bright, wet, impossible present that continues in every direction without needing to become anything else.
