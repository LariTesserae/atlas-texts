The highland rises as if it has only just decided to exist. A broad, fractured plateau, still sharp at every edge, still shedding sheets of itself into the steamy air. Nothing here is old enough to have been rounded. The rock—if it was ever rock—has been replaced completely. What holds the elevation is a single continuous organism, or a confederacy of them, grown into load-bearing architecture and then left half-finished, sparse, and furiously alive.

From a distance the place looks almost empty: vast open intervals of glowing green-black substrate stretched between solitary megastructures. Each structure is a tower, a pavilion, a cantilevered gallery, or a collapsing arcade made entirely of living tissue. Trunks thicker than houses braid themselves into load-bearing columns; leaves the size of sails have stiffened into translucent roofing that still photosynthesizes while it sheds water in continuous sheets. Roots and rhizomes have been trained—or have trained themselves—into staircases, aqueducts, and flying buttresses that end in mid-air because the next connection has not yet grown or has already died back. Between these scattered giants lie kilometers of open, breathing ground: a deep spongy mat of mosses, liverworts, and fine aerial roots that hold water the way lungs hold air. The mat is never still. It pulses upward in slow waves, then subsides, releasing warm fogs that condense immediately on every surface.

It is hot. The heat does not come from a sun alone; it wells up from inside the living mass, a metabolic fever that keeps the entire plateau several degrees above what the altitude should allow. Water is everywhere and restless. It weeps from the cut faces of brand-new growth. It pools in the hollows of leaves that serve as cisterns one hour and split open the next, dumping cascading curtains into the moss below. It rises as thick white breath from exhalation pores the size of doorways, then cools and rains back within a hundred meters, so that parts of the plateau live in their own private cloudburst while neighboring spans remain only damp and steaming. The air tastes of crushed chlorophyll, mineral sap, and rain that has never touched stone.

There are no animals. No insects stitch the air; no birds test the thermals. The only motion is vegetal and hydrological, yet the sound is loud and nearly constant. Cell walls rupture with the noise of wet timber splitting. Entire galleries twist themselves into new orientations overnight, groaning like ships in heavy seas. Pent-up reservoirs inside the larger trunks find sudden outlets and fire jets of warm water sideways into the open air, slapping down onto the moss with the force of small waterfalls. When two growing fronts meet they do not gently graft; they grind, braid, and sometimes burst, spraying sticky aerosols that smell of copper and nectar. Wind—when it finds a path through the sparse towers—rubs the stiff leaves together until they roar like distant surf.

Everything is constructed, yet nothing is mechanical. The plateau has no bare substrate left; every gram of material has been metabolized into plant. Paths appear because rhizomes choose to thicken in a line for a few days, then deliquesce again. Chambers open inside the larger trunks, lined with luminous cambium, only to be flooded and reabsorbed when the structure decides it needs the volume elsewhere. The scale is regional: you can walk for hours between one great living keep and the next, always within sight of several others standing like unfinished thoughts across the steam. The emptiness between them is not barren; it is simply less dense, a deliberate sparse tundra of green architecture under a sky that is usually pearled with its own evaporate.

Change is the ruling weather. A tower that stood straight at dawn may list by noon, its lower trunks softening into marsh while its crown continues to climb, seeking a light that keeps shifting as the fog banks roam. Whole colonnades collapse into themselves with wet thunder and are already being mined for nutrients by their neighbors before the echoing stops. New spires erupt from the moss mat in jagged, almost crystalline flushes of growth—sharp because they are minutes old—only to be sculpted by cascading water into softer forms within a day. The plateau feels less like a landscape than like a single fevered mind trying stolen shapes for a body it has not finished inventing.

At the rare moments when the light clarifies, the place reveals its full strangeness: an entire highland basin converted into sparse, monumental garden architecture that builds and unbuilds itself without pause, hot and drowning in its own breath, utterly without creatures or tools, loud with the labor of becoming.
