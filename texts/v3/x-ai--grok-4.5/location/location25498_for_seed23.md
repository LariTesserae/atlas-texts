The place is a hanging cirque no larger than a cathedral nave stretched wide, a shallow stone bowl hung high on the flank of a larger massif you cannot see. Its boundaries are absolute: a sheer horseshoe of weeping rock that rises three storeys and then folds inward, and a lip of polished bedrock that drops into white nothing. Everything that matters happens inside this cup.

Water is the first and last fact. It does not fall so much as exude. The cliffs sweat in slow sheets that never quite freeze despite the cold; they glaze, crack, re-form, glaze again. Rills braided finger-thick across every surface seek the lowest seams and disappear into them with almost no sound. Underfoot the ground is a skin of saturated scree and dark peat no thicker than a hand, floating on a lens of melt that never drains. Breath condenses instantly into micro-crystallite fog that hangs at knee height until the next bright hour burns it into glitter and then builds it again. Pools form in every hollow left by an old hoof or a collapsed wall; they are ink-black until the light hits them full and then they become mirrors of unbearable white.

There is almost no true soil and almost no true plant. What vegetation exists is a sparse punctuation of tough, low things: glass-bladed sedges the colour of old pewter, mats of black lichen that look poured rather than grown, and occasional upright stalks of something like frozen thistle that shatter if touched hard. They cling to the leeward sides of stones and to the wet runnels where nutrient scrap collects. Their sparseness makes the density of everything else more shocking.

The settlement is packed into the bowl so tightly that “street” and “wall” and “roof” have traded meanings. Dry-stone courses, scavenged plates of schist, stacked and gabled with slabs of clear lake-ice cut in some previous season, lean into one another like exhausted animals. Doorways are low, lintels made of single basaltic ribs worn concave by centuries of shoulders. There is no metal beyond a few soft-copper awls and fishhooks hammered cold; everything else is bone, sinew, lashed willow brought up from far below, and the patient fitting of stone to stone. The architecture has the settled patience of something that has been rebuilt in place a hundred times after the same slow disasters. Corners are rounded by wind-driven grit. Thresholds hold the ghost polish of bare feet. In places the walls themselves weep the same water as the cliffs, so that interiors are permanently beaded and the air inside tastes of wet iron and cold moss.

Light is total and without mercy. There is no soft hour. Whether the sky is a hard lid of cobalt or a white-out of ice fog, the illumination arrives as glare off every wet surface, every ice roof, every eye. Shadows exist only as thin indigo knives under overhangs; elsewhere the world is overexposed, edges bitten white. You squint until tears come, and the tears freeze at the corners of your eyes into grit you must blink away.

Despite the cold and the altitude and the scarcity of green, life crowds every volume. The air is thick with small bodies. Pale moths the size of thumbnails clinch and unclinch on the undersides of ice eaves. Hairless ground-dwelling birds scuttle in platoons between the stone feet of houses, their calls reduced to dry clicks. Something like a fox but longer in the spine and with eyes the colour of wet slate pours itself along rooftops, pausing to taste meltwater from the weeping walls. In the black pools, translucent crustaceans row in dense, silent schools that turn as one whenever a shadow passes. Higher, against the bright cliff face, colonies of swallow-like things with membrane wings cling in layered ranks, unmoving for hours, then peel off in sudden whispered sheets that rearrange the entire upper air before settling again in a different order. The density is such that stillness never lasts; a gap of empty space is only a breath before something occupies it.

All of this should roar, yet the bowl keeps an uncanny quiet. Water moves without chatter. Wings make only the softest paper sound. Voices—when the people speak—are low and close-mouthed, words shaped more by gesture and exhaled plume than by volume. Even the collapses are muted: a section of ice roof detaches, slides, powder-bursts on the lane below, and the only lasting noise is the faint ticking of residual crystals settling into new seams. The silence is not empty; it is padded, as if the thin air itself has been stuffed with invisible felt.

The turbulence lives mostly in the eye and in the slow-motion violence of materials. Nothing stays fixed for a full day. A wall that was dry-stone at dawn may be partially re-mortared by afternoon with a translucent blue ice the builders never intended; they simply accept the new bond and continue. Paths of packed scree liquefy into ankle-deep black slurry and then re-freeze overnight into ridged glass that must be chipped open with bone wedges. The hanging colonies of membrane-wings rearrange their hierarchy three or four times between one bright hour and the next, dropping a fine rain of guano and moulted skin that the ground-birds harvest instantly. People move among these changes with the same settled fatalism as the architecture: they shore, they lash, they re-stack, they accept that the bowl is permanently unfinished and permanently complete. Sometimes a seam in the weeping cliff opens wider and a new sheet of water finds a route through someone’s sleeping niche; they wake, shift their sleeping skins two body-lengths left, and go back to the work of fitting stone. The place has the exhausted equilibrium of something that has been coming apart and coming together for longer than anyone’s lineages remember.

There is a strangeness under the strangeness. Reflections in the black pools occasionally lag a half-second behind the motions they mirror. The bright light sometimes carries a second, cooler brightness inside it, as if a dimmer sun were nested within the glare and flickered on its own obscure schedule. Certain stones, when struck for tool-making, release a brief smell of thunderstorm that has never belonged at this altitude. The people do not discuss these things. They simply work around them the way they work around the water and the cold and the crowding wings. The bowl itself seems to regard all of it—settlement, animals, ice, glare—as a single weathered organism that has reached its own long balance of destruction and reassembly. You stand inside it for an hour and begin to feel the same quiet density pressing against your ribs: wet, cold, saturated with lives, dazzling, hush-filled, and forever arriving at the shape it already is.
