The basin has no proper name anymore, only designations stenciled on half-dissolved bulkheads and repeated in the low-frequency thrum that passes for cartography here. It sits in a shallow concavity of the world’s crust, elevation barely above the old abyssal plains, and the water has claimed it completely. Not a lake. Not a drowned valley. A continuous warm medium, syrup-thick in places, clear as gin in others, extending without surface or floor you can trust. You move through it the way thought moves through fever.

Light arrives late and exhausted. What reaches this depth is a bruised indigo, sometimes punctured by the momentary flare of a fail-safe still cycling inside some sealed housing. Everything is edged in that half-seen quality; edges themselves refuse to stay put. The terrain is young—geologically arrogant. Basalt ribs and fresh fault scarps stand sharp, barely softened by the warm current. They look poured rather than eroded, as if the rock finished cooling only last century and has not yet learned patience. Between them the sediments are black and fine, disturbed into slow underwater dunes that continually unmake and remake themselves.

Vegetation thrives with the single-minded density of something that no longer negotiates. Kelp-like columns thick as smokestacks rise in staggered ranks, their holdfasts gripping the young stone so tightly the rock itself seems to crease. Between them billow translucent veils of photosynthetic film, layered three and four deep, each membrane slightly out of phase with the next so that looking through them produces a moiré of green-black and sudden electric lime. The plants do not sway; they twitch. Growth here is visible, restless. A frond lengthens while you watch, joints dilating, then retracts as if embarrassed by its own appetite. In the denser thickets the vegetation has begun to incorporate metal: copper salts turning leaves turquoise, iron filings jewelled along midribs, small turbine blades exhaled like seeds and already sprouting secondary foliage.

Scattered through this green machinery are the industrial remnants—never ruins exactly, more like organs that have not been told the body died. A cooling tower half-swallowed by a forest of tube-worms made of polymer and chlorophyll still vents a column of superheated water that screams as it rises. The scream is ultrasonic at first, then drops into the auditory murder range, then becomes a pressure you feel in the teeth. Catwalks twist away into the gloom, their gratings clogged with living filter-feeds that pulse in time with distant pumps. Here and there a full assembly hall remains pressurized, windows glowing the dull orange of emergency sodium. Inside, robotic arms continue their last assigned motion—spot-welding emptiness, stacking invisible crates—while the exterior plates blister and grow barnacles of pure silica that chime when the current strikes them.

The sound is total. Not noise layered on silence but a continuous demolition of silence. Turbines the size of cathedrals still spool somewhere below the sediment, their imbalance transmitted through the water as a physical shove. Metal fatigue sings in frequencies that make vision stutter. Bubbles the size of cars tear loose from vents and collapse with the concussive authority of anti-aircraft fire. Under it all, a wetter, organic roar: the vegetation respiring, millions of stomata opening and closing in imperfect unison, like a crowd trying to remember a song. When two growth fronts collide they do not merge; they grind, producing a continuous fibrous shriek.

Reality is a local ordinance and poorly enforced. Distance dilates. A latticework of pipes thirty meters away will suddenly occupy the space directly before your face, dripping warm condensate that tastes of ozone and pollen, then snap back with a rubber-band recoil that leaves afterimages of itself stranded in the water. Causality frays at the edges of the denser plant stands. You may watch a girder oxidize into lace and then, without transition, watch the same girder under construction, rivets spinning themselves tight in reverse. Time pools in the lee of large objects. In one such pocket a single drop of coolant has been falling for what must be years, elongated into a silver thread that never quite breaks. In another, an entire work-crew’s worth of empty hard-hats orbits a lamp post in perfect silent ellipses, the only motion that makes no sound.

There are no animals. No fish, no amphipods, no scavengers. The ecology is a closed argument between plant and machine and the water that mediates them. Nutrients cycle through sap and hydraulic fluid interchangeably. Pollen is metallic and conducts. Occasionally a section of fern will tear free, thrash once in an approximation of panic, then reverse its own buoyancy and drill down into the sediment to become a new foundation. The water itself feels purposeful—warm, slightly viscous, carrying a faint electrical charge that makes exposed nerves glitter with phantom signal.

Storm-states are the default. Currents reverse without warning, slamming living curtains of weed against turbine intakes until the blades shred them into green-black avalanches that then reconstitute downstream as denser, angrier thickets. Pressure fronts roll through like invisible architecture, folding space so that a distant smoking chimney is suddenly bordering your shoulder, its heat blooming against skin, its industrial stink of burnt lubricant and nectar overwhelming. Then it is gone again, leaving only the tactile memory and a new bruise of colder water.

At the limits of the basin—if limits is even the word—the young scarps rise toward a surface that may or may not exist. The water does not lighten. It only becomes more densely itself, stacked with refraction and unresolved process. Somewhere above, or below, or folded adjacent, larger engines turn. Their vibration arrives as weather. You feel it first in the marrow, then in the way the plants lean as if hearing a name, then in the sudden, deafening unanimity of every unfinished machine starting, or finishing, or starting to finish, all at once.

This is not a place that was flooded.  
This is a place that learned how to breathe liquid and never saw a reason to stop.
