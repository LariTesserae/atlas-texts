The lowlands do not so much begin as they forget to stop. Standing anywhere inside them is to stand already deep, on a floor that is not soil but a compression of past green—petiole mats, collapsed petiole ribs, the flattened ghosts of leaves that once held canopy and now hold weight. Elevation is a rumor here; what rises does so only because something living decided it should, and what falls does the same. The air is warm in the way a mouth is warm, close and faintly sweet with the chemistry of ruptured cells. Light arrives normally, as day, but it is strained through so many successive screens of foliage that it arrives as a green-gold pressure rather than a direction. Shadows do not fall so much as pool and then get reabsorbed.

There is water, but it does not commit. It beads on the underside of every surface, gathers in the crooks of stilting roots, threads itself along veins until a leaf tip can no longer bear the weight and a single drop falls the short distance to the next living layer, where it is taken in before it can become a puddle. Occasional rain does not so much wet the place as remind it of its own internal weather; after each shower the entire mass exhales a warm fog that hangs, thickens, and is drunk back by stomata the size of open hands. Pockets of true wetness exist—old hollows where three or four trunks lean together and form a temporary cistern—but they are temporary, inherited, always on the verge of being grown over or drunk dry. The damp is a constant negotiation rather than a presence.

Everything is plant. Not plants-on-top-of-something; plant-as-the-something. The ground is a rhizome plate yards thick, knitted so tight that a footfall does not sink so much as get accepted and then gently resisted. From it rise the architectures: trunks that bifurcate into corridors, branch-systems that weave themselves into vaults and then unweave overnight, walls of living bark that pulse with slow hydraulic pressure. There are streets here if street means a linear absence maintained by continuous competitive shading. There are plazas if plaza means a momentary equilibration where five or six canopy giants have agreed, for now, not to overtop one another, leaving a shaft of thicker light. The agreement never lasts. This is a city that never finishes being built because it never stops being undone. New storeys corkscrewing upward in a matter of hours; older ones slumping into mulch while still occupied by the vines that used to be their plumbing. No mortar, no cut stone, no metal—only the continuous argument of apical meristems.

The density is near-solid. To move is to shoulder through curtains of hanging roots, to step over sleeping coils of liana that will be taut as cables by evening, to feel the air itself thickened with pollen, with spores, with the fine green dust of abraded cuticle. Leaves press against leaves until the distinction between individual and colony evaporates. Photosynthesis is a roar you can almost see: a continuous shivering adjustment of orientation as every surface hunts the shifting light. Where one plant dies, three others have already begun the work of occupying its volume. There is no empty cubic handspan that is not either occupied or contested.

And yet the scale remains open. You can climb a spiral of fused twiners to a temporary platform and look out across a valley that is itself only a larger expression of the same process: low ridges of older, slower growth; fresher sinklands where the rhizome plate has heaved and torn; distant pale spires that might be record-height canopy or simply the current generation of aspiring monuments. The horizon is a furred green line that occasionally twitches as some massive failure or sudden ascension rewrites a silhouette. It feels regional rather than infinite—bounded by something just out of clear view, perhaps the cooler uplands where this total plant regime begins to fray, or by a coast the water never quite reaches.

Sound is the medium everything else travels through. It is deafening in the precise sense that it leaves no quiet in which to hear yourself think. Not one note but a stacked continuum: the deep hydraulic groan of water being forced through xylem under anomalous pressure; the higher, continuous creak-and-pop of cells elongating; the sudden rifle-crack of a branch failing under its own ambition; the whisper-roar of millions of leaves adjusting; the wet velvet tear of a fruiting body opening somewhere in the mid-canopy; the subsonic thud when a whole architectural mass loses coherence and settles. Over all of it, a high insectile whine that is not insects—there are no animals here, or almost none, only the rare softmoth or translucent hopper that seems less like fauna than like a mobile plant organ that learned to detach. The sound never pauses. It only changes key when the light does, or when a rain passes, or when one of the larger reality failures begins.

Those failures are no longer rare. Reality here has grown porous under the sheer metabolic insistence of the vegetation. In places the geometry simply gives up: a corridor you have followed for fifty paces opens into the same corridor seen from the opposite direction, as if space folded and glued itself wrong. Elsewhere, time shears—young shoots on one side of a trunk lignify into heartwood while the other side remains meristematic jelly, the two ages grinding against each other with a noise like wet stone. Gravities multiply; pollen falls up into a hanging garden that is somehow below you. Color ceases to respect wavelength; a leaf will flash ultraviolet home-signal and then settle back into ordinary green as if embarrassed. The most unsettling failures are the quiet ones: a perfectly ordinary courtyard of moss and knee-high ferns that, if you stand in it long enough, begins to remember a different layout, walls rising and dissolving in the corner of your eye, the memory of a street grid that was never built trying to assert itself through pure plant process. The city dreams of being planned even as it refuses every plan.

The dynamism is total. This is not a landscape that changes with seasons; it is a landscape that is the season, continuously. Growths surge and collapse in hours. Whole districts of architecture can be declared, inhabited by the softer epiphytes, and then reabsorbed while the light still holds. Vapor storms of self-generated humidity rise without warning, condense into brief torrents, and are gone. The youngness of the place is legible in its sharp edges: every new trunk still bears the pale sheath of its emergence; every leaf edge is still serrated with the violence of its unfolding; the rhizome plate, where it has torn, shows white, wet, almost mineral fracture planes that have not yet been softened by succession. It feels recently decided, as if the pure plant regime only lately won whatever contest used to exist here and has not yet learned restraint.

No tools. No devices. Nothing that is not grown or shed. The few soft animals that persist are irrelevant to the metabolism; they are tolerated the way a healthy body tolerates its own minor flora. Navigation is by gradient of scent, by the grain of the densest growth, by the memory of which failures are currently open and which have sealed. To live here—if anything can be said to live here in the way minds understand living—is to accept that every path is temporary, every shelter provisional, every silence a myth told by the deaf. The plants are not evil and not kind. They are simply the entire remaining alphabet of the world, and they are spelling themselves as fast as they can, louder than thought, warmer than blood, denser than any permission.

Stand long enough and the damp will bead on your skin and then the nearest surface will begin the slow work of deciding whether you are substrate or rival. The sound will not lessen. The light will not clarify. Somewhere a vaulted gallery of braided trunks will finish becoming, hold its shape for the length of a held breath, and then begin, without malice or warning, the next necessary collapse.
