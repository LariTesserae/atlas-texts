The basin holds itself the way a cupped hand holds rain that never drains. It sits high, where the air thins and the rock still remembers the violence of its own making—sharp shoulders of basalt and pale rhyolite, edges unsoftened, fresh fracture planes catching the cool light like wet knives. Nothing green has ever taken root here. The water arrived too soon and stayed too completely. What little atmosphere remains above the surface is a thin skin of mist and cold spray; everything that matters lives beneath.

You descend through that skin and the world goes blue-gray and close. The pool is not large. From one sheer wall to the opposite takes perhaps the length of a nave, the depth of a choir loft twice over. Yet the volume is densely occupied. Bodies move in slow, deliberate currents that never quite still. The water is cold enough to ache in the teeth, heavy with dissolved minerals that catch the daylight filtering down from the open sky above and break it into shifting lozenges of ordinary brightness—no gloom, just the patient illumination of a high place that has been drowned.

The floor is a clutter of young rubble: angular blocks still wearing the bright faces of lately broken stone, some the size of a clenched fist, some large enough that a swimmer must turn sideways to slip between them. Among and upon these blocks the fauna thrives in numbers that feel almost indecent. They are not fish. They are not anything with a familiar silhouette. Pale carapaces jointed like piston housings, soft mantles that flex with the measured rhythm of bellows, clusters of articulated limbs ending in flat grinding plates that rasp continuously against the rock. They work the stone. Slowly. Patiently. Turning sharp edges into finer grit, sorting mineral grains by density, stacking thin tablets of precipitated silica into precarious towers that already lean and will one day topple under their own slight weight. There is no hurry. The processes are industrial in scale of repetition and in the specialised labour of each form, yet nothing here was stamped or forged by mind. The machinery is flesh and shell and the slow chemistry of cool water against newborn rock.

Sound fills every hollow. Not the roar of catastrophe, but a layered, continuous loudness: the wet clicking of myriads of grinding plates, the low chuffing of water forced through living siphons, the occasional deeper knock when two larger bodies collide and rebound without haste or aggression. Bubbles rise from fissures in the basin floor—gas vents that have not yet learned subtlety—each bubble a brief dull knell as it detaches and climbs. The whole small world hums and clatters and sighs at a volume that would be oppressive on land; underwater it becomes the medium itself, a pressure of noise against the eardrums and the ribs.

Above, the jagged rim cuts a hard circle of sky. Light enters cleanly and spreads without drama. No forests dappled it; no algae clouds it. The water carries only the fine white dust of its own slow abrasion and the faint iridescence of whatever oils or films the creatures secrete while they labour. Currents are weak, almost respectful. A body can hang motionless for long minutes and drift only the width of a hand. Change happens, but measured in seasons of grinding rather than in moments. A tower of silica leaves grows a little taller. A carapace darkens with mineral varnish. A vent’s note deepens by a fraction as the crack widens one more grain. The place is still becoming what it will be, and it will take a very long time.

There are no paths, no markers, no remnants of intention. Whatever intelligence the movement suggests is distributed, combinatorial, older than purpose. You are simply another density among densities—another slow equation of drag and buoyancy—inside a high, cold, crowded machine that no one built and that does not know it is running.
