The Great Dry begins where the last fracture of the upper world pinches shut. There is no doorway, only a gradual surrender of surface logic: rock closes overhead like a slow eyelid, light dies by degrees that feel personal, and then the volume opens. It does not open into a cavern. It opens into a country.

You are already inside a darkness that has weight. The dark is warm—blood-warm, skin-warm, the steady internal heat of a body that never cools. No sun has ever reached this far; whatever furnace keeps the temperature constant lies deeper still, or perhaps resides in the rock itself. Moisture is a theoretical substance here. The air tastes of powdered feldspar and something sharper, like the ghost of salt that has forgotten how to dissolve. Tongues crack. Eyes, if you still trust them, gum shut with mineral grit that was never wet.

The land is young in the way a broken bone is young before the body begins to knit it. Everywhere the mineral has spiked. Black volcanic glass and pale labradorite thrust out in blades, needles, chevaux-de-frise, some thin as hairs, some broad as buttresses, all of them edged as if flaked this morning. They grow from floor, from walls that are only other floors tilted, from ceilings that curve until orientation loses meaning. The formations lock into one another so densely that the remaining space is a network of narrow, many-angled corridors never wider than a body with its arms tight. In places the crystals have crossed and recrossed until they form rigid foams, chambers the size of lungs, then the size of Cathedrals, then the size of weather systems—except there is no weather. The air does not move. Dust, once loosed, hangs for a time measured in centuries and then settles into the microscopic serrations and stays. There are no gradients left to drive a current. The vastness is real; you could walk a thousand years in any consistent direction and still find the same interlocking symptoms of recent, violent crystallization. Yet nothing erodes. The edges stay knife-strict. Time has been persuaded to stop revising its work.

Life occupies almost every remaining interval. The fauna are abundant the way darkness is abundant. Soft, slow rivers of flesh the color of unlit basalt pour themselves between the needles, tasting the glass with rasping fields of microscopic teeth. They leave behind faint matte trails that dull the crystal shine for a while; then the polish returns of its own frozen accord. Larger shapes cling where the voids widen—angular, many-jointed, carapaces like unfinished dual pyramids. They wait. When something warm brushes a standing leg, there is one economical movement, a sound no louder than a dry twig flexed inside a fist, and then perfect stillness resumes. Nothing advertises itself. Communication happens by contact: flanks pressed to flanks, heat exchanged, chemical ledgers updated. A traveler moving carefully feels the population as successive gentle resistances, a crowded hush of bodies that part just enough and close again. They do not flee. They do not pursue unless touched. Their numbers never thin or swell; the total mass of living tissue appears to have been fixed at the same moment the crystals finished erupting, an ecology balanced on the head of a pin and thereafter unwilling to tip.

Sound is almost absent. Once in a great while a distant tick marks the infinitesimal contraction of some cooling blade. Softer still: the whisper of dorsal plates sliding past one another, the nearly inaudible crunch of a successful wait. No wind. No drip. No voice. The ear begins to invent pulses simply to confirm it still functions.

Scattered through this endless mineral thicket are the marks of hands. They are never dense enough to form a settlement, only sudden clarities: a short run of dry-stacked slabs fitted by trial and geometry alone, a spiral shelf cut into a pillar thicker than a watchtower so that someone could ascend a few body-lengths and stop, a floor where every needle has been deliberately snapped at root height and the stumps ground smooth with sand and patience. The tool signatures are plain—hammerstone dimples, the crescent bites of antler wedges, cordage grooves polished by long use. No smelted metal, or only the rare beaten nodule of native copper used as a pure ornament and then abandoned. Ochre lines remain vivid because nothing here fades; rows of nicks count something that no longer needs counting. The current inhabitants treat these places as minor variations in topography, convenient absences of sharpness. Whoever stacked the stones either left or became something that no longer stacks stones. The structures feel less like ruins than like hypotheses set down and never retrieved.

Nav­igation is tactile and gustatory. The tongue learns the subtle differences in mineral dust: this corridor tastes of lithium sharpness, that vault of warmer magnesium. Certain of the greatest blades, when palms are pressed flat against them, transmit a subsonic hum that briefly rearranges the inner ear into a map—distances, densities, the locations of the next three stacked-stone clearings—then withdraws the knowledge the moment contact breaks. Gravity itself is polite but untrustworthy; “down” can describe a long curve that returns you to your own footprints from above. The warmth never stratifies. You could hang upside-down for years and feel no blood rush, only the same even heat.

Nothing changes. A body that dies is dismantled with the same unhurried precision as everything else and the nutrients re-enter the closed count. A mark cut today will be found, edge still bright, by whatever passes here after the upper world has forgotten its own name. The Great Dry is not hostile. Hostility would require the expenditure of energy toward alteration. It is merely finished, densely occupied by lives that perfected their stillness early, and lit by no light except the occasional faint sea-green stitched through a translucent gut when digestion briefly admits a photon or two into the permanent dark. Those photons show nothing new. They only confirm the sharpness, the crowding, the quiet, and the scattered, stubborn evidence that someone once tried, with primitive care, to make a floor level enough to sleep on.
