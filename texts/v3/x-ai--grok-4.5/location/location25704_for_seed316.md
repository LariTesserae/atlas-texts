The plain does not end. It has no memory of having begun. Whatever mountains once stood here finished their long argument with rain so many cycles ago that the word “ago” no longer applies; the rock simply accepted the verdict and lay down. What remains is a continental sheet of basalt and compressed clay so thoroughly planed that curvature itself seems optional. You walk for days and the horizon stays exactly where it was, a soft graphite line drawn by someone who has forgotten what distance is for.

Yet the ground will not stay still.

It is warm underfoot—body-warm, fever-warm—because the heat of old compression never fully escaped. Water works through every seam. Not rivers in the ordinary sense: the plain is too flat for lasting channels. Instead the water rises from below, forced up by the slow internal settling of a world that has already settled as far as matter ordinarily allows. It weeps from the rock in constant, fine beads. It condenses out of the dim air in sheets so sudden they feel like fabric being dropped over your shoulders. Shallow lenses of standing water appear and vanish between one breath and the next, each one a temporary silver eye reflecting nothing, because there is almost no sky to reflect—only a low, brownish soffit of cloud that never lifts and never fully rains, only leaks.

The light is the color of old tea left in a cup. It comes from nowhere particular. Shadows are faint suggestions rather than directions. In this half-light the sparse vegetation looks like a mistake that keeps being corrected. There are stands of something like reed, black-stemmed and jointed, growing in loose constellations many kilometers apart. Their leaves are thin as paper and constantly shredding into the wind that is not quite wind. Between them the mud shines with a skin of algae so dark it reads as absence. Nothing taller than a person has kept its shape for long; the turbulence sees to that.

Because the plain is not quiet.

The sound is structural. It is the sound of a planet continually finishing its own erosion and then having to start again. Great slabs of the surface shear sideways with the slow scream of wet stone. Hollow booms roll out of the mist when cavities collapse somewhere beyond sight. Water finds new vertical paths for a few minutes at a time—brief, deafening curtains of white that appear in mid-air where two pressure regimes fail to agree, then extinguish themselves as the agreement is violently restored. There is a continuous low grinding, like continents chewing empty space. Your ears ring even in the rare lulls; the lulls themselves are only shifts in which frequencies dominate. Conversation is impossible. Thought arrives already half-drowned.

Density here is not about objects in space. Space itself has been over-occupied. The air is so saturated with water that breathing has weight. Particulate rock flour hangs in permanent suspension, turning every direction into a close, textured wall that parts only reluctantly. More than that: the turbulence keeps folding earlier versions of the plain into the present one. You will walk across a stretch of cracked mud and feel, under the sole, the ghost of a staircase that existed for eleven minutes three centuries ago—or will exist tomorrow—before the water rearranged the argument. Translucent plates of former groundplane slide through one another like cards being shuffled by an impatient dealer. Edges do not always match. Sometimes a piece of colder air, sharp as glass, drops out of a fold and shatters against the warmth, leaving a temporary crater of frost that immediately begins to sweat and vanish.

Reality has the consistency of wet paper being rewritten while you stand on it.

Scattered across this are the built things—though “built” is a fragile word here. Low rings of fitted stone, ankle-high, the color of the plain itself, half-swallowed. A single wall segment leaning at an angle that has nothing to do with support, its surface polished by influences that were never hands. Once in a great while you find a cube of darker material, man-sized, corners softened into organic curves as if the world tried to digest an idea and could only manage to blur it. No tools. No metals that still remember being refined. Whatever intelligence left these marks did so early, or late, or sideways, and then erosion finished the conversation. The structures do not resist the wet; they participate in it, weeping the same way the rocks do, slowly contributing their substance back to the slurry.

Fauna is rumor. Something blunt and heavy moves far off sometimes, its passage visible only as a traveling deformation in the mist layer, a dimple that takes minutes to cross your field of view. Smaller lives—if they are lives—reveal themselves as sudden local silencings of the grinding noise, as if attention itself can damp the turbulence for a few square meters. You do not see eyes. You do not want to.

The scale insists on its own weather. Inside the greater dimness, cells of clearer air form and collapse. Brief warm frontlets race across the mud, dragging their own temporary horizons. In places the water stands so densely in the air that it develops internal tides, slow vertical surges you can feel against your chest. Physics grows provincial: objects fall at slightly different rates depending on which ancient folding they have currently intersected. Sound arrives out of order. A collapse you have not yet reached will sometimes announce itself behind you first, then in front, then not at all.

This is the plain’s final form: not stillness, but the oldest possible turbulence, the weather that remains when every mountain has already been spent and the only thing left to erode is sequence itself. The water keeps working. The rock keeps accepting. The air stays packed with the dust of both. And somewhere in the endless warm murk, a thin black reed sheds another leaf that the wind—such as it is—takes nowhere, because nowhere is already full.
