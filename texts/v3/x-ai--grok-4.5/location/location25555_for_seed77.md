The horizon does not so much end as thin. From any vantage the constructed plain runs outward until the eye loses patience and the air itself becomes the only remaining surface. This is low country—basins within basins, a continental sag of fabricated ground whose highest ridges are still only waist-high to a standing figure, if any figure stood. Everything underfoot and stretching away has been made. There is no bedrock that was not poured, stacked, fitted, or grown into place long enough ago that the method has vanished into the material itself. The substrate is artificial absolute: interlocking tesserae of pale ceramic, veins of compressed dust-glass, long runs of something like fired clay that flex almost imperceptibly in the heat. No natural stone shows through because none was left.

Heat owns the place. It sits at a steady, muscular temperature that never quite burns the lungs but never relents either. Surfaces too hot to rest a hand on for long; air that shimmers only lightly because the light itself is ordinary—clear, mid-spectrum daylight that simply happens, without drama of angle or filter. Shadows are short and decisive. In that light the made ground reveals its unfinished childhood. Erosion has barely begun. Edges remain crisp. Joints between plates are still sharp enough to catch a fingernail. Here and there a corner has sloughed a single granule, or a hairline crack has opened like a first thought of weather. The landscape is embryonic geology wearing the mask of architecture: raw para-continents of tile and slab still deciding what a canyon might one day mean.

Moisture arrives as interruption rather than climate. For days the air is merely dry-hot, then a skin of condensation blooms across the cooler undersides of cantilevered plates at dawn, droplets racing along engineered gradients into shallow collector grooves that themselves look freshly extruded. Occasional rain—never a storm, only a brief vertical dampening—darkens the ceramics to the color of old bone and fills the lowest hex-basins with water no deeper than a palm. These ponds last hours, sometimes a day, before the heat drinks them back. In their brief existence they mirror the inverted architecture of the sky and leave behind faint mineral rims, the first shy signatures of true erosion.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought, sparse to the point of punctuation. In the joints where two plates meet and a thread of grit has collected, a few blade-narrow plants force upward—gray-green, succulent, no taller than a finger. They neither spread nor cluster; each stands alone in its crack like a decision that will not be repeated. Their roots must freeload on the intermittent damp and on whatever slow chemistry still leaks from the aging binders of the constructed ground. No moss, no lichen carpet, nothing that would soften the geometry. The living green is only a sparse annotation on an otherwise finished sentence of heat and plane.

There are no animals. No insects tick against the stillness. No birds cut the air. The ecology, such as it is, is mineral, thermal, and barely botanical. What moves is the place itself. Heat expands the great plates by day; night (when it comes) contracts them. The resulting sounds are the quietest possible language of stress: a distant tick, a long muffled groan that travels through the ceramic like a thought through bone, the sudden dry click of a micro-fracture accepting its future. Wind, when it rises, has nothing to speak through but the low rectilinear ranks of the terrain; it produces only a soft, continuous polishing hiss. After rain the water makes its own brief music—thin rivulets finding the pre-cast channels, a sound like someone far away quietly sorting glass beads—then silence reasserts, heavier for the interruption.

Because the scale is truly vast, the plain manufactures its own weather cells. A hundred kilometers off, a column of hotter air may rise and shear, dragging a veil of suspended dust that never quite becomes a storm. Light remains normal even inside these events—only slightly milked. One can walk for days between any two concentrations of form. Density is low: most of the world is interval. Structures exist—long stepped embankments, sunken courts the size of dry lakes, freestanding walls that run ruler-straight until perspective dissolves them—but they are scattered like thoughts that did not need to finish. Between them the empty fabricated pavement is so complete it feels like a held breath.

Processes continue without hurry and without witness. Plates settle another micron. A condensate droplet gathers, reflects the empty sky, falls. A single plant angles one leaf toward moisture that has already left. The made world keeps making itself in reverse: heat, rare water, and time collaborating on the first draft of ruin that will take geologic ages to write. For now the place remains what it was engineered to be and has not yet remembered how to become anything else—an endless, quiet, sun-stunned fabrication whose only native motion is the slow, certain work of becoming weather-worn.
