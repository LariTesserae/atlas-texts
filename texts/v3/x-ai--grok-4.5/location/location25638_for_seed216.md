The high places were never stone. They were planted.

Long ago—before memory calcified into anything like history—someone or something set the first cuttings into the thin air of the upper world. What grew from them did not stop. The result is not a forest and not a city. It is a single, continuous fabricated organism the size of a continent, raised so high that the true ground exists only as rumor and weather rises from below like breath from a sleeping mouth. Everything you stand on, touch, or pass beneath is made: plaited, grafted, pollarded, inosculated, and trained across millennia with nothing more advanced than bone awls, obsidian-edged sickles, fiber cordage, and patience. There is no bare substrate. The architecture is the plant, and the plant is the only ground there is.

It is mostly empty.

Immense vertical galleries open for days of walking without a wall coming into reach. The living walls themselves—colossal braided trunks fused into ribbed membranes, hollowed buttresses thick as hills, pendant root-vaults—are dense with vascular tissue and slow secondary growth, but the volumes they enclose are near-void. Sound dies quickly. A drip can take a long time to land. Light arrives already exhausted, a dim green-gold dusk that seems to sweat out of the tissues rather than fall from any sky. In places the canopy of the structure has grown so thickly over itself that day and night are only gentle oscillations in the gloom. The air is warm and permanently damp, like the inside of a mouth. Water does not so much fall as exude: it beads on every surface, weeps from nodes and lenticels, gathers in the cupped scars of old pollarding cuts, and threads downward in slow silver lines that never quite become streams. Where the great hollows bottom out, the floor is a trembling marsh of mosses, bladderworts, and spongy aerial roots that have debated gravity and reached a compromise. You sink only a little. The water table is everywhere and nowhere—held in the body of the world-plant itself.

The structure is old enough to have weather of its own. Internal clouds form in the largest atriums, drift, and dissolve into warm mist that settles as another kind of dew. Pressure gradients pull faint winds along the galleries; they make almost no noise, only a continuous soft abrasion of leaf against leaf at scales too large to see as motion. Sometimes a section of wall will slowly torsion over years, adjusting its load, and the floor will tilt by the width of a hand. Inhabitants—whoever still tends the grafts—speak of these movements the way other peoples speak of seasons. Their tools are simple: living wedges driven to open or close vascular channels, bark-cloth bandages, cuttings taken with a single practiced twist. They do not build so much as edit. The world is already total; they only keep it from becoming something else.

Fauna is abundant yet sparse in the way stars are sparse. Things live in the wall-thickness and launch themselves across the voids on gliding membranes or long, prehensile tapestries of their own growth. Soft-bodied grazers the size of clouds drift between the hanging gardens, rasping the perpetual new shoots. Smaller lives tunnel the cork layers in clicking multitudes you feel more than hear. Predators are patient geometry: coils that wait in the dim for something to silhouette itself against a weeping membrane, or filter-feeders that spread translucent nets across an entire gallery and simply inhale whatever weather brings. Everything is adapted to the quiet and the wet and the long vertical distances. Nothing hurries. Nothing needs to.

And then there is the way the place fails to remain consistent with itself.

Distances lie. A gallery that took three days to cross last cycle may now be traversable in an afternoon, the walls having grown denser with unspace or thinner with forgotten directions. Reflections in the standing water sometimes show the structure from angles that do not exist, or from times when the grafts were younger. In certain warm hollows the air condenses into shapes that have weight and curiosity; they dissolve when approached but leave wet footprints that steam. Graft-points occasionally remember other configurations and begin to open into rooms that were pruned away centuries ago—rooms still furnished with the slow pulse of sap and the same dim light, as if the plant cannot bear to discard any version of itself. The wardens treat these shrinkages and expansions of continuity as just another kind of weather. They mark the unstable nodes with braided red fiber and walk a little faster past them. The world-plant is vast enough, old enough, and sufficiently alone with its own processes that local laws of containment have grown optional. It holds together because it has always held together, the way a dream holds together until the moment you notice the seams.

At the outermost membranes, where the structure finally thins toward the true sky, the wind is stronger and the light a fraction less dim. Water freezes there on some nights into delicate rimes that the morning warmth turns back into mist. Looking outward you see only more of the same living megastructure curving away in every direction, continent-fold after continent-fold of constructed vegetation vanishing into its own twilight. Looking inward you see the same, endlessly hollowed and endlessly growing, quiet as a held breath, dripping, rearranging, and occasionally forgetting which parts of reality it has already used.
