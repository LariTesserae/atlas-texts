The highland is not a mountain range so much as the worn-down memory of one. Whatever peaks once stood here were planed away so long ago that the stone itself has forgotten the angle of a slope. What remains is a continental table—an elevated plain the size of weather systems—lifted so high that the air thins and cools just enough to keep the warmth from ever becoming hot. Moisture rides that air constantly. Clouds do not pass over this place; they live in it, snagged and thickened by the endless upward reach of green.

There is almost no bare ground. The substrate is plant. Roots and rhizomes and compressed leaf-mold and living cork have built a spongy, walking surface kilometers deep in places, a slow-growing geology of cellulose and sap. You do not dig here so much as part living tissue. Water does not run in rivers so much as migrate: it wells, seeps, hangs in curtains of condensation, beads on every surface, and threads downward through the root-mass in darkness until it finds some ancient fissure in the buried bedrock and vanishes. After rain—which is frequent, soft, and warm—the entire plain steams. Rocks that still manage to shoulder through the vegetation weep continuously, their faces dark and glazed.

Light arrives dim and green. The canopy is not a ceiling so much as a depth. Layers upon layers of broad translucent leaves, hanging mosses, and flowerless fronds filter the sky until day is permanent underwater twilight the color of old bottle glass. At certain hours a ruddier glow moves through the lower strata—bioluminescent fungi or the slow pulse of sap in translucent trunks—but it never brightens enough to cast a sharp shadow. Night is only a deeper green.

Everything grows into everything else. Trunks fuse. Aerial roots descend and become new trunks. Platforms of interwoven branches form and dissolve over centuries. In the denser reaches the plant-mass closes overhead and underfoot until travel becomes a matter of crawling through humid tunnels of living wood, brushing past clammy orchids the size of thrashing animals and seed-pods that tick softly as they cool. The air smells of wet pollen, crushed mint, and the faint iron of old sap.

Sound is rare and small. A single heavy drop falling three canopy layers. The creak of a trunk adjusting its weight across a decade. Somewhere far off, the low continuous hush of water working through the sponge. When wind comes it is timid; the vegetation drinks its force before it can become a voice. The quiet is not empty. It is full of withheld noise, as if the plain is listening to its own slow metabolism.

Animals are everywhere and mostly soft-footed. Herds of low, many-legged browsers move like shadows between the buttress roots, their coats patterned in the same mottled greens and greys as the bark. Spindly climbers with too many joints pick their way through the mid-story, tasting leaf surfaces with long tongues. Clouds of midges and translucent moths turn in the beams of dimmer light. Larger shapes exist farther in—things that carry their own gardens on their backs, or that have become nearly sessile, rooting themselves for seasons at a time while mosses and companion flora colonize their flanks. Nothing hurries. Predation, when it happens, is a gradual enfolding rather than a chase. A vine that was not a vine yesterday closes around a sleeping form and begins the patient work of digestion. Bones are rare; most bodies are simply reabsorbed into the green.

Here and there the living architecture parts around something that does not grow. A low wall of fitted stones, softened by centuries of moss until it looks like a natural ridge. A single arch, half-swallowed, its keystone carved with a pattern no living thing remembers how to read. Small cairns of intentionally placed pebbles on the few true rock outcrops. These are not ruins of cities. They feel more like punctuation—marks left by hands that passed through and did not stay. No metal. No cut glass. No devices. Whatever intelligence once moved here did so with the same materials the plain itself offers: fiber, resin, stone, bone.

The strangeness is quieter than spectacle. Time thickens. Distances misbehave; what looks like a half-day’s walk under the green gloom may take a week, or an afternoon, depending on whether the root-mass is swelling with water or slowly contracting in a drier cycle. Some clearings are older than the trees that ring them, held open by a consensus among the plants that no one can explain. In certain hollows the air tastes faintly of lightning even when the sky is calm. And sometimes, at the edge of hearing, there is a rhythm too slow to be called a heartbeat and too regular to be wind—the plain itself settling another fraction of a degree into its final, almost-flat form.

Change happens, but on the scale of wood grain adding a ring. A new species of fern may take three human lifetimes to cross a valley. A canopy gap created by a rare collapse of ancient fused trunks will still be closing when the grandchildren of the creatures who saw it open have themselves become soil. The highland does not regenerate so much as continue. It has already become what it was always going to be: a single vast, warm, wet, dimly glowing organism wearing the mask of a landscape, breathing so slowly that rain and root and occasional bone are only the details of one long exhalation.
