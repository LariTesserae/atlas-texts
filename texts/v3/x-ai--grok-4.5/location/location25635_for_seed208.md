The plain has forgotten its mountains. Whatever ranges once stood here were planed away across spans that make continents feel provisional. What remains is a low, open expanse of smoothed bedrock and packed siltstone, stretched beneath a sky the color of wet slate. Elevation barely registers; you could walk for days and the horizon would stay level, interrupted only by sparse, rounded bosses of harder stone that rise like the backs of half-buried animals. Everything has been worn to its oldest stable shape. Edges are rare. The world here feels settled into a final form it has practiced for a very long time.

Water is constant without ever committing to a single body. It seeps from the stone itself. The bedrock is porous with age, laced with fine fractures that weep in slow, shining beads. Thin sheets of water glide across the polished surfaces, gathering into ankle-deep rills that braid and unbraid without loyalty to any channel. The air hangs cool and heavy with suspended droplets; every surface stays dew-slick. There is no true rain and no true dry—only this endless post-rain condition, the ground forever shedding what it cannot hold. Mist moves in slow coils, thickening and thinning the already dim light until distance becomes a suggestion rather than a measurement. Shadows are soft and indecisive. Day is a rumor.

There is no green. No moss, no lichen worth counting, no root, no leaf. This is a mineral surface now. The palette is iron, ash, old bone, porcelain whites where silica has bloomed, charcoal lenses of compressed matter that no longer remembers being alive. In places the stone has been fretted by water into delicate flutes and scallops, cold and intricate as coral. Underfoot it ranges from glass-slick pavement to yielding silt that drinks boots and releases them with a soft protest. The emptiness of plants is not barrenness exactly; it is simply a different ecology, one that long ago finished with photosynthesis and never looked back.

Life thrives anyway. Fauna is abundant and utterly adapted to the wet mineral waste. Low-slung, many-legged creatures the size of dogs move in loose aggregations, their jointed legs ticking against stone as they rasp bacterial slicks and mineral crusts. Larger forms shoulder through the shallows on pillar legs, shoveling the same films with yellowish beaks or hinged plates; they leave broad, dissolving wakes. Things with wet membranous wings make brief heavy flights that never rise far above the mist. In every rill, pale eyeless swimmers flicker, pursued by longer shadows that glide with patient hunger. Shed carapaces and broken plates litter the margins until the water softens them back into the general slurry. The entire system runs on slow rock chemistry, on scavenged dust, on one another. It is busy. Hungers and molts and brief violent matings keep the plain in motion even when the mist lies still.

The sound is total. It is deafening in the literal sense—layered, continuous, inescapable. Water alone would be loud: a million rivulets rattling over gravel, the constant drip from every low overhang, the suck of footing in wet silt. But the animals own the air. The armored ones stridulate as they walk, producing a dry electric chorus of spur on shell. The heavy grazers boom from resonating chambers in their chests, mapping territory in long, chest-shaking tones. High, glass-edge screams drop from the low fliers. Under everything runs a subterranean grinding, as if something vast and patient is still adjusting the bedrock. Because the land is so open and so sparsely interrupted, the din travels unrefracted for kilometers. It presses on the ears and the ribs until thought frays at the edges. Speech becomes mouth-to-ear guesswork. Silence is a historical concept.

Across this roar stand the scattered marks of someone. Not cities, not even villages—just occasional insistence. A ring of waist-high stones, their tops pecked with simple cupules. A low wall of stacked slabs enclosing nothing in particular, already half-returned to the seeps. Upright poles of dense black wood (fossil, or carried from some unimaginable elsewhere) hung with knotted sinew and pale shell. Tiny cairns that may be graves or waymarkers or both. The craft is careful and limited: stone against stone, bone tools, the rare cold-hammered bead of native metal. Paths appear as faint grooves in the softer siltstone, often shared with the herds. Once in a long while you find a shelter—three slabs leaned into a rough dolmen, floor lined with gravel scraped dry from somewhere, a fire-pit stained with resinous dung. The makers of these things are sparse themselves. They wear sewn hide and overlapping carapace. Their eyes are large in the permanent gloom. They fish with bone gorges, gather newly molted shells to boil into sour broth, and move along the same wet routes the animals use. When the sound drops a fraction—during certain rare cold stillings when the shallows skin over with ice that shatters like sugar glass—they sometimes sing, though the songs do not travel far.

A quiet wrongness threads the place. Refections in the water never quite resolve into faces—only smeared suggestions that lag behind movement. Some of the low rock bosses ring when struck, pure tones that call answering notes from far out on the plain, as if the entire worn surface were a single cracked bell still remembering one ancient blow. Herds occasionally turn in perfect unison toward empty horizons. Shadows gather in hollows that should not hold them. The light itself seems to thicken and thin according to no celestial schedule. After enough time you begin to suspect the erosion is not finished—that the plain is still being planed by something older than water. Perhaps by the sound. Perhaps the deafening chorus is the final agent of flattening, abrading the world toward an ultimate level and an ultimate quiet that have not yet been earned.

The processes do not pause. Mist uncoils. A herd of boom-chested grazers shoulders past, their calls making the eyelashes vibrate. Water rises a finger’s width for no clear reason and turns square kilometers into a single dull-silver mirror; the creatures wade through it without interrupting their ticking and booming. Somewhere a distant cairn stands black against the sheen. Then the water eases back, writing new temporary channels and leaving the smell of wet iron. The roar never falls below the threshold that would destroy a quieter landscape. This is the oldest stable state of the place: worn down, wet, loud with residual and voracious life, dim, cool, open, finished with growing things, and still, in its slow way, ferociously alive.
