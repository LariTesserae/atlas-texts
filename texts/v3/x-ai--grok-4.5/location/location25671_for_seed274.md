The place is a shallow basin, no wider than a long hall and no longer than two, set so low that the surrounding land simply ends at its lip and does not continue. What remains inside is almost pure volume of stone and water held in permanent balance.

Water moves everywhere yet almost nowhere. It beads on every face, sheets down the flanks of the standing masses, gathers in skin-thin lenses on the horizontal surfaces, then vanishes again into seams too fine to see. The air itself is saturated; breath leaves a brief ghost that does not quite dissolve. The temperature is the same as skin left too long in a warm bath—neither soothing nor harsh, only constant. Nothing freezes, nothing boils, nothing dries. Whatever balance of seepage, condensation, and slow drainage keeps the wetness exact has been exact for longer than the forms themselves remember.

There is no green. No lichen even. The stone is bare mineral in every register: pale sinter the color of old bone, darker bands of iron-stained calcite, occasional lenses of something glassy and black that catch what little light arrives. The surfaces are long-smoothed, not freshly broken. Edges have forgotten they were once sharp. Corners are thumb-worn by centuries of running film. The whole assemblage looks settled into itself the way a sleeping animal settles—weight accepted, no further rearrangement required.

The space is packed. Standing or fallen slabs, blunt pillars, rounded bosses, and low platforms occupy nearly every cubic measure. Gaps between them are shoulder-wide at best, often narrower, so that any path is a continuous negotiation of wet shoulders and slick footings. Overhead the openings taper; light enters only as a diluted grey that never quite names a direction. In the deeper recesses the dimness thickens until the stone is known more by cool pressure against the palm than by sight. Sound is reduced to the intimate register: the tick of a single drop finding its pool, the continuous whisper of sheets no thicker than parchment sliding over mineral, often nothing at all. The silence is not empty; it is simply full of something that does not speak.

A few marks interrupt the pure geology. Here and there a face has been dressed by hand—shallow, irregular tool scars left by something harder than the rock yet softer than metal, or only barely metal. One low platform carries three cupped depressions whose rims are too even to be natural; another bears a single line of pecked dots that might once have been a count or a path-mark. These interventions are sparse, weathered nearly flush with the surrounding stone, and no longer serve any visible purpose. Whatever hands made them have long since departed, and the water has continued its quiet erasure without urgency.

Life is almost absent. Once in a long while a pale crustacean no larger than a fingernail pokes from a flooded seam and withdraws. Once in a longer while a film of something bacterial iridesces for an hour across a quiet pool and then collapses back into clarity. Nothing larger claims residence. The basin does not change. Droplets fall at the same intervals. Mist coheres and frays at the same rate. The packed stones neither grow nor slump. Time here is not stopped so much as already finished; the place has reached the shape it will keep, and the keeping is the only event left.
