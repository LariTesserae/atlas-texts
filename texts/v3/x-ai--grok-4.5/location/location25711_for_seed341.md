The Weeping Yard sits in a shallow basin no larger than a village green haunted by industry. A ring of older dikes and silted retaining walls holds it a little below the surrounding flats, so that water is always deciding whether to arrive or leave. It never quite does either. The ground is a dark sponge of peat, black clay, and century-old cinder, forever exhaling. Stand still and the soles of your boots darken. Kneel and the depression fills.

It is hot in a way that does not burn so much as *claim*. The air arrives already full—thick with the mineral breath of warm groundwater and the sweet rot of lush growth that never quite dies back. Vapor hangs in slow curtains. It beads on every upright surface and runs. Brick weeps. Iron weeps. The broad leaves of the swamp figures (tall, jointed things like reeds that learned architecture) drip in uneven tempos onto corrugated roofs and into open gulleys. There is no true rain most hours; there is only the continuous regional sweating of a low place that cannot cool.

Light here is a dim green-bronze, the color of old bottle glass held to a storm. It comes less from any sky than from diffusion through steam, through the overlapping canopies, through the stained clerestory panes of the works. Midday and dusk feel related. Shadows have soft edges. Metal does not glare; it broods.

The built things are scattered with the confidence of something that has had time. This is not a dense street grid. It is a hamlet of industry: six or seven principal structures—pump halls, a spinning shed, a low foundry with its throat open to the wet air, a boiler house whose stack never quite clears the leaf-layer, two long packing floors on brick piers—and the noble clutter of what connects them. Pipe bridges on lattice legs. Catwalks polished by use and rusted where no one walks. Elevated flumes that carry warm water from one process to the next like artificial brooks. Everything has been repaired in place for longer than memory. Cast-iron columns wear collars of newer steel. Brick has been repointed with three different mortars, each stained by the same slow copper green. The yard has reached the kind of equilibrium that weather and work invent together: nothing looks new, nothing is allowed to finish falling down.

Between the buildings the lushness takes over with almost formal spacing. Camps of enormous horsetails. Mats of moss thick enough to hide a dropped tool. Climbing things that prefer hot pipes to trees and have braided themselves into the steam lines until some valves must be reached through living tunnels. The vegetation is not chaotic; it is *arranged by heat and drip*. It thrives in the exhaust. It finds the condensate traps and turns them into hanging gardens. In the open pans of standing water, pale rooted masses pulse slowly with the thermal seep from below—no fish, no insects skimming the surface. The silences where animals should be are exact and clean. Nothing darts. Nothing calls. The green simply occupies and thickens.

What fills the ear instead is industrial weather. The Yard is loud with overlapping mechanical seasons. Deep pumps pull warm water up from the sponge and send it hammering through headers; their stroke is the bass line you feel in your ribs. Steam traps fire in stuttering applause. A long belt-drive in the spinning shed keeps a fleshy, rhythmic slap. Occasional blowdowns roar like brief waterfalls into the receiving canal. Overhead, the big extractor fans in their cages pull the thick air through screens of wet fiber and fling it out again, louder when the humidity spikes. Water itself is never quiet—sheet-flow off roofs, the gutturals of full downspouts, the high continuous sizzle where hot casing meets soft rain-that-isn’t. On active shifts the sounds braid into something almost processional. On the quieter hours they thin but do not stop; something is always cycling, equalizing, leaking with purpose.

The strangeness is mild and structural, like a rule you only notice when you try to leave. Heat should rise; here it also *pools*, so that the catwalks can be cooler than the ground. Condensation sometimes runs uphill for a handspan along riveted seams before remembering itself. The lush growth leans toward the loudest machines as if sound were a kind of light. And time-of-day has trouble sticking: the dimness and the vapor erase solar angle, so workers navigate by which engine is singing and by the slow tide-marks that climb and fall the brick piers. There are no vermin in the meal stores. There are no birds on the ridgelines. Even the idea of a frog feels misplaced, like a word from another climate.

Processes keep turning. A front of heavier steam will roll through from the foundry and leave every surface beaded and new. The packing floors empty and fill. Somewhere a valve wheel is spun by a gloved hand and a distant flume changes its note. The peat ground faintly rises after a long pump cycle, then settles, as if the basin itself were breathing through its industrial gills. Lush spears push another knuckle taller overnight along the warmest culvert. Rust advances in maps that match the drip lines. Nothing here is finished; everything is maintained in the middle of its becoming.

The boundary is modest and definite—a mossed parapet, a last pipe bridge, the sudden openness of the outer flats where the vapor thins and the ordinary wind begins. Inside that rim the Weeping Yard continues its hot, wet, ringing work: a small city of scattered halls and green weights and water that will not make up its mind, loud with engines and soft with endless leaves, empty of every animal and full of motion.
