The descent is not a tunnel so much as a gradual surrender of the idea of surface. Light persists—ordinary, directional light the color of late afternoon through high dust—yet no sky answers it. It arrives in long slanted sheets through fracture-systems that never quite resolve into ceilings, washing a terrain that is still deciding what shape it means to keep.

You stand inside a regional hollow, wider than any valley you have walked, its far walls lost in stacked haze and moving bodies. The floor is raw geology in the act of becoming: basalt plates shoulder upward overnight, cool to a skin of dull glass, then shear and subside under their own unfinished weight. Fresh scarps steam. Rubble fields rearrange themselves with the slow violence of things that have not yet learned permanence. Pockets of warm dampness collect in every new depression—condensation beading on cooling stone, thin sheets of water that appear after muffled subterranean rains, then sink again into porous, still-settling substrate. The air tastes of wet mineral and animal heat.

Nothing green lives here. The world is mineral and flesh. In place of roots there are only the white, calcified threads of extinct things, or the glassy ropes of congealed lava that the current upheavals sometimes twist into temporary arches before snapping them. What grows, if the word applies, is simpler and hungrier: mats of pale film that bloom on damp rock faces for a few hours and are scraped clean by tongues; mineral blooms that animals mistake for food and carry as temporary armor until the next collapse sheds them.

The density is overwhelming. Fauna occupies every register of space. Low to the ground, riverine herds of low, armored browsers surge along the temporary watercourses, their shells clicking like loose stone, leaving only polished tracks that the next upthrust will erase. Above them, mid-air is a constant brown-and-iridescent weather of winged things—leather, chitin, membrane—whose collective wingbeat is a low continuous thunder. They nest in the temporary overhangs, abandon them when the rock groans, reform the colonies elsewhere within hours. Larger shapes move at the edges of vision: multi-limbed grazers that prune the fungal films with scissoring mouthparts, and predators that seem composed more of joint and velocity than of settled anatomy. Burrowers stitch the new talus with tunnels that collapse behind them as fast as they are dug; the ground is always faintly crawling. The soundscape never drops below a roar—stone fracturing, water heel-drumming into fresh pits, the layered shriek and boom of too many living throats in contested volume.

Scattered through this are the marks of something that plans, however scantily. Crude cairns of stacked basalt chips stand on momentarily stable ridges, each crowned with a single worked flake whose edge still holds the gloss of recent percussion. Here and there a shallow scrape has been deepened into a sleeping hollow and lined with shed plates of chitin still warm from the previous occupant. Once you pass a freestanding ring of upright stones no higher than the knee, the gaps between them chinked with red mud that is already cracking in the geothermal warmth; inside the ring the ground has been swept bare, as if some brief ceremony of counting or naming required emptiness. The tools that made these things are simple: hammerstones still carrying the powdered ghost of their last work, awls of split bone, cordage twisted from sinew and the fibrous rind of something that may not have been plant. Their makers are visible only as quick upright shadows among the herds—bipedal, sparse-haired, moving with the same harried purpose as everything else, pausing to strike a new flake only when the ground under them stops shaking long enough to allow aim.

The turbulence is the true climate. A slope that was passable at morning is a scree chute by afternoon; a water pocket becomes a short-lived cataract that carves a fresh slot canyon and then subsides into damp sand. Light angles shift as distant fracture-panels grind open or seal. Colonies of fliers lift as one body, darken half the hollow, and resettle three ridges away. The air itself seems to buffet in time with the geology, carrying grit, spray, and the hot iron smell of newly exposed interior rock. Nothing is finished. The place feels embryonic and already overfull, a basin still being hammered into existence while its first citizens swarm, feed, quarrel, and scratch faint intentional marks onto surfaces that will not outlast the week.

Yet the openness remains. Between the chaos of near bodies and collapsing stone there are long sightlines, pale corridors of calmer light where the herds thin and the only motion is the slow breathe of steam rising from faults. In those intervals you can feel the regional scale of the hollow—the sense that this same unfinished country continues past the haze, repeating its cycles of upthrust and swarm, damp and din, for distances that would take days to cross if the ground ever held still long enough to try.
