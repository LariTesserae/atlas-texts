The plateau does not begin so much as it asserts itself. You come up through cloud-forest that grows denser and hotter with every hundred meters of climb, until the air itself feels thickened, syrupy with water that never quite falls as rain and never quite stays as mist. At four thousand meters the trees do not thin the way reason says they should. Instead they fatten. Trunks the color of wet ironwood fuse into one another, buttressed and moniliform, their bark sweating a clear gel that runs in slow sheets down into the leaf litter. The litter never dries. It steams.

Everything here steams.

The heat is geothermal first, solar second. Deep fractures in the plateau crust leak mineral vapor that rises through black basalt and older, pale limestone until it meets the perpetual condensation layer hanging fifty meters overhead. The result is a dual weather: gentle warm rain falling almost continuously from the low cloud deck, and hotter, mineral-laden exhalations punching upward in irregular curtains from the ground. Where the two meet, rainbows stand for hours like vertical standing waves, slow to form, slower to dissolve. The light is ordinary noon light—white, slightly flattened by the vapor—but it is bent and re-bent so often that shadows have soft multiple edges.

Water is never farther than a step. It sheets across stone in films a finger thick. It wells from every low place into tannin-dark pools whose surfaces are stitched with the walk of insects that have evolved skates instead of feet. It weeps from cliff faces in permanent dark streaks that have, over centuries, etched shallow flutes into the rock. The flutes themselves now host hanging gardens—epiphytic ferns whose fronds are transparent at the edges, bromeliads the size of storage drums, and long aerial roots that dangle and drip and sometimes pulse, very slowly, as if tasting the air. The vegetation is not merely lush; it is stacked. Canopy, subcanopy, shrub, herb, epiphyte, and something that grows only on the undersides of leaves, a violet film that photosynthesizes in the green-shifted underlight and smells faintly of copper when crushed.

Sound is constant and layered. Water hitting broad leaves. Water hitting stone. Water hitting other water. The low, wet slap of large soft bodies moving through undergrowth. A continuous low-frequency thrum that seems to come from the rock itself—more felt in the chest than heard—whose source is the slow cycling of the plateau’s buried heat exchangers. Those exchangers are not natural. Every few kilometers the forest opens around a structure that is neither ruin nor intact building: a ribbed spire of matte gray material that drinks heat and returns it as a cooler exhalation, keeping a disc of ground around its base at a temperature living things find survivable. The spires are old. Lichen maps have grown across their lower thirds in complex overlapping geometries that look almost deliberate. No one living remembers installing them, yet the engineering is beyond any physics that governs the rest of the world; the material refuses to conduct in expected ways, rejects most spectra of radiation, and, if cut (a rare and discouraged act), heals over decades by knitting at the molecular edge. They are the only “built” things that remain whole. Everything else is scatter: a run of hexagonal paving half-swallowed by moss, a toppled pylon whose outer casing has weathered into something that looks like petrified wood, a single doorway standing alone on a saddle ridge with nothing behind it but more steam and the distant next ridge.

The fauna treat the spires as frankincense trees or salt licks. Herds of long-necked browsers—skin like wet slate, six limbs, eyes set in armored turrets—come at dusk to stand in the cool discs and drink the condensed water that sheets down the gray flanks. Smaller things live in the canopy in interlocking densities that make the air seem crowded even when you cannot see the bodies: gliding membranes, jointed tongues longer than the animals that own them, insects that sew leaves into temporary lungs and breathe the wet air through silk. There are predators that hunt by taste alone, trailing the mineral signatures left in footprints. There are filter-feeders the size of dogs that hang from branches and sieve midges from the steam with curtains of sticky cilia. At night their cilia fluoresce a deep cold blue, so the forest acquires a second, slower lightning.

The whole system moves on timescales that feel patient even while you stand inside the noise. Sap rises and falls with gravity tides measured in days. The hanging rainbows drift a hand-span per hour. The spires cycle their heat on a twelve-day rhythm that the browsers have learned to anticipate; you can watch an entire valley’s worth of animals begin their gradual migration two days before the next cool phase begins. Erosion has long since finished its dramatic work. The cliffs are smooth, the valleys broad, the river courses locked into meanders that have not shifted in living memory. What changes does so by accumulation: another millimeter of gel on a trunk, another layer of translucent leaf-skeleton on the forest floor, another slow white bloom of salt on the leeward side of a spire.

And threaded through all of it is the strangeness that never quite resolves. Sometimes the steam curls into temporary corridors that hold their shape for minutes after the wind should have ruined them. Sometimes a pool will reflect a sky that is not the sky above it—darker, with stars in unfamiliar geometries—out of phase by only a few degrees, as if the water were remembering a slightly different altitude. The aerial roots occasionally braid themselves into temporary lattices that hum at the edge of hearing and then unbraid again over the course of an afternoon. No one has determined whether these are side-effects of the buried exchangers, properties of the native biology, or simply the way a place this wet, this hot, this high, and this old eventually learns to think.

You can walk for days across the open breadth of the plateau and never leave the sound of falling water or the press of green bodies. The horizon is always another softened ridge stewing in its own breath. The air tastes of iron, nectar, and warm stone. Everything is slow. Everything is loud. Everything is, in its saturated, sweating, impossible way, exactly as it has been for longer than the spires have stood.
