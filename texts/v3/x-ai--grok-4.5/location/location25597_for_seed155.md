The plateau has forgotten the shape of mountains. What remains is a high, cool table of stone worn to a long indifference—ridges softened into slow waves, valleys filled and leveled by the patient grind of ages until the land itself feels like a single, enormous floor tilted just enough to drain. Elevation keeps the air thin and sharp; breath fogs even at midday. Moisture arrives as lithic condensation, as night-sweat beading on every surface, as the occasional cold rain that does not so much fall as condense out of the low cloud layer and streak sideways. Pockets of standing water collect in the ancient root-cradles and in the rusted basins of abandoned catchments, never deepening to rivers, never quite drying.

Everything that stands is plant. Not forest—architecture. The substrate itself is living mass: trunks wider than city blocks rise at irregular intervals across the open sprawl, their bark plated like welded iron, their higher galleries opening into hollow chambers that once housed industrial floors. Between these titans the ground is mostly bare stone and a thin lichen bloom, so the eye travels far. Density is sparse; kilometers of wind-scoured pavement separate one living spire from the next. Yet each spire is a city district. Catwalks of braided liana and riveted gantries link the outlying towers into loose metropolitan webs. Elevators of living vine and piston rise through hollow hearts. Entire factories have grown into the cambium—piston halls whose cylinders are chambered wood, conveyor belts of continuous vascular tissue that still pump resin and sap as coolant and lubricant. Vents exhale sweet, metallic steam. The machinery never fully stopped; it simply became circulatory.

Light is ordinary daylight, high and clean, broken only by the moving shadows of the great canopies and by the brown underglow of furnace mouths still burning low-grade peat cut from long-dead undergrowth. Sound never rests. The plateau howls: wind threaded through a thousand tubular branches produces organ-pipes and banshee drones; industrial clatter—gears meshing inside heartwood, pressure valves flopping open with wet coughs, chain drives rattling—fills every interval. When rain streaks across, the entire network becomes a percussion orchestra of drops striking leaf-armor and corrugated metal leaves that the plants themselves have grown to shed the wet. Occasionally a deeper bellow rolls from far away: a pressure dome collapsing, or a root-system shifting under its own mass after centuries.

Fauna is almost rumor. A pale moth the size of a hand may ghost between gantries at dusk; something multi-legged and chitinous clicks once in a coolant duct and is gone. The ecosystem has little room left for animals. The plants eat light, stone dust, condensate, and the slow metal of the machines they have engulfed. Growth continues—rings still add, though by geological patience—yet the overall form has settled into a final, strange equilibrium: living megastructure locked in industrial function, neither dying nor fully wild.

Reality frays at the edges the longer you remain. Distances fold. Walk toward a particular chimney-crowned bole for an hour and discover you are already inside its central atrium, the outer plateau gone as if it had been a projected skin. Reflections in resin pools show galleries that do not open behind you. Sap that drips onto cooling copper traces writes temporary circuits; for a few seconds the copper remembers other geometries and the air fills with the ghost-scent of ozone and flowering. Gravity softens near certain root flares so that loose tools drift upward like slow pollen before the field forgets and they clatter down again. Time stutters in the deepest machine-rooms: a flywheel completes the same three revolutions for subjective minutes, then jumps forward with a bang of displaced air. The inhabitants—those who still move through the sparse urban web—treat these faults as weather. They bolt brass barometers to living walls to measure local causality density. They speak of “thin moments” the way lowlanders speak of fog.

The whole expanse feels mid-sized yet endless: a city-region of perhaps fifty major spires and their connecting arterials, bounded by the haze of further towers half-glimpsed where the plateau continues. Weather cycles turn slowly—mist walls advancing like armies of shredded linen, sudden clearings that drop the temperature another few degrees, rare electrical storms that climb the living lightning rods and leave new scars of charred, fragrant bark. Everything ages in place. Nothing is finished and nothing is new. The plateau simply continues, cool and damp and loudly alive inside its own ancient skin, a settled catastrophe of plant and engine where the distinction between growth and manufacture has long since dissolved into something cooler and stranger than either.
