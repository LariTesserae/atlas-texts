The high country here is a half-finished argument between stone and root. The ground itself is still raw: pale, splintered ridges of young basalt and uncompacted scree that look as if they were poured only a season ago and never settled. Rain beads on them constantly. The air is warm and thick with moisture, so every surface sweats. Water does not so much fall as condense, weep, and sheet. Sheets become thin silver cataracts that never stop, never pool for long, only braid and rebraid across the immature rock before vanishing into the mat.

What covers that rock is not soil and not wilderness. It is construction. Every mass you see is grown architecture—walls, vaults, galleries, and suspended roads spun from living tissue. Trunks the color of wet ironwood rise in sparse, deliberate columns, far enough apart that the eye can travel for kilometers between them. Between the columns the air is empty, luminous, filled only with drifting seed-down and the low roar of water. The columns are not trees in any ordinary sense; they are load-bearing members. Their bark has been persuaded into buttresses and flying buttresses. Lateral branches lock into perfect arches. Secondary stems have flattened into floors dense enough to walk on, cushioned with a living moss that drinks the continual damp and glows a soft emerald under the ordinary daylight. Vines thicker than a waist lace the voids into lattices and hanging cloisters. Everything is load-path and habitability, yet nothing was sawn or hammered. The whole region is a single continuous organism that decided, long ago and once only, to become habitat and then ceased deciding.

Because the dynamic is frozen, the decision never revises itself. Leaves do not turn. New shoots do not appear. The arches do not thicken or sag. The place exists in permanent mid-growth, an equilibrium so total it feels like a stopped clock. You can walk a hanging gallery for an hour and the same droplet will still be gathering at the same leaf-tip when you return, fattening toward the same inevitable fall. The sound of that fall, multiplied by millions of identical slow drips across every vault and colonnade, produces a continuous low thunder—wet, metallic, unending—like a waterfall heard through bone. Wind moves through the sparse intervals and finds taut membranes of leaf and bark stretched drum-tight; it plays them. The result is a braided, organ-like chord that never resolves and never quiets.

The structures themselves feel wrong in scale and intention. A single “room” may be three hundred meters across, its ceiling a single translucent lamina of fused petiole that filters the sky into green-gold twilight. Other chambers taper into spirals too tight for any body larger than a hand. Some corridors simply end in smooth, blind nacre where the plant-mass decided a wall was finished. Elsewhere the living timber opens into balconies that overlook nothing but more warm mist and the next distant column, kilometers distant, identical, uninhabited. There is no city plan, only the frozen memory of one.

Fauna is almost absent. Once in a great while a pale, wingless mite the size of a thumbnail skitters across a moss floor and disappears into a lenticel. Birds do not come. The only motion is water and the almost imperceptible flex of the great stems adjusting to their own weight, a flex so slow it registers only as a change in the pitch of the wind-song every few hours.

At the margins the raw geology still shows: raw knives of rock thrusting up through the vegetal pavement, not yet overgrown, still sharp enough to cut. The plants treat them as future foundation points and simply wait. Everything that will ever be built here is already present, already complete, already moist and warm and ringing with the same unchanging chord. The high sparse forest-city has finished becoming itself and will remain exactly this wet, green, loudly dripping stasis for as long as the world allows.
