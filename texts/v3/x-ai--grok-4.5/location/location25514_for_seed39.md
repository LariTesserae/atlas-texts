The highland rises as if the planet is still deciding its own height. Fresh crust, barely cooled from the shove that lifted it, buckles into a ragged tableland of unfinished ridgelines and half-collapsed scarps. Nothing here has been sanded by time; the rock is still angular, still bleeding grit. Basalt and pale tuff split under their own weight, weeping thin mineral water that freezes in hairline sheets overnight and thaws into silver runnels by the low gray noon. The air itself feels half-condensed—cold enough to sting the lungs, wet enough that breath becomes visible fog the moment it leaves the body. Elevation is extreme: the horizon sits somewhere far below, and the sky presses close, a dim pewter lid that rarely brightens past late dusk even at midday.

Water does not pool so much as migrate. It sheets across the bare stone in slow, braided films, gathers in shallow, ice-rimmed sinks, then slips again through cracks that have not yet decided whether they are faults or future valleys. Every surface glistens. Moss has not established; instead, dark algal slicks and crusts of cryophilic bacteria paint the rock in irregular bruises of olive and rust. Vegetation is a thin, stubborn afterthought—tufts of hairgrass anchored in wind-scoured pockets, low cushions of saxifrage clinging to the lee of boulders, the occasional dwarf willow no higher than a clenched fist. Between them stretch long reaches of naked scree and frost-shattered pavement where nothing roots at all.

The emptiness is almost architectural. Distances open without interruption; a single standing stone may hold the eye for a kilometer before another presence appears. Yet the place is not lifeless. Fauna moves through the intervals in quiet abundance. Herds of compact, thick-coated ungulates—something between goat and antelope—pick their way along the wet ledges, their hooves clicking softly on wet basalt. Clouds of small, pale insects hatch from the melt films and are combed from the air by flocks of ground-nesting birds whose plumage matches the gray stone. Larger shadows slide farther out: low-slung carnivores with frost-rimed fur, patient, circulating in wide, slow circuits. Predation happens without spectacle—one moment a calf is there, the next only a darker smear on the damp rock and a busy quiet of scavengers.

Sound stays low. Wind is almost constant but muted, a long exhalation across raw edges rather than a howl. Water ticks and whispers as it freezes and frees itself. Hooves, wings, the occasional short bark or whistle from the birds—these rise and fall like distant machinery idling. The industrial note is not imposed by any structure; nothing here has been built. It is inherent in the scale and tempo of process. Whole weather systems manufacture themselves against the young peaks: moisture-laden air forced upward, slam-cooled, wrung out as sleet or fine rain that never quite stops. Frost heave works the ground like a slow press, lifting and resetting blocks of stone each night. Meltwater carves temporary canals that last a day, then are abandoned when the cold returns. The geology itself feels mechanized—tectonic blunt force still engaged, faults slipping in micro-steps, slopes calving fresh talus with the regularity of a conveyor. Energy moves through the landscape at production volume: heat stolen from the thin air, momentum taken from falling water, biomass converted and reconverted by the dense animal traffic. Everything is in early shift, unfinished, running hot and cold by turns.

Light never quite commits. The sun, when it is present, is a diluted coin behind stratus. Most hours are a cold twilight in which colors flatten to slate, iron, and the faint green of wet lichen. Shadows have soft edges. At the rare clearer intervals the peaks snag thin banners of cloud that tear themselves to rags and vanish, revealing sharper silhouettes before the dimness seals over again.

There are no paths, only the repeated decisions of animals and water. A traveler would leave tracks that fill with melt within the hour. The rules are simple and absolute: stay moving or freeze into the stone; drink often from any film that has not yet iced; watch the herds for early warning of weather that builds faster than thought. The highland is still being born—raw plates, open sky, abundant brief lives, and the vast cold engine of uplift and precipitation turning without cease. Nothing finished. Nothing empty of motion. Only the long, wet, quiet work of a world assembling its own extreme edge.
