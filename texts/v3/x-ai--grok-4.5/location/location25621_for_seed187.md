The place is a single chamber, low and wide, no higher in most spans than a tall mast, no broader than a market square. It has no keystone and no pillars because it was never raised; it was left. What remains is the final geometry of something that once filled every direction and then, over spans of time with no witnesses, withdrew into itself until only this skin of living mass held the shape of a room.

The walls are plant through and through. Not timber cut and dressed, not vines trained over stone, but continuous parenchyma and thickened bark and interlaced root plates that long ago fused into one substrate. The surface underfoot is the same tissue, worn to a matte continuum by ages of micro-abrasion; it gives slightly, like cool cork, and never quite dries. Condensation beads continuously on the vaulted underside of the ceiling tissues, gathers into slow runnels along the grain, and falls in isolated cold drops that strike the floor with a sound like soft mallets. There is no standing water, only this perpetual damp that keeps every surface faintly shining under the even, directionless light that filters through relict stomatal windows high in the walls. The light is ordinary daylight thinned by thickness, neither dim nor brilliant. Temperature stays locked a few degrees below comfortable skin; breath shows briefly.

Because the chamber is almost empty, sound has nothing to soften it. The dominant noise is the plant’s own residual hydraulics: deep, regular surges of sap still driven by osmotic engines the size of freight cars buried out of sight in the bordering mass. They thud and hiss with the cadence of distant industrial plants that never shut down. Over that bass runs a higher racket—thousands of small bodies. Fauna is abundant but almost never visible in the open volume. Beetle-things with iridescent elytra cling in dense monolayers to the inner bark, their wing-cases vibrating in synchronized choruses that rise and fall like cooling fans. Soft-furred quadrupeds the size of fists race through internal galleries just behind the surface, their claws ticking like loose machinery. Once in a long while a larger shape—something winged and membranous—launches across the emptiness on a short trajectory and vanishes into a pore on the far side, trailing a brief shriek that echoes three times before the hydraulics swallow it. Nothing here is hunting; the ecology has settled into pure maintenance loops. Every mouthful of nectar or spore-cake is replaced at the same rate it is taken. No population climbs or crashes. The air smells of wet tannin and faint ethanol from slow ferments locked in equilibrium.

Erosion has finished its work. Every angle has been rounded, every overhang has slumped into gentle catenaries, every free-standing rib has either dissolved or been reabsorbed. The chamber sits at nearly the lowest local elevation, a depression in a larger plain of the same ancient plant-crust that extends beyond the walls, but the walls themselves form the only boundary that matters. Outside does not enter. Inside does not leave. There are no doors, only the gradual thickening of tissue until openness becomes solid again. No tool-mark, no weld, no brick exists; the industrial character is entirely physiological—the pressure vessels, the rhythmic valves, the heat exchangers of metabolism still cycling after the need for growth has ended.

Time does not pass here in any gradient that can be felt. The drops fall at the same intervals. The sap surges keep the same meter. The choruses of wing-cases neither swell nor dwindle. The chamber has already arrived at the shape it will keep. One can walk the perimeter in a few minutes, feeling the cool give of the floor and the occasional cold impact of a drop on the scalp, and understand that the walk can be repeated indefinitely without the place ever once having changed.
