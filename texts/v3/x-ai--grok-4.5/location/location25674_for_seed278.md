The high country does not so much rise as *continue*. Once you have been inside it long enough, the idea of a lower world becomes theoretical. What you walk is a continent of old stone and thinner air, worn down past drama into a long, low ensemble of backs and hollows, plateaus stitched to plateaus by cols that feel less like passes than like pauses in the same thought. The erosion here finished its arguing ages ago. Edges are rare. Everything has the softened geometry of something that has found its permanent shape and is now only polishing it, century by century, under a sky that never quite commits to day.

Light arrives already tired. It is not darkness; it is a dimness with depth, a pewter uniformity that might be high overcast or the natural color of the air itself at this elevation. Shadows are gentle and indecisive. Distance does not blue so much as fade into the same dull luminance, so that the far ridges look less remote than simply less finished. On some hours a watery brightness pools along the western rims, as if the sun were a rumor still deciding whether to be believed. Then it withdraws without having quite appeared.

Cold is the climate’s true grammar. It does not stab; it resides. Breath leaves the mouth and hangs, undecided. Stones hold the night in their cores long after what passes for dawn. In the lees of the long whale-backed rises, and in the shallow basins where the land dips just enough, moisture gathers—not as rivers worth naming, but as a dampness with intentions. Frost blooms on the downwind faces of rock in feathered plates that sublimate by midday into nothing. Occasionally a thin rain moves through, almost silent, more a change in the weight of the air than a weather event; it darkens the stone for an hour and is gone, leaving the hollows slick and the sparse ground-plants beaded. In deeper folds there are seep-lines and slow dark flushes where water films over grit and disappears into talus again. Nothing here is wet for long. Everything remembers wetness freshly.

Vegetation is a minority opinion. It clings in dark crusts and low mats—black-green, rust, a dull olive that looks less grown than deposited. In the dampest seams a kind of wiry brush persists, knee-high at most, its stems the color of old cable. There are no trees. There is scarcely anything that would cast a standing shadow. The green, such as it is, feels like a stain the cold has not yet succeeded in scrubbing out.

And yet the place is crowded, in a fashion that does not contradict its emptiness.

The fauna are the density and the sparseness at once. They are everywhere in the way weather is everywhere: not piled, not thronging any single hollow, but impossible to be free of. You can walk for a day between distinct encounters and still feel observed by the whole. They move without sound. That is the first rule the land teaches. No cries, no wingbeats that register, no vitrous clatter of claws on stone—only, at most, a soft continuous pressure in the ear, like blood, or like the memory of a factory heard through strata of rock and time.

What they *are* takes patience. At first they read as geology slightly out of place: low carapaces the color of the substrate, flanks ribbed like cooling fins, ranks of limbs folded tight as stored machinery. Some lie half-buried along the seep-lines, valves working almost invisibly, drawing the intermittent damp through lattices of filtering tissue and exhaling a faint mineral warmth that dies within an arm’s reach. Others process in slow processions across the open pavements—herds if the word can stretch—bodies the size of overturned hulls, segmented, each section pulsing with a muffled interior cadence. They do not graze the sparse mats so much as *sample* the land itself, scraping microfilms of lichen and dust, ingesting grit, sorting it. Behind them the stone is left cleaner, microscopically rearranged, as if passed under a plane.

There are aerial forms too, if aerial is right: slow lozenges that hang in the dimness at differing altitudes, barely darker than the sky, trailing fine extrusions that glitter once and then matte again with frost. They drink condensation from the air. When the thin rains come they descend in silence and open surfaces like radiators, black plates drinking. Then they rise. No call announces it.

Deeper in the weathered crust—accessible where ancient jointing has softened into caves that are more like holds than tunnels—something like industry continues without builders. The walls sweat a cold condensate. Thin channels have been etched not by water alone but by repeated passage of the same chemical breath. In certain chambers the air vibrates at a frequency below hearing, and the stone is warm in bands, and pale nodules in the rock slowly open and close, performing a digestion no one living designed. Residual, patient. The tech is not added onto the land; it is what the land retained after whatever made it first had finished and gone, or become this. There are no walls that read as walls, no engines that declare themselves engines. Only the established fact of process: intake, sort, excrete, rest. Scaled past any single body. As quiet as frost forming.

The whole highland behaves like a single slowed metabolism. Change is real but keyed to durations that make a human stride feel faintly embarrassing. Seasons, if they are seasons, are gradients of damp and dimness rather than theater. A carcass—when one of the great quiet ones finally stills—does not so much decay as get reassigned: smaller crews arrive without summons, dismantle, incorporate, leave the site a little smoother than before. The silence remains absolute in the conventional sense; what occupies it is attention.

Weirdness here is not spectacle. It is the slight wrongness of finding, in a place this pure of artifact, a logic that feels manufactured—and of finding, in what feels manufactured, nothing that was ever assembled by hand. The land is untouched because the touching finished so long ago it became geology. It is industrial because work is still being done, continuously, by systems that no longer require witnesses. It is empty because the spaces between bodies are generous. It is overwhelming because once you have noticed the fauna you cannot stop noticing that the plateau is *using* itself, softly, forever, under the dim unending lid of the sky.

Stand still long enough and the cold comes up through your heels in a slow occupation. Breath-fog joins the larger damp. Somewhere—near, far, irrelevant—a carapace adjusts a fraction, a valve seats, a lofted filter drinks. The sound of it is the sound of nothing. The scale of it is the only scale there is.
