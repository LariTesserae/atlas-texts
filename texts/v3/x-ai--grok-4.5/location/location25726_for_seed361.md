The high plateaus have been aging for so long that age itself has become topography. Whatever mountains once stood here have spent their substance; they are low, broad swells of stone now, their flanks planed by weather until the horizon is only a faint, permanent grin. Wind has howled across them for eras measured in the thickness of its own silence. Yet this particular crease in the stone—no deeper than a collapsed lung, no wider than a worn amphitheatre—still remembers how to hold on.

You do not see the pocket until you are inside it. The ancient surface simply fails, by a few body-lengths, to stay flat. Moisture has preferred this failure for longer than forests have nouns. Condensation beads on the undersides of every overhang before dawn, then slides in slow silver threads that never quite become streams. Once or twice a day a thin rain combs through, warm as skin, and vanishes into the rock almost before it finishes falling. The damp is intermittent, shy; it leaves the broader stone dry and pale, but inside the crease it is persistent enough to lilac the air with the smell of wet mineral and green blood.

Vegetation thrives in exactly those moist seams and nowhere else. Between them the stone lies bare, the color of old teeth, so that the green arrives in islands: dense, almost indecently lush mats of fern and moss-tree and low broad-leafed things whose veins carry a coppery sheen. They crowd one another gladly—overlapping canopies, hanging root curtains thick with epiphytes—yet the islands themselves are infinite distances of open rock apart. Since the whole place is pocket-scale, that sparseness feels intimate rather than grand: you step from one living thicket into another across a stretch of sun-warmed pavement no longer than a corridor. The plants lean. They reach. They never manage to fill the emptiness between.

Hot light (ordinary sunlight, yet thickened by altitude and residual heat stored in the stone) falls straight in. Shadows are short, clean, and densely populated. The fauna here is a rolling chaos of bodies and noise that makes the sparseness of the vegetation feel like an oversight. Every available cubic handspan is contested by wings, by stridulating legs, by soft armor plates that flash oil-slick colors as they climb. Clouds of small flyers do not merely pass through the air; they densify it until breathing has a grain. Larger things—long-limbed, translucent, their organs faintly visible as glowing knots—pick their way across the open stone on too many feet, pausing only to snap something smaller out of the air. The sound is constant and layered: a low pulse like distant thrusters, a mid-range clicking that sounds like glass beads poured forever, and above it the shrieks and warbles of creatures advertising territory or simply surviving loudly. Nothing is quiet for more than a few heartbeats. Even the plants sometimes answer, sealed seed-pods explosive enough to startle birds into secondary panic.

Scattered through this intimate violence sit the remnants. They are never whole. A partial ring of dark metal half-melted into stone yet still cool to the touch. A lattice no larger than a ribcage that refuses to cast a consistent shadow. Thin spires of something that looks like glass until you notice it is rearranging its internal facets whenever you blink. None of them are powered the way you understand power. They thrum in frequencies that make the air itself hesitate; occasionally a sphere lying half-buried will lift a finger-width off the ground, rotate once as if checking local gravity for unbroken seams, then settle again. Vines have grown through several of them, and the vines, for short stretches, take on the same unnerving iridescence, as though the unknown engineering has begun to colonize biology in return. Whatever made these things worked past the edges of physics you were taught. The residue is polite, almost shy, but it does not fully consent to being archaeological.

The weather cycles are small and relentless. Mist rises from the damp pockets every afternoon and hangs in wavering veils; the flying things dive through it in silver streaks, hunting or simply playing. Once the mist clears, the rock surface begins to tick softly as heat expands microscopic fractures already old when the first true trees learned to stand. At irregular intervals the light takes a strange sideways cast for a few breaths, as though the sky has briefly remembered a second sun or a deeper night, and every creature freezes until the world resumes its previous geometry. Then the noise crashes back.

Nothing here is finished, yet everything feels final. The stone has reached the patient equilibrium of things that have eroded past ambition. The life, by contrast, is still rifling through every available adaptation at once, loud and wasteful and startlingly fecund inside its tiny, hidden bowl of warm wet rock. The scattered machines—if machines is even the word—continue their slow, inexplicable talk with whatever forces keep them half-alive. You can walk the entire place in minutes. You can also spend hours merely listening as the pocket performs its endless, densely occupied version of stillness.

In the evenings the temperature stays high. Condensation starts again almost immediately. Status lights (or whatever they are) glow faintly among the vines. Somewhere a creature larger than the others begins a call so deep it is felt more than heard, and the whole moist crease of the plateau answers in overlapping waves until the stars, ordinary and steady, seem quieter than they have any right to be.
