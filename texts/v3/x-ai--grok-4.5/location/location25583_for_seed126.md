The plain has no horizon that can be trusted. What looks at first like a continuous flat glare resolves, as the eye learns its habits, into a single constructed surface of planetary extent: interlocking plates of pale refractory alloy and sintered mineral composite, each plate the size of a former nation-state, their seams fused long ago into hairline ridges that no longer catch wind because wind itself has been engineered nearly out of existence. The whole expanse sits at low elevation relative to any residual planetary curvature, as if the original world was planed down and then paved over until only this final, settled geometry remained. Nothing rises higher than a few tens of meters except the occasional slender pylons that double as light-wells; even those feel less like towers than like the last upright vertebrae of something that once stood taller and has since accepted its repose.

Everything here was made. There is no bedrock, only older layers of the same deliberate fabrication, stacked and compressed across deep time until the lower strata have the density and silence of compressed ash. The materials are no longer fully legible to any physics that still uses the word “material.” Surfaces reconfigure at nanoscale when unobserved, knitting micro-fractures closed, redistributing thermal load, exhaling faint plumes of monomolecular dust that settle again within minutes. The dust is the closest thing the place has to soil. It is warm underfoot—amably, steadily warm, the residual heat of an energy economy so efficient it has nowhere left to go except gentle infrared. No open water exists. What moisture remains is locked inside sealed micro-channels running beneath the plates, circulating as pure coolant or process fluid; the surface itself is desert-dry, tasting of metal and old salt when the rare breeze lifts a film of dust against the tongue.

Light is total and directionless. The sky is not sky but a luminous canopy of the same engineered substance, stretched so thin and so perfectly translucent that it behaves like an infinite diffuse source. Shadows are soft, almost courteous, and they migrate according to schedules that have nothing to do with stellar motion. Brightness never falters. Day is a constant state rather than a phase. In that unrelieved clarity the sparseness of living things becomes almost architectural. Vegetation appears only as isolated colonies of photosynthetic lattice—low, brittle structures the color of dried citrus and bone, growing in hexagonal patches where the substrate has been deliberately left porous. They do not spread. They are maintained at exact densities by underlying protocols, enough to scavenge stray volatiles and keep certain reaction chains running, never enough to suggest a wild ecology.

Fauna is rarer still. Once in a great while something moves that is not a maintenance drone or a drifting sensor mote: a low, many-limbed automaton the size of a dog, its carapace the same pale alloy as the ground, following invisible gradients of thermal stress or material fatigue. Or, thinner and quieter, a translucent walking membrane that might once have been biological and is now only the idea of a creature, filtering dust through itself as it drifts. Encounters last seconds. The silence afterward is deeper for having been briefly broken. Sound itself is nearly absent—no insects, no leaves, no water. The loudest ordinary events are the almost-subsonic clicks of plates adjusting their hitch against one another, or the dry whisper of dust sliding into a fresh micro-seam before the seam heals. On certain cycles a broader weather gathers: vast, slow fronts of charged particulates rolling across hundreds of kilometers, rearranging the light into pale curtains, scrubbing the upper surfaces clean. These storms are nearly silent too, felt more as a change in pressure against the skin and a temporary thickening of the air than as noise.

The density of the place is not biological; it is artefactual and layered. Above the primary surface float coda-tiers—thin, transparent platforms and filament networks held aloft by field gradients that current physics would call incomplete. They are crowded with slow traffic: cargo cystals gliding along predetermined vectors, archival lattices unfolding and refolding data, garden-modules the size of rooms in which the sparse lattices of vegetation are cultivated under even stricter control. Below, through translucent sections of flooring, deeper strata remain visible—older iterations of the same built world, still warm, still faintly active, their lights dimmed to amber maintenance glows. The entire volume, from sub-surface to canopy, saturates with presence: objects, fields, residual code, heat. Yet because the motion is deliberate and the noise floor is near zero, the saturation feels like stillness rather than clutter. The world has finished becoming what it was always going to be. Its processes continue—self-repair, thermal equalization, the quiet migration of resources, the brightness cycling through prescribed spectra—but they continue the way a finished theorem continues: without drama, without surplus, without end.

In the farthest reaches the geometry begins to admit gentle curves and half-familiar ornaments, as if the original builders, eons past the point of necessity, had allowed themselves a last decorative impulse before the design locked. Those curves hold dust the way old stone holds lichen. They are the only places where the absolute brightness softens into something almost like evening, and where, very rarely, one of the walking membranes will pause, as if remembering a different temperature, a different silence, a world that still had water and unplanned seed. Then it moves on. The plates reseal. The light remains.
