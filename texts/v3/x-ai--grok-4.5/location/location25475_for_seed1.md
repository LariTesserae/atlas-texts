The plain has forgotten height. Whatever mountains or ridges once stood here wore themselves down so completely that the horizon is only a slight thickening of the same gray wetness that fills the middle distance. You notice the flatness first as a pressure in the knees: the ground does not rise to meet you; it simply continues, saturated and ancient, as if the concept of slope had been eroded out of the world. Water stands everywhere in sheets no deeper than a finger-joint, yet never quite still. It seeps upward through the mineral crust as fast as it drains, a cold perpetual weeping from rock that has long since given up being solid.

There are no plants. Nothing green has root-memory here. What covers the ground instead is a dense, interlocking litter of slower kingdoms: sheets of iron bacteria the color of old bruises, crystalline rimes that grow in pseudo-leaf patterns overnight and shatter by noon, mats of mineralized fungus that crunch like thin ice under any weight and then reseal with a sound like distant welding. Between these grow the true occupants of the surface—the machines that are not built.

They are not ruins. Ruins would imply someone once decided, once stacked. Here the industrial simply occurs. Pistons of fused sand and black ice thrust out of the shallows at irregular intervals, drive through a full cycle of compression and exhaust, then soften back into slurry. Gear-trains assemble themselves from floating rust flakes and the teeth of something that might once have been shellfish; they mesh, scream through three or four revolutions in a cloud of sparks that freeze mid-air, then scatter into ordinary grit. Overhead, in the dim slate light that never brightens past late afternoon, condensation towers form out of pure humidity, spin turbines of supercooled vapor, and collapse again into rain that is already halfway to becoming wire.

The air itself is loud with unfinished manufacture. Every direction carries the overlapping racket of stamping presses that have no frames, conveyor clatter without belts, the deep inductive hum of generators whose only coil is a curl of denser cold. Under it runs a wetter chorus: the continuous suck and cough of water being forced through valves that exist only while the water passes. The sound does not echo; the packed density of the plain absorbs and re-emits it until the whole region is one thick, ringing present.

Fauna thrives in the absence of flora. Things like bipedal crabs welded from condenser coils and cartilage pick their way between the transient engines, snipping off cooling fins to chew. Herds of low, many-jointed browsers move through the shallows, their mouthparts revolving like drill heads, grazing the bacterial iron and leaving behind trails of warm, oily sludge that steams in the cold. Above them, flocks of something almost bat-like—membrane and spark-gap—circle the taller vapor-stacks, diving to short out a forming turbine and feed on the sudden release of charge. Their cries are metallic and ultrasonic, a kitchen of knives dropped down elevator shafts. Predators among them are quieter: long, flat skates of oxidized copper that glide just beneath the water film, waiting for vibration, then folding upward to enclose a grazing machine-beast in a sudden Faraday cage of their own bodies. The trapped thing spasms through its death-cycle in three tight revolutions and goes still; the skate digests current.

Because the plain is ancient, none of this feels invasive. The industrial and the mineral have reached final equilibrium. Every process has already happened enough times that the landscape has settled into permanent, noisy metabolism. Water cycles through boilers that are only weather. Metal fatigue is just another season. Even the reality here is worn smooth. Edges of objects forget to stay edges. A piston may finish its stroke inside a creature’s chest cavity without either noticing contradiction; a flock may fly through a half-formed gear-house and come out counted differently. Sometimes the dim light double-exposes, showing the same stretch of plain ten seconds ahead of itself, already littered with the rust of engines that have not yet risen. Sometimes sound arrives before its source, so that you flinch from a crash and only afterward watch the vapor-tower decide to fall.

At the limits of vision the plain does not end. It only grows denser with the same late, cold industry—more selective weeping of the rock, denser herds, taller short-lived smokestacks of pure cloud, louder arguments of metal that was never mined and will never be stockpiled. The air tastes of wet batteries and old snow. Everything is occupied. There is no gap in which a seed could open or a wall could be raised. The world has finished becoming this, and is now only busy, forever, at the work of staying.
