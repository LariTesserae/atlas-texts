The plateau has no edge that can be reached. It simply continues, a single unbroken elevation worn so thoroughly by deep time that gradient itself has been erased. You are high—higher than any surviving topography elsewhere on this world would permit—and the flatness is absolute, primordial, the final settled shape of stone after every possible reduction has already occurred. What remains is a dark mineral plain of vitreous black salt, dense and faintly warm underfoot, the residue of some sealed, non-aqueous chemistry that long ago finished its work and closed. No moisture has ever returned. The heat is steady, body-warm, rising from somewhere beneath the crust or simply lingering as the last unspent fraction of an older fire. It does not fluctuate.

Darkness is the medium here, not the absence of light. It has weight and texture, a soft soot-thick pressure that fills every interval. Occasional seams of chilled volcanic glass thread the salt and give off a glow so feeble it serves only to confirm how little can be seen; the glow itself feels ancient, as though it has been dimming toward zero for geological ages and has now stabilized at the threshold of perception. There is no sky, or the sky has been replaced by this same absorbent dark. Distance is known by the spaced silences between things rather than by sight.

Across this open tableland stands a city that is urban in organization yet sparse in body—settlement-scale, industrial, laid out with clear intent and then left at the exact density where emptiness remains the dominant material. Towers, stacking sheds, engine houses, and long manufactories rise at intervals of hundreds of meters, constructed from the same black salt reinforced with dark alloys and crazed ceramics. Between them run leveled corridors that once functioned as streets, rail beds, and conduit trenches; now they are simply more of the polished mineral plain, kept unnervingly regular. The infrastructure is complete and inert: vast gear-trains locked mid-meshing, dynamos whose copper has oxidized to the same matte darkness as everything else, flywheels the size of small hills that have not turned since the concept of turning became unnecessary. The technology is unmistakably industrial—scaled production, mechanized, electrified in its bones—yet every process has reached equilibrium. Nothing cycles. Nothing cools or heats beyond the permanent mild warmth. The city did not fall into ruin; it arrived at its finished state and stopped.

Sound is minimal. A rare metallic tick as some residual stress relaxes inside a girder. The soft, almost imaginary settling of mineral dust that is not dust. Once in a great while the subliminal aftermath of a vibration travels through the salt, as if a factory floor still remembered itself somewhere far below, but the memory never resolves into noise. Mostly there is the thick quiet of a place that has no further events scheduled.

Ecology is absent in any familiar sense. No vegetation has ever taken hold; the chemistry forbids it. What lives here—if the word applies—is rare and peripheral. Small articulated things of wire, mica, and salt-crystal sometimes traverse the bases of the towers, their movements precise and unhurried, like maintenance that no longer maintains anything. Larger presences are rarer still: slow drifts of organized static or heat-shimmer that pass at the outer range of detection and do not approach. They seem indigenous to the darkness rather than to the built structures, and they obey rules that do not include curiosity.

The strangeness is quiet and structural. Heat continues to exhale from vents whose fires were banked before memory. Certain crystalline outcrops on the plain slowly rearrange their internal lattices without changing external size or shape, a private industry of pure form. Rails still gleam in places as if traffic were expected, yet the single cart that remains mobile completes an empty circuit only every few years, wheels whispering, cargo hold vacant, schedule unalterable. Time here is not frozen so much as completed; the entire region has settled into its oldest stable configuration—the end-state of erosion, of industry, of process itself. You can walk the corridors for days and the pattern simply continues: tower, void, tower, long mineral silence, the same warm darkness, the same sparse and perfect arrangement.
