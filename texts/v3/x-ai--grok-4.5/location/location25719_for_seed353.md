The plateau has no edge you can reach in a lifetime of walking, only a gradual steepening that never quite becomes a cliff, as if the world itself decided long ago to rise and then forget why. Underfoot—and under everything—is not ground but a single continuous body of lignified tissue, black-brown and warm to the touch, scored by successive centuries of growth and collapse into long, shallow troughs like the beds of vanished rivers. Water does not flow here. Moisture is a rumor sealed inside cell walls, a stoichiometric secret the living mass keeps from the air. The heat is dry and absolute, a midsummer forge-heat that never breaks, radiating upward from the plant-body itself and downward from the low, interlocking canopy that serves as sky.

That canopy is not leaves in any ordinary sense. It is a compressed vault of fused petioles, bladder-like air sacs, and translucent cuticle plates grown edge to edge until no seam remains. Light does not enter; whatever illumination exists is a slow, brownish phosphorescence leaking from the deeper vascular tissues, just enough to show texture, never enough to cast a true shadow. In this near-dark the architecture asserts itself by touch and by the faint residual glow: buttresses thick as basalt columns, galleries bored through living wood by centuries of directed cambial flow, and conduits—some rigid, some peristaltic—carrying resins, latexes, and superheated aromatic vapors instead of sap. Every surface has been worked. There is no wild substrate left. The entire regional expanse is artifact and organism at once, an industrial organism settled into its final, near-flattened geometry.

Yet the settled look is a lie of timescale. Inside the trunks and along the raised causeways of compressed root-matter, the growth is turbulent. Whole sectors swell overnight, fiber reorienting with audible creaks that quickly subside into the baseline hush. Entire manufacturing vaults—chambers where oils are cracked by enzyme stacks, where cellulose is spun into cable and then reeled by contractile tendrils—can collapse into soft mulch and be rebuilt three times in a single long heat-cycle. The rebuilding is not planned so much as inevitable; the mass has forgotten how to stop. Pollen-storms of microscopic machines (no fauna, only these drifting industrious spores) occasionally whitestorm through the sealed galleries, settling as a fine abrasive flour that the next growth pulse simply incorporates. The sound of all this is mostly absence: a low infrasonic pressure against the inner ear, the occasional dry pop of a pressure vessel failing and being shaken open by new shoots, then silence again, thick as the heat.

Elevation keeps the air thin enough that the chemical engines run rich and hot. No animals move. Nothing flies or crawls except the slow migratory bulges of the architecture rearranging itself. The rarity of free water has driven every process into closed loops; even the cooling systems are evaporative only in the microscopic sense, beads of solvent cycling inside translucent bladders that never rupture. In places the ancient breeding programs have left geometric orchards of heat-exchange fronds, each frond a black radiator fin telescoping upward until it fuses with the canopy vault. Elsewhere the mass has grown enormous flywheel trunks—cylindrical, densely spiraled wood—that store torsional energy for decades and then release it in a single silent uncoiling that shifts an entire district’s alignment by several degrees.

The weirdness is quiet and structural. Distances do not stay honest; walk a straight gallery for an hour and you may find yourself two levels lower without having descended, the plant-body having gently peristalsed you through a fold that did not exist when you entered. Time-of-day is a roaring absence: the canopy phosphorescence brightens and dims on no schedule an outside clock would recognize, sometimes holding a dull ember glow for what feels like weeks, sometimes dropping to near-total black while the industrial tissues continue their blind, overclocked work. In the deepest manufacturing hollows the walls exude a tar that hardens into mirrors; travelers (the few who come, and fewer who leave) report seeing their own backs walking away from them down corridors that should not connect.

Everything is old in the way a river delta is old—worn into the shape of its own exhaustion—yet nothing is still. The plateau is a single saturated engine the size of a modest nation, built entirely of more-plant-than-plant, running hot and nearly dry, rewriting its own floorplan in the dark while making almost no sound at all. The final form, it turns out, is not stillness. It is this ceaseless, quiet reconfiguration under the sealed, glowing vault, forever approaching a stability it will never grant itself.
