The high cold has a name older than speech, but no throat left to speak it. What remains is a continent of living wood thrust unfinished into thin air, a single protracted event of emergence that has not yet decided whether it is mountain, forest, or organism. From any ledge you can see only a fraction: ribs of pale trunk the color of frostbitten bone rising through miles of violet dusk, their flanks still sharp with the splinters of recent rupture. The wood is not bark over heartwood; it is all surface, all cambium, endlessly folding outward so that what looks like stone cliffs are living faces still wet with phloem. Between them the voids open—cathedral hollows large enough to weather their own weather, chambers where a cry takes half a minute to die and the floor is only more interlocking root-plate, thin as parchment over drop.

Light almost never arrives whole. What filters down has already been broken by terrace after terrace of canopy kilometers higher, leaves the size of village roofs that never green, only silver and black. The result is a permanent caustic dimness the eye never fully trusts: shapes resolve late, distances lie, and every vertical surface sweats a fine cold dew that freezes into lace by the hour and melts again when the slow internal heat of the plant-mass pulses. Sometimes real weather finds a wound in the upper stories and falls through—sheets of rain that turn to sleet before they reach the lower galleries, or dry snow that hisses against the living walls and is drunk before it can settle. Water lives here as intermittent translucence: pockets of melt collecting in root-cups, curtains of condensation that peel free and walk downward as temporary rivers, rime that blooms overnight across new fractures. Nothing stays wet long; everything stays damp forever.

Because the growth is young, nothing is rounded. Edges stay knife-bright. Fresh upheavals still bleed sap the consistency of glycerol; the smell is mineral and sweet and faintly electrical. Whole buttresses shear free under their own weight and lean at impossible angles, caught and re-absorbed by slower neighbors, so the architecture is a history of interrupted collapses. In the largest atriums the air itself has currents—cold cataracts sliding down one flank, warmer updrafts carrying spore and the musk of animals along the opposite. The plant-mass respires. You feel the pressure change in your inner ear when a distant chamber opens or seals.

Sound is the true climate. The wood never stops tuning itself: deep tectonic groans as fibers realign, higher rifle-crack reports when a tensioned spar finally lets go, the continuous silk-tearing whisper of surface cells dividing in the cold. Wind is rare in the deep interior but when it comes it arrives as a single horizontal fist, booming through the hollows like an organ pipe the size of a province. Over that baseline the fauna adds its own weather. They are abundant the way silence is abundant in a library—everywhere, mostly unnoticed until they move. Pale, long-limbed grazers the height of watchtowers cling to vertical trunks with keratin scythes, rasping at the frost-sweet cambium; their chewing is a wet percussion that can fill an entire gallery. Smaller things tunnel just under the living skin, ticking like cooling metal. In the mid-air voids, membranes of skin and cartilage ride the thermal seams, their wingbeats low enough to feel in the teeth. Predators are rarer and louder: something like a wolverine the size of a haywain that screams when it launches between trunks, the scream Doppler-shifting as it falls through darkness toward a sound only it has heard. Everything here navigates by echo and vibration. Eyes are secondary, often milky or absent.

Scattered through the galleries are the marks of hands. Not cities—nothing so dense—only the occasional assertion that someone paused and altered the grain. A spiral stair of hammered living wood still warm to the touch where the craftsman encouraged the cambium to braid itself into steps; a cluster of huts woven from stripped root-fiber and sealed with sap resin, abandoned when the wall they clung to decided to grow two meters overnight and pitch them into open air; cairns of deliberately broken branch-tips marking paths that no longer connect. The tools that remain are simple: adzes of hardened sap-crystal laced with grit, bone levers, cordage of braided root-hair. Whoever made them understood the plant-mass as both material and weather. They cut only what would heal around the cut and become stronger. Some of the older interventions have been entirely swallowed and now exist only as subtle swirls in the grain, like scars under new skin. In one vast chamber a single free-standing arch of artificially trained wood still stands, its keystone a knot of six species forced into collusion; wind through it produces a note just below hearing that the long-limbed grazers avoid.

The ecology is pure plant yet never still. Every surface is substrate; there is no soil that is not already root or fallen leaf-meal being re-absorbed. Epiphytes here are simply slower provinces of the same body, flowering in colors the darkness almost erases—dusty blues, vein reds—sending perfume in slow cold pulses. Mycelial sheets thicker than cable knit the undersides of every span, glowing a faint sickwards green when bruised, enough to sketch a path for an hour before fading. Nothing rots in the ordinary way; everything is either growing or being rewritten into growth. Even the cold is a kind of busy-ness: ice crystals nucleation along fresh fibers, expanding microscopic wedges that the plant then lignifies around, turning damage into buttress.

At the extreme upper margins, where the final canopies thin against the actual sky, the darkness lessens to a hard violet noon that lasts weeks, and the temperature drops until spit freezes before it lands. Here the trunks narrow to spears and the wind never stops. Rime feathers grow a meter a day and break off with the sound of distant applause. Crossing from the deep hollows into this lightless light feels like surfacing from a long dive. Most of the fauna will not follow. The few structures at this height are only windbreaks of lashed spars, and the people who built them (if people is still the word) came to listen to the planet-sized instrument breathing beneath them, then went back down. The plant-mass does not end; it simply becomes too thin to hold chambers, and after that there is only the long fall of air toward whatever lowlands still exist, far below the roots, in a darkness even the wood has not yet filled.
