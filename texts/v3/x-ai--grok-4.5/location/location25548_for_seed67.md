The Low Fabric extends past any horizon that matter might still bother with. It is not a city and not a plain and not a machine, though it has been all of those words at once and discarded them. What remains is continuous manufacture held at a single, finished temperature: warm as the inside of a hand that has been working a long time and has just stopped. Everything you stand on, lean against, or breathe was poured, stamped, bolted, or grown in a mold. There is no basement geology. The substrate is plate after plate of dark ceramic alloy, seam-welded in patterns that never quite proceed in a straight line, as if the original plan had been corrected mid-execution by something that preferred slight irresolution.

Water is everywhere without being deep. It beads on every upright, sheets across floors that tilt by fractions of a degree, gathers in shallow trays the size of lakes, and weeps steadily from the undersides of gantries and overhead ducts. The air itself is near saturation; breathe and you feel the lungs already half-full. Nothing runs hard enough to make a stream. Instead the moisture is held in a permanent post-rain state—fresh, mineral, faintly metallic on the tongue—kept exactly there by heat exchangers buried in the plates and by the endless, slow sigh of evaporators that never cycle off or on. They simply are. Condensation draws thin bright lines down every vertical face, so the world looks continuously newly damp, as if someone wiped it clean an hour ago with a cloth the size of weather.

There is no vegetation. No lichen, no moss film, no opportunistic film of green. Where organic films might once have tried, the surfaces have been sealed or simply refuse parity. In their place the density of the place is given over to fauna so numerous the eye first registers them as texture. They occupy every recess, every cable tray, every shadowed elbow of pipe. They are soft-bodied, mostly, or carapaced in the same dull ceramics as the architecture, or composed of overlapping plates that click once in a lifetime and then never again. They do not travel. They hang, pool, nest, and overlie one another in strata of stillness so complete that motionlessness becomes a kind of pressure. Some are the size of a thumbnail and pack the joints of walkways like wet sand. Others drape across entire span-sections of conduit, flat and heavy, their many slow eyes half-lidded. A few larger silhouettes rest in the open trays of standing water, backs awash, breathing so shallowly the surface film barely dimples. The warmth keeps them idling at the edge of metabolism. No predator moves. No prey startles. The arrangement has reached equilibrium and stayed there; arrivals and departures, if they ever occurred, finished long before duration began to matter.

Sound is almost absent. The industrial under-structure—turbines big as weather systems, conveyors frozen mid-link, pressure vessels the color of old bone—produces only the lowest continuous brown note, felt more in the teeth than heard. Water makes less noise than expected: a constant intimate ticking of drops that never quite accelerates into rain. The fauna contribute almost nothing. Once in a great while a multiple limb adjusts by a millimeter and the wet surfaces kiss; that is all. The quiet is not emptiness. It is saturation without agitation, the acoustic equivalent of the water film that will neither dry nor deepen.

Light falls in ordinary strength from a high, featureless expanse that might be sky or the underside of yet another fabricated deck too distant to resolve. Shadows are soft-edged because the moisture scatters everything. Edges themselves remain sharp: the Fabric is young in the way a casting is young, all corners still crisp, no century of abrasion having been permitted to drink them down. Erosion was planned out of the system. What looks weathered is only stained by the permanent damp—streaks of mineral white, blooms of iron ghosting, a sideways glitter where salts have crept and then stopped.

Scale keeps practicing itself. Walk long enough in any direction and the same motifs recur—inspection catwalks that widen into piers, stacks of honeycomb vaults that open into cantilevered basins, lattice towers that fork and rejoin like thoughtful decisions—but the recurring never quite becomes familiar. Distances behave as if they have their own local climate of perspective. Far structures appear both nearer and less detailed than they should. Weather, such as it is, stays locked: the same warm breath, the same hanging veil of vapor, the same refusal of wind. Time-invariant. The engines idle at the exact revolutions required to keep the humidity and the heat and the silence perfectly balanced against one another. Nothing is being produced anymore except the conditions for the overwhelming quiet life that has filled every vacancy and then ceased to revise itself.

There is a mild wrongness that never quite names itself. Angles that should close sometimes leave a hairline gap filled with darker water. The fauna’s eyes, when they open at all, do not track the same directions as their bodies face. Occasionally a duct will exhale a taste of air that has never been outside a lung. These discrepancies do not accumulate into threat or wonder; they simply sit inside the larger equilibrium like the rest. The Low Fabric does not develop. It continues, wet and warm and densely occupied, an endless manufactured marsh of metal and stillness whose only weather is the faint, endless ticking of water that has already decided how much of itself to be.
