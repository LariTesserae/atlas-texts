The plateau does not begin so much as continue until the world runs out of ways to climb. You have been ascending for a time that feels geological rather than personal, through air that has long since stopped pretending to be generous, and then the ground simply flattens into an expanse of pale, wind-scoured stone that keeps going past every reasonable horizon. This is not a mountain summit. It is a high place that forgot to end.

The city occupies it without occupying it. From a distance the silhouette reads as urban—rooflines, towers, the stacked logic of settlement—yet the nearer you come the clearer it becomes that no one built this in any ordinary sense. The structures are continuous with the plateau itself: the same dense, dry mineral, the same cool grey shot through with veins of something that might once have been salt or might still be something stranger. Walls rise and break and rise again in long, settled profiles, weathered into softness at every edge. Doorways open onto toured emptiness. Windows hold nothing but dim sky. There are streets, yes—broad avenues of packed dust and fractured flagstone that run for miles between façades—but they are streets the way a riverbed is a river after the water has finished with it. Form without the life that once required it.

There is almost no water here and has not been for a very long time. What moisture remains is theoretical: a faint mineral bloom in the deepest lee of certain walls, a taste of iron on the tongue when the wind turns. The chemistry of the place is pure desiccation. Surfaces chalk under the smallest pressure. Dust does not so much blow as sift, endlessly redistributing itself according to laws that feel older than weather. In the long colonnades and hollowed plazas, the silence has weight. You hear your own steps with embarrassing clarity, and between them only the occasional tick of cooling stone or the soft collapse of a grain-pile that has decided, after centuries, to finish falling.

Light arrives diluted. Whatever sun serves this height does so through a permanent high overcast the color of old bone, so that the whole city lives in a perpetual late afternoon that never quite commits to evening. Shadows are long and collaborative. They pool in corners and then, without abruptness, forget their origins and drift. This is one of the first signs that the rules here are not intact.

Because the city is active. Not with traffic or voices—there are almost none—but with slow, structural process. Sections of wall re-angle themselves over the course of hours, so gradually that only prolonged watching reveals the change. A tower three streets over will, by the time you reach its square, have developed a new buttress of pure extruded mineral, cooled and already weathering into the common style. Elsewhere a courtyard sinks by a handspan overnight, its paving stones tilting into gentle new grades as if the plateau beneath is still deciding its own elevation. The erosion is forged into balance but not into stillness. Everything has been here long enough to know its shape, and everything is still becoming that shape.

Fauna is rare enough to feel like error when it appears. Once, between two leaning tenements of fused stone, something many-legged and the color of the dust itself crossed the avenue with the unhurried patience of a thing that has never needed to hurry. It left no tracks that lasted. Another time, high in a window aperture, a pale membrane fluttered and then was only shadow again. Whatever lives here does so in the negative spaces, in the vast cubic empties between constructions, and does not advertise. The ecology, if it qualifies, is one of extreme sparsity: a few hard lives running on mineral gradients and the faint heat differentials of the cooling rock, nothing that would recognize a leaf or a raindrop.

And then there is the way the place fails to stay consistent with itself.

Walk far enough along any major axis and the geometry begins to offer alternative readings. Parallel streets refuse to remain parallel when unobserved. A stair that descended into a dry cistern yesterday now ascends toward a balcony that overlooks a district you do not remember passing. Distances inflate and contract; a plaza that should take ten minutes to cross instead consumes an afternoon of walking under the same unchanging dimness, the surrounding colonnades sliding past with dreamlike reluctance. Sound behaves worse. Your footsteps sometimes arrive a half-second early, or echo from a direction that contains only open air. In certain empty forums the silence thickens until it acquires a texture, a resistance, as if quiet itself had weather.

At the outer edges—where the city finally thins into solitary spines and half-collapsed terraces before the plateau drops away into valleys so deep their floors are only suggestion—the breakdown grows frank. There the stone remembers other arrangements. You may glimpse, in the corner of the eye, the same tower standing in three slightly different positions at once. Or a section of wall that is fully solid until you accept soft focus, at which point it becomes a lattice of porous absences through which a colder wind moves. The drop beyond is extreme enough that looking over it produces a physical vertigo unrelated to height: a sense that the world below is not merely distant but optional, one possible continuation among others that the plateau has currently declined to ratify.

No tools litter the streets. No residual mechanism ticks in any wall. Whatever organized this place into urbanness did so without device, or so long ago that noise and metal and purpose have all been sanded off by the same patient forces that rounded every corner. The city is pure construction and pure nature at once—settled mineral doing an extremely long improvisation in the shape of habitation. It does not need inhabitants to complete the gesture. The gesture has become the substrate.

Stand in the central emptiness of what might once have been a market and the cool air moves around you in slow cellular currents. Dust lifts, hangs, decides against falling. Far off, a façade releases a single flake of itself with a sound like a held breath finally let go. The light dims another fractional degree, or your eyes adjust downward, or the difference between those two events has ceased to matter. Above, the bone-colored sky continues its refusal to deepen into night. Below and around, the worked and unworked stone keeps conducting its quiet, relentless negotiations with gravity, time, and the permeable idea of location.

You could walk for days here and meet no one. You could also turn a corner and find the same long avenue repeating with only the smallest treacherous variations, as if the city were testing how much continuity you require before you stop trusting it. The altitude does not care. The dryness does not care. The old, active emptiness simply persists—weathered, organized, almost vacant, and at every scale a little less obedient to the rules that things are supposed to obey.
