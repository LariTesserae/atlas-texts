The pocket sits lower than everything around it, a shallow depression no wider than a railway car is long, dropped into the cracked apron of what was once a loading yard. You come upon it by accident—step through a gap in a chain-link fence half-swallowed by rust, pass between two freestanding brick piers that no longer hold anything up, and the ground gives way in a single, deliberate step. The air changes temperature before your eyes adjust. Heat rises from the wet ground in slow, visible sheets.

Water is everywhere and nowhere decisive. It weeps from the broken concrete lips of the depression in thin, steady films. It stands in irregular mirrors only a few centimeters deep, each one imperfectly round, edged with a pale mineral crust that has not yet had time to soften. The water is warm—almost body-warm—and it smells faintly of iron and old oil. Every surface sweats. Even the air condenses; droplets form on the undersides of protruding rebar and fall with soft, percussive ticks that somehow carry farther than they should. The whole place feels post-rain, though the sky above (what little of it you can see between the surrounding walls and gantries) is a hard, unclouded white. The heat is industrial rather than solar: it radiates upward from buried pipes that still carry something hotter than the day, and sideways from the dark mouths of two large ducts that open into the pocket like truncated throats.

The rock and concrete here are young. Edges remain sharp. A recent subsidence or deliberate cut has left the walls of the depression stepped and angular, the fractures still pale, unweathered. Fresh spalls of concrete litters the wet floor like discarded pottery sherds. Nothing has been rounded yet. Nothing has been given time.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. A few blades of something grass-like push through seams where the concrete has pulled apart; their stalks are translucent yellow-green and glassy with heat. In one corner a single clump of reeds has taken hold in a deeper seepage, their leaves monofilament-thin, barely moving. Algae paints the permanent wet zones in dull metallic greens and coppery browns, but even this film is patchy, interrupted by stretches of bare, steaming mineral. There is space between every living thing. The emptiness is part of the composition.

No animals. No insects skim the water. No birds call from the gantries above. The silence of living mouths makes the mechanical noise absolute.

That noise is the pocket’s true weather. Somewhere nearby—perhaps through the duct mouths, perhaps under the apron—an engine still turns. It is not frantic. The rhythm is slow, almost bored: a deep, reciprocating thud spaced far enough apart that each impact arrives as its own event, followed by a long metallic exhalation and the rising hiss of steam finding release. Pipes knock. Valves cycle with the patience of tides. Water drips into resonant chambers and the echoes multiply until the small space feels louder than any open factory floor. The sound does not peak; it simply continues, a thick, layered loom of industrial breath that has forgotten how to stop.

Scattered through the pocket are the built remnants that belong to the same slow empire. A single cast-iron stanchion stands off-center, its flanged base half-submerged, bolt holes empty. A short run of narrow-gauge rail emerges from one wall and ends in mid-air above the water, the cut so clean it looks surgical. On a higher step sits a small electric pump housing, peeling blue paint, its cable still plugged into a weatherproof outlet that occasionally ticks with residual current. Nothing here is monumental. Everything is fragment, tool, leftover. The technology is heavy, scaled for repetition rather than elegance—pistons, flanges, threaded rods thick as wrists—yet reduced now to these few surviving gestures inside a space the size of a room.

Light falls normally: hard daylight from above, softened only by the steam that never quite becomes fog. Shadows are short and sharp where the angular walls cast them. In the brightest shallows the water throws trembling coins of light back onto the undersides of ducts and the undersides of your own hands if you reach down.

The pocket changes, but only on the timescale of rust and mineral accretion. Water levels rise and fall by millimeters across hours. The engine’s cycle drifts a few degrees cooler or hotter across a day. A new fracture may open somewhere in the surrounding apron within a season, bleeding fresh water into the depression, sharpening another edge. Nothing hurries. The place is a valve left slightly open in a larger, abandoned system—an accidental microclimate of heat, moisture, and persistent industrial pulse, sealed off by its own smallness and by the simple fact that almost no one still walks the yard.

Stand still long enough and the moisture beads on your skin the same way it beads on the steel. The sound settles into your chest cavity. The sharp new edges of the world continue their almost imperceptible work of becoming slightly less new.
