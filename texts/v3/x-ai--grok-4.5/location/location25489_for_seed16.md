The Hollow Meridian sits where a greater waste folds inward on itself. From the high waste you would never find it: the basalt shelves tilt and lock like a lid, and the only entrance is a vertical seam no wider than a body, polished to glass by centuries of abrasive wind. Drop through and the world changes scale without changing size. What opens below is throat-like, a shallow oval basin no more than two kilometers across at its longest, low enough that the heat pools and stagnates. Elevation is a remembered rumor. Everything here has already settled to the bottom of itself.

There is no water and never was. The air itself is a desiccant, sharp with the taste of powdered feldspar and old copper. What falls from the rim is only dust and the occasional flake of basalt, and these become the soil—if soil is the word for a medium that is more archive than earth. Yet vegetation thrives at a stubborn mid-lush. It is not green. It is the deep oxide red of cooled slag and the matte black of charcoal that still remembers flame. Vast feather-headed reeds stand in loose canopies, their stems hollow and resonant, leaves like thin hammered plates that clatter instead of rustle. Between them sprawl low, cushioned mats of something lichenous that almost looks soft until you touch it and feel the glassy spines, each one a miniature heat fin. These plants drink temperature gradients. Their roots lace through the black sand toward the residual warmth of the basin floor, which never fully cools. Photosynthesis here is a negotiation with infrared; the canopy drinks the dim amber half-light that filters through the perpetual dust haze hanging under the stone lid, then spends the rest of its metabolism on the slow radiation rising from below. Nothing flowers. Everything seeds by rupture: pods swell over weeks and detonate with rifle-crack reports, flinging silicate-coated spores on the next thermal updraft. That is the closest thing to rain the Meridian knows—an occasional dry hiss of new life finding purchase in old grit.

The heat is not seasonal. It is structural. Air shimmers in slow vertical curtains. Stone too long in one attitude will ding and split with the sound of bells struck underwater. Midday (a term of convenience under the permanent dusk) pushes the basin past the point where exposed skin would blister in minutes; the plants answer by angling their leaf-plates edge-on and going nearly black, a collective dimming that makes the whole pocket feel deeper. Night is only a lesser furnace. Heat has nowhere to escape; it cycles.

Across this baked oval the built world is sparse, almost ceremonial. This is a city by intention and emptiness by result. Wide plazas of fitted basalt flags, each stone hand-pecked into rough polygons with nothing sharper than longer stones and patient abrasion, lie separated by stretches of open sand so broad that two structures can feel like different countries. Towers rise—some cylindrical, some stepped like incomplete ziggurats—rarely higher than six human heights, their silhouettes softened by long weathering into the shapes of melted candles. Walls are double-skinned adobe over rubble cores, the outer skin flaking in elegant scabs. Doorways are low, lintels monolithic, and many stand empty of doors; the builders preferred thresholds that could not slam. Between clusters run raised causeways whose original purpose is lost—too high for flood (there is none), too narrow for carts (there were none). Some end in circular platforms open to the dusty sky. On these rest the remaining machines of the Meridian: great vertical harps of stretched sinew and hollow reed, limb-thick resonators carved from denser trunks of the black plants, and conglomerations of chimes hung so that every convective gust plays them. The sound is constant and large. It is not music. It is the basin talking to its own emptiness—deep middle tones that roll under the higher clatter of leaf-plates and the percussive barking of seedpods. On the rare days when the wind stills, the heat-cracking of stone itself keeps the silence from arriving.

No animals have ever lived here. The ecology is a closed conversation between mineral, plant, wind, and the slow chemistry of surfaces. Dust devils walk the plazas in deliberate circuits, gathering loose spores and redistributing them like postal spirits. The mats of spine-lichen advance a finger-width per year across abandoned thresholds, binding sand into fragile crusts that the next big thermal shift will shatter and recycle. Buildings shed grit that becomes new matrix for roots. Everything is weathered into balance: corners rounded, tooling marks erased, the whole settlement looking less constructed than precipitated from the heat itself and then patiently edited by abrasion. The city feels long-inhabited and long-empty at once, as if its makers learned the place’s rules and then become another of them.

Light stays dim by habit. The dust never fully settles; it hangs in stratified lenses of ochre and iron, thinning the already filtered glow to the color of old bronze. Shadows have soft edges. At the basin’s nadir a faint welling of deeper red sometimes shows—geothermal memory, nothing more—enough to throw the nearest reed stems into silhouette so they look like the bars of an enormous dying lantern. Walking the empty avenues you hear your own footfalls absorbed almost immediately, yet the louder world continues without you: the harps, the pods, the stones complaining of expansion, the long hollow note the wind makes when it finds the seam that leads back out and tries, endlessly, to play it.

The weirdness is quiet and architectural. Distances misfire. Cross a plaza that should take three minutes and arrive sandpapered by an hour of heat. Turn a corner between two towers and find the same corner again, as if the basin gently insists on certain vistas. The plants lean toward unfinished thoughts. And somewhere under the oldest platform the ground is the wrong temperature—cooler by a margin that should be impossible—suggesting that something once buried still refuses the common furnace. No one digs. The Meridian has been finished for longer than memory, and its only remaining law is that whatever still moves does so according to gradients of heat and dust and resonance, nothing else.
