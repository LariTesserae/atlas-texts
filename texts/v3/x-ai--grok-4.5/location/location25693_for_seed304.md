The place is a hamlet locked into a shallow basin no larger than a cathedral nave, a depression that still feels newly cut. The land here is young—soft mudstone and pale clay cracked into knife-edged ridges and half-formed ledges that have only recently finished pushing up through the wet. Everything remains sharp: fracture lines run clean and pale, unrounded by time, and the air still carries the mineral scent of fresh breakage. Water comes freely and often. It weeps steadily from every exposed face, beads along the underside of ledges, and pools in the shallow gutters between dwellings until the whole floor of the basin stays glossy and shin-deep in places. After rain the weeping becomes a continuous thin curtain; even in drier hours the stone sweats.

There is almost no vegetation. A few wiry rushes force themselves through joints in the clay, and a dull green film of algae clings to permanently damp walls, but the ground is too new and too trampled for anything taller. What grows is incidental, quickly scraped away or trodden under.

The settlement itself is dense past comfort. Walls share walls. Houses of packed clay, split reed, and unshaped stone abut one another so tightly that the interior lanes are little more than vertical seams a person can edge through sideways. Roofs of layered turf and bark almost touch, turning the upper level into a second broken pavement of mist and dripping eaves. Where the young ridges rise, dwellings climb them in staggered steps, each foundation cut fresh into the still-settling rock. There is no true street—only a network of wet corridors, stoops, and ladder-notches that force every movement into single file. The entire built mass feels saturated, every surface claimed, every gap already occupied by a stoop or a drying rack or a storage jar.

Technology is hand-simple. Edges are flake-stone or fire-hardened wood. Bindings are sinew, split root, and a chalky mortar made from the same weeping mineral that crusts the ledges overnight. Tools are renewed constantly because the damp softens wooden handles and the soft stone dulls quickly. No metal shows; everything is abraded, lashed, or pressed into shape while wet and left to set in the cool air.

Light falls ordinary and gray-white, diffused by the low cloud that habitually sits in the basin. It never quite becomes gloom, yet shadows stay soft and directionless. Temperature holds cool—the kind of cool that keeps skin aware of itself and makes the constant moisture feel colder than it is. Breath shows. Clay walls stay clammy to the touch.

Sound is muted. The dominant register is water: the thin continuous ticking of seepage, the occasional heavier drop from an eave into a cistern, the soft suck of bare feet in the shallows. Voices stay low by habit; the packed walls carry conversation farther than people intend. Once in a long while something living moves outside the human pattern—a water vole slipping between reed clumps at the marsh edge that still rings the basin, or the single remote croak of a frog—but fauna is scarce. The new ground has not yet invited much life.

The place is quietly active. The young stone continues to settle in small, almost polite shifts; hairline cracks open overnight and must be packed with fresh mortar before they weep too freely. Mist thickens and thins on a short cycle, beading every surface then slowly clearing so that for an hour the wet ridges stand out bone-pale. People move through their narrow routes in steady low traffic: carrying water jars, re-lashing a roof edge, scraping algae from a threshold. At the outer fringe, where the basin meets the remaining marsh, the water level breathes up and down a hand-span with weather and season, sometimes flooding the lowest doorways, sometimes dropping to reveal new ledges still slick and uncolonized. The settlement adjusts without drama—houses are rebuilt a course higher, thresholds raised, the dense fabric shifting like a single organism that has nowhere else to expand.

There is a mild strangeness that never quite resolves into rule. The mineral that weeps from the fresh stone hardens, when collected and dried, into a translucent crust that takes an edge almost like glass yet dissolves again if left too long in open air. People use it for cutting tools and for thin window panes that cloud and soften within a season, so every dwelling slowly loses and regains its dull amber eyes. No one treats this as miraculous; it is simply another maintenance cycle, another reason the place never finishes becoming solid. The basin feels less like a location chosen than like a brief consensus between rising ground and persistent water, and the hamlet has answered by packing itself into every available inch before the consensus can change.
