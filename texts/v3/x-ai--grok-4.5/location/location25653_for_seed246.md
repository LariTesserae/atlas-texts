The plateau is a worn coin of stone, lifted and left nearly flat by ages that no longer remember their own weather. What remains of elevation is a shallow bowl rimmed by low, slumped scarps that once might have been cliffs; now they are only wet grey shoulders furred with lichen. Within this bowl—scarce larger than a cathedral close or a mountain hamlet’s single street—the air stays cold enough that breath hangs and beads on every surface. Moisture never quite arrives as proper rain and never quite leaves. It condenses. It films. It runs in slow, intermittent sheets down the insides of things and freezes into thin vertical panes that fracture with sharp, irregular cracks throughout the short day.

The built things are here, but they have forgotten the difference between wall and outcrop. Blocks of pale, sugar-grained stone stand in loose street-grids and shallow courts, their corners melted by freeze and thaw into the soft geometry of very old bones. Doorways gape without doors; roofs have long since slumped inward or been prized apart by root and ice, leaving open wells of pale sky. The structures are spaced farther apart than any living city would allow—great empty intervals of scree and moss between one hollow hall and the next—so that the place feels both urban and almost vacant, as if the settlement had been laid out for a denser populace that simply never arrived, or arrived and then folded itself into the stone and left only echoes.

Vegetation thrives in the damp cold with a quiet shrewdness. Thick, glaucous moss carpets every horizontal plane and climbs the vertical ones in slow green tides. Pale ferns unfurl from cracks in a constant soft ticking of growth. In the lee of every wall hang curtains of ice-whitened liverwort and strands of a hairlike alpine grass that ring faintly when the wind moves through them. The green is lush yet spectral, the color of things that photosynthesize more by stubbornness than by sunlight. Normal daylight reaches the bowl in a high, thin wash—neither dim nor brilliant—filtered through a near-constant low ceiling of moving mist that keeps the light diffuse and shadowless.

Sound is the true weather here. The bowl amplifies everything. Wind never simply passes; it is caught, fluted, and thrown back by the hollowed architecture until it becomes a continuous low roar threaded with higher whistles and sudden organ-like moans. Meltwater drips from lintels into shallow stone basins with loud, arrhythmic percussion. Sheets of rime detach from cornices and shatter like dropped porcelain. Somewhere deeper in the maze a deeper resonance builds and collapses—perhaps only air descending a long stairwell that no longer leads anywhere, or perhaps the slow breath of the plateau itself adjusting to another century of settling. The noise is constant, layered, almost conversational; in the empty intervals between buildings it feels personal, as if the place is trying to remember speech.

Fauna is rare enough to startle. Once in a long while a pale hare ghosts between moss hummocks, or a flock of small grey birds shears through a ruined arcade and is gone, their wingbeats swallowed by the greater din. Nothing stays. The cold and the emptiness discourage residence; whatever lives here is always just passing through the bowl on its way to somewhere kinder.

The strangeness is quieter than the sound yet more persistent. Time does not move evenly among the stones. A patch of frost that forms on one wall at dawn may still be sequined and perfect at dusk while, three paces away, an identical patch has already melted, refrozen, and cracked into grey lace. Certain doorways open onto interiors that seem fractionally larger than their exteriors allow—enough to notice, never enough to map. The moss occasionally grows in glyphs that dissolve when stared at. None of this feels like magic imposed; it feels like the exhaustion of ordinary physics after having been asked the same questions for too many millennia. The bowl has settled into its final, peculiar form and now merely continues, loud and damp and almost empty, under the high thin light.

Processes continue without urgency but without rest. Freeze lifts another millimeter of stone dust from a threshold. A curtain of moss advances one more handspan across a floor that was once polished. Mist thickens, thins, thickens again. The wind finds a new note in a freshly cracked spire and holds it until the next thaw reconfigures the instrument. Everything that can erode has already eroded down to something nearly eternal; what remains is only the slow, noisy maintenance of that eternity inside a space small enough to walk across in a few cold minutes, yet empty enough that the walking never quite accumulates into arrival.
