The place is called, by nothing and no one, the Hollow Congeries. It occupies a shallow depression no wider than a cathedral close or a neglected market square, low enough that the land around it lifts in polite, unnoticed berms, as if the world had once settled its weight here and then forgotten to rise again. From outside you might mistake it for a thicket that grew wrong; step in and the scale reverses. The congestion of green is only the skin.

Everything that holds shape is plant. There is no stone, no worked wood, no clay, no metal—only living mass that long ago decided to imitate the idea of a city and then outgrew the imitation. Trunks the girth of towers have fused lateral roots into walls three stories high, their bark scored by centuries of weather into a script of ridges and runnels. Canopies have knitted overhead into vaults and clerestories that leak only carefully rationed daylight, a normal, honey-colored light that arrives already soft, already filtered through a thousand chlorophyll tensions. Air-roots hang in organized curtains that divide avenues; buttress-flanges form stair-ramps that no foot has ever finished wearing smooth. Balconies of living epiphyte platforms jut at irregular heights; bridges of braided liana breathe and slowly tighten or slack with the day’s humidity. The whole arrangement has the patient geometry of something that has been allowed to weather into balance: established, settled, neither growing wildly nor eroding away, simply continuing in its own long negotiation with gravity and rain.

And yet the density inside is almost nothing. Between the living walls lie vast hollows—chambers large enough to lose thunder in, corridors that feel like the lungs of some sleeping continent. You can stand in the central undercroft and see an entire weather system of dust-motes turning in a column of light while the nearest some of vegetation is twenty paces off in every direction. The Congeries is total plant, yet it is also near-void: a city whose rooms are mostly air, whose streets are canyons of green silence waiting to be filled. The architecture does not crowd; it frames emptiness and then lets the emptiness do the work of presence.

Water is intermittent and intimate rather than abundant. Mornings bring a slow exhalation of condensation that beads on every surface and then, with no warning, lets go in soft vertical rains that last less than a minute. These rains never reach the ground as streams; they are caught halfway in the upturned basalt-dark cups of tank-bromeliads, in the folded elbows of strangler figs, in the temporary gutters formed by overlapping palm sheaths. A single sluggish filament of blackwater creeps along the lowest channel of the Congeries, appearing and disappearing between root-buttresses, more a rumor of river than a river itself. The air stays damp enough that skin never fully dries and every breath tastes faintly of tannin and warm pollen.

The temperature is a constant mild fever—warm without cruelty, the kind of warmth that makes growth audible if you know how to listen. And listening is unavoidable, because the sound here is total. It is deafening in the way a waterfall is deafening when you stand inside its throat. Every surface resonates. The hollow trunks act as organ pipes for the wind that funnels through the low depression; they produce a continuous subsonic choir that you feel in the hinge of your jaw. Over this rides the higher cacophony of the fauna, which is abundant past counting. Clouds of metallic-green beetles turn in ordered vortices between the buttresses, their wing-cases clacking like shaken gravel. Troops of small, fox-faced primates move along the living cables in waves, each animal screaming a slightly different pitch so that the troop becomes a traveling chord. There are flightless birds the color of wet slate that drum on resonant root-plates with their beaks, staking claims that last only minutes before the claim is overwritten by a rival. Somewhere always there is the wet tearing sound of something being opened—seed-pod, chrysalis, or softer prey. The Congeries never hushes. Even at the rare moments when the animals pause, the plants themselves creak and adjust, and the residual echo of previous noise hangs in the cavities like a held breath that refuses to end.

All of it moves. The dynamic is not chaotic but multi-threaded: slow hydraulic shifts as water is redistributed through xylem overnight; sudden territorial flares among the canopy mammals; the arrival and dissipation of mist banks that rewrite visibility every quarter-hour; the almost geological inching of a strangler fig that has decided, over decades, to close a certain thoroughfare and open another. Predator and prey rewrite the same looping paths. Flowers open and are emptied by tongues within minutes. Nothing is static, yet nothing feels hurried; the pace is simply the pace of a complete ecosystem that has nowhere else to be.

And then there is the weirdness, the reality breakdown that lives in the grain of the place like a second moisture. Distances lie. A corridor you have just walked may return you to its own entrance, the walls having performed some internal rotation while your back was turned. Colors detach from their objects: a patch of moss will continue its particular green in mid-air for several heartbeats after you have passed, like an afterimage that forgot to fade. Sounds sometimes acquire mass—the scream of a primate will drop out of the air as a visible, glistening thread that drapes itself across a root and then dissolves into new beetles. Time stutters. You may watch a single leaf take an hour to fall, or glance away and find an entire vertical garden has flowered, fruited, and gone to seed in what felt like a breath. The Congeries does not obey the same causal etiquette as the flatter lands outside its berm. It is as if the total vegetable mass, having spent millennia dreaming the shape of a city, has begun to dream other geometries as well, and the dreams leak. Boundaries soften. Once in a while the hollows open onto brief, impossible annexes: a plaza that is also the memory of a plaza, a stair that ascends into a sky that is only more root. These annexes collapse again when the light shifts, leaving only the smell of crushed mint and ozone.

No tool has ever been made here. Nothing is built in the dead sense; everything is grown, pruned by mouth and claw and time, occupied, abandoned, recolonized. The urban density is real—the organization, the layered infrastructures of resin trails and pollen-roads and watering routes—but it is an urbanism of pure biology, pre-tool and post-need. The Congeries simply continues, warm and damp and thunderously alive inside its small, hollowed, waiting skin, a city that is also a single organism that is also a loophole in the way places are supposed to behave. 

Stand still long enough and you will feel the next soft rain begin on the upper leaves, hear it gather, and realize the sound it makes while falling is already being answered by ten thousand throats that were waiting for exactly that cue. The emptiness between the walls cups the noise and gives it back louder. The plants lean in by the width of a breath. The world, for the duration of the shower, forgets to be singular.
