The highland opens without hurry. It is a broad, elevated basin—valley-scaled yet high enough that the air thins slightly and the light falls clean and unfiltered—ringed by young ridgelines whose edges have not yet rounded. The rock is pale, almost chalky in places, fractured into sharp planes and fresh scarps as if the whole mass had only recently finished rising. Water moves everywhere across it, not in dramatic cataracts but as a constant, quiet presence: sheets of seepage darkening the stone, thin silver threads that never dry, shallow marsh hollows collecting in every gentle depression, and a fine, persistent mist that beads on every surface and hangs in the cooler hollows like withheld breath. The temperature is mild, almost body-warm, so the wet never bites; it simply saturates.

Vegetation is patchy and intentional-looking in its placement. Low, dense mats of moss and fine-leaved herbs cling to the weeping faces of the rock. In the damper flats, stands of slender reeds and broad-bladed plants rise in scattered clusters, lush where they grow yet separated by wide stretches of bare, glistening stone or thin panels of wet gravel. Nothing sprawls unchecked; the green feels deliberate, almost groomed by the same quiet geometry that shapes the rest of the place. There are no animals. No insects skate the water, no birds cross the open light, no distant call or rustle breaks the hush. The only sounds are the soft continuous trickle of water finding lower ground and the occasional slow drip from an overhang.

Across this open, damp expanse a settlement is scattered rather than concentrated. Low structures of fitted stone and packed earth stand at generous intervals, linked by raised walkways of the same pale rock so that feet stay above the worst of the seepage. The buildings are simple and solid: thick walls, flat or gently pitched roofs of layered turf and slate, doorways framed in undressed timber where timber can be found. There is no metal beyond the occasional carefully worked copper blade or awl; everything else is stone, bone, wood, fiber, and knowledge of how wet rock behaves. Paths are cut shallow and cambered so water runs off them. Catchment basins and narrow stone-lined channels gather the perpetual moisture and lead it where it is wanted—into cisterns, under floors for coolness, out again to keep the walkways from drowning. The whole arrangement feels settled into permanence, as though the same layout has been maintained without alteration for a very long time.

Light is ordinary daylight, clear and steady, neither harsh nor dim. It reveals every wet gleam and every sharp edge without drama. Nothing here is hurried or decaying or advancing. The mist does not lift or thicken; the marsh levels neither rise nor fall; the stone remains freshly broken yet never sheds new flakes; the sparse green stays exactly as lush and exactly as limited as it is. The town neither expands nor contracts. People—if one imagines them—would move quietly along the raised paths, maintaining what already is, in a place that has reached an unbroken equilibrium of water, rock, green, and spare human order. The highland simply continues, open, wet, elevated, and still.
