The high plateaus of the Anvil Crest stretch beyond any single horizon, a single weathered continent heaved so far above the forgotten lower world that the sky itself seems to begin underfoot. The land is all elevation and patience: soft-shouldered mesas the color of old iron, salt-crusted tables of mineral slag that run for hundreds of kilometers without a fold, and long ridges worn into elegant, almost anatomical curves by wind that has blown for geologic ages. There is no water. What little hydrogen remains is locked inside the crystal lattices of the rock or sealed in the black glass veins that thread the plateaus like frozen lightning. The air is warm and thin, tasting of heated copper and dry ozone; it carries heat upward from deep industrial blood without ever gathering into true weather. Days pass under ordinary white suns that never quite glare, only illuminate with a flat, museum calm.

Settlements appear only at great intervals, each one a compact city-machine half-excavated from the mesa it crowns. These are not temporary camps but long-settled organs of the landscape: tiered foundries, cooling towers that have become part of the skyline’s weathered silhouette, rail spines that end abruptly at the cliff edge as if the next mesa were once close enough to touch. The construction is industrial and layered—riveted plating over packed mineral concrete, gantries that still turn though their original cargo has been forgotten, chimney forests whose mouths no longer smoke but only exhale slow heat. Between the cities lie vast emptinesses measured in days of walking. Density is a deliberate scarcity; each settlement has its own weather of low machine-hum and its own night-glow, yet the spaces between them dominate, balanced and quiet.

Almost nothing grows. The few dark crusts that might once have been lichen are long mineralized, indistinguishable from the host rock. Instead the ecology is animal and strange. Herds of low, multi-legged browsers move across the salt pans in unhurried arithmetic, their bodies plated in the same iron-stained minerals as the ground. They rasp at the surface with slow mandibular mills, ingesting grit and excreting finer powder that the wind redistributes over centuries. Above them drift translucent envelope-creatures the size of small houses, barely denser than the air, filtering the warm updrafts for charged dust. Flocks of jagged, wingless gliders launch from tower flanks at dusk, riding thermals that rise from still-hot slag beds; their calls are nearly subsonic, felt more than heard. In the shadowed undercuts of the factories live denser populations—segmented things that rustle through cable ducts and gear-trains, scavenging lubricant and metal filings, their life cycles measured in the same decades as the machines they haunt. Nothing here is frantic. Even predation is a matter of gradual interception, of one body simply outlasting another until the weaker settles into dust.

The quiet is nearly total. Wind moves like cloth across stone. Engines deep inside the cities turn on multi-year cycles, producing only a sub-bass presence that the body registers as warmth rather than noise. Occasional distant clangs— a cooling plate contracting, a rail joint settling—arrive minutes after the eye has already accepted the stillness. At night the ordinary light fades to a clear dark punctuated by the dull orange of banked industrial hearts; constellations are sharp, and the envelope-creatures phosphoresce in slow blue pulses as they feed.

The rules of the place are those of long equilibrium. Erosion and construction have reached a truce: every cut made by scavengers or by the cities’ slow expansion is matched, over centuries, by fresh deposition of wind-borne grit that hardens into the same mineral concrete. Time itself feels viscous. A tower may lean a few degrees across a human lifespan and still be considered upright. Storms of abrasive dust arrive once a generation, scouring surfaces just enough to keep edges honest without ever reshaping the grand profiles. The fauna migrate on circuits that take decades to close. Even the weirdness is patient: sometimes the salt flats refract the sky so that an inverted second landscape hangs overhead for hours, complete with ghost-cities whose lights flicker in antiphase to the real ones; sometimes a browser herd freezes in perfect geometric formation for a full season, as if listening to something under the stone. No one speeds these phenomena. The Anvil Crest does not reward haste. It simply continues, warm and elevated and sparsely inhabited, converting its own slow fire into form and form back into heat, forever becoming more precisely itself.
