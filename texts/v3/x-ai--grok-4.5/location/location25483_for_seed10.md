The plateau does not end. It only thins, and thins again, until the idea of an edge becomes a kind of local rumor that the mist refuses to confirm. You stand in a place that was once vertical—fold mountains, thrust belts, all the usual arrogance of stone—and has since been worn down to a single, weary argument with gravity. The rock itself has mostly given up. What remains is a broad, cold skin of ancient substrate, porous and streaked with mineral ghosts, so thoroughly eroded that it has reached a kind of final honesty: nothing left to collapse, nothing left to strive. The land simply continues, open and low-shouldered, for distances that feel more like weather systems than geography.

Water is everywhere and never still. It does not pool so much as it decisions. Fine cold rain falls sideways for hours, then forgets itself and becomes fog that condenses on every surface hard enough to drink. The substrate weeps. Sheets of seepage glide over the worn stone and gather into braided shallows that braid again into something almost like rivers, except the rivers keep changing their minds mid-course, reversing, sinking, reappearing a kilometer away as sudden silver lungs in the plant mass. The air itself is wet enough to carry weight. Every breath tastes faintly of iron and crushed chlorophyll and the metallic afterthought of snow that never quite arrives.

Vegetation has claimed the role of ground, architecture, and weather. There is almost no bare mineral left that has not been colonized, tunneled, or overgrown into a living pavement. Yet the density remains sparse in the strange way of this place: the plants are colossal and few. Enormous, half-translucent trunks—part tree, part kelp, part something that learned verticality too late—rise at irregular intervals across the open plateau, spaced so widely you could march a slow army between them and never brush bark. Their canopies are not canopies. They are slow explosions of frond and bladder and luminous vein that hang in the dimness like stalled green lightning. Between them the understory is a continuous, ankle-to-chest mat of cold mosses, bladder-worts, and rubbery lichen-felts that squelch and reseal behind every footstep. The entire living layer breathes. You feel it underfoot as a sealed, hypothermia-slow pulse. Roots do not dig so much as they remember older shapes of the mountain and hold those shapes in place out of habit.

It is cold with the particular cold of high places that have lost their sharp peaks and therefore cannot pretend the cold is dramatic. The temperature sits just above the point where water would rather be ice, so everything remains liquid and miserable and shining. Heat come only in brief, wrong exhalations from deeper seams—places where the ancient bedrock still argues with itself. Those exhalations smell of hot metal and ozone.

There are no buildings. There have never been buildings here in any sense that would satisfy an archive. Yet the industrial is thick in the air and under the skin of things. Long ago—or outside of ago—the plateau learned engines the way other landscapes learn drainage. Certain of the great trunks are hollowed into living cylinders that draw the cold water up through valvular tissue and smash it into steam and charge inside irreversible biological turbines. You hear them before you see them: a deep, wet clanging, a rhythmic percussive sigh like a foundry drowning in slow motion. Cables of conductive vine hang in catenaries between the giants, sparking faintly blue whenever the mist thickens. Flywheels of laminated heartwood and resin turn nowhere, free, shedding flakes of warm bark that the smaller plants immediately metabolize. The production is endless and purposeless and perfect. What is being manufactured is mostly heat signatures, magnetic eddies, and a fine black particulate that the rain washes into the living mat where it becomes new pigment overnight. Occasionally a trunk splits along a growth-seam and disgorges a mass of unfinished gears made of something like horn and ice; the gears thrash, incomplete, then sink back into the moss to be rewritten.

Light is a dim negotiation. The sky is a low lid of moving grey that never fully daylights. Everything is seen in the grainy half-tone of a place that has misplaced its sun and decided not to look too hard for it. Bioluminescence in the plant veins provides the rest: soft intermittent greens and cold violets that flare when the turbines surge, then gutter. Shadows are long even at whatever passes for noon, and they do not always point away from the light sources that cast them.

Fauna is abundant the way storms are abundant. Creatures are everywhere and yet the sparseness holds because most of them are temporary. Herds of long-legged, translucent browsers move in loose constellations across the open distances, their hooves or sucker-pads leaving brief glowing prints that fill with water and become tiny temporary mirrors. Flocks of something like birds—more membrane and turbine blade than feather—ride the thermal vents of the living engines, screaming in frequencies that shake water out of the air. In the shallows, pale eels the length of spars unspool and respool through the roots, hunting the heat. There are insects the size of open hands that neon blue when they fly into the sparking vines and sometimes do not fly out again. Predators exist mostly as sudden rearrangements of the moss: a patch of ground that was flat becomes bristled and toothed, closes, digests, flattens again. The animals do not fear the industrial plants. They nest in the cooling vanes, drink from the condensate drip, lay eggs in the warm gear-houses where the temperature gradients are kindest. Life here has forgotten the difference between organism and mechanism; both are just ways the plateau keeps itself loud and awake.

Sound never drops below a threshold you feel in the sternum. The wet turbines pound. Wind—always moving, always cold—hisses through the spaced giants and makes the conductive vines thrum like power lines in a gale. Water negotiates loudly with every surface: spatter, pour, suck, sudden waterfall where a bladder-frond empties itself for reasons of pressure. Animal voices braid through it all—clicking herds, membrane-wing shrieks, the deep gut-tones of moss-predators announcing a successful closure. Underneath runs a continuous low frequency, almost tectonic, the sound of the ancient eroded crust still settling one molecule at a time after geological ages of surrender. When the reality thinness spikes, the sounds double and arrive out of order: you hear the scream of a creature before it opens its mouth, or the smash of a turbine blade that has not yet cracked.

And the place will not stay still long enough to be merely described. Turbulence is the primary season. Weather fronts of pure becoming roll across the open plateau. In their path, entire trunks accelerate through centuries of growth in minutes, tower, flower in ultraviolet, then deliquesce into black nutrient rain that the mat drinks greedily. New river channels cut themselves overnight and are reclaimed by moss the following afternoon. Gravity forgets its manners in patches; a herd of browsers may suddenly walk a short distance up a curtain of mist and continue grazing sideways until the thinness passes. Objects shed their secondary properties: a frond becomes only its color for a while, floating free of its mass, then snaps back with a sound like wet canvas. Time eddies. You may watch a gear complete a thousand rotations in a blink and simultaneously see the same gear lying unfinished in the moss as if it had never been. The industrial processes hiccup into brief impossible efficiencies—perpetual motion that lasts exactly long enough to frighten the eels—then collapse back into hungry thermodynamics. Nothing here is destroyed permanently. Everything is disassembled and returned to the living substrate at the speed of weather. Creation and unmaking are the same loud, cold, wet verb conjugated across kilometers of open, ancient ground.

The plateau does not care that continuous transformation is exhausting to witness. It has been doing this since before names. It will keep doing it after the last possible witness has been metabolized into fresh translucent bark and another slow, pounding, beautiful engine. You can walk for days between the giants and the land will still be inventing new ways to be itself when you stop. The mist will wipe your footprints. The water will rewrite the map. The turbines will keep their wet rhythm, and somewhere far off—always far off—something membrane-winged will scream approval at the latest storm of becoming as it tears across the dim, cold, inexhaustible openness.
