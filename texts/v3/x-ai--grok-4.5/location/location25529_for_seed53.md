The place is a hanging cirque no wider than a cathedral nave, gouged into the knife-edge of a peak that has forgotten the rest of the mountain. You reach it only after the air has thinned to a bright ache and the world below has dropped into rumor. Here the stone does not rise so much as it has already risen and then settled into a long, patient density. Every surface is mineral, every interval crowded.

Needles of smoky quartz and plates of volcanic glass grow so close they nearly touch, a thicket of transparent blades and hexagonal towers that lean and interlock like the ribs of some forgotten mechanism. They are old. Wind and frost have rounded their lower flanks into soft shoulders while their tips remain razor-fine, catching and fracturing the light until the whole basin burns with a cold, shadowless glare. There is no true night here; the altitude and the reflective saturation keep the place bathed in a hard, white radiance that merely changes color by degree—silver at the thin hours, pale gold when the mists thicken. The light does not illuminate so much as it inhabits the crystal. Walk among them and you carry five or six versions of yourself, sheared and stacked, flickering across every faceted face.

Moisture arrives at irregular intervals: a sudden condensation that beads on every edge, runs in thin silver threads, then freezes again into lacy rime. Sometimes a brief curtain of rain falls from a cloud that has nowhere else to go, and the water clings in hanging films between the densest clusters, turning them briefly into dark, gleaming organs. Then the cold takes it back. The air always tastes of damp rock and ozone. Pockets of meltwater form in the hollows where three or four blades meet and hold their secret for hours before vanishing into vapor or ice. There is no stream, only these temporary sequesterings—dampness as memory rather than presence.

Nothing green has ever lived here. Nothing that breathes with lungs or drinks with a mouth. The ecology is mineral and slow. Freeze-thaw cycles work the crystals themselves: hairline fractures open overnight, new growths of rime and secondary quartz fill them by next cold, and the whole thicket rearranges its interior geometry across seasons like a contemplative animal shifting in sleep. On quieter days you can hear it—a low, irregular ticking as stresses release, a soft crystalline grind when two neighboring blades settle closer. Mostly the place is silent. Wind is rare up here, and when it comes it is thin, whistling through the denser groves with a sound like distant glass being rubbed.

Scattered through this geometry are the marks of earlier hands. Not many. A cairn of flat stone plates still standing against one wall of the cirque, its top stone drilled with a neat, primitive hole as if once meant to hold a staff or offering. A short tunnel chipped into a massive black-glass outcrop, just deep enough for a body to crouch out of the brightness; inside, the walls show the parallel scars of stone-on-stone tools. Several quartz needles bear deliberate notches or have been snapped and laid carefully end to end, perhaps for signalling or for some forgotten measurement of light. These are the only built things: faint, weathered, half-absorbed back into the mineral order. Whoever made them understood the place well enough not to build large. They left only what the crystals would tolerate.

The weirdness is quiet and structural. Because the density is so high and the light so absolute, orientation fails in subtle ways. Walk ten paces and the reflections rearrange; a path that looked open turns out to be a false corridor of mirrored stone. Sometimes a hanging sheet of condensation acts as a lens and throws a perfect inverted image of the distant lower world onto a nearby blade—tiny rivers, forests, weather systems flickering for a few heartbeats before the water freezes opaque again. Time itself feels denser here. The processes (cracking, rime-growth, mist arrival and departure) run on many overlapping clocks, so that the cirque is always slightly different yet always recognizably itself, like a face seen across decades.

Stand still long enough and the cold settles into the joints, the bright silence presses against the eardrums, and the only motion left is the slow, glittering respiration of the glass thicket itself: condensation beading, freezing, subliming, the occasional soft clang of a crystal adjusting its weight. Everything else has been edited out—plants, animals, tin roofs, conversation. What remains is a small, extreme room at the top of the world, dense with ancient mineral bodies, damp in its pockets, cold enough to keep its secrets, and almost perfectly bright.
