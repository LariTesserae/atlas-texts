The descent is not through rock so much as through a throat that has forgotten it was ever stone. What opens below is neither cave nor canyon but a single continuous under-chamber the width of a wide valley, roofed in living thickness and floored in the same. Light falls in ordinary daylight bands—neither noon-harsh nor twilight—yet no sun is visible; the illumination seeps from broad translucent leaves that hang like imperfect lanterns and from mineral seams that pulse with a steady, sourceless white. The air is warm and dry as a kiln left to cool overnight. There is no water here. Not a seam, not a breath of humidity. The plants drink something else.

They drink the slow bleed of the rock itself.

The vegetation is the architecture and the ground and the weather. Massive pseudo-trunks—some vertical, some cantilevered at impossible angles—split and rejoin into platforms, galleries, and hanging bridges of living tissue. Their bark is a dull jade-copper, hard as fired clay yet constantly flexing. Where they touch the raw geology they fuse with it, sending capillary roots that crack new basalt even as the basalt pushes upward in embryonic ridges and half-formed columns. The place is still being born. Fresh mineral faces appear overnight: pale, sharp-edged, not yet worn. Tendrils rush to colonize them within hours, boxing the new stone into green cauls that then harden into tomorrow’s walkways. Collapse and growth occupy the same gesture. A platform you crossed at dawn may be a vertical wall by mid-afternoon, its former underside now leafing outward into open air.

The density is nearly total. You move through layered curtains of frond, vine, and bladder-leaf that brush both skin and thought. Spaces between are rare and temporary. Where they open they are instantly claimed by fauna.

The animals are legion and they do not wait. Swarms of hard-shelled fliers the size of thumbs drill spiral galleries through the softer cambium, their wings producing a continuous metallic drone that never falls below a shout. Larger browsers—six-legged, plated, mouths like rotating rasps—move in herds across the broader spans, scraping nutrient layers and leaving raw green arteries that reseal behind them with audible wet snaps. Predators inverted from the ceiling drop on silk-thick cables of their own spun fiber, seize prey, and are themselves seized mid-descent by things with too many joints. Every surface crawls. Even the air is particulate with mites that glitter like reversed stars. Their collective noise is structural: a deafening, morphing chord of chitin-on-chitin, of ruptured plant vessels hissing resin-gas, of the deep subsonic groan when a new upthrust of stone shoulders an entire living gallery aside. Conversation is impossible without pressing mouth to ear. Thought itself arrives already half-drowned.

Scattered through this thrash are the marks of hands. Not cities—nothing so ambitious. Here and there a platform has been widened by lashed cross-braces of fallen stalk, the lashings themselves living and slowly digesting the joints they hold. Occasional lean-tos of peeled bark stand empty or occupied by figures who move with the stiff economy of people who have never known metal. Their tools are edge-shards of the same embryonic rock that keeps rising underfoot, hafted into uncured resin handles that still weep. They harvest the thicker vines for cordage, carve shallow bowls from bladder-wood to catch the oily sap that serves them as drink and lamp-fuel both. They do not stay long in any one place; the floor rearranges itself too quickly. Their paths are faint compressions in the leaf-litter that the next surge of growth will erase. Sometimes you find a small cairn of meaningless stones, or a spiral scratched into a trunk that has already begun to swell and blur the mark. These are the only built things, and they feel less like settlements than like arguments temporarily won against the green.

The strangeness is not decorative; it is metabolic. Some trunks pulse in slow peristalsis, transporting not sap but entire colonies of translucent shrimp-things that live their whole lives inside the xylem. Patches of ground flip orientation without warning—walkers find themselves suddenly descending what had been a rise—yet the plants remain rooted to whichever way is “down” in that instant, so the canopy becomes the new floor and the old floor becomes a sky of dangling roots that immediately flower. Time stutters in pockets: a swarm freezes mid-air for three long breaths then resumes as if nothing intervened. Certain leaves broadcast low memories when touched—glimpses of the surface desert far above, or of the chamber when it was smaller, hotter, emptier. The fauna sometimes pause en masse and turn the same direction, listening to a frequency no visitor can hear, before erupting again into their ferocious economy.

Everything is under construction and everything is already complete. The raw rock keeps arriving from below like a half-formed thought; the plants embroider it into workable geography before it can cool into permanence; the animals eat the embroidery and force new patterns; the sparse tool-users salvage what moments they can and leave almost no scar. Overhead the roof continues its slow respiration. Underfoot the next ridge begins to shoulder upward. The sound never drops. The warmth never breaks. And still there is no water—only the dry, resinous blood of a world that has decided moisture is an obsolete intermediate step between stone and green.
