The depression has no proper name yet. It opened within living memory—three generations at most—when the plain simply failed, stone parting along seams no one had mapped, and a broad sag of ground sank in a single season of cracks and groans. What remains is a raw-walled basin several valleys wide, its floor still uneven with the unweathered rubble of that collapse. Fresh faces of pale rock rise in steppes and broken shelves; they catch the light cleanly, unsoftened by age. Nothing here has had time to wear smooth. The geology is embryonic, still deciding its angles. Loose plates shift underfoot after rain. In places the floor bulges slightly, as if something deeper has not finished settling.

Cool air pools in the lower levels. Moisture arrives in thin, irregular ways: night condensation beading on the raw stone, a brief midday shower that finds its way down the fracture lines and runs in temporary silver threads before soaking into the rubble, pockets of standing water in hollows that smell faintly of wet clay and crushed mineral. There are no true rivers, only these intermittent dampnesses and the slow seep that keeps the deepest cuts glistening.

Into this unfinished hollow the builders have pressed themselves without leaving gaps. The settlement is town-scale and dense beyond reckoning—structures packed wall to wall, roof to foundation, so that alleys are merely the width of a turned shoulder and courtyards exist only as vertical shafts of air. They build with what the collapse provided: stacked slabs of the new stone, chipped into rough blocks with hand mauls and antler picks, mortared with clay dug from the damp pockets. Timber is scarce and precious, brought down from the rim in long, groaning ropes; most roofs are corbelled stone or densely laid brush sealed with mud. Where the natural ledges step upward, houses climb them in terraces so continuous they look like a single fretted cliff of apertures and smoke-holes. Higher still, galleries have been cut directly into the alive rock—short tunnels, storage niches, sleeping cells—so that the city and the raw geology interpenetrate. Construction never stops. The sound of striking stone is constant yet strangely quiet, muffled by the density of walls and the cool damp air; it arrives as a low, irregular ticking rather than a ringing.

Light reaches most places in the ordinary way. The basin is open to the sky. Daylight pours in, color-correct and steady, broken into long rectangular bars by the imperfect skyline of roofs. In the deeper cuttings, people whitewash the walls with powdered lime to throw the light farther. At night they burn resinous knots and shallow stone lamps; the glow stays local, absorbed quickly by the mass of masonry.

Vegetation is an afterthought. A thin green film of algae follows the seep lines. Here and there a mat of moss takes hold in a permanently damp corner, or a single tough shrub roots in a crack where dust and bird droppings have made a spoonful of soil. No one encourages more. Space is too valuable. The rare trees that lean over the rim send down only seeds that fail.

Animals are rarer still. A few rock doves nest on the highest ledges. Pale insects thread the moist crevices. Once in a long while a fox or feral dog descends the switchback paths looking for scraps, then leaves. The people keep no herds inside the basin; the packed streets allow no room, and the forage above is poor. What moves here is almost entirely human or the slow readjustment of stone.

The quiet is structural. Voices carry only a few paces before the next wall takes them. Foot traffic on the gravel paths is a soft, continuous hiss. The true activity is slower and deeper: the daily choreography of carrying water from the seep tanks, the raising of another course of wall, the careful shoring of a shelf that has begun to list, the weather itself—clouds dragging their shadows across the pale rock, the sudden cool darkening when rain begins, the return of glare afterward. Everything is still deciding how to stay. The city grows by occupying the newest instabilities, fitting itself into a landscape that has not yet finished arriving.
