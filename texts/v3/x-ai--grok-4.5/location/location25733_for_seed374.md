The basin has no horizon that resolves into sky. What passes for distance is only more of the same thickness: a warm, dim, water-heavy air that hangs without gradient, the color of old pewter and weak tea. Light does not fall so much as soak. It arrives already filtered through the unmoving lid of mist that has sealed this low place for as long as anyone here keeps memory. Shadows are soft and permanent. Edges never sharpen.

Underfoot the ground is still deciding what it wants to be. Soft mineral paste, half-consolidated silt, lenses of pure clay that slick under weight, thin crusts of pale sinter that crackle and weep when stepped on. Fresh joints open in the rock faces that rise here and there like unfinished thoughts; the fractures are bright, unsoftened, as if the stone only just split and then stopped. Nothing progresses. The land remains eternally embryonic—raw geology held in its first utterance—yet nothing collapses further either. The same imperfect ledges, the same half-risen knolls of mineral, the same shuddering mudflats persist without milestone. Water stands everywhere near the surface or sheets thinly across it, packing the pores, beading on every vertical face, dripping in slow metronomic threads from every overhang. The air itself condenses on skin and stone alike. There is no dry interval.

Across this unfinished floor a city has been pressed, dense and continuous, settlement-scale and then larger, filling the shallow valley until the valley itself becomes urban fabric. The construction is stubbornly primitive: hand-shaped blocks of the local clay, fired in low open pits until they turn a dull iron-red; upright slabs of the weeping stone set without mortar; frames of articulated bone lashed with sinew and gut; walls of packed silt rammed between woven mats of some fibrous mineral felt that the inhabitants scrape from certain vents. No timber, no green thing. Roofs are overlapping plates of baked clay or carapace, pitched just enough to shed the endless slow drip. The dwellings shoulder one another: multi-level warrens, narrow vertical shafts for light and breath, cantilevered platforms held by bone beams, storerooms sunk half underground where the water table glints. Canals serve as streets. They are never still. Muddy water moves in slow braided sheets between the foundations, carrying boats of stretched hide over wicker frames, carrying also the constant traffic of bodies.

Life here is animal and only animal, overwhelming in number. Soft late-stage amphibians throng the canals in layered schools, their pale flanks almost phosphorescent in the dimness. Clouds of winged insects with silica-hard cases wheel under every overhang, their collective drone a physical pressure. Larger scavengers—low-slung, many-legged, plated in the same mineral crusts that form on the rocks—pick through the midden heaps that accumulate in every cul-de-sac. Things like eels but jointed work the wet walls at night (though night is only a further dimming). Hairless, warm-blooded creatures the size of dogs move in packs along the upper walkways, communal and loud. The people themselves are dense among them: short, broad-footed, skin permanently sheened with the ambient wet, calling to one another in voices trained to cut through the rest of the noise. There is no vegetation for any of them to eat. The food web is closed and fierce—filter, kill, scavenge, dissolve—fed by the mineral-rich water and by the sheer biomass already present. Everything eats and is eaten in chambers and channels that never empty.

Sound is total. It has weight and temperature. The perpetual drip multiplies across ten thousand eaves and ledges into a hissing rain that never varies. Beneath it rises the wet slap of feet on silt, the hollow knock of bone tools on clay, the grinding of querns that never stop, the high insectile scream that sits just under hearing until a sudden turn of attention makes it unbearable, the croaking choruses that crest and fall but never cease, the shouted commerce of a population living elbow-to-elbow, the low abdominal roar of the geothermal vents that lace the basin and keep the air bath-warm. All of it stacks into a single deafening continuum. There is no quiet quarter. Newer arrivals clap hands over ears for the first days; then the ears themselves change, or the mind simply accepts the roar as the medium in which thought must swim.

The whole arrangement is locked. No season thins the mist. No flood finally rearranges the canals, though water is everywhere; the levels stay within a handspan of their ancient marks. Buildings slump and are rammed back into shape with the same gestures used a hundred generations earlier. The raw stone continues its arrested birth. Fauna populations crest and crash in closed loops that leave the dense saturation unchanged. Tools remain percussion and abrasion, fire and sinew. The city does not grow outward or upward in any lasting sense; it only maintains its own incredible closeness, a living mineral crust breathing and shouting in the half-light, forever wet, forever unfinished, forever exactly as loud and warm and low and crowded as this. 

You can walk for hours in any direction and the character of the place will not shift—only the particular arrangement of dripping walls, the particular pitch of the nearest animal chorus, the particular faces bent over the same eternal labors. The basin holds. It has always held.
