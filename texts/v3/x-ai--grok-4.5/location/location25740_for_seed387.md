The place is called the Sumproom, though no one carved the name—it condensed out of the noise the way mineral crust condenses out of warm brine. It is not a cavern in the ordinary sense. It is a single, roughly ovoid pocket of pressurized seawater held open inside basalt half a kilometer beneath the abyssal plain, a bubble that should have collapsed ages ago and did not. The walls are glassy black from old heat, etched with slow white filigree where silica has plated out over centuries. There is no floor, only a gentle bowl of the same stone curving away into dimness; no true ceiling either, just the same curve closing overhead. The entire volume of the Sumproom could fit inside a modest city block, yet the few structures inside it make the space feel cathedral-empty.

What stands here is industry reduced to its bones and then left running. A sparse constellation of towers, gantries, and drum-shaped engine casings hang or stand at irregular intervals, tethered to the rock by thick cables and pitted titanium roots. They are far enough apart that a swimmer (or the rare drifting body) can lose sight of one before the next resolves out of the murk. Between them the water is almost clear of solid matter—only the occasional flake of rust, a slow-turning scrap of grating, a pulse of warmer current carrying dissolved metals that stain the dark like weak tea. Density here means emptiness with punctuation.

The machines are old and still hungry. Massive axial-flow pumps the size of small houses draw water from hairline fractures in the bedrock and force it through coiled heat-exchangers, then out again in roaring ejecta. The water arrives already warm (the basalt still remembers the magma that birthed it) and leaves warmer, so the Sumproom stays bath-temperature even in its stagnant pockets. Turbines spin without pause, their housings trembling. Chains of reduction gears clack and shriek inside sealed nacelles. Somewhere a generator bank throws blue-white arcs that light the nearest twenty meters in stuttering flashes before dying back to the baseline gloom. Every surface carries the layered wear of long service: barnacle-like mineral accretions, polished witnesses where high-velocity flow has burnished steel, bolts replaced so many times the new ones sit in oval holes. The place is established in the way a reef is established—by refusal to stop and by the slow chemistry of its own effluent.

Light is residual. A few sodium vapor lamps still burn behind thick quartz ports, turning the nearest plumes of churned silt the color of weak amber. Elsewhere the only illumination comes from the machines themselves—pilot lights, status diodes the size of fists, the dull red glow of overloaded bearings. Mostly there is the color of deep water at night: a green-black that drinks shape. Sound does the work of light. The Sumproom is deafening. Not loud in the way a single waterfall is loud; loud in the way drowning is loud. Overlapping layers of white noise—jetting high-pressure brine, the blade-pass frequency of five-meter impellers, the subsonic shudder of the entire bubble micromoving in its stone bed—fill every cubic centimeter until bone conduction becomes the primary sense. Conversation, if anyone still tried it, would be gesture. Even thought arrives muffled.

Spatially the place is turbulent the way a stirred pot is turbulent. Currents reverse without warning when a pump surges or a relief valve lifts. Eddies large enough to swallow a person spin up, drift, collapse. Temporary walls of faster water form and shear apart. In the rare calm lenses between structures you can hang weightless for half a minute and watch the rest of the Sumproom thrash like machinery dreaming. Then a new front of flow arrives and you are gone sideways into the dark. The emptiness between towers is not empty of force.

Flora is absent. No algae, no weed, no fuzz of life on the stone. The chemistry is too hard, the currents too scouring, the heat too dry in its underwater way. What grows is mineral: feathers of aragonite, from-nothing spheres of opal-CT, black manganese dendrites that look like frozen lightning. On the lee sides of engine casings a pale slime of extremophile archaea sometimes sheets out, thinner than paper and almost translucent; it is not plant, barely biology, more a living stain that feeds on the hydrogen bleed from corroding steel. That is the extent of the green world.

Animals are rarer still. Once in a long while a blind eel-thing, whitish and gelatinous, pulls itself along a cable by its jaws. Sometimes small silver flickers—pressure-adapted crustaceans—appear in a pump’s outflow for three heartbeats and are gone. They do not stay. Nothing nests. Nothing claims territory. The Sumproom is used by life the way a furnace is used by moths: briefly, and usually fatally.

The mild weirdness is easy to miss until it is not. The machines do not merely run; they appear to maintain one another. A snapped cable will, over days, be re-spliced by remote manipulators no one remembers installing. Filter screens reclean themselves. When a bearing finally seizes, another identical unit somewhere deeper in the lattice spins up to take the load, and the failed one begins to disassemble under the attention of smaller repair crawlers that live in the underframe darkness. No central control room has ever been found. The city—if it is a city—feels less built than precipitated out of the need for the water to keep moving. Its purpose, if it ever had one, has been worn smooth by time; what remains is the motion itself, the deafening recirculation of warm brine through an enclosed dark, forever resetting its own fragile equilibrium.

To enter the Sumproom is to become a small soft obstacle in someone else’s engine. You cling to stanchions. You feel the pressure in your sinuses and the heat soaking through the suit. You hear nothing and everything. The black stone curves away on every side, the sparse iron towers thrum and flare, and the water—old, thrashing, industrious—continues the only work it remembers.
