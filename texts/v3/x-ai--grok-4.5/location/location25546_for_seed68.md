The place is a shallow basin no wider than a long shout, pressed into soft low country where the land has only just begun to remember it has edges. The walls of the basin are not old cliffs but fresh breaks—clean planes of pale clay and half-set conglomerate that still hold the marks of whatever brief force cracked them open. Rain has not yet rounded them. Mist clings to the sharp lips morning and evening, beading into slow runs that darken the stone without carving it. Water here is never a river and rarely a standing pool; it arrives as night-sweat on metal, as brief silver curtains that pass between the high leaves and leave everything glistening, as dark stains that creep up from the basin floor and then withdraw again. The air stays cool enough that breath stays visible a moment longer than expected.

Into this young hollow someone once set a town the size of a single large cathedral close. Not a city, not even a proper village—more a dense knot of function dropped into green. Iron frames rise like unfinished choir stalls, their rivets still bright where paint has not yet decided to stick. Brick chimneys stand in pairs and triplets, none taller than the surrounding canopy that presses close on every side. Between them run catwalks and pipe-bridges lightly freckled with moss. The structures are sparse enough that each casting shed or boiler house sits in its own halo of open ground, yet the layout is deliberate: rail spurs that end in buffers of living root, loading platforms half-swallowed by fern, a central plaza of poured slag that still remembers the molds. Everything industrial here runs at a patient tempo. Great flywheels turn so slowly you can watch each spoke darken as it enters shadow. Steam does not shriek; it sighs in long measured breaths from valves that open only when the pressure has thought about it. Belts travel with the soft slap of wet leather. The sound is loud—unignorable, a constant metallic liturgy—but it never accelerates. Change happens across shifts that last seasons.

Vegetation does not wait for permission. It spends the cool damp with extravagance. Broad-leaved trees whose names no one has needed lean over the highest gantries; their drip-tips channel every night’s condensation into perfect glass beads that shatter on corrugated roofs. Vines braid through railings with the patience of iron itself. Between the sparse buildings the ground is a deep layered green: mood moss over springy peat, then knee-high stands of something like skunk cabbage that smell clean and mineral when bruised, then sudden vertical bursts of flowering canes that push up through grating. The lushness is not jungle tangle; it is generous and spaced, leaving corridors of air and sight. Light falls normally—neither dim nor harsh—filtering through high leaves into the slow-turning geometry of belts and balustrades so that every surface carries its own soft moving shadow.

Animals treat the whole arrangement as habitat already decided. Insect clouds rise in slow columns from warm brick after rain, their wings adding a brass shimmer to the mechanical hum. Small red deer with soot-dark legs step carefully across slag paths at dusk, browsing the tender shoots that force themselves through expansion joints. Colonies of pale bats roost inside cooling towers, emerging in soft explosions when the evening temperature dips another degree. Birds of several bright, ordinary kinds nest in the crooks where pipe meets girder; their calls stitch through the clank and hiss until the two soundtracks become one continuous weather. Now and then something larger moves in the green walls of the basin—an outline that might be bear or only deeper shadow—and the town’s few human silhouettes pause on their catwalks, watching until the shape dissolves again into leaves.

There is a mild strangeness that no one remarks on because it has always been here. Certain pipes sweat a thinner water that tastes faintly of copper and crushed mint and that never freezes even when the morning chill sharpens. Ferns loot the heat from still-warm casings and grow in perfect radial clocks around them, fronds lengthening by the width of a fingernail each day. The slow machinery seems, at rare intervals, to answer the drip of condensation with a change of rhythm so slight it might be imagination. The place does not feel haunted so much as companioned—by green, by rust, by the patient animals, by the young stone freshly broken and not yet tired of its own sharp edges. Time moves the way the flywheels do: heavily, evenly, with room inside each revolution for moss to advance another fraction and for the next thin rain to arrive and gloss every surface once more.
