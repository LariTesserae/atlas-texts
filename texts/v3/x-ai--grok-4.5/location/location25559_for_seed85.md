The place is called the Spirefall Anchorage, though no one remains who might have named it. It occupies a roughly basin-shaped volume no larger than an old cathedral close, suspended in absolute black water at an altitude that would, on maps of drier ages, have been a knife-edge ridgeline. The ridgeline is still there—young basalt and glassy obsidian extruded only centuries ago, unsoftened by any weathering cycle—but the world has filled past it. Water stands everywhere, cold and heavy, a single continuous medium with no surface in sight. Pressure should have crushed the stone long ago; pressure has been persuaded otherwise.

What remains of the settlement clings to the ridge like a sparse constellation of hard points. Habitats are not buildings so much as engineered interruptions in the water: smooth, matte ovoids and truncated pyramids of a metal that refuses movements of heat or charge, spaced far enough apart that each sits alone in its own pocket of dark. Between them stretch slender, tensioned filaments—some load-bearing, some carrying data or smoothed spacetime—so thin they vanish after a few body-lengths. The whole arrangement feels provisional and absolute at once, like a thought held carefully upside-down. There is no density of streets, no press of façades. Emptiness is the larger architecture. You can travel the entire Anchorage in minutes if you know the paths; most of the volume is simply cold water and the slow, indifferent silt that never quite settles.

Light does not live here. What illumination exists is local, grudging, and temporary: faint bioluminescent markers etched into the metal at hatch seams, or the residual glow of reactionless drives when a transit pod slips along a filament. Mostly the eye finds only absence. Sound is rarer still. The water carries almost nothing; the few mechanical systems that remain around the habitats run in regimes quieter than thermal noise. Occasionally a filament frets against its mooring and produces a single pure tone that dies before it can echo. That is the loudest event the place admits.

No plant has ever grown here. The rock is too new, the chemistry too sealed, the light too absent. Even the simpler kingdoms—mats, films, drifting mineral-organic hybrids—failed to take. The substrate remains purely mineral: black glass fractured into sharp, stair-like ledges, young pillow lavas whose skins still look wet and plastic, crystalline vents that exhale slow curtains of supercooled brine. These features have not been rounded. They keep their first edges.

Fauna appears only as rumor or exception. Once every long interval a pale, ribbon-like organism—more density fluctuation than animal—drifts through the filaments, tasting the electromagnetic residue of the habitats, then shears away into the greater dark. It leaves no trail and makes no sound. Nothing hunts it. Nothing thrives.

The technology that holds the Anchorage against the water is not merely advanced; it is post-descriptive. Certain bulkheads are thinner than probability should allow yet sustain one-atmosphere differentials without measurable stress. Interior volumes sometimes share the same geometric coordinates while remaining distinct rooms; visitors learn not to map them. Power arrives without cables or cores, simply present, as if the local vacuum energy had been polite enough to organize itself. Time here is under the same courtesy. Clocks advance, but gradients do not. No sediment accumulates differently on one side of a structure than another. No thermal plume rises. The cold remains evenly cold. The dark remains evenly dark. The sparse geometry of the place has reached a settled equation with the ocean and does not care to recalculate.

Move along the outer ridge and the strangeness concentrates. Here the basalt drops away in artificial steps that pretends to be natural; each step is exactly the height of a resting body. At the lowest terrace a single open arch leads into an unpressurized gallery flooded to the ceiling. The gallery’s far wall is missing. Beyond it the true depth begins—an unstarting descent into water that has never seen a photon. If you float at the threshold long enough, the stillness starts to feel like a held breath that will never need to be released. The Anchorage does not change. It simply continues, small and high and drowned, an urban thumbprint left on a mountain that the sea decided to keep.
