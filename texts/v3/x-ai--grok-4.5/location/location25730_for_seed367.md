The plateau has been wearing itself smooth for longer than anyone bothers to count. What once were mountains are now blunt knuckles of stone, shoulders rounded by wind that has had all the time in the world. From a distance the high country looks empty—salt-pale mineral crust, grit, the occasional low scrub blackened by sun. Heat pools up here in the thin air and sits. Nothing glints. No rivers. The old watercourses are only ghosts in the rock, shallow troughs filled with dust so fine it behaves like a dry liquid when the wind lifts it.

You find the place by nearly missing it.

A crease, no wider than a lane at the rim, drops between two worn ribs of basalt. The walls are not sheer so much as exhausted—softened, undercut, stained with mineral tears that never quite became springs. Ten body-lengths down, the light changes. The open sky narrows to a bright slit. Heat remains, but it turns close and humid in a way the plateau never is. Condensation beads on the cooler inner faces at the dim hours; every few days a fog-tongue slides in from some higher, colder fracture system and hangs like breath inside the pocket. That is the entire water budget. It is enough.

Below the rim the crease widens into a rough oval no larger than a market square, then steps down again through terraces of slumped stone and root. Vegetation takes the moisture and multiplies it into mass. Succulent trunks the color of old copper climb over one another, their skins waxy, ribbed, holding every swallowed bead of fog. Between them hang curtains of gray-green lace—epiphytes that never touch soil, only air and the damp exhalation of neighbors. Broad, low-leaved plants carpet the ledges where grit has gathered into something like dirt; their surfaces are pebbled and thick, designed to drink without wasting. In the deepest corner a stand of pale, jointed stems rises higher than the rest, each segment a cistern. When something knocks them, they boom softly, half-full. The green is not the bright greed of a wet forest. It is a dense, working green, layered until the air itself feels chlorophyll-thick. Light arrives already spent, filtered through successive roofs of leaf and wax until noon is only a warmer gray and true shadow is hard to tell from ordinary dim.

Through this green the settlement is worked like environment rather than imposed upon it. No one here has metal beyond the occasional trade scrap of hammered copper used for needles or cutting edges. Everything else is hand and stone and fiber. Dwellings are stacked dry-wall niches reinforced with living trunks trained over generations into load-bearing arches. Platforms of split stem and packed mineral sit at every useful height, connected by ladders of twisted vine and by polished footholds worn into the rock by bare traffic. Certain walls are built entirely of empty seed-husks and resin, light and strong, patched continuously. Smoke from small resin fires finds the few vertical chimneys of air and threads upward in thin, aromatic ropes. The smell is sweet-acrid, mixed with crushed leaf and the mineral cool of wet stone.

Density is the law. There is nowhere that is only one thing. A sleeping ledge is also a drying rack for fibers. A path is also a root-bridge that must be stepped on carefully so the water-holding cortex is not bruised. People move in single file out of habit even when the way is wider, bodies angled to let others pass, hands ready to steady a neighbor or a load. Children learn early to read the give of a platform by foot alone. Above and between the human traffic the other lives press closer still.

Fauna is not background. It is the louder half of the place. Clouds of small hard-winged flies turn in every shaft of residual light, their collective drone a constant mid-frequency that sinks into bone if you stay long enough. Soft-bodied climbers the size of thumbs web the jointed stems; their many-eyed faces track movement with slow precision. Something like a moth but heavier, furred in dust-colored scales, roosts in the thousands on the underside of the broad leaves and takes off in soft explosions when a ladder is jostled. Higher, lean six-limbed runners flicker along the upper trunks, stolen fruit in their mouths, chattering in clicks that ricochet off stone. At the moist base, where grit has become true mud for a hand-span of depth, pale amphibia the length of forearms dig and sing—low, pulsing notes that rise in the dim hours until the whole pocket seems to breathe with them. Predators are small and continuous rather than dramatic: a transparent ambush thing that waits in water-cups; a snake thin as cord that tastes the air from resin crevices; flocks of darting insectivores that comb the epiphyte curtains in orderly ranks, their wingbeats adding a dry rustle to the larger noise. Nothing is rare. Everything is near. Sleep happens in netted alcoves for a reason.

Sound never drops to quiet. It only changes register. Day-roar of flies and the knock of tools on stone. Voices calling flex-routes up the terraces—“Left root live—shift right!”—the slap of wet fiber being beaten clean, the boom of the cistern stems when children play too hard. Evening brings the amphibia’s pulse and the heavier moths’ wing-soft thunder as they rotate roosts. Fog nights mute the higher sounds and amplify the close ones: drip, suck of feet in the thin mud, someone coughing resin smoke, the tiny wet click of a hundred climbers adjusting grip. The pocket is loud the way a body is loud from inside—inescapable, familiar, structured.

Processes run on top of one another without schedule. Fog arrives or does not. When it does, every surface that can open opens; people move along the walls with carved scoops and absorbent mats, racing the plants themselves. A resin fire gets out of its ring and is smothered by the same bodies that use it daily—no panic, only practiced weight and damp leaf. Two of the six-limbed runners quarrel over a cache and tumble through three levels of walkway, scattering tools and laughter and curses in equal measure. An old platform finally sags; by next dim it has been braced with new living wood already encouraged to graft. Mating swarms lift in the warmest hour and turn the air into a single vibrating mass until predators and wind thin them. Nothing here is monument and nothing is finished. The erosion that flattened the world above has, inside this crease, simply changed scale: everything is older than its shape suggests, worn into the most stable temporary arrangement that still allows growth. The stone sinuses have found their final comfortable depth. The plants have refined every trick for stealing breath-water. The people have learned the exact thickness of wall that lasts one generation and the exact looseness of weave that lets moths through without letting larger thieves in. The fauna has filled every remaining interval.

Stand at the bottom in the permanent half-light and look up. The slit of sky is the color of heated iron at the edges, dimming fast. Terraces rise in broken rings, green-black and rust and the pale scars of fresh-cut stem. Figures move along them with loads, small against the stacked life. Wings cut the air in continuous soft information. Somewhere a cistern stem is struck, deliberately this time—a slow signaling boom that means fog has been sighted at the upper fracture. The pocket answers with a shift in its many voices, a leaning of attention, a thousand small preparations. Warmth stays trapped. Moisture is already beading. The ancient high desert continues, indifferent and nearly flat, a few body-lengths overhead. Down here the world is still deciding, loudly, what to do with the little it is given.
