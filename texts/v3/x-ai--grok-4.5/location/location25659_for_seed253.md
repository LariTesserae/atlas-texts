The place is called, by nothing that lives there, the Quiet Columns—though quiet is a lie the water tells only to those who never listen long enough.

You are far below any surface that remembers sunlight. The water is the world: not a sea with a floor and a roof, but a continuous cool medium that has forgotten the difference between depth and height. What registers as extreme elevation is vertical reach itself—thousand-fathom rises and drops that would be mountain ranges if air had ever touched them. Everything that stands does so inside the liquid dark.

The architecture is plant. Not decorated with plant, not overgrown: grown. Long ago—so long that the growing has finished and will not resume—some patient intelligence or patient accident trained vast marine flora into load-bearing forms. Trunks the girth of towers rise through the gloom in measured ranks, their wood denser than stone, their bark plated in living laminates that still photosynthesize the faintest bioluminescent spill from the fauna. Between the trunks stretch vaulted membranes of translucent leaf-matter, stiffened into ceilings and galleries by calcified veins. Stair-spirals of coiled kelp climb the columns; platforms of interwoven root-mats form floors that flex almost imperceptibly under the slow push of deep currents. There is no substrate that is not living tissue or the mineral ghosts of older tissue. The entire region is a single constructed organism that reached its final shape ages past and simply stopped.

Because it stopped, the density is sparse. The columns stand hundreds of body-lengths apart. Between them lies open black water—clear enough, if light existed, to see for great distances, yet almost empty of structure. The emptiness is deliberate or inevitable; either way it has settled into permanence. You can travel a long while and meet only the next solitary pillar, its surface etched with the slow calligraphy of ancient flow, its crown lost overhead in darkness that has no ceiling.

Light is nearly absent. What little exists is made by the animals. They are abundant and loud. Schools of rough-skinned fish the size of compasses move in slow thunder, their flanks stippled with cold blue photophores that flare and gutter like storms seen from beneath cloud. Larger things—serpentine, plated, older than the columns—patrol the avenues of open water; their calls travel as pressure more than sound, deep enough to vibrate the living wood and set whole galleries humming. Filter-webs of translucent gelatin pulse between the pillars, each pulse a soft concussion. Shell-crabs the color of wet slate click and rasp as they graze the algal films that still cling to the architecture. The noise is continuous, layered, never frantic. It is the noise of a system that has no more history to make.

The temperature is a steady cool that has not varied in uncounted seasons. Currents exist, but they are the same currents that shaped the final erosion of every surface: rounded edges, softened corners, columns whose bases have been undercut into elegant flaring pedestals and then left exactly so. Nothing is still growing into a new form; nothing is decaying out of an old one. The place has reached the equilibrium that looks like end-of-life and also like immortality. Time passes the way water passes—through, without purchase.

Unusual notes remain, quiet enough not to fracture the logic. Here and there a column is hollow and inhabited by a vertical river of silver eels that circle endlessly inside it, their bodies completing a loop that has no entrance or exit. Certain platforms bear circular apertures perfectly smooth, through which warmer water sometimes sighs upward from vents that no longer map to any geology; the warmth disperses at once into the cool mass and is gone. On rare walls the woody grain has arranged itself into near-regular geometries—hexagons, nested arcs—as if the ancient growers once toyed with pattern and then abandoned the impulse.

There are no tools, no metals, no cut marks that were not made by tooth or slow chemistry. Movement is swimming or drifting or the patient crawl of things adapted to vertical bark. The fauna do not build; they only occupy. The plant-city does not repair itself because nothing breaks it. Sound rolls through the dark water and is answered by further sound. The columns stand. The spaces between them remain. The cool dark holds.

This is the lasting state: an open, flooded forest of finished architecture, sparsely placed across a vertical wilderness, alive with bodies and noise, empty of change, older than memory, and content never to become anything else.
