The plateau does not yet know it is finished. It rose within living memory of the stone itself—within the slow grammar of basalt cooler than the air and still exhaling. At this height the sky is a hard, close lid of ordinary daylight, neither merciful nor dramatic, simply present. Heat collects under that lid the way water once collected in low places: thickly, without apology. The air tastes of iron filings and distant sulfur, damp only in the seams.

There is no green. There has never been green here. What grows is mineral and mechanical: stacks of foundry brick the color of dried blood, lattices of oxidized gantry, sheets of cooling slag poured into forms that look almost accidental. The city occupies the plateau the way crystals occupy a geode—crowding every face, every overhang, every newly split crevasse. Buildings lean against one another for structural honesty rather than affection. Walkways of riveted plate zigzag between smelter halls whose walls still radiate yesterday’s pours. In the narrow vertical spaces between them, condensation gathers each dawn and each artificial dusk, thin films that never quite become rain before the heat reclaims them. These damp breaths darken the metal for an hour, then vanish, leaving faint white ghosts of mineral.

The factories run, but they do not hurry. Massive flywheels turn with the patience of tectonic plates. Conveyors advance one plate, pause, advance again. The sound is not silence—there is always a low, continuous pressure against the ear, like blood in a fever—but it is quiet in the way deep water is quiet. No klaxons. No shouted shifts. The engines speak in infrared and in the almost-inaudible fretting of expansion joints. Workers, when they appear, move with the same gradualism: figures in heat-suits the color of the stone, checking gauges that change by fractions of a degree across watches measured in days.

Elevation here is not scenic. It is structural. The plateau drops away on every side into raw scarps that have not yet decided their angles. Fresh faces of rock shine where the land is still tearing free of whatever basement it rose from. At the edges of the city, scaffolding of a stranger order begins: towers that are half construction crane and half geological accident, bolted into basalt that is still warm to the touch. Some of these towers simply end in mid-air, waiting for the next slow surge of uplift to give them purpose. Others have already been claimed by the dense packing of habitation—stacked living cubes, observation galleries, pump houses that draw the rare subterranean seeps and flash them into steam for the turbines.

Water is a visiting ghost. It arrives as condensation on the underside of cooling ducts, as brief nocturnal fogs that roll up from the unformed valleys below, as the sweat that beads on any surface colder than the air. Collections of it are guarded: cisterns cut into the rock, sealed, watched. A thin black canal, more pipe than river, threads the lower terraces, carrying used process water that is already half vapor. Where it leaks, the stone steams and the air briefly becomes breathable in a different register—wet metal, hot copper, the ghost of something almost organic that never quite was.

Fauna is rumor and rare flash. Something small and many-legged sometimes crosses a gantry at the edge of vision, its body the same dull oxide as the rails. Larger things are known only by the holes they leave: smooth, glass-lined tubes bored through cooling slag overnight, as if heat itself had decided to migrate. The workers leave offerings of cooled metal filings at certain junctions; whether this is propitiation or merely habit is unclear. The city itself seems to regard these creatures as another slow industrial process—neither pest nor partner, simply present at a density too low to schedule.

The strangeness is not decorative. It is load-bearing. In the oldest core of the plateau, where the first foundries were poured directly onto still-plastic rock, the geometry has begun to argue with itself. Corridors lengthen between shifts. Staircases reverse their handedness when unobserved. Heat gradients run backward for hours at a time, so that the hottest chambers sit higher than the cool ones, defying the simplest convection. No one discusses this. The gauges are watched, the flywheels tend their patient arcs, and the city continues to densify—new levels collapsing inward onto old ones, new scaffolds biting into the next raw scarp the plateau offers up. Everything is under construction and everything is already ancient in the only timescale that matters here: the timescale of metal learning the habits of stone, and stone learning the habits of engines.

At the outermost edges, where the plateau’s skin is thinnest and newest, the wind arrives empty of spores or seeds. It carries only heat and the faint permanent hum of the works. Below, unformed distances wait. Above, ordinary light persists. Between them the city endures its own slow becoming—damp in the joints, scorched at the heart, quiet as a held breath that will not be released for centuries.
