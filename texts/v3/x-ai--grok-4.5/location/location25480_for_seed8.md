The plain has forgotten how to rise. Whatever mountains or ridges once stood here have been planed away so completely that the horizon is a single unbroken thread, a line so level it seems drawn rather than grown. What remains is a vast, low-lying sheet of country—hundreds of square miles of it—settled into its final geometry: a near-featureless expanse of dark, mineral-rich mud and pale, water-smoothed stone, barely higher than the sea that once claimed it and will claim it again, slowly, without drama.

Water is everywhere and nowhere decisive. It does not run in rivers so much as it lives in the ground. The water table rests inches beneath the surface; press a heel and a dark coin of moisture wells up, cold at first then quickly matching the air’s steady warmth. In the shallowest depressions permanent sheets of open water lie like discarded mirrors, each edged with a thin crust of dried algae that crackles under a careful step. Between these lagoons the ground weeps. Seepage lines crawl down every slight incline that erosion has left, leaving trails of rust-colored mineral and films of iridescent bacteria. After the frequent, soft rains—warm rains that fall more like a thickening of the air than a storm—the entire plain becomes one continuous, shallow wetland, water pathless and sky-reflecting. Mist lifts in slow curtains at dawn and dusk, condensing on every cool surface until the few scattered stones shine as if lacquered.

Vegetation has long since surrendered ambition. What grows here does so sparingly and close to the ground: mats of salt-tolerant moss the color of old bronze; thin stands of reed no taller than a shin, spaced so that each clump seems an afterthought; low, leathery succulents whose thick leaves store the warmth of the day and release it at night in faint vegetal breath. There are no trees. There have been none for geological ages. The roots that do exist are shallow, tentative, more like negotiations with the saturated soil than claims upon it. In places the ground is bare altogether—slick black mud or expanses of fine, pale grit that wind and water have sorted into subtle ripples, the last patient handwriting of erosion.

Yet the place is loud with life. The sparseness of plants has not discouraged animals; it has simply made their presence more audible. Clouds of small, metallic flies rise and fall in dense, shifting columns, their collective wings a constant dry sizzle. Frogs—some the size of thumbnails, others heavy as fists—call from every wet pocket, their voices overlapping into a throbbing, metallic chorus that never fully stops, only modulates with the slow drift of temperature and light. Wading birds stalk the shallows on absurdly long legs, their bills darting; when they take flight in sudden numbers the sound is like heavy cloth torn slowly. Crustaceans click and rasp under half-submerged stones. At night the chorus deepens with the addition of larger, slower voices—amphibians that surface only after dark, their calls rolling across the open distances like distant drums. There is no predator large enough to silence any of this permanently; the ecology has settled into a long, noisy equilibrium of abundance without density, of lives spaced widely across the flat wet world yet filling every acoustic niche.

Light here is ordinary and mercilessly clear. The sky is broad, often hazed with warm moisture but rarely dramatic. Day arrives without ceremony and leaves the same way. Shadows, when they exist, are short and soft; there is little vertical relief to cast them. Heat builds patiently through the afternoon until the air above the wetter ground shimmers, turning the far horizon unstable, as if the plain itself might be breathing.

Everything changes, but only on the scale of patience. Seasons are gentle gradients rather than events. Water levels rise and fall by inches across months. Sediment settles grain by grain, slowly sealing older mud under newer. Stones that once formed part of some vanished upland now lie half-buried, their surfaces dimpled and furrowed by millennia of the same soft attrition, still being reduced, still becoming flatter, smaller, more finally themselves. There is no ruin and no monument because there was never any achievement of height or permanence to mourn. This is the landscape after the last argument between rock and weather has been settled in weather’s favor: a warm, wet, ancient floor kept saturated and alive by the sky, thronged with voices, empty of haste, stretching outward in every direction until the eye gives up.

Stand long enough and the loudness itself becomes a kind of silence—the steady, overlapping insistence of small lives continuing inside a place that has already finished becoming what it is.
