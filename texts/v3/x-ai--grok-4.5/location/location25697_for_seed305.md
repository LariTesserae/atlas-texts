The high country does not end. It simply continues, a single eroding plate of young stone lifted so recently that its fractures still hold the memory of the shove that raised them. Ridges stand knife-edged and unfinished, their flanks glittered with fresh scarps and hanging scree that has not yet learned to rest. Between the blades of rock stretch plateaus wide enough to breed their own weather: pale, slow-rolling fogs that condense out of nothing, drift a few kilometers, and rain themselves back into the moss in thin, intermittent veils. The air is always damp in the way a cold engine housing is damp—beads of meltwater and breath collecting on every surface, never quite enough to make a river, yet enough to keep the world glistening.

Light arrives without mercy. It is high-altitude light, unfiltered, almost metallic, ricocheting off quartz faces and the pale undersides of leaves until the entire vastness feels overexposed, as if the sky itself has been turned up past any reasonable setting. Shadows are short and absolute. In that glare the vegetation thrives with obstinate lushness: not jungle, not tundra, but a packed, cold emerald dense enough to erase the ground. Mosses thick as pelts smother every ledge. Soft conifers no taller than a person grow in continuous carpets, their needles blue-black and fat with antifreeze resins. Between them rise stands of translucent, tubular plants that look like organ pipes or cooling towers; they exhale slow plumes of spore-fog and a low industrial hum, as though photosynthesis here has learned the discipline of a factory floor. Everything touches everything else. There is no understory, no clearing, no interval—only saturation.

The animals have claimed the rest of the space. They are not visitors; they are the medium in which the highlands exist. Herds of long-legged, wool-plated browsers move in continent-spanning sheets, so numerous that their collective breath adds a second weather system of soft frost. Their gait is deliberate, almost geological; a single migration front may take years to cross a plateau. Above and between them wheel flocks of rigid-winged gliders whose hollow bones resonate like turbine housings when the wind passes through. Smaller forms carpet the moss in clicking, whirring layers—armored grazers the size of fists that chew rock as readily as leaf, excreting fine metallic grit that the tubular plants then draw up as micronutrients. The sound never drops. It is a permanent low roar of millions of mouths, wing-cases, resonating throats, and the occasional deep bellow from something larger moving out of sight inside a cloud. The noise has texture: part abattoir, part foundry, part distant surf made of living bodies.

Nothing here was built. No walls, no roads, no tools left lying. Yet the processes feel industrial in scale and patience. Whole slopes function as slow refineries. The browsers’ dung fields heat and reduce under bacterial mats, releasing warm vents of methane that the tubular plants flare off in soft, continuous blue pilot-lights. Screes sort themselves into graded ore piles through nothing more than the repeated passage of clawed feet. Even the light seems harnessed: at certain hours the quartz veins catch the glare and throw it in focused blades across the plateaus, and wherever those blades linger the vegetation grows denser, darker, more loudly photosynthetic, as if the landscape itself were aligning solar concentrators.

Change happens, but only the way continents change. A ridge may slump a meter in a century. A new species of glider may eclipse an old one across ten thousand generations. The fogs thicken or thin according to cycles too long for any single creature to notice. The overwhelming life does not frantic; it simply occupies every available gram of snow, mist, sharp stone, and cold sunlight, locking the high country into a bright, roaring, damp stasis that feels less like an ecosystem and more like a single vast engine left idling at altitude, its parts all flesh and leaf and raw mineral, waiting out the slow cooling of whatever force first shoved these mountains into the sky.
