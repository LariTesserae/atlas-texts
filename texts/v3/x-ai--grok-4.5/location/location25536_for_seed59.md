The place is a bowl of green held in a high saddle of stone, no larger than a cathedral nave and its cloisters, rimmed by low scarps that weep. Cool mist hangs in permanent layers; it does not so much fall as condense on every surface and then drip, so the air itself is always faintly wet and the ground never dries. What ground there is has long ago become root and sponge. The saddle has been green for so long that rock only appears as occasional dark knuckles or as the wet black of an upthrust rib where erosion has settled into its final patient shape. Everything else is living mass.

Walls are not built so much as trained and thickened. Thick ropes of aerial root have been twisted, over generations of hand-work, into load-bearing columns and interlaced screens. Living buttresses lean against one another, their bark scarred by old pruning cuts and by the constant rub of traffic. Platforms of densely wattled moss and compressed leaf-mould step upward and inward, forming tiers that feel more like the galleries of a hollow tree than like floors. Narrow channels—half ditch, half gut—run everywhere, carrying the perpetual seepage; they rattle and chuckle even when no one is near. Where wood denser than the living stems was needed, it was cut with stone and shell adzes and lashed with vine, then left to be colonized until the difference between timber and host vanished. The result is denser than any ordinary grove and more continuous than any ordinary hamlet: a single small urban organism of plant architecture, cool to the touch, always damp, smelling of crushed chlorophyll and cold mineral water.

Light arrives as ordinary daylight cooled and greened by the canopy shells overhead. It shifts constantly because the canopy itself is never still. Leaves the size of shields and spears of grass thicker than a wrist rearrange whenever wind fingers the saddle. Water beads flash and vanish. Shadows of things that fly cross the platforms in continuous stutter.

The air is loud. Not with one voice but with the stacked roar of many small engines of life. Insects whose wing-cases are the stained glass of the place click and drone in layered volumes. Amphibians the color of wet slate pipe from every cupped leaf and every overflowing channel. Birds the size of fists and the size of forearms scrabble through the vertical thatch, their calls overlapping into a single turbulent glitter of sound. Larger shapes move too—climbing things with grasping feet, low-slung browsers that shoulder through the lower galleries leaving dark wet trails, sudden gulps of striders that take whole platforms as highways. There is nowhere to stand that is more than an arm’s length from something alive and discontinuous. The animal mass does not merely inhabit the plant mass; it abrades it, pollinates it, buries itself in it, dies into it, and is replaced so quickly the turnover itself becomes a kind of weather.

That weather is restless. Nothing here holds a finished shape for long. A screen of living lattice that served as a wind-break yesterday may be browsed open by morning; new shoots are already weaving the gap closed by afternoon. Platforms slump when heavy rains magically thicken the mist into brief vertical rivers, then are jacked back into usefulness by the slow hydraulic lift of fresher roots. Paths that were clear become tunnels overnight and must be recut with hand tools—the same polished stone blades and fire-hardened digging sticks that have always been used here. People move through the density the way fish move through a reed bed: turning sideways, ducking under dripping arches, calling warnings that are half-heard under the general din. Their settlements are simply denser thickenings of the same continuous body—sleeping hollows lined with dry (or dryish) moss, food niches where certain fungi and seed-heads are encouraged, tool caches wedged into forks that will soon grow over them. Construction and demolition are the same patient, manual, never-finished conversation with the living bulk.

Because the scale is small, the turbulence stays intimate. You can hear individual water-drops detonate on broad leaves even while the chorus of frogs and insects tries to drown them. You can watch a single buttress root slowly shoulder a stone out of its bed over the course of a day, the stone inching, the water brightening in the new gap. At the same time the whole bowl feels like it is breathing too fast—canopy rising and falling, animal routes rewriting themselves, channels silting and then being kicked clear again by hurried feet or by the sudden slide of a loosened mat of vegetation. The place has existed long enough to have worn its own habits into permanence, yet those habits are habits of constant minor catastrophe and constant green repair.

Stand near the center, where the mist is thinnest and the light is only ordinary, and the sensation is of being inside a single cool, wet, deafening organism that happens to contain streets, and of those streets being walked by more creatures than the plant-structure can quite hold, so that every surface is in use, every gap contested, every drop of water claimed before it finishes falling. The saddle does not enlarge. It simply remains this loud, saturated, forever-half-finished room of root and rain and restless life.
