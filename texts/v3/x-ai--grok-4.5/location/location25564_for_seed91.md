The place lies well below the surface, in a network of chambers and connecting voids that open out to the scale of a broad valley system. The rock is young—sharp-edged, freshly fractured, still shedding thin plates where the cold has worked at the seams. Nothing here has been rounded by long weather. Ceilings rise in abrupt angles; floors drop through clean breaks into lower galleries. The air holds a steady chill that never lifts, the kind that settles into stone and stays.

Moisture is present only as dampness: a continuous film of condensation on every surface, slow beads that gather and fall at long intervals, shallow seeps that darken the rock without ever becoming streams. In the lowest folds, small pockets of standing water collect—still, black mirrors that give back the dimmest reflections. The damp feeds everything that grows.

Vegetation has claimed the substrate itself. What would be floor and lower wall is a living mass of pale, cold-adapted growth—dense mats of moss and liverwort fused into continuous carpets, shot through with the woody cables of root and rhizome. From these mats rise the larger forms: columnar trunks the color of old ice, branching into flat, overlapping plates of translucent tissue that catch what little light there is. In places the growth thickens into solid architecture—arches of fused stem, hanging curtains of root that divide one chamber from another, entire platforms of compressed plant matter strong enough to walk on. Yet the distribution is sparse. Between these living masses stretch wide empties of bare, glistening rock. The plant world does not carpet every inch; it occupies, dominates, then leaves great intervals of silence and stone. You can stand in an open gallery and see three or four separate plant islands at once, each a self-contained thicket, each separated by cold bare ground.

Light is dim and local. A few industrial fixtures still burn—cage lamps bolted to rock or to the iron skeletons of the scattered structures, casting cones of weak yellow that never reach the higher vaults. Elsewhere the only illumination is the faint internal glow of the plant plates themselves, a residual phosphorescence that does little more than mark their edges. Shadows are long and soft-edged.

The sound is total. It comes from the machines. At irregular intervals across the network stand the remnants and still-working fragments of an industrial presence: low blocky buildings of riveted plate and poured concrete, half-swallowed by the encroaching mats, their roofs patched with living tissue. Inside and beside them, engines continue to run. Great reciprocating pistons, turbine housings the size of houses, generator halls whose cooling fans never stop. The noise is continuous and deafening—metallic thunder that ricochets off the young rock faces, fills every chamber, and travels the connecting voids until it becomes a single pressure against the ears. There is no quiet corner. Even in the furthest plant thickets the deep industrial roar arrives as a physical vibration in the stems. The machines appear to serve no present purpose; conveyor frames stand empty, hoppers hold only dust and leaf litter, yet the engines cycle on with the same unvarying rhythm they have kept for a very long time.

Change here is slow. The drips lengthen and fall on their own patient schedule. The plant masses advance by fractions of a season, sending exploratory roots across bare rock that may take years to anchor. The engines neither accelerate nor fail; they simply continue. Occasional pale fauna move through the system—thin, leggy things that freeze when the sound pulses louder, or small eyeless swimmers in the water pockets—but they are rare enough that hours can pass without a sighting. The whole subterranean region feels held in a long, cold equilibrium: damp, roaring, half-consumed by its own vegetation, and in no hurry to become anything else.
