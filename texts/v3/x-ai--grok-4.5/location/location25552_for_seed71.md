The high plain has forgotten how to end. It rolls in every direction without horizon-break or shore, a single worn table of stone so old the mountains that once stood on it have been ground down to stubborn knuckles and long, diluted ridges. Elevation is everywhere and nowhere; the air stays thin and sharp, the cold a constant low pressure behind the eyes, yet the ground itself never quite climbs or falls enough to offer a true vantage. Weather makes its own distant kingdoms out across the distance: pale curtains of rain that never quite arrive, sheets of granular ice that rattle for an hour and then lift, banks of fog that walk on slender legs of their own condensation.

Moisture is intermittent and sly. It beads on every surface at dusk, gathers in shallow stone basins that look carved by nothing more patient than time, then vanishes into the next hard blue morning. Where it lingers longer—along fault-lines that no longer move, inside the lee of those last low ridges—vegetation answers with a lushness that feels almost indecent. Thick, low mats of blue-black fronds, translucent stalks that hold leftover rain like blown glass, slow-spilling lichen that glows faint green under its own frost. The plants do not compete so much as wait. They occupy the damp pockets and leave the long dry intervals empty, so the plain remains sparse: great reaches of bare, wind-polished stone interrupted by sudden gardens that look dropped from another age.

There are cities. Not many, and never close enough that one can see the next. Each sits in its own shallow depression as if the plain once tried to cup them for safekeeping. The buildings are stacked fieldstone and heavy timber, roofs weighted with more of the same blue-black fronds, streets laid in fitted blocks whose joints have been sealed and resealed by hand over centuries. Doorways are low. Windows are slits. Smoke from peat and slow resin rises thin and white, then is pinched out by the cold before it can make a proper sky. The tech is all lever, wedge, adze, and fire. Rope is twisted plant fiber. Metal, when it appears, is soft and hammered into blades that dull in a week. Everything is repairable and everything is being repaired, constantly, by the same quiet hands.

No animals move here. No birds cut the air. No insects tick in the fronds. The quiet is nearly total—wind across stone, the faint drip of melt into a basin, the occasional wooden groan of a roof adjusting to cold. Yet the place is not still. Weather systems the size of nations grind past one another. The frond-mats bloom, seed, blacken, and regrow in long, visible cycles. Stone sheds microscopic dust that the rare rains rearrange into new patterns. And underneath all of it, something older than erosion continues to work.

Reality does not hold evenly. Distances breathe. A street that took three hundred paces yesterday may take thirty today, or three thousand; the stones remain the same stones. Looking too long at the horizon can bring the next city sliding into view like a ship through fog, only for it to thin and camera-fade when you blink. Corners of buildings sometimes forget they are solid and become the same blue-black translucence as the plants, briefly, before remembering themselves. Sound, already scarce, can pool: a single dropped chisel will ring for minutes in one plaza and die instantly in the next. At night the normal light of stars arrives fractured—some constellations complete, some missing limbs, some present in duplicate a few degrees off. People speak of this the way they speak of weather. They board windows on nights when the math of the sky feels wrong. They mark safe paths with stacks of pale stones that occasionally rearrange themselves into spirals overnight.

The plain keeps its final form the way an old animal keeps lying down in the same hollow. Everything that can be worn away has been. What remains is settled, sparse, cold, and quietly busy with its own long processes—growth, weather, the slow green seizure of damp, the quieter seizure of geometry by volumes that no longer agree. The cities endure because their people keep fitting stone to stone with hand tools and patience, even as the distances between the stones begin, just slightly, to argue.
