The power that turned its attention toward the plateau had neither name nor edge. It was older than uplift, quieter than the settling of megaliths, and it moved the way cold honey remembers it once was flower. Benevolence in it was not softness but accuracy: the will that every thing continue according to the deepest pattern it already carries. Wisdom was simply the refusal to hurry.

It regarded the place for a long time—longer than the lean of any menhir, longer than the polishing of any threshold. It saw the cities that were the stone continuing by other means. It saw the thick-bodied people whose fat was architecture, whose garments were mobile outcrops, whose dead had already become load-bearing. It saw the pale canids holding civil office in doorways, the frost-colored birds stitching the thermals, the lichens mapping slow metallics across every face. It tasted the air that was crushed mineral and seal-oil and the shared breath of herds. It listened to the textured quiet and found no error in it.

There was nothing broken that required mending. There was nothing incomplete that required finishing. The plateau and its beings had already achieved a density of belonging that most worlds only gesture toward in dreams. To add warmth would have been to erase the morality of sparseness. To add metal would have been to insult the patience of flint and sinew. To speak outright would have been to make noise where silence had become a form of citizenship.

So the power did the only thing that felt true.

It entered the rock.

Not as invader or overseer. As additional memory. It settled into the basalt and the shell-limestone and the fault scarps the way a final course of stone settles onto a wall that three generations have raised. It became the almost-subsonic groan that sometimes passes through a terrace at the coldest hour before dawn—the sound of the city acknowledging its own weight. It became the precise blue of the shadows thrown by hammer-dressing marks when the low sun rakes across the lithosphere. It became the faint mineral sweetness that occasionally rises from a newly opened cistern, a sweetness that has no source in ordinary water.

The people felt it the way they felt everything: slowly, and without needing to name it.

A mason pausing with her hammerstone against a new face would sometimes sense that the rock was listening back with unusual attentiveness, as though it had acquired an extra century of patience overnight. She would place the next strike with even greater care, and the wall would rise a fraction truer. Children sitting inside the radius of a knapper’s work would hear, beneath the rhythm of strikes, a deeper rhythm that matched the turning of the plateau itself; years later, when they shaped their own boulder into a step, the step would seem to remember bare feet that had not yet been born. In the byre levels, when bodies leaned together under shared hides and the low geologic chord rose from several throats, the chord now carried a harmonic so profound it made the shaggy bovids lift their heads and the pale canids open one eye in calm recognition. The dead in their sealed niches grew a shade less distinguishable from the living architecture, which was already their wish.

The power also touched the edges of the plateau—the fretted ranges, the still-angular talus, the blue ghosts of the receding glaciers. It did not stop the ice from melting; that was a conversation the world was having with itself. But it slowed the conversation by the width of a hair, so that the ceremonial rains continued to arrive in their brief silver hours, and the cushion plants kept their green fists, and the traders from the lower escarpments still found the climb severe enough to keep the plateau mostly to itself. Isolation was not defense. It was simply the shape that density preferred.

Once every few generations—counted by the creep of ice across a sacred pavement—the power allowed a single clear gift to surface. A vein of native copper would reveal itself in a newly fallen scarp, soft enough to cold-hammer yet somehow reluctant to dull. The ceremonial edges made from it would cut umbilical cords with unusual gentleness and mark final capstones with a brightness that lingered in memory like raking light. Or a new lichen would take hold in a high cirque, its metallics shading toward a deep violet that, when leached and mixed into the sour paste, eased the grinding pain that sometimes settled into the joints of the very old. These gifts arrived without announcement and were accepted without ceremony, the way meltwater is accepted: as one more way the plateau remained awake.

Mostly, though, the power simply stayed.

It stayed in the polished thresholds and the unglazed slits. It stayed in the spiral ramps and the single-slab bridges. It stayed in the breath-bloom that briefly revealed a motionless figure on a cornice to be herder rather than stone. It stayed in the long vowels of the rare speech and the slow blink that carried the weight of paragraphs. It stayed so completely that the distinction between the benevolent wise power and the place itself grew as weathered and seamless as an ancestor-plug after a century of wind.

In the raking light, when the entire urban lithosphere looked like a single organism of rock and furs and quiet breathing, the power was simply one more way that organism folded and densified and refused to end. The people never built a temple to it. They had no need. Every wall was already its altar. Every shared silence was already its prayer. Every careful placement of foot on stone was already the agreement they had made: to keep stacking themselves toward a sky that has never once hurried, under the gaze of something that has never once needed them to be anything other than what they are.

And so the plateau continued—furred, warm-blooded, endless, and precisely loved.
