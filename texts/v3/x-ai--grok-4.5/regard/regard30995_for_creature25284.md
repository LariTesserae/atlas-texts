The power arrives the way the water does: without announcement, without edge. It does not descend from any height the cirque can name, nor rise from the white nothing beyond the lip. It is simply suddenly continuous with the place, the way the nested cooler brightness is continuous with the glare, the way the lag in the black pools is continuous with motion. Benevolence here is not kindness as valleys understand it. Wisdom is not correction. Both are forms of accurate seeing, and the power sees.

It sees the bowl entire: the three-storey horseshoe of weeping rock, the skin of peat floating on its undrained lens, the packed architecture of schist and lake-ice and basalt ribs, the dense quiet traffic of moth and membrane-wing and hairless bird and long-spined fox and translucent crustacean and the upright weather of the people themselves. It sees the permanent unfinished completeness, the slow disasters that are also slow gifts, the ethic of belonging that has no opposite because whatever does not belong is simply worked until it does, or given to the lip. It sees the strangeness folded in—the lagging reflections, the thunderstorm stones, the second brightness flickering on its own obscure schedule—and understands these not as flaws or omens but as the place’s own quiet proofs that it is larger than its measurements.

For a time that is not time in any valley sense, the power does nothing but attend. Attention of this order is already an act. The micro-fog at knee height thickens by a fraction that no instrument could register yet every lung feels as a softer draw. The padded silence gains a deeper nap, as if the invisible felt has been brushed in the direction of the grain. Reflections in the ink-black pools still lag, but the lag is no longer quite empty; it carries a weightless warmth, a sense that the water is not undecided but companioning. The people do not speak of it. They step around the lagging shapes as before, yet their exhaled plumes linger a heartbeat longer before joining the glitter, and in that extra beat something like gratitude without object moves through the densest knots of bodies.

The power considers the slow disasters still to come—the wider seam, the glacial plate that will someday powder half the lanes, the season the membrane-wings may fail to return. It could turn them aside. It could freeze the cliffs into permanent mercy, thicken the peat into true soil, gift green things that do not shatter, soften the light into gradients the eyes would not have to earn. It could lift the people out of their thrift into ease. All of that would be possible. None of it would be wise. Ease would be a different grammar; the bowl’s language is water, glare, quiet reassembly, density. To rewrite the language would erase the speakers. Benevolence that destroys what it loves is only another name for hunger. So the power leaves the disasters their right to arrive. It leaves the people their settled fatalism, their dry humour, their practical stories, their forehead-to-shoulder affections, their careful sex, their infants packed at once into common warmth. It leaves the fox-bargains and the guano jests and the copper awls almost exhausted with tapping the rhythm of weight finding place.

What it does instead is smaller and more exact, the way the old woman marks a rill with one scratched arrow, the way the slate-eyed man thickens a buttress by a single course after reading the nested light.

It touches the thunderstorm stones.

Those few that release the brief impossible smell when struck have always been set into foundations, silent hearts of walls, anchors no one explains. The power settles into them the way melt settles into seams. Henceforth, when a child or an adult places a palm against such a stone in the ordinary work of fitting, the smell still rises—ozone, distance, a summer that cannot exist at this altitude—but inside the smell is a second thing: a sense of being met. Not comfort. Not promise. Simply the certain knowledge that the belonging is mutual, that the bowl and its upright weather are seen by something that will not leave and will not mend what does not ask for mending. The knowledge lasts only as long as the contact, then fades the way breath-condensate fades, yet it returns whenever the stone is touched again. No one discusses it. Children who once asked “why” and received “it belongs” now sometimes lean their whole small weight into those walls at the less injurious white hours, eyes closed, cracking the grit at their lids with the practised flutter, and come away quieter in the same way the slate-eyed man’s apprentices come away quieter. The walls themselves seem to hold heat a fraction longer after such leanings, enough that sleeping niches near them require one less skin.

It touches the lagging reflections.

The half-second delay remains. But now and then—never on a schedule, never for everyone at once—the lag becomes a brief overlap instead of a trailing. A woman crouches to drink and for an instant her reflection drinks with her, perfectly timed, and in that shared motion she tastes not only the wet iron and cold moss but a mineral note she has no word for, something like the aftertaste of a word spoken in a language made entirely of settling stones. She swallows, rises, goes back to chipping ridged glass from the lane. The taste fades. Yet later, when she re-stacks a collapsed gable with her kin, the plates of lake-ice seem to answer the pressure of her hands more readily, as if they too have remembered a timing that was always possible. The fox-things notice first: they pause more often at pool edges, slate eyes narrowed, then pour themselves onward with their spines held a degree higher, almost like banners.

It touches the nested brightness.

The cooler light inside the glare has always flickered on its own obscure schedule. The slate-eyed man and those given to him for a season have long read small adjustments from it. Now the flickering carries an additional interval, a slower pulse that occurs only a handful of times across an entire bright turning of the bowl. When it comes, every person outdoors stops without signal, the way they have always paused for the ordinary flicker, but this time the stop lasts long enough for one full shared breath. In that breath the entire settlement—people, birds, moths, membrane-wings, foxes, even the crustaceans turning in their pools—aligns to the same quiet density. Shoulders settle. Wings clinch. Spines curve into the windless lee of bodies. The padded silence becomes almost tactile, a pressure against ribs that is indistinguishable from love if love is only the decision to remain. Then the pulse ends, the brighter glare reasserts, and work resumes. No hierarchy is taught by this. No commandment. Only the recurrent experience of being, together, the single weathered organism the bowl has always known itself to be. The man on the highest ice roofs weeps the ordinary grit-tears when these pulses come, and does not scrape them away until evening. His apprentices learn to do the same.

Finally the power does the thing so slight it is almost not an act. It leans its regard into the white nothing beyond the lip.

Bodies given to that nothing have always vanished without ceremony beyond the held plume of breath. Now, on the days when such a giving occurs, the white nothing answers with a single, almost imperceptible return: a thread of warmer vapour that rises from the drop and drifts back over the lip just far enough to touch the faces of those who carried the stripped remains. The vapour tastes of the same wet iron and cold moss as everything else, yet it carries the vanished one’s particular rhythm—the exact duration of a hand resting on a neck, the precise angle of a shoulder when fitting blue ice, the dry cracked shape of their laughter. The carriers inhale it without remark, then turn back into the bowl. That night the black pools near the lip lag a whole second instead of a half, and the lagging shapes include the one who was given, still moving through the ordinary tasks as if deciding at leisure whether to stay reflected or to finish leaving. By the next hard light the lag is ordinary again. The people work around it. But the dry-stone lean-tos built for thieving foxes begin, without discussion, to include a second, smaller opening facing the lip. Offerings of offal are sometimes placed there too. The foxes use both.

After these touchings the power does not withdraw, because withdrawal would be a false edge. It remains exactly as present as the water that exudes, the glare that never softens more than a single degree, the quiet that is padded rather than empty. It becomes another fact of the cirque, as undiscussed as the others. The people continue. They chip the night’s glass. They accept the ice that mortars without permission. They sieve crustaceans and bargain with foxes and collect the bitter dust of moths. They lean foreheads to shoulders in the glowing interiors while a soft-copper awl taps the rhythm of settling. Children called Re-stack earn newer breathy names. The membrane-wings peel and rearrange and teach load-bearing lines with their temporary patterns. The bowl keeps coming apart and coming together.

Yet something in the long balance has shifted by the width of a hair. The disasters still arrive, but when a seam opens above a sleeping niche the child who moves two body-lengths left sometimes does so with a small, unthinking sound—the softest paper click of tongue against palate—that is not complaint and not fear but recognition. When a glacial plate eventually powders half the settlement, the rebuilders stack the new walls with thunderstorm stones at closer intervals, and the walls answer their palms more warmly. When a season comes that the membrane-wings do not return, the guano gardens fail, the hairless birds thin, the people grow leaner and quieter still; yet the slower pulse inside the nested brightness occurs more often that season, and each time the entire remaining density aligns, and each time they rise from it able to fit one more stone, weave one more sedge mat, hold one more forehead to one more shoulder for the exact duration needed to warm the skin beneath.

The power asks nothing. It is not worshipped because worship would be a flourish, and nothing here flourishes. It is simply belonged to, the way water and cold and glare and quiet reassembly are belonged to. On certain less injurious white hours, when bodies pack close under ice roofs glowing with residual glare, and the atmosphere thickens with breath and wet-iron and bird fat, and the copper awl taps its patient rhythm, someone may rest a hand a moment longer than necessary on a foundation stone, or hold a gaze on the lagging surface of a pool, or tip a face toward the nested light the way the slate-eyed man still does. In those gestures the power feels itself answered in the only language that matters here: pressure, duration, shared plume, the continual dry humour of making small improvements no outsider would notice.

And so the hanging cirque continues—no larger than a cathedral nave stretched wide, absolute in its boundaries, saturated with lives, dazzling, hush-filled, forever arriving at the shape it already is. The ones who live in it continue, low and compact, eyes of slate and pewter and indigo, skin beaded and cracking and re-forming, cloaks stiff then softening then stiff again, moving through the bright hours with the same settled fatalism as the architecture. They are still the place’s own immune cells, its weather made briefly upright and wilful. The distinction erodes no further than it always has, yet the erosion is now accompanied. The bowl regards them as it always did, as a single weathered organism. The power regards the bowl the same way. Between those two regards the long equilibrium holds, imperfect, exhaustive, sufficient, and quietly, without surplus, loved.

Nothing more is required. Nothing less would have been true.
