The power that turns its regard upon the plain does not arrive as light or as thunder. It has no need of either. It is older than the ranges the stone has forgotten, and quieter than the idea of silence the walkers sometimes chase on nights of sugar-glass ice. It notices the place the way a deep current notices a single grain of silt that has begun, against all likelihood, to remember the shape of a mountain.

For a span that would empty empires of their chronicles, the power only attends.

It feels the porous bedrock weeping its slow beads. It tastes the iron and ash and the bacterial slicks that feed the ticking legs and the boom-chested grazers and the pale eyeless flickers. It maps every braid of water that refuses to keep a channel, every fretted flute of stone cold as coral, every carapace softening back into slurry. It hears the total sound not as assault but as the plain’s long exhalation—the residual life rasping, stridulating, screaming, grinding, refusing the final level even while it helps abrade toward it. And it feels the quiet wrongness threaded through: the lagging ghosts in the mirror-sheets, the bosses that ring like a cracked bell still answering one ancient blow, the herds turning as one toward absences, the suspicion (correct, in its way) that the roar itself is the last chisel.

Then it finds the walkers.

Low, jointed, plated in harvested iron and ash-white, eyes wide enough to drink the dim by the lungful. Moving in provisional strings, children tethered by sinew, hands that know the difference between silt that bears and silt that swallows. The careful cupules pecked between booms. The black poles hung with shell so that thin high notes can cut what voices cannot. The three-slab shelters and the resinous dung fires that paint orange glints across lidless eyes. The mouth-to-ear speech. The shallow scrapes that become cairns that become slurry again. The songs—long, low contours of vanished elevations—offered into the rare stilled cold until the ice shatters and the chorus reclaims everything.

The power recognizes them as another roughness the plain has not yet worn smooth. It recognizes, too, the dignity of their insistence: small geometries against the open, deliberate tone against the deafening residual, the choice to stack one slab upon another knowing the seeps will take the work back. They are not separate from the ecology; they are its most intricate expression of refusal. They thrive on the same slow chemistry, yet they shape. They listen with their skeletons. They remember, in contour if not in word, what the stone has forgotten.

A lesser benevolence might have flooded the plain with green, or raised sudden peaks, or struck the roar dumb in a single merciful gesture. Wisdom declines the temptation. Such gifts would be violence dressed as kindness—the imposition of an earlier world upon a later one that has already paid, across uncounted ages, for its present form. The walkers are not incomplete. Their large eyes, their clicking plates, their broth of molted shells and bacterial film, their songs that travel only a few dozen paces before the mist eats them: these are perfections fitted to the wet mineral waste. To unmake the fit would be to unmake them.

So the power does less, and more.

It reaches first into the wrongness, not to erase it (for even the lag and the ring and the unison turning have become part of the oldest stable state), but to gentle its edges. Reflections in the standing water still smear, yet the ghosts now finish their gestures only a fraction late, and children who stare too long no longer feel the vertigo of uncoupled time. When a ringing boss is struck, the answering notes still travel beyond the horizon, but they arrive unaccompanied by sudden drownings or herds that should not be so near; the plain is allowed to remain itself without the extra cruelty of ambush. The subterranean grinding continues, yet it no longer tilts the world a finger’s width without warning; the elders who press palms to stone floor and hum can now feel the adjustments coming days in advance, enough to move the tethered young to higher siltstone.

Next the power touches the sound—not silencing it, never that, for the roar is the breath of every ticking leg and booming chest and glass-edge scream, the very medium in which the walkers have learned to think with their ribs. Instead it opens slender fissures of possibility inside the din. On certain nights, when the cold is just enough and the shallows begin to skin over, the drop in volume lasts a few dozen heartbeats longer than it once did. In those stretched intervals the songs carry. Not far—still only a hundred paces, perhaps two—but far enough that one string of walkers can hear another, and answer, so that the contours of lost mountains briefly braid across the open like the rills themselves. The mist still eats the tones, yet more slowly, as if it too has been asked to listen.

The power leaves no commandment and no temple. It does, however, leave three subtle insistences of its own, scattered so widely and working so patiently that the walkers will take generations to notice they were not always there.

The first is a single low boss of stone, smoother than the others, that does not ring when struck. Instead, when a walker rests a palm against it and hums the old contours, the stone returns the tone an octave deeper and a fraction clearer, so that the singer feels the shape of the vanished range in the bones rather than merely remembering it. The boss becomes a place of quiet pilgrimage; strings of four or seven detour to it when the routes allow, and leave nothing behind but the polished dark of frequent hands.

The second is a change in the black poles. The fossil wood and the carried deadfall still hang with shell and sinew, but now and then a shell will catch a note that has never been heard before—a thin, pure partial that seems to come from above the mist rather than within it. The walkers learn to listen for these strangers. They do not know they are hearing the power’s own attention glancing off the decorations; they know only that the high notes sometimes open a brief bright corridor through the roar, long enough for a warning to travel, or a greeting, or the simple fact of another’s continued walking.

The third is the smallest and the most intimate. In the three-slab shelters, when the resinous dung fire is lit and faces lean close, the orange light now holds a steadiness it lacked. Shadows no longer gather in hollows that should not hold them. The lagging ghosts stay outside. For the span of the fire, speech can rise a little above mouth-to-ear. Words—still sparse, still shaped as much by bone conduction as by air—can be shared among five or six at once. They speak of routes, of deepenings felt in the soles, of a child whose carapace is hardening well, of the taste of a particular broth. Sometimes they speak of the songs. Sometimes they do not speak at all, and only breathe together in the sweet-sour smoke while the power, unnoticed, keeps the small dome of lesser din intact until the fuel is ash.

After that the power does nothing else.

It does not stay as a presence the walkers could name. It does not rescue them from the plain or the plain from its finished ecology. It simply continues to attend, the way the bedrock continues to weep and the herds continue to boom and the walkers continue to stack slabs knowing the seeps will reclaim them. The attention itself is the gift: a benevolence so spare and precise that it alters the rate of erosion by less than a grain per generation, yet alters it toward continuance rather than toward the ultimate level and the ultimate quiet. The plain remains worn, wet, loud, dim, cool, open, finished with growing things, and ferociously alive. The walkers remain another roughness—jointed figures becoming, with every generation, a little more perfectly shaped to their world.

Only now, on the stretched cold nights, when the ice forms and the songs braid farther than they used to, something in the mist seems to lean closer, listening. And in the polished dark of the silent boss, and in the stranger notes caught by shell, and in the steadier orange of the low fires, the walkers feel—without ever finding a word for it—that the world has noticed them back.

They keep walking. They keep pecking cupules between the booms. They keep hanging shell on poles. They keep singing the contours of mountains no one living has seen. The mist uncoils. Water rises and falls. The roar continues, yet inside it there are now slender, durable mercies.

The power, satisfied that kindness has been done at the exact scale the place can bear, turns the greater part of its regard elsewhere. A fraction remains, folded into the stone and the sound and the large dark eyes, an unnoticed covenant: that this oldest stable state will not be hurried toward silence, and that the small insistences of the walkers will continue to matter for as long as they choose to make them.

And that is all.
