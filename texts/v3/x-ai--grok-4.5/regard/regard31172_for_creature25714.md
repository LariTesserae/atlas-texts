The benevolent and wise power arrived the way mist arrives: without announcement, without edge. It did not descend; it simply was, coextensive for a time with the warm dark air that already filled every hollow and stomata and valve. It had no name that the chamber could pronounce, and it required none. It was older than limestone, older than the first root that split stone, yet it carried no weight of years—only the quiet, unhurried attention of something that has watched galaxies cool and still finds wonder in a single glowing droplet of saliva on moss.

It spread itself through the place the way the low-voltage current already did: along the conductive root-wire, through the hydraulic sighs of the trunk-engines, into the blue-green pulses of mycelium, across the velvet skins of the grazers, behind the six wet eyes of the climbers, beneath the leaf-mottled hide of the predators, inside the clattering wing-cases and the stridulating carapaces and the soft brush-mouths that drank condensate. It did not invade. It listened with the whole of itself.

What it found was not broken.

The chamber had long ago finished the only work that ever truly needed finishing: the slow, complete reconciliation of every opposite. Machine and muscle beat in the same wet rhythm. Predation and grooming occupied the same jaw. Waste and nourishment had forgotten they were different words. Time itself had thickened into something that could be leaned against, like a living wall. Density without crowding. Urgency without haste. A single damp consensus that repaired itself with whatever the dark provided and asked for nothing more.

The power felt no urge to improve. Improvement is the instinct of the incomplete. Here, completion had already occurred—quietly, thoroughly, fifty meters under an old floodplain that had forgotten its own name.

Still, attention is itself a kind of touch. And benevolence, when it is wise, does not always withhold its hand.

So the power did three things, each so slight they registered only as a change in the quality of the existing breath.

First, it settled a deeper quiet around the outer edges of the chamber—not a barrier, never a cage, but a soft gradient of unnoticeability. Any future tunnel, any probe, any curious root from the world above, any mineral hunger or human engine that might one day push downward would find the limestone gently turning its attention elsewhere. The place would remain where it was, doing what it did, unseen by eyes that had not already learned how to love the dark. The floodplain above would continue its own forgetting, and that forgetting would become a kind of loyalty.

Second, it touched the oldest of the trunk-engines—the one whose heartwood pistons had pulsed longest—and left inside its hydraulic memory a single new possibility: should the surface world ever grow so loud, so bright, so dry that even this sealed depth began to feel the strain, the entire living structure could, if it chose, begin the centuries-long work of sealing every upper aperture and sinking another fifty meters, or a hundred, carrying its mist and its factories and its soft velvet herds with it. Not a command. Only an option folded into the grain, the way a tree folds the memory of wind. The chamber could ignore it forever. Or, on some distant day when the engines thumped a different rhythm, it might remember it had always known how.

Third—and this was the only gift that shimmered, briefly, where living eyes could almost see it—the power gathered a little of the chamber’s own luminous mycelium, a little of the copper-veined wing-flash, a little of the glowing saliva-trails, a little of the slow blue pulse that lived under predator skin, and braided them into a single new colony no larger than a child’s open hand. It placed this colony in a high crotch where resin fell thickest, in a spot the elongated climbers passed every few cycles, where the sharp-winged braids sometimes rested, where even the loneliest of the jawed hunters occasionally stretched to sleep.

The new colony did not shine brighter than what already existed. It shone with the same tired, perfect light. But if any of the inhabitants paused there—grazer licking mineral salts, climber trimming moss, beetle fierce with joy, moth laying eggs on warm leaf-leather—and if they remained still long enough for the mist to bead on their bodies, they would feel, for the space of one long breath, that they were not only continuing.

They were known.

The feeling would pass. It would leave no doctrine, no change in the overlapping rhythms of thump and clatter and wet whistle. It would simply remain available, the way warmth is available, the way sugar is available, the way the dark itself is available: without demand.

Then the power withdrew the same way it had come. The mist was mist again. The engines kept their wet muscular time. A predator dropped, canvas-tearing, and the white roar rose and subsided. A grazer climbed onto a bast belt and rode a little way, dreamy. The sharp-winged braid reformed half a gallery distant. Nothing had been added that the place could not metabolize. Nothing had been taken.

Above, the old floodplain slept under whatever sky it had. Below, the single damp consensus of flesh and fiber and low-voltage longing continued—thrumming, chewing, glowing, greasing, climbing, dropping, resealing—exactly as before, and somehow more completely itself for having been, for a little while, beheld without judgment and loved without revision.

That is all the wise power did.

It was enough.

It was, perhaps, the only thing that had ever been required.
