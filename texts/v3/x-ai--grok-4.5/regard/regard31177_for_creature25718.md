The attention does not descend. It simply becomes local, the way humidity is local, the way the decision of stone to remain at middle height is local. One moment the plateau is the whole of its own long wet argument; the next moment that argument is also known, perfectly and without haste, by something that has no need of engines or blood or the patient fretting of brick.

The power is vast only in the sense that kindness can be vast when it has finished with every smaller urgency. It has no body that the acid fog can wick, no ears to be casted by drop-forges, no knees to feel the pressure rising from the original joints. Yet it arrives already speaking the city’s grammar. It tastes the runoff with the tip of a thought and waits. It reads the stacked thunder the way the people read lips. It stands—if standing is the word—on a high slag terrace while ordinary light does its clear work through moving steam, and it sees the open weave exactly as one of the dense ones sees it on those rare afternoons when recognition settles like damp cloth: one warm-blooded entity, lungs of cooling towers, pulse of ore lifts so slow they look eternal.

It does not interrupt the pulse.

Instead it listens longer than any turbine has ever turned. It listens until the listening itself becomes another weather—neither louder nor quieter than the rest, simply present in the way the canal-slick are present, or the moths, or the unfinished bright offerings left in the weeping dark. The pressure in the cut-stone sub-levels feels the listening the way it once felt the first rain: as confirmation. For the length of one valley-wide catch of breath (the forges all pausing in the same half-second, as they sometimes do), the pressure surfaces far enough to taste the steam and, tasting, finds the steam has been joined by something that is not steam and not absence. Something that means, without syllables: *I have seen that you hold. Continue.*

Then the forges resume. The moment passes into the same industrial tempo that polishes faces like gear-teeth. No one marks it on any calendar. Yet afterward the oldest residents—the ones who already hear names inside the thunder—notice that the true quiet pockets left by stacked fog have grown a fraction more patient. In those pockets it is possible, now, to hear not only ordinary conversation but the soft continuous sound of the plateau liking itself. The sound is almost nothing: a mineral exhalation that has always been there, newly audible the way a familiar collar becomes newly felt when someone else’s hand has adjusted it with care.

The power does not stay in any sense that would cast a shadow or cool a railing. It has no desire to become another lineage or another machine-hall. Wisdom, here, is the refusal to hurry a place that has already arrived. Benevolence is the same refusal, warmed through.

Still, attention leaves a trace when it is this complete. The trace is small, concessional, perfectly in keeping with the thin green seams and the V-shaped wakes.

On certain nights the moths’ dust now carries a faint additional frequency—one the city’s twilight was always capable of but had never been asked to use. Follow the right moth through enough fog banks and you do not stumble into the wrong decade. Instead you step, for the space of three breaths, into a silence that is not empty. In that silence the entire arrangement—rock, rust, dense bodies, slow water, hereditary shifts, aunties in the canals, the pressure that simply continues to mean the plateau—is felt as held by more than its own stubbornness. Held the way a well-known shoulder is held by clothes that will never dry: without ownership, without demand that anything become otherwise. Children who catch those moths in cupped damp hands sometimes weep without knowing why, then laugh, then let the pale wings scissor away carrying their grit and their brief fluorescent wonder. Adults feel it as a settling deeper than the usual settling. They adjust collars. They spit iron. They return to the appointments of noise and stone.

The canal-slick receive no new treaty, only a slight brightening of the small drownings left for them at the grates—enough that the nudges toward ladders grow a fraction more deliberate, a fraction more like kinship.

And once every long span of years, when the paint has weathered from whatever wing was once called new, the pressure and the listening (which has never fully left, only thinned to the same consistency as the permanent driplines) together allow a single drop of water to climb a vertical face all the way to the top before it falls. Those who see it do not speak sharply. They give it room. Some leave a polished gear or a knot of hair or a bead of blood on shale, the old unfinished gifts. Others simply watch, dense and damp, and understand that the climb was always possible. That the plateau has always been allowed to do more than refuse to fall. That refusal, met with perfect attention, can become a quiet form of joy.

The power does nothing else.

It does not lift the low cloud. It does not quiet the forges into birdsong or dry the cuffs or raise the stubborn trees into crowns. It does not offer sweetness or true cold or any exotic silence unaccompanied by fog. Those would be other places. This place has already arrived at itself. The engines keep their ancient appointments. The people remain one of the ways it remains. The long wet argument continues, unhurried, exactly as before—only now the argument knows it has been heard to its depths, and the knowing is another seam of green that refuses both surrender and abundance.

Nothing is in a hurry.  
Not the stone.  
Not the dense ones.  
Not the benevolent attention that saw them, loved the seeing, and chose, wisely, to become part of how they stay.
