The highland does not announce its dwellers. At first the eye finds only the evidence of tending: the cambered paths kept clear of algae, the turf roofs renewed in even layers, the narrow channels whose stones have been reset so many times the joints no longer show tool marks. Whatever walks here does so without hurry and without waste, and leave almost no secondary trace. No dropped fiber, no ash scatter, no footprints that last beyond the next thin sheet of seepage. The stillness is not emptiness; it is occupation so complete it has become indistinguishable from the place’s own breathing.

They are upright, but only just. Bodies long and attenuated, built more of tendon and quiet flex than of bulk, so that when they stand at rest beside a weeping scarp they register as another pale vertical plane among the fractured rock. Skin the color of wet limestone, slightly translucent at the wrists and throat, always beaded. The moisture does not chill them; it is their medium. Fine cilia, almost invisible, cover the backs of the arms and the nape, constantly combing the mist, tasting mineral content, temperature, the faint electrical gossip of water moving through stone. Hair, when they have it, is sparse and dark with retained damp, worn short or bound with twisted reed so it never whips or unsettles the air.

They do not keep animals because nothing else has ever been needed, or perhaps because nothing else can remain. Their own presence is so precisely calibrated to the highland’s equilibrium that any additional metabolism would tip the quiet arithmetic of seepage and growth. Insects do not settle on them. Birds, if any ever cross the ring of young ridges, veer off long before the basin’s center; the air itself seems to thin further around these walkers, not hostile, merely already claimed. Their food is the deliberate green—reeds whose hearts they draw out in slow spirals, mosses scraped with bone blades and pressed into dense cakes that taste of cold iron and sweetness, the occasional pale tuber coaxed from under gravel. They eat without fire most days. When fire is required it is small, built in recessed stone bowls under roof vents, fed with the driest upper stems, and extinguished before the smoke can travel far. The copper tools—needles, awls, one or two crescent blades—are relics of some earlier covenant with heat and ore; they are used sparingly, sharpened against the same chalky rock that forms the walls, and stored in dry niches lined with layered slate.

Movement is rhythmic with the water. They prefer the raised walkways but do not fear the marsh hollows; their long feet spread, the toes webbed by thin folds of skin that leave almost no print. When two meet they do not speak in the ordinary sense. Throat and chest produce a low, continuous susurrus—part breath, part recall of the trickles all around them—modulated by minute changes in posture. Meaning travels more through shared attention to a particular channel’s flow, or the way one of them turns a shoulder toward a wing of the settlement that needs new packing of earth, than through anything like argument or announcement. Children (there are children, though few, and they grow with the same unnerving patience as the reeds) learn by walking behind, matching stride, placing palms on wet stone until the stone’s slow messages become intelligible.

Time does not accumulate on them the way it does elsewhere. Faces remain smooth; the slight translucency never clouds. They remember the exact geometry of every path because they have always maintained that geometry. Some among them are older than the present height of the ridges—hard to say how, in a place where stone still looks freshly broken and yet sheds nothing new. These elders spend long intervals standing half-immersed in the shallow catchment basins, cilia fully extended, body temperature matched so precisely to the water that the surface does not steam or shiver. What they listen for is never explained. Afterward they may adjust a single slate on a roof, or deepen a channel by the width of a finger, and the highland continues exactly as before.

At night—though night here is only a gentle graphite dimming, never true dark—the settlement reveals a further quiet. Doorways stay open. Figures sit on thresholds or lie on warm packed-earth floors, listening to the mist bead and drop. Sometimes a hand extends to catch a drip, bring it to the mouth, as if tasting a syllable of the place’s endless sentence. They do not dream of elsewhere. Elsewhere would be drier or louder or unfinished. Here the work is only to keep the balance that has already been found: stone fitted to stone, water led where it must go, green kept lush and limited, every surface gleaming under the clean, unhurried light. They are the highland’s way of remaining itself—elongated, damp, deliberate lives threaded through the seepage, neither guests nor masters, simply the part of the basin that knows it is awake.
