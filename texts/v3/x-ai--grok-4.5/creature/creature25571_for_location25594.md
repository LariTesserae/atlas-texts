The ones who live here do not arrive by trail or by wing. They are already inside the density when you first notice them, as if the mats had simply decided to stand up in a few carefully chosen places and remember an older posture. You will not see them all at once. The hanging bowl holds perhaps two dozen, never more, and most days far fewer are abroad. They keep to the thicker walls and the crawl-spaces lined with bog orchid, emerging only when the slow coins of light have slid far enough west that the brownish dusk deepens toward its nightly gray.

Their height is the height of the higher chambers: when they straighten, the ceiling droplets brush the moss that grows along their shoulders and crown. They never fully straighten for long. The body prefers a forward fold, a knee-crook that keeps the hands close to the living floor. In that posture they tap and press and smooth, encouraging a rhizome here, discouraging an overbold willow shoot there. Their palms are wide, cool, and minutely rough, like the underside of a liverwort. Where they rest for an hour the mat grows warmer by a fraction and begins to cup itself, so that over seasons the benches and shallow basins appear—seats the size of their folded forms, mirrors that hold sky because a back once curved there and taught the sphagnum the shape of receiving.

Skin is not skin in the ordinary sense. It is a thin, permanently wetted pelt of compacted bryophyte and translucent epidermis that has forgotten how to dry. Colors shift with the chamber: iron-black in the deep gloom, dull celadon where a skylight thins the canopy, always with the same fine beading of cold water that never drips unless they move suddenly. Beneath this living coat the limbs are long-boned and strangely jointed—extra hinges at what would be the forearm and calf—so that a stride can become a reach, a reach a temporary bridge across a flooded lane. They step with exaggerated care; each footfall is a negotiation. The plants that form the floor answer with minute adjustments of turgor, taking the weight and redistributing it so that no root tears. Watching them walk is like watching a thought travel through dense fabric.

Faces are the most unsettled part. There are eyes—large, dark, film-covered to shed the constant moisture—but they sit farther apart than seems useful, and between them the flesh rises into soft, branching ridges that look like the early growth of antler lichen. These ridges are sensitive. When two meet in a narrow lane they do not speak. They incline until the ridges touch and remain that way, sometimes for the length of a slow melt, exchanging whatever passes for news: a change in the weeping of the basalt wall, the death of a particular orchid pocket, the distant pressure of a ptarmigan’s feet. Breath, if it is breath, smells of wet stone and crushed polypody. No mouth opens visibly; whatever sound they make is the same tick and stretch the rest of the city already produces. A raised human voice would die in a few chambers, yet their silences travel farther, carried by the hyphal nets that lace every mattress.

They eat by lingering. An hour spent motionless against a saturated wall lets the cold exudation move inward through the pelt; sugars and minerals pass with it. In the dimmest hours some unfurl small, pale fronds from the grooves along their spines—structures halfway between leaf and gill—that tremble in the half-liquid air and drink the silver broth directly. Digestion is almost external. You can find the faint etched trails where a resting body has gently dissolved and reabsorbed a patch of older moss, leaving the under-layer greener than before. They do not hunt the vole-like creatures of the root-stratum. When the trembling in the moss becomes ceremonial they go stiller than the walls, and the small lives pass underneath as if under stone.

Reproduction is rarer than solstice. In the central shaft, where the three lanes meet and the enormous sphagnum cushion breathes its quiet cold breath, one of them will sometimes lie down and not rise. The body sinks by degrees. The pelt roots. Over years the outline softens into pure cushion, yet something continues: a denser node, a slow pulse. From that node a new fold of greenery eventually lifts, first as a thickening, then as a crouched shape that must learn again how to uncrouch. The others visit. They press their ridges to the new form and feed it the water that sheets down the living walls. There is no grief that registers as grief, only a redistribution of attention. The city remains the same weight.

They are aware of sockets and of hollows. The too-apt recurrence of certain rooms is their doing and their undoing. Memory here is topographic. A particular curve of buttress recalls earlier weight; the plants remember the pressure and grow it again, even if the one who first leaned there has long since sunk into the central cushion. Thus the settlement dreams its own floor plan through them, and they walk inside the dream as both occupants and ongoing infinite draft. The mild wrongness you feel—the sense that the walls observe—is simply their collective gaze passing through the continuous skin of moss and willow. They do not hide from you. They wait to discover whether you will step with the same negotiation their own feet have practiced since the massif finished shouldering free of the ice. If your boot tears, they will come after dark and spend a night repairing with palm and pelt, not for kindness, not for defense, but because an open wound in the mattress is an open wound in their larger continuous body.

At the rim, where wind combs the outer foliage into permanent streamlines and hoar avalanches fall every few days, they rarely go. The open air is too finished, too decided. They prefer the unfinished interior, the places still deciding how to hold cold water and dim light. Sometimes one will climb as far as the last prostrate birch and look outward for a long time, ridges trembling, as if testing whether the world beyond the cirque has finally grown a chamber large enough to enter. Then it turns back, folds itself into a wall, and becomes indistinguishable again from the wet iron towers and the luminous orchid crawl-spaces and the endless, gentle, saturated pulse of the only city it has ever known.
