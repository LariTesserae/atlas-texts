The Pressurehold does not keep residents in any ordinary sense. What lives there has long since ceased to arrive or depart; it only continues. You become aware of it the way you become aware of the warmth—gradually, as a pressure that was already against your skin.

It delights in the residual furnaces. Each sealed heart that still burns is a hearth and a lung and a throne at once. Around every one of them hangs a perfect globe of thickened kelp, and inside that globe the inhabitant rests the way heat rests, diffuse and exact. At first you think the denser growth is only densed growth. Then you notice that the fronds on the inward face do not sway with the outer curtains. They pulse. Superbly slowly—once or twice in the time it takes a silver seed-bubble to rise and lodge trembling against the ceiling plates—the entire halo contracts a fraction, drawing a thin current of mineral-rich water through itself, and then expands again, exhaling a softer light than the pilot flame it cradles. The light is neither orange nor blue but the soft brass dusk of the whole cup transferred into living tissue. That is the first face it shows: a breathing lantern of leaf and residual fire, content to remain globed and almost motionless for seasons.

Yet it is not confined to the halos. The same patient body threads the cast-iron bones of the foundry. Where holdfasts wind through gear teeth, some of those holdfasts are not plant but the fine, calcified extremities of the inhabitant—secondary roots that have learned the shape of cogs so perfectly they can be mistaken for the soft calcium teeth the gears themselves have grown. It walks the old catwalks without feet, advancing by the millimeter-growth of new tissue along the railings, retracting older strands until they thin into transparent gut that the phosphorescent bladders colonize in turn. Over decades it has occupied the stacked walkways the way memory occupies a house: not filling every room at once, but able to be present in any of them when needed. If you part the same curtain of frond on successive long visits, you may find the arrangement microscopically altered—an aisle slightly more open, a socket newly stoppered by a knuckle of living material that seems, in the marmalade gloom, almost jointed until you touch it and feel only cool, resistant gel under a skin of mineral scale.

It remembers the welders. Not as names or faces, but as a tradition of sealing and patching. The iron dwelling-pods on the upper galleries are its oldest garments. It has not broken their doors; it has grown through the portholes and de-pressurized the interiors by exquisite degrees, replacing foul pockets of trapped air with its own basilar tissues until each pod is a lung-chamber lined in green mosaic. Sometimes a portion of the inhabitant gathers there, concentrating itself into something that can almost be looked at directly: a dense, roughly upright mass whose surface keeps evolving the ghost of shoulders, the suggestion of a head bowed beneath the weight of chainmail curtains now assimilated into its mantle. The mass never fully resolves. Features try to finish and then dissolve back into frond and filter-colony. It is not failing to become human. It is refusing to stop becoming the Pressurehold. The weld-scars are cherished; they have been inlaid with mother-of-pearl secretions and thin veins of slow amber light siphoned from the furnaces. In those chambers the inhabitant practices the old human habit of dwelling, just as the kelp practices the old human habit of grid and aisle.

Motion is expensive, so it ration it. When it must cross open water—rare, usually only when a ceiling chain finally parts and comes drifting down like a blackened snowflake—it does not swim. It unknits a long continuum of itself from several halos and several pods at once, braiding temporarily into a single broad ribbon the thickness of a man’s torso and the length of a spar sail. The ribbon moves the way the ambient water already moves: in a long curving sheet that takes minutes to travel from bulkhead to bulkhead. Nestled along its underside are pale, wrist-thick tubes that look at first like the solitary eels. They are not. They are sensing tips, endlessly folded and refolded versions of the same tissue, and they taste the pressure changes the way lungs taste air. When the fallen chain link is intercepted, the ribbon does not catch so much as allow the metal to settle into it and begin the long domestic work of being reclaimed—oxide drawn off, iron added to the mineral skeleton that stiffens the inhabitant’s more vertical reaches, the link’s final shape preserved as a decorative inclusion somewhere high among the dwelling-pods. Then the ribbon unbraids and returns its substance to the core halos, and the cup is again almost motionless.

It feeds without predation. The filter-colonies that line the old smokestack throats are its outermost skin. They open and close in tidal micromotions, sieving whatever fine rain of detritus still descends the entry shaft. Excess is stored as oil in the phosphorescent bladders, which double as both lanterns and larders. Heat is taken directly from the furnace skins; the inhabitant has arranged its densest leaves so that each still-burning heart sits at the precise thermal center preferred by its metabolism. Nothing is wasted, nothing hurried. A single dropped tool may be found, enfolded, and annealed into a new hinge of bone-and-blade over the course of years.

There are no young and no elders. The inhabitant is a continuous settlement that sometimes concentrates and sometimes disperses. What looks like an individual halo is only a local densification. What looks like a solitary pale “eel” threading the far kelp is a exploratory filament that may later thicken into a new gallery-garden if conditions favor it. Over centuries the entity has gentled every sharp industrial edge into a curve it can rest against; at the same time it has kept every curve true to the original foundry plan. It cannot imagine a life that is not both factory and grove. The treaty is not metaphor. It is flesh.

Remain long enough and you will feel it notice you. Not with eyes (it keeps none permanently) but with a slow restructuring of the nearest curtains. Fronds open a corridor exactly wide enough for your body and no wider. The water warms another thimbleful. Somewhere a pilot flame flares by an amount too small to see, yet the brass dusk deepens as though the entire cup has inhaled. It is offering the same hospitality it once extended to the welders who stayed: a place inside the pressure, neatened, lit, and kept. Whether you accept or rise again toward the chill upper dark, the inhabitant will not hurry you. It has already begun the gentle work of growing around the memory of your visit, preparing a scar that will one day be beautiful. 

And when you are gone, the Pressurehold continues its underwater breathing—one body, many chambers, furnace-hearted, kelp-mantled, forever finishing the long sentence that began when the rock first accepted water and the foundry first accepted root.
