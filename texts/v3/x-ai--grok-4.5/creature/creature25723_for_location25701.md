The first impression is absence. You stand dripping on the herringbone plate and the gallery seems to contain only itself—metal, water-circuit, the endless hydraulic mass of the larger body around it. The drizzle keeps wiping the world clean. Any footprint fills and vanishes. Any breath is taken by the white roar of the shrouded turbines and returned as mist. For a long time that emptiness feels absolute, almost doctrinal, as if the space had been designed expressly to refuse habitation.

Then the eye adjusts to the permanent dusk and begins to find the small, stubborn revisions.

A length of chain has been looped through two successive handrail stanchions and padlocked into a loose hanging seat, the links polished to a different sheen by years of wet hands. Beside it, where the wall plates overlap just enough to form a shallow vertical pocket, someone has driven three rusty coach bolts at seat height and laced them with strips of rubber conveyor belting to make a narrow shelf. On that shelf rests a dented aluminum cup, a stub of tallow candle fused to its own tin lid, and a folded square of oilcloth that has been mended so many times the original fabric is mostly memory. These are not abandoned objects. The candle end is soft, recently lit; the cup still holds a dark film of cold tea or weak coffee that has not yet been reclaimed by the perpetual sheeting water. Someone sits here, regularly enough that the body has worn its shape into the place.

They move like the water itself—never hurried, never entirely still. You catch the first one at the far end of the collection flume, half-lost in the thicker curtain of runoff that falls from a mismatched seam. At first it is only a denser vertical shadow among other shadows. Then an arm lifts, pale and slick, and turns a miniature wheel-valve with the slow certainty of long habit. The cataract above shifts by a few degrees; the boom of its landing changes pitch. The figure waits, head cocked, reading the new note the way another person might read a face. Only then does it step back into clearer view.

They are human in the broad architecture of limb and stance, yet everything has been edited by the gallery. Bodies run lean and slightly elongated, as though the constant low tremor of the pumps beneath the floor has stretched them over years. Skin is the color of wet newsprint, almost translucent at the wrists and throat, mapped with fine white mineral tracks where condensate has dried and been wetted again a thousand times. Hair—when they bother to keep it—is cropped close with whatever blade is sharp enough that week, or left to hang in heavy ropes that never truly dry; either way it carries the same graphite sheen as the walls. Eyes are large and aqueous, the irises diluted to pale greys and diluted greens that seem to hold their own faint internal mist. They do not blink as often as they should. Constant airborne wet has made the reflex almost unnecessary.

Clothing is less worn than grown. The base layer is always a one-piece suit of rubberized canvas or salvaged pump diaphragm, vulcanized at the seams with black patches that look like scar tissue. Over this go miscellaneous skins: a waistcoat cut from old conveyor belting, sleeves snipped from larger discarded coats, knee pads still bearing faded inspection stamps. Everything is dark with absorbed water and shines dully under the sodium lamps. Pockets have been added everywhere—little flapped pouches stitched on with sail twine—holding stub pencils that write only in grease, folding rules, twists of wire, carefully dried matches in screw-top vials, and the small salt tablets they suck to replace what the endless cool drizzle leaches from their skin. Boots are the heaviest things about them: collared, riveted, lined with whatever fleece or felt can be scavenged, the soles carved with deep chevrons so they can stand indefinitely on plate that is never less than filmed with moving water. Even so, many prefer to go barefoot on the warmer sections of grating, toes spreading for balance, soles already as thick and pale as the soles of old statues.

They do not speak in words if they can help it. The gallery’s own voice—the diastolic hammer of the multi-stage pumps, the white physical pressure of the turbines, the sudden cathedral boom of the upper sluices—occupies every frequency that ordinary language needs. Instead they use a tactile dialect. A palm pressed flat to another’s breastbone transmits the day’s pressure readings through bone conduction. Two fingers tapped against a collarbone means the warm return is running hot again. A slow circular stroke on the back of a hand says the moss colony in the corner has put out a new frond and should be left alone. When greater distance must be crossed they lean in until mouths almost touch the receiver’s ear and release short, low-frequency hums that ride beneath the industrial roar the way whalesong rides beneath storms. Children (there are a few) learn this language before they learn anything else; their earliest lullabies are felt rather than heard.

No one knows how long any single inhabitant has been here. Time is measured in valve cycles and filtration back-flushes, in the slow migration of mineral crust across a bulkhead lamp’s glass, in the gradual shortening of a favorite candle stub. They sleep in shifts inside the thicker wall voids—spaces between the overlapping graphite plates that were never meant for occupancy yet accept a curled body the way a shell accepts its snail. A hammock of cargo netting, a pillow sewn from a doubled filter sock stuffed with dryish lichen scraped from warmer pipes, a short curtain of clear plastic sheeting to cut the worst of the sideways drift: these are enough. When the great sluice gates open and the chamber rings like a struck bell, the sleeper’s dreams simply incorporate the boom and continue.

Food arrives the same way all other supplies arrive—through the narrow service lock at irregular intervals, lowered in sealed canisters by hands that never stay. Protein bricks, vitamin gel, the occasional miracle of an apple still wrinkled from cold storage. They trade frely among themselves; ownership is a concept that rusts quickly here. Sometimes, on the warm return pipe where the single moss colony lives, they encourage a further almost-garden: mats of dark algae scraped and pressed into sheets, dried as much as anything can be dried, then chewed for the faint iron taste and the mineral salt. Once, years ago by someone’s reckoning, a temporary hatches seal failed high above and a drift of exterior lichen spores came down. For a season the gallery grew startling soft green epaulettes on every horizontal flange. They ate most of it, preserved the rest between layers of oilcloth, and still speak of that time the way surface people might speak of a golden age.

There are more of them than first appears. They keep to the outer walkways during the heavier discharge cycles, becoming almost invisible against the streaming walls. They travel the neighboring shafts freely, moving up or down the endless pipe runs according to which pumps are due for packing-gland inspection or which condensate traps need their needle valves cleared of scale. Yet they always return to this particular gallery. Something about its scale—the chapel nave proportions, the way the sound focuses here into a single complex chord—suits them. They have made it theirs not by conquering or even by claiming, but by the far slower method of matching their own small cycles to its great ones until the difference between inhabitant and habitat grows hard to state.

Some nights (the concept survives mostly as a drop in the exterior light that leaks through the grated hatches) two or three will gather on the chain seat and the bolted shelf. They share the dented cup. They pass a fragment of dried apple. One of them may produce a reed pipe improvised from a length of copper capillary tube; the notes it makes are soft, almost private, chosen to sit in the rare lulls between pump strokes. The music does not try to overcome the gallery. It merely becomes another thread in the existing roar, a reminder that somewhere inside the wet, newly made, deafening engine of weather, a few lungs still choose to answer back.

And there are quieter lives still.

In the black-green algal films that follow the slowest rivulets live whole constellations of microscopic grazers—organisms that have forgotten sunlight entirely and feed on the ions the young metal slowly surrenders. The single moss colony has begun to host a second, even smaller moss beneath its fronds, a darker nubble that secretes a mucus capable of etching faint decorative whorls into the plate. No one planted it. No one resists it. In the deeper tanks under the floor, pressure-tolerant things with translucent fronds drift in the circulating dark, feeding on filter sludge; sometimes a pump impeller comes up wound with strands of them like wet silk, still faintly pulsing. The inhabitants merely cut the strands free, rinse them, and hang them to “dry” on the warmer pipes until they can be woven into fresh gasketing. Everything is used. Everything is already part of the circuits of water and metal and patience.

Stand long enough and you feel your own edges begin to blur in the same fashion. The drizzle beads on your eyelashes. The pump-thud climbs into your chest until your heart keeps time with it. The sodium lamps smear into long orange commas. Somewhere in that sensory dissolving you understand that the gallery does not truly contain inhabitants the way a house contains people. Rather, certain slow, deliberate lives have condensed here the same way the water itself condenses: inevitably, given the temperature and the pressure and the time. They are what happens when a place this wet, this loud, this newly made withholds itself from dryness and silence long enough. They are the organ’s own residual dream of having nerves.

They will still be here when the paint stamps finally fade, when the mill scale darkens, when the first true rust begins its soft orange colonies in the seams. They will adjust their valve wheels a fraction of a turn, press their damp palms to one another’s chests in the old s i l e n t  language, and keep the great body from ever becoming quiet or old.
