They are dense people.

Not in the sense of stupidity or of crowding—there is no crowding here—but in the packed, low-center way of bodies that have spent generations leaning into a Continuous fact. The plateau’s thin edge-air has deepened their chests without heightening them; they stand as if the ground might at any moment remember it is allowed to slant. Shoulders broad from the unconscious hunch against steam-wind and noise. Hips narrow. Legs short and relentless, made for shale yards and the sudden chipped steps of foundations that predate every living memory. When they walk the wide intervals between halls, they do not so much cross space as measure it with the mismas patience the water uses on stone.

Their skin holds the color of the plateau after rain: dark mineral gray with a slow oxide flush that never quite leaves the creases of knuckles, eyelids, the inner wrists. It is always faintly damp, always shedding a grit that is both their own dead cells and the airborne dust of fretted brick and cooling slag. They taste of wet iron if you kiss them (they do kiss, mouths close enough to share breath, because every important thing is said that way). Clothing is never dry at the cuffs. Heavy weaves, boiled wools, canvas the color of soot and old grease, cut clumsily generous so the damp can move. Metal buttons and zippers are preferred; they warm quickly to the hand and never pretend to be cold. Many go bare-headed or tightly bound, because loose hair becomes a slow wick for the acid fog and the mineral exhalations that rise from every cut face.

Eyes are narrowed by lifelong negotiation with moving white breath and the glare that bounces off wet pewter ponds. Irises run the palette of the city’s materials—slag gray, dilute rust, the pale bilious green that collects in the lined trenches. They read lips the way other people read weather. Hearing has differentiated into castes. Children still startle when a drop-forge speaks without warning. Adults have mostly surrendered the roar; it has become the floor of the world, a warm barometric pressure against the skin. What they listen for are the rare and meaningful deviations—the half-second stutter in a turbine’s note, the wet shriek that means a gasket has decided to die, the soft absence that means fog has stacked sound into a wall and left a pocket of true quiet on the other side. In those pockets whole conversations can happen at ordinary volume, soft as cloth. The old ones navigate by these absences the way bats navigate by unanswered cries.

They live wherever the city’s patient grammar left rooms: the stepped housing blocks that follow residual slopes, the upper lofts of machine-halls where the vibration is a constant bed, the brick stacks whose lower floors still weep the original groundwater, the corrugated sheds that hold the day’s heat long after sunset. Boundaries are acoustic more than architectural. A family may consider a certain fog-bank their evening parlor. Another may claim the dry rain-shadow beneath a particular gantry. Doors are less important than the knowledge of where noise goes to die.

Work is continuity, not employment. Shifts are hereditary in the way rivers are hereditary. One lineage has listened to the same row of pressure chambers for so long that the chambers’ long exhalations are mirrored in the family’s own sleeping breath. Another knows every Boltzmann mood of a cooling tower by the precise white shape of its bannered steam. They bolt new wings onto foundries and then walk the catwalks until the paint is gone and the metal has the cowed, familiar silk of something that has always existed. When they say “the new wing” they mean the one added before their grandparents were born. Time has accepted the industrial tempo; people age inside it like gear-teeth—use polishing them more than years. A face may look forty for three decades and then, almost overnight, look like the fretted brick itself.

Children are uncommon and carefully made. They are taught the liturgy of surfaces: never trust a railing that feels the same temperature as your skin; taste the runoff only with the tip of one finger and wait; if water begins to crawl up a vertical face, give it room and do not speak sharply near it. They play with the pale moths that stitch the streetlamps, catching them in cupped damp hands to watch the faint green fluorescence of their dust before letting them scissor away. They know the V-shaped wakes of the canal creatures and call them, affectionately, aunties or uncles depending on the size of the wake. At night the valley’s single enormous machine-dream rocks them to sleep. Some grow up to hear voices inside the stacked thunder; these are neither celebrated nor cured. They become good at predicting seizures in the great engines.

Food is what the place does not reject. Salt-tolerant scrub steamed until it surrenders. Pale fungi harvested from the permanent driplines. The oily, dense flesh of the web-footed canal things (taken sparingly, with muttered thanks at the grate). Nutrient bricks the color of old snow that come out of the same chemical architectures that produce the bilious runoff. Everything tastes faintly of fretted metal and steam. They prefer it that way. Sweetness is exotic and slightly suspect, like a silence that has arrived without fog to explain it.

There are other lives braided through theirs.

The canal-slick are more than animals and less than neighbors. Oilskin bodies, eyes high and patient, feet that push water without breaking its skin. They rise at dusk. The people leave them the army of small drownings the city always produces—meat scraps, spoiled oil cakes—and in return the warm drainage ways stay open, the chemical greens dilute, and every so often a child who falls in is nudged to the ladder instead of the grate. No treaty was ever spoken. The arrangement is simply one more weather.

The moths carry dust that remembers light in frequencies the city’s ordinary twilight cannot use. Follow one through enough stacked fog banks and time stutters; people have stepped out into shifts that already ended, or into conversations they will not have for another decade. The effect is treated as a mild occupational hazard, like rust-lung or hearing the turbines speak names.

Deeper still, in the cut-stone sub-levels where the plateau’s first joints continue their slow bright weeping, a pressure lives that is neither ghost nor god nor machine. It is the original decision of the rock to hold at this middle height and not fall. The people feel it in the knees and in the sudden sure knowledge that a particular stack will lean one more finger’s width this century. They leave it small bright unfinished things: a gear polished to mirror, a knot of their own grit-shedding hair, a single drop of blood on a flake of shale. The pressure does not answer. It simply continues to mean the plateau. On certain nights when the forges all pause in the same half-second (a rare, valley-wide catch of breath), the oldest residents say you can feel the pressure surface just far enough to taste the steam, as if checking that the arrangement still holds.

And sometimes—standing on a high slag terrace while the light does its ordinary clear work through moving steam—one of them will look down the open weave of yards and ponds and halls and feel the entire place as a single warm-blooded entity whose lungs are cooling towers and whose pulse is the rise and fall of ore lifts so slow they look motionless unless you dedicate an afternoon. In that recognition there is no ecstasy and no terror. Only a settling, the way heavy damp clothes settle onto well-known shoulders. They are not inhabitants. They are one of the ways the place remains itself. They adjust a collar that will never dry. They spit, and the spit tastes of the same wet iron the clouds leave on the higher ridges. Then they walk back down into the deafening, humid now that is not a moment but a climate, and take up again the ancient appointments of noise, water, and stone.

Nothing is in a hurry. Not them. Not the engines. Not the green seams of moss refusing both death and conquest. The plateau holds. They hold with it, dense and damp and perfectly continuous with the long wet argument that made them.
