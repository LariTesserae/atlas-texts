The plateau does not keep its residents so much as it grows them, the way a lung grows the slow necessity of breath. What lives here has learned, across spans that outlast memory, to stop distinguishing itself from the steam.

Begin with the ones that never quite settle into singularity. In the perpetual understory, where violet films drink the green-shifted light and copper-scent rises from crushed cells, there are soft lattices of awareness that have no single body. They are the gel-sweat of the ironwood trunks drawn upward into temporary skins. Over hours a lattice will thicken, lift free of its parent bark, and walk—if walk is the word for a progressive anchoring and release of countless adhesive pads. Each pad tastes the mineral film on the stone, chooses the next warmer or cooler gradient, and the whole colony flows forward like a thought deciding its own next clause. They do not hunt. They audition. A lattice will drape itself across a filter-feeder’s cilia at night, share the cold blue glow for a while, then incorporate a few glowing cells into its own mass before drifting on. By morning the borrowed light has been metabolized into a new chemical memory: the precise taste of last night’s steam density, filed away for the next generation of pads. When two lattices meet they do not fight or merge; they trade edge-patches, a slow embroidery of identity that leaves both slightly rewritten. Locals—when there are locals—call them the revising people. The name is older than any tongue still spoken.

Higher, in the stacked canopies, the six-limbed browsers are only the most obvious citizens. Their wet-slate skins are mapped with living lichen that changes hue according to the twelve-day pulse of the spires. At the cool phase the lichen goes pale silver and the herds gather in the temperature discs, necks intertwining until the animals form temporary towers, six or seven bodies high, so the uppermost can scrape salt blooms from the gray flanks with slow, rasp-like tongues. Their turreted eyes are not for seeing predators—there is little that can threaten something so large and so chemically noxious—but for watching one another’s lichen. Patterns freckle and fade in sequences that carry news: a blocked meander two valleys over, a steam corridor that has held its shape for three nights running, the birth of a new aerial-root lattice that has begun to hum in a key the browsers find soothing. They answer with travel. Entire drainages empty and refill according to conversations conducted in chromatophore semaphore older than the spires themselves. Calves are born already knowing the vocabulary; it is written into the mineral signature of the milk.

Yet even the browsers are only lumen and vessel. Threaded through their bowels, their lymph, the very fluid of their eye-turrets, live the true cartographers: filamentous symbionts that remember every previous cool-phase gathering for centuries. When a browser dies—and they die rarely, usually by simply lying down in a warm vent and allowing the gel-sweat of the forest to reclaim them—the filaments rise as a fine white mist and are inhaled by the nearest living kin. Knowledge continues. Grief, if it exists, is expressed as a temporary darkening of lichen across the whole herd, a communal bruise that lasts exactly one heat cycle.

There are faster lives. The leaf-sewn lungs of the canopy insects expand and contract with a sound like wet silk being drawn through fingers. Inside each temporary lung an entire generation may hatch, mate, and dissolve, their brief intelligence spent entirely on the problem of moisture gradients. They have no names for themselves. The plateau names them by the shape of the leaves they choose: knife-wing, cup-heart, tongue-of-the-thirsting. Predators that hunt by mineral taste leave no tracks, only absences—sudden clean circles where the violet under-film has been licked away down to bare cuticle. These hunters are translucent, roughly the size and geometry of a human hand splayed open. They fold themselves into the steam and unfold only when a signature matches the inherited template of hunger. Their mouths are not mouths but assay plates; they sample, judge, and either pass on or unspool a single hollow fang that drinks until the fang itself becomes too heavy with dissolved metal and drops off, already seeding the next hunter where it falls.

And then there are the ones who stand upright, sometimes.

They are met rarely, usually at the hour when the hanging rainbows begin their imperceptible drift and the dual rain makes every surface into a mirror. Their shapes are suggestions first: a taller density in the mist, a place where the steam corridors swerve as if around a courtesy. When the air clears enough to admit detail, what appears is neither animal nor artifact but a negotiation between the two. Skin like the matte gray of the spires, but pliant, beaded with the same clear gel that runs from the ironwoods. Beneath the skin, slow lights move—heat signatures borrowed from the buried exchangers and returned at a calmer frequency. They have two arms, two legs, a head, the old pentangular plan, yet each limb is a colony: smaller revising lattices live in the crooks of elbows and knees, continually rewriting muscle memory to match the day’s particular gravity of moisture. Their faces are smooth, almost featureless except for a vertical seam where sensory petals can unfurl—petals the deep cold blue of the filter-feeders’ night-cilia. When the petals open, the being tastes the entire valley at once: every mineral footprint, every root-lattice hum, every slight wrongness in a pool’s reflected stars.

They do not speak aloud. Speech is too brittle for a place this wet. Instead they press their palms to the spires and let the self-healing material carry vibrational packets down into the crust and out again through neighboring spires. Conversations travel at the speed of geology. The upright ones drink that answer and alter course accordingly. Their purpose, if purpose is the right frame, appears to be maintenance of thresholds. They encourage certain steam corridors to persist longer, discourage others. They braid aerial roots into lattices that hum in frequencies which soothe the browsers’ young. They stand for long hours at the edges of pools whose reflections show unfamiliar constellations, and with infinite patience they coax the reflected sky into closer agreement with the sky above, or sometimes—deliberately—into greater divergence, as if keeping open a door whose use has not yet arrived.

No one knows whether they built the spires or were built by them. The question has the wrong tense. Time on the plateau is not a line but a tincture; everything is always steeping in everything else. The upright ones are simply the densest local expression of the plateau’s habit of thinking. Where the forest is loud they are quiet. Where the erosion has finished its drama they supply a softer, ongoing revision. They are born—or rather, precipitate—when a revising lattice grows complex enough, borrows enough blue light, and anchors itself around a scaffolding of heat-mapped memory drawn from the nearest spire. They dissolve the same way: an old one will walk into a warm exhalation curtain and open every seam, allowing the component lattices to stream back into the greater respiration of the place. For a few hours afterward the rainbows in that valley lean toward violet.

At night, when the filter-feeders light their slow lightning and the low-frequency thrum of the exchangers becomes almost a lullaby, you can sometimes see the upright ones gathered in the cool discs. They stand among the browser towers with their sensory petals fully unfurled, translating lichen patterns into lasting vibrational records, tasting the newborn calves’ milk for the continuity of old filaments, translating the interlocking densities of the canopy into something that can be stored in the perfect, non-conductive memory of the spire-metal. They are archivists of vapor. They are priests of the gradient. They are the plateau looking at itself through borrowed eyes and deciding, again, to continue.

Everything here is eventual. Everything is permeable. What lives on the plateau is the plateau, differentiated just enough to move, to taste, to remember the slight wrongness of stars, and to keep the long, wet, luminous conversation from ever quite ending.
