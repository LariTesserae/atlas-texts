They are slight.

Not starved—never that, for the pocket feeds what it keeps—but compacted by long habit into frames that waste no heat and snag no hanging root. Their bones have the density of soaked driftwood: light enough to cross a root-mat without announcing weight, dense enough that a fall into black water rarely ends in drowning. Skin takes the stain of tannin until it is the same bruised indigo as the light, then goes further, developing a faint reticulation like the veins of a liverwort. In the rare moments when one of them stands near a membrane-fire, you see that the pattern is not quite decorative; it wakes and shifts, microscopic channels opening and closing to wick moisture away from eyes and mouth or to draw it in when the air grows razor-dry with cold.

They do not cut their hair. It is coaxed, the way they coax walls. Long strands are woven with living strands of bog myrtle while still attached to the scalp, so that a person’s head becomes another small architecture—drip-points, wind-breaks, places for springtails to shelter. Children are born already tangling; by the time they can walk the walkways their hair has been trained into loose lattices that catch midges for later empty­ing into the communal resin pots. Adults sometimes disappear entirely into their own canopies and must be gently pruned free by kin before they forget which parts are self and which are graft.

Eyes are large, but not luminous. They have learned that shine is a liability when every surface already wears its own wet gleam. Instead the pupils open to impossible widths in the perpetual dusk, drinking texture rather than light. An inhabitant can read the tension in a grafted arch by the way moss grows across it, can taste a coming ice-fall in the altered mineral bite of the air, can track the elongated furred thing in the channels by the negative space it leaves in the salamander-pulse. They speak little. When they do, the voice is pitched below the register of ordinary speech—more felt in the sternum than heard—so that it is not stolen by the constant water-tick or the soft clack of pale crustaceans. Words are short, fibrous, concerned almost entirely with tendency: this root is wanting leftward, that pool is thinking of rising, the pocket tonight is longer toward the north.

Their hands are the clearest signature. Fingers longer than seems useful, the outermost phalanges flexible as wil­low and slightly flattened, perfect for sliding between bark and cambium without tearing. Nails are kept thick and slightly cupped; they double as delicate scoops for resin and as quiet scrapers when a bladderwort has grown too ambitious around a storage spiracle. At rest the hands move constantly in small, unconscious weaving motions, rehearsing the next season’s binding even while the body sleeps. Sleep itself is brief and communal. They hang in woven pouches not for safety alone but because the gentle sway of the living limb synchronizes their breathing with the slow fluid redistribution of the plant-mass. Dreaming, they say, is just another way the pocket walks.

They are not many. A full census would strain to reach three dozen, and some of those are more absences than persons—elders who have allowed themselves to be half-swallowed by the older arches, still breathing, still offering advice in the sub-audible creak of wood, but no longer troubling to unlace themselves for meals. Births are rare and carefully timed to periods when the pocket’s corridors are stable. The newborn is rubbed with a paste of crushed bladderwort and spider silk so that its scent matches the town’s; otherwise the midges treat it as open water and the larger things in the channels grow curious. Names are not given so much as grown into. A child might be called Soft-Drip for years until the day its private weaving produces a new kind of load-bearing knot; thereafter it is that knot, spoken as a soft click of tongue against palate.

They keep no animals. The fauna is already too complete, too much a single breathing weather. Instead they maintain relationships of careful non-interference edged with small thefts. Dew is lifted from spider guy-lines before the spiders wake. Empty egg-sacs are harvested and turned into the translucent fire-membranes. When the leggy crustaceans molt, the abandoned shells are collected, fire-hardened, and knapped into the glassy blades that serve for the few true cuts the town still requires. Nothing is chased. Nothing is permanently claimed. If a pale salamander larva takes up residence in a water-pouch, the pouch is simply reassigned to that larva and a new one woven.

Yet there is an older presence moving through them, or perhaps it is simply the pocket thinking with their hands. Certain individuals—never the same ones twice—wake with mud under nails they cleaned the night before, and know they have walked the sonambulary corridors while the rest slept. They return carrying designs no living person taught them: a new way to graft three willows so the resulting arch flexes with ice instead of fracturing, a pattern of resin dots that keeps the furred channel-thing from rising beneath a particular sleeping cluster. These gifts are accepted without ceremony. The town understands it is still being dreamt into final shape, and that they are both dreamers and the dream’s careful editors.

They do not pray. They listen.

At the turning of the miniature weather, when the canopy sheds its load and everything is briefly silvered and loud, the sparse hearths bank higher for a few breaths. Faces lift. In that moment you can see how much of them is already elsewhere—roots of attention sunk deep into the peat walls, awareness dispersed through midges and drip and flexing wood. Then the quiet reseals, the springtails close over the walkways again, and the people return to their pruning, their weaving, their long apprenticeship inside a place that has not yet decided whether it is home or mouth or simply another kind of body learning how to breathe.
