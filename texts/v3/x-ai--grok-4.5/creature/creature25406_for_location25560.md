The ones who live here do not arrive. They precipitate.

In the long damp dark where the plant-mass never stops rewriting its own ribs, life is less a set of bodies than a set of answers to vibration. Everything that persists has learned to read the wood the way other creatures read weather or water. The first answers are the simplest and the most numerous: pale grazers whose limbs have lengthened across uncountable cold seasons until they are mostly tendon and scythe. They hang weightless against vertical faces, seven or eight body-lengths tall when fully extended, their weight distributed across keratin blades that sink only a finger’s depth into cambium and hold. Their mouths are rasps. They harvest the frost-sweet outer cells in slow, deliberate arcs, leaving shallow glyphs that the wood begins to heal before the animal has finished its meal. Between feedings they sleep fused to the wall, heartbeat dropped to a crawl, skin taking on the same faint mineral sheen as the living surface so that a gallery can hold hundreds and still feel empty. Their young are born already gripping. A newborn’s first act is to cut its own birth-caul free with hind scythes and anchor before it can fall into the atrium winds.

Beneath them, just under the skin of the world, move the tickers. Soft, translucent, barely wider than a wrist, they chew connecting galleries that follow the sugar gradients the way roots follow water. Their tunnels are temporary; the plant fills them in days, so the tickers are always migrating, always just ahead of closure. When many pass at once the wall itself seems to seethe, a low metallic ticking that travelers learn to trust as a sign the surface is still alive and not about to shear. Sometimes the tickers surface in wet clusters at a fresh fracture, drink sap, then vanish again. They have no eyes. They taste tension.

In the open hollows the air-livers turn. Membranous, ribbed with cartilage lighter than dry reed, they ride the permanent cold cataracts and warmer spore-laced updrafts. Their flight is not wingbeat so much as continuous adjustment—flaps and vents opening and sealing so they can hang motionless for hours in a thermal seam, listening. What they listen for is the wet percussion of grazers, the scream of something falling, the particular silk-tear of new cells unfolding that signals a flush of richer sap. When they fold and drop they do so in near silence; the sound is only the low pressure-change in the ear. They catch with barbed undersides and feed without landing, then peel away again leaving the grazers still anchored, diminished, already healing.

Higher in the food-web, rarer, the launchers wait. They are dense where everything else is attenuated—thick-bodied, furred in oily gray that sheds rime, limbs built for sudden horizontal power. They scream when they leap so that the Doppler shift itself becomes a ranging tool; the changing pitch tells them how the hollow is shaped, where the next trunk waits. The scream is terrible in the enclosed dark, a sound that scrapes the inner ear and can stampede whole walls of grazers into panic shedding. A launcher that misses its grip does not survive; the drop through multiple galleries is long enough to think, and the landing is final. Those that live grow cautious and enormous, haywain-sized, scarred, their screams half-memory of all the near falls.

And yet these are only the bright, moving notes. The deeper residents are slower.

There are the ones who braid.

They are upright, roughly the old human height though longer in shin and forearm, and their skin holds a permanent faint frostburn flush. Eyes are dark and wet, adapted to caustic dimness; many wear thin membranes of cultivated mycelium across the lids as further filter. They do not cut the wood so much as negotiate with it. Their adzes of sap-crystal are used sparingly, only to encourage a bend already latent in the grain. When they want a stair they massage the cambium over weeks, feeding it saliva and powdered leaf-meal, singing the low continuous tone that tells the wood where to thicken. The stair grows under their hands. When they want a dwelling they weave living root-hair into flexible lattices and pin them into a chosen cleft; within a season the plant has begun to fuse and seal the weave with its own resins. The dwellings are never permanent. The wall will eventually surge or twist, and the braiders simply cut the tethers, harvest what fiber is still useful, and move with the surge, treating the upheaval as weather.

They keep no herds. They follow the grazers’ glyphs and harvest secondary sap from the wounds, boiling it in thin stone cists until it thickens into travel-cakes that taste of mineral sugar and cold lightning. They eat the tickers when the tunnels surface, quietly and with thanks. They fear the launchers but also harvest their oil after a fall, using it to waterproof cordage and to anoint the membranes of the air-livers they sometimes manage to snare for long-distance gliding between distant ribs. Everything is temporary partnership.

Their dead are not buried. A body is carried to a fresh rupture and offered to the exposed cambium. The wood accepts. Within days the corpse is webbed over, drawn in, rewritten; within a season there is only a denser swirl in the grain and, sometimes, a faint warmth when a living hand is laid against it. The braiders say the dead continue as architecture, holding open some gallery that would otherwise have closed, or stiffening a buttress against the next shear. It is not metaphor. They can point to load-bearing walls that once had names.

They are not alone in their niche. Other voices move through the same galleries—smaller, quicker groups who tunnel more than braid, who wear cloaks of ticker-skin and navigate entirely by tongued clicks, mapping the hollows in sound so precise they can return to a single resin-seal years later. There are solitary walkers who have let the mycelial sheets colonize their lungs and spine, who glow faintly sickwards green in the deep dark and no longer need to eat as often, drawing slow sugar straight from contact. There are children born already listening so hard that ordinary speech pains them; they are given to the upper margins to become ears for the whole people, lying for weeks in the thin spear-zone where the wind never stops, reading the planet-sized instrument’s breath and sending news downward in patterns of broken rime.

All of them understand they are not inhabitants so much as a continuing gesture of the wood itself. The plant-mass is still deciding what it is. Every scar they encourage, every stair they persuade into being, every arch they force into collusion becomes another sentence in that undecided language. When they climb to the hard violet noon of the highest spars and stand in the ceaseless wind, they feel the entire continent of living wood tuning itself beneath them—groans, rifle-cracks, silk-tearing whispers—and they know the same vibrations are traveling through their own bones, rewriting them in slower time. Some elect to stay too long in that lightless light until the cold lignifies their joints and they become permanent windbreaks, still warm at the core, still faintly singing.

What lives here is the argument between gravity and emergence, fought in wood and tendon and learned ambition. It has no final form. It only continues, folding outward into the thin air, still wet with its own becoming.
