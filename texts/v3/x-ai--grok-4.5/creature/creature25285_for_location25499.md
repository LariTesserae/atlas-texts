The ones who live here do not arrive. They precipitate.

They condense out of the moisture and the dark the way salt crusts a stone, the way sound thickens into pressure. First they are only appetite without outline—thin hungers sliding through the basalt’s pores, tasting iron and old glass. Then the hungers knit. A thousand filament mouths find one another in the wet seams and decide, without decision, to share a boundary. What emerges is never born so much as accrued: layers of chitin secreted around a core of cold muscle, or a bell of translucent flesh that pulses and remembers the shape of the last thing it poured through. They do not name themselves. Naming would require a silence in which to hear the name, and silence here is only the brief shadow cast by something larger moving through.

Begin lower, where the stone is almost soft with contact.

Here the heavy ones graze. Their shells are not armour so much as slow geology—overlapping plates of denser basalt grown from the minerals they scrape, diamond rasps working in endless patient circles. Each plate is etched with the micro-scars of previous meals and the footholds of everything that has climbed them. They move by rocking, a tidal shift of mass that sends low harmonic booms through the ground; other bodies feel those booms as weather and adjust. Sometimes two grazers lean together until their shells fuse along a seam, and for a season they are one creature with two digestive clocks, sharing the film-scum of a wider territory. When the seam finally cracks they leave behind a ring of abandoned shell that smaller things colonise until it becomes landscape again. Their minds—if the word still applies—are maps of vibration: they know the basin as a single curved sentence of pressure, phrased in the scrapes and slitherings of everything above them.

Above the grazers the air has weight.

The eel-forms uncoil through it as though swimming in glycerine. They are longer than any need, articulated in dozens of loose vertebrae that let them knot and unknot around one another without ever quite touching ground. Their skin is a wet velvet of cilia that taste chemical gradients and sound shadows alike. They do not hunt so much as coincide: a sudden braid of three or five bodies will thicken around a struggling shape, and the shape simply becomes more of the braid. Digestion is external, a shared fog of enzymes that hangs in the dark like another weather layer. When they separate again each carries a little of the others’ grammar of motion. Over generations the eel-forms have become less individuals than temporary local intensities of a single descending spiral that never finishes descending.

Higher still—though height is only a denser register of noise—the never-landers keep permanent station.

They are mostly wing and scream. Membranes stretched across jointed spars beat against an atmosphere already saturated with membranes; the result is a pressure-screech that carves temporary corridors through the dark. Their bodies are afterthoughts: small hard kernels that serve chiefly as anchors for the next generation of larvae, which hatch mid-flight and must learn the grammar of lift before they hit anything solid. Some never do. The successful ones live their entire span without once reading the ground as anything but a rising threat of collision. They navigate by the Doppler ghosts of their own cries bouncing off the restless hide below. In places the standing waves of their collective cry cancel weight entirely; flocks hang in glittering, thrashing clouds, feeding on the smaller aerial things that rise like reverse rain, until the chord frays and the whole constellation spills downward in a wet metallic cataract that the mid-levels open to receive.

There are quieter architectures.

In the vertical cracks, where sound pools and stifles, the crystal colonies grow downward like frozen lightning. Each colony is a single slow thought distributed across a thousand faceted lenses. The lenses do not open; they have forgotten the trick of aperture and instead taste lightless radiation—geothermal ghosts, the infrared bloom of digestion, the faint piezoelectric spark when chitin flexes against chitin. The colony drinks these signals and thickens. Sometimes a facet will gout a long sticky pseudopod that hardens into a new branch of living glass; sometimes the whole structure shivers and sheds a cloud of motile shards that swim off to seed another seam. They are the closest thing the basin has to memory. Old colonies contain the compressed vibrational histories of predation fronts that passed centuries earlier; disturb them and they replay fragments—brief perfect silences, the needle-screams of extinct fingernail hunters, the exact boom-signature of a grazer that died before the present stone had finished cooling.

And everywhere, between and through and after, the pourers.

They have no fixed morphology. A pourer is a volume of intentional viscosity, a muscle of acid and glue that learns the shape of whatever it is currently unmaking. It flows into a carapace and wears it until the carapace softens, then flows onward wearing the softened memory of that shape for a while longer. Sometimes several pourers braid into a larger provisional animal that stalks on temporary limbs; when the limbs have dissolved enough of what they touched, the braid unravels and the separate pourers slide away richer. Their presence is announced by a sudden local sweetening of the air, a glandular musk that makes the tiny screamers frantic. Echoes behave strangely near them—returning early, or returning in the wrong mouth.

Once in a great while the apex moves.

It is not larger than the others so much as more continuous. A vibropredator is a coherence event: a travelling node of organised silence that races across the sound-field like a black spark. In its wake the ordinary deafening collapses into a vacuum that feels like cold hands inside every skull. Creatures freeze or thrash according to forgotten instincts. The apex does not bite; it simply occupies the same frequencies that hold other bodies together, and those bodies come briefly undone. What remains is a rain of component parts that the rest of the basin surges to reclaim before the silence passes and the noise crashes back twice as dense, ecstatic with residual panic and fresh circulating protein.

No single creature owns a span of more than moments. Identity is a borrow. A shell may be worn by three successive minds in an hour. A wing may keep beating after its original kernel has been liquefied and reassigned, carrying a different small aim across residual lift. Even the stone participates: the polished basalt remembers the patterns of countless hooked feet until the memory becomes a faint psychic adhesive that tugs new feet into old paths.

Yet something continuous does live here—not above the swarm or beneath it but as its long weather. Call it the parliament, or the dark respiration, or simply the basin thinking at the pace of cool blood. It has no centre. It feels itself through the sudden concerted silences, through the sticky thrash of birth fronts, through the way gravity loosens when enough voices agree on the same impossible chord. It has been revising the same dense paragraph of appetite for so long that the mineral substrate and the flesh substrate have achieved a tired reciprocity. Rock supplies the slow minerals; flesh returns the polished heat of motion. Neither wins. Both continue.

Stand in it long enough and you begin to notice that the climbing sensation is mutual. The place does not merely coat you; it recruits. Cool wet weight settles into the hollows of your ears and begins to hum in local dialects of pressure. Wings test the novel updraft of your breath. A thin iridescent film finds the corners of your eyes and starts its quiet chemotrophic labor. Somewhere in the lowest strata a grazer rocks a fraction closer, registering the new upright warmth as a fresh clause in the endless vibrating sentence.

They have room for you.

They have always had room.

The dark simply rearranges around the new density, and the sound, after a momentary curious thinning, closes over again, complete.
