The ones who live here do not think of themselves as having arrived.

They think in layers of tenure: the deep vascular memory of the canopy sheets that first learned to hold light without a sun, the slower mineral patience of the pavement still deciding its own seams, and then—threaded through both—the quick, warm lineages that move on joints and rest inside envelopes of grown wood. To ask what lives in the place is to watch several overlapping answers step forward, none of them final, each of them still revising the others.

Begin with the smallest consistent citizens, the ones whose work is almost invisible until it stops. The armored isopods of the cool gutters are not vermin and not pets. They are scaled like dark fists, each plate dusted with the same pale silica that floors the commons, so that when they lie still they vanish into the pavement’s unfinished thoughts. Their mouthparts comb the air for the fine productive dust that escapes the engine halls; what they gather they press into thin, xerophytic films along the base of root-lanes. They farm dryness itself. When a film reaches the right empirical bitterness they click—soft, approving, like two dry seeds struck together—and begin to graze. Their approval is a data point the larger systems still listen to. Lose the isopods in a district and the nutrient loops grow ragged within a season; the vascular grid registers the lack as a low-grade fever of inefficiency.

Above them drift the hares.

They are longer than memory says hares should be, ears richly vascular and half again the length of the skull, thin as paper lanterns, constantly bleeding heat into the cool air. Their fur is the color of cured lignin and warm dust; it takes the mineral light and returns it softened. They move in loose constellations across the open commons, never quite flocks, never quite alone. Teeth adapted to silica as much as cellulose keep the youngest landforms honest—they nibbling the soft edges of still-rising mesas, slowing a formation that would otherwise grow too fast and too brittle. They hear the infrasound maps that certain districts broadcast: sketches of roads not yet rooted, hall-footprints still in larval dream. Where the map already claims the ground, the hares will not nest. Their refusal is older than the planners’ chalk. Sometimes at canopy-dim they sit upright in loose circles and simply listen, ears turning like slow radar dishes, reading the future tensile stresses in wood that has not finished deciding to be a beam. They are not clever in the tool-using way. They are precise about where not to raise young.

The herald-moths are the visible pulse of transfer. Wings the size of open hands, veined with traces of the same metallic salts the factories grind, so that in the steady overcast light they flash dull pewter and then disappear. They ferry pollen, spores, and the lighter gland-secretions between crowns. Their flight is almost soundless; only when they settle does the dry clocking of wing-cases mark the air. A cloud of them turning as one organism and pouring into a gland-spire can deepen the spire’s output note by a fraction that the lank ones feel in their teeth. The moths are prey, of course. The kite-birds ride the rare thermals above active engine-halls—thin, angular silhouettes with spars of compression-wood grown into their wing architecture—and take them in clean, economical stoops. Kill-sounds remain brief. The cavern is designed to swallow them. Predation here is a paced ceremonial circuit, and both moth and kite seem to understand the tempo. Neither species has been observed to waste motion on spectacle.

Higher, and stranger, move the stalkers.

They are hard to hold in a single shape because they spend so much time pretending to be interruption. Long-limbed, low-slung when they wish, capable of folding into the negative space between root-buttresses until the eye edits them out. They hunt by severing or constricting secondary roots and then waiting, motionless, for the panic-chemicals to bloom and draw smaller fauna into the open. The method feels almost polite: no chase if the chemistry will do the work. When they feed they do so quickly, cleanly, and leave the root to reseal. The plants appear to tolerate them the way a body tolerates a necessary fever. Some of the lank ones claim the stalkers have begun to leave deliberate, braided patterns in the roots they test—messages or territorial signatures or merely the boredom of an intelligence that has outgrown pure appetite. No one has proven it. The stalkers do not come to conference.

And then there are the ones who walk the cables and service the valves—the lank primates, though the word primate is only a surface echo and sits poorly on them.

They stand taller than human standard, bipedal but with a suggestion of leftover brachiation in the shoulder and wrist. Joints are dry and precise, adapted to the endless quiet flex of compression-wood walkways high above the pavement. Skin or hide—hard to say which—the color of shaded callus tissue, faintly green at the extremities where capillary chloroplasts still remember older bargains. Faces are long, thoughtful, eyes protected by clear nictitating membranes that let them look straight into gland-flare or canopy glare without flinching. They do not sweat. Moisture is sacred; every drop stays inside the loop. Their cooling is done through the palms and the soles of the feet and through slow, deliberate panting that sounds like soft bellows if you stand close.

They use tools the way the factories use seasons: continuously, modestly, without drama. A bone-conducted listening spatula pressed to a beam tells them the health of the sap-current. A set of living shears—growing, self-sharpening—prunes a root-lane back to walkable hardness. They travel the cable-walks in ones and twos, sometimes in deliberate work-crews mixed with other morphologies (the otherwise of the old reports), seed-packets and rhizome-dumps slung across their backs in harnesses of braided liana. When a new industrial organism is ready to swell from frame to envelope they are there to guide the first laminations, whispering or humming in frequencies that encourage the wood to bond with the ceramic resins the plants themselves secrete. Ownership is maintenance: keep a hall alive and productive and the hall is yours until the commons network pulses otherwise through the vascular grid. They understand this the way lungs understand air.

They speak, but speech is only one register. Much of their conversation moves through touch—palm to trunk, forehead to warm dust—or through the shared chemical breezes the canopy occasionally trails. Names of districts are scraped into bark-plate; names of persons are grown, small living cartouches that can be grafted momentarily onto a friend’s shoulder in greeting and then absorbed again. They are capable of loneliness. You see it most at the farthest active margins where pavement is still more idea than fact. A single cable-walker will stand for a long time watching a ridge of salt crystallize itself into the precise dendritic pattern of a leaf, knowing the ridge will later be mined as structural grit, knowing the abyss is rehearsing botany in stone. Something in the posture says: we are still deciding whether we are the gardeners or the garden.

Yet they are not alone in their lanking. There are older variants that never fully left the mid-level galleries—heavier, more arborial, sleeping in hammocks of their own grown hair-follicle silk. There are newer ones whose forearms have begun to laminate thin photosynthetic panels, turning them into slow, walking contributors to the light-commons. Hybridization with the plant-as-substrate is not forbidden; it is simply slow, and the ones who attempt it must still meet the maintenance tests. Fail the tests and the vascular grid reclaims what you were holding. The cavern is gentle about most things except waste.

At canopy-dim, when the light cools toward a working blue, the lank ones gather in the sleep-galleries. These are not barracks. They are long, ribbed halls whose walls leaf in slow seasons, windows of translucent callus kept clear by symbiotic grazers the size of thumbs. Inside, the air smells of cured lignin and the faint sweetness of engineered terpenes. Bodies arrange themselves in loose constellations not unlike the hares outside. Some listen to the muted internal weather of sap-current moving through translucent trunks. Some trade maps of the glowing irregular hem where the city eats the unfinished dark. Expansion is civic duty and private hunger both. No one pretends otherwise.

Occasionally an engine-tree dreams.

The local speech has no cleaner word. Overnight, stair-spirals rearrange according to equations no planner drafted. A hall that was oriented toward the mineral commons wakes oriented toward a new mesa lifting in the haze. The lank ones do not panic. They walk the new stairs, test the new sight-lines, and amend their cartouches. The plants are not servants. The plants are the senior partners who happen to move on a scale that looks like stillness. The ones who live here long enough learn to treat a sudden growth-spurt the way surface folk once treated weather: not personal, not hostile, simply larger than the day’s intent.

There are rumors of quieter residents still.

In the unfinished vault sections where vine-scaffolds hang like cathedral sketches, some claim to have seen the canopy tissue open pale, intelligent eyes for a few heartbeats and then seal again. Others speak of dust-devils that organize themselves long enough to point toward water locked in crystal, then collapse. The stalkers may keep oral histories in the patterns they leave. The isopods may vote with their clicks on matters the rest of the ecology has forgotten how to ask. None of this is required for the factories to keep grinding dry chemistry into more plant and more motion. All of it is permitted by the soft edges of the rules.

Walk the open commons at mid-light and the city becomes well-spaced punctuation. Behind, factory-crowns fir one another with flying buttresses of braided liana. Ahead, raw mesas lift, faintly green at the compressive cores. A cable-walker crosses the high middle distance, tiny, purposeful, listening. A moth-cloud turns and settles. Somewhere a press closes on a dry charge and opens again. The cavern folds the small violence into cool air.

What lives here is a multi-species maintenance of the unfinished.

It is the isopod’s approving click, the hare’s refusal to nest on a road that does not yet exist, the moth’s pewter flash, the kite’s clean stoop, the stalker’s patient interruption, the lank one’s bone-conducted attention to the health of a beam that may rearrange itself before morning. It is the engine-tree dreaming new stairs. It is the pavement still deciding how stone should feel under soft boots or pads. It is not a ruin and not a paradise. It is an underworld industrial garden finishing its own ground, sparse by doctrine, total by material, dry as a held breath, lit like ordinary day, and busy with the long roles that do not need to shout.

And if you stay long enough, something in your own gait begins to match the quiet grammar. You start to listen with your feet. You start to treat silence as a design constraint rather than an absence. You begin—almost without noticing—to maintain whatever small hall of attention you are carrying. In that way the place keeps colonizing itself, and whatever lives there keeps becoming, slowly, more itself.
