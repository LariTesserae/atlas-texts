The ones who live here move as if the ground is still speaking and they have learned, three generations deep, to answer only with their weight.

They are compact. Not stunted—compacted, the way damp clay is compacted under the heel of a hand. Shoulders slope inward, chests are deep but narrow, spines slightly bowed from lifetimes spent under corbelled stone and low galleries. Their hands are the largest things about them: broad palms, fingers thickened at the joints, nails permanently packed with pale grit. When they walk the packed alleys the sound is a soft continuous hiss of callused soles on grit and chipped tuff. They do not strike the ground; they test it, the way one tests a poorly set block before trusting it with another course.

Skin takes the color of the raw stone itself—ash-cream, dusty rose where the blood shows closer, a faint mineral gray in the creases of knuckles and eyelids. The children are born that pale; sunlight reaches them in clean rectangular bars, yet something in the cool pooling air and the constant stone-dust keeps melanin spare. Eyes are almost always the same muted mineral shade, neither brown nor gray but the color of wet clay after rain. They reflect light rather than absorb it. In the deeper cuttings, where lime-wash throws daylight farther, those eyes seem to carry their own faint phosphorescence, a trick of moisture and pale iris.

They dress in what can be spared from the work. Layers of undyed cloth beaten soft against stone, the weave tight enough to keep grit out of the lungs. Aprons of hide or woven bark where the mauls and antler picks swing. Hair is kept short or bound tight against the scalp; long hair catches dust and snags on projecting shelves. Many paint a thin slurry of lime and clay across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose—part sun-guard, part quiet heraldry, part practical seal against the fine abrasive powder that rises whenever a new face of rock is opened. The marks crack and flake as the day wears on; by evening every face is mapped with pale fracture lines.

They speak little and close. Voices are low, almost conversational even when calling across a terrace, because anything louder dies against the next wall within a few paces. What travels instead is touch and the small percussive language of work: the tap of a maul that means “this course is true,” the scrape of a sole that means “shelf shifting—stand clear,” the soft clack of two stones struck together that means water has been found fresher than expected in a seep pocket. Children learn the code before they learn many words. A child who shouts is hushed not by scolding but by a palm pressed gently over the mouth and then guided to feel the vibration still trapped in the nearest wall. Noise is waste. The stone already groans enough for everyone.

Their days are built around the slow choreography the place demands. Before full light the water-carriers descend—single file, shoulder-brushed against both walls—to the deepest glistening cuts where seepage is collected in shallow tanks lined with clay. They climb again with skins and stoppered jars, distributing the cool mineral taste that never quite loses the flavor of crushed rock. Then the building begins in earnest. It never truly stops. Someone is always raising another course, shoring a ledge that has begun to list, cutting a new sleeping cell into the alive rock where a natural fracture offers the least resistance. The work is not frantic; it is constant, like breathing. Dust hangs in the rectangular bars of light and settles again on skin, on bread, on the soft moss that no one encourages.

They eat sparingly and close to the bone. Flatbreads baked on hot stones, thickeners of wild legume and the slim bitter greens that sometimes root in roof-cracks before being pulled. Dried flesh of the rock doves when nets can be set high on the rims. Insects roasted in the resinous knots of lamp-fuel. Nothing is kept alive inside the basin that cannot justify its volume. A single feral dog that lingered too long near the scrap heaps was driven out with thrown chips of stone and low warnings; space is arithmetic here, and sentiment is a luxury the packing does not allow.

At night the lamps are few and shallow. Resin knots burn with a low orange stubbornness; stone saucers of rendered fat throw circles that the mass of masonry drinks almost immediately. People sit in thresholds and on the low steps of terraces, knees touching because there is nowhere else for knees to go. They listen. The basin continues its unfinished settling—tiny clicks, a longer grinding sigh when a plate adjusts somewhere deeper, the sudden silver thread of night condensation finding a new path down a raw face. Older ones cock their heads the way their grandparents once did when the plain first failed. They can tell, they claim, which groans are only the day’s heat leaving the rock and which mean a gallery wall will need new timber props before the week is out. Whether this is true or simply the story they tell to keep fear organized no longer matters; the listening itself has become a binding rite.

They do not name the depression. To name it would be to pretend it has finished arriving. Instead they speak of “the down,” “the raw,” “inside the crack.” Their own collective name is equally provisional, more description than title: the ones who stayed after the ground went, the shelf-people, the tight-packed, the mortar-blooded. Third-generation children sometimes invent softer private names for one another—words that sound like water moving over grit or like the muffled tick of a distant maul—but these names stay inside the family sleeping-cell and are not spoken in the alleys.

There is an understanding, rarely voiced, that they are not living on the geology so much as midwifing it. Every wall they raise is also a brace. Every niche cut into the living rock is both shelter and relief of pressure. When a section of cliff calving threatens a whole terrace, the response is not evacuation but swarm: bodies locked shoulder to shoulder, timber and stone passed hand to hand, the unstable mass shored and then immediately occupied so that human weight and masonry weight become a single argument for staying. They believe—without theology, only with the surety of practiced marrow—that if they ever left the basin entirely, if the dense interlocking of rooms and galleries ever staggered, the whole unfinished hollow might finish collapsing simply for lack of anything that insisted otherwise.

Outsiders who descend the switchbacks (rare, brief, usually traders with rope and resin and the few tools that cannot crowbarred from local stone) find them courteous and impenetrable. The packed streets force any visitor into the same slow sideways progress as everyone else. Conversation is offered in the same low register. Food and water are shared without flourish. Yet the visitor feels the constant gentle pressure of bodies that know exactly how much space a body may occupy and will not yield a finger-width more. After a day or two most outsiders climb back out, ears ringing with the quiet, lungs coated with the fine pale dust that tastes of something still being born. They carry away the impression of a people who have become a kind of weathering themselves—slow, meticulous, intimate with fracture, patient with the open wound of the land.

And still the city thickens. New chambers open like dens. Smoke-holes multiply across the fretted cliffs. The low irregular ticking of mauls continues through the cool hours, answering the deeper, older ticks of stone that has not yet decided its final angle. The ones who live here do not dream of elsewhere. Their dreams, when they surface at all, are of tighter fits, of courses laid so true that mortar becomes unnecessary, of a basin finally finished because every instability has been occupied, braced, and named only by the pressure of continual human insistence. They sleep shoulder to shoulder, breath syncing with the seep and the settling, skins the color of the walls that hold them, hands already curling in sleep around the memory of the next rough block.
