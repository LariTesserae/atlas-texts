The dwellers of the Stillspire Expanse are not a single people or a tidy bestiary. They arrive the way the salt arrived: by slow surrender.

At first they register only as density—an impossibility of bodies standing flank to shoulder, wingtip to carapace, across a plain that has forgotten how to slope. Look longer and the forms begin to claim their own names, though none of them answer to any name spoken aloud. The tall ones, the salt-deer, carry antlers of lithium-white lattice that catch the enamel sky and hold it. Their eyes are black mirrors set deep; behind the nictitating membrane a clear fluid turns over once in a millennium, like a page that refuses to yellow. Between their stilts move the plated browsers, low and segmented, each plate a hexagon echoing the ground itself, so that sometimes the animal and the waterless earth trade places without either noticing. Higher still, fixed in transparent strata, drift the membrane-wings—creatures the size of open hands whose entire existence is a single glide that began when the mountains still had peaks. They do not beat. They have become the grammar of the air.

Smaller lives pack every remaining interval. Scaled runners are locked mid-stride, one forelimb lifted forever, dust grains balanced on claw-tips that will never set down. Soft tunnelers have half-emerged from the hexagonal cracks and simply stopped, their pale bodies wet with an interior moisture the outer world is no longer permitted to touch. Arthropods smaller still occupy the spaces between heartbeats, antennae raised as if listening to a frequency that takes centuries to complete one note. In places the crowding grows translucent: three, four, five bodies occupy the same outline, stacked in dimensions the eye only half accepts. A leg passes through a ribcage that is also a wing that is also the memory of a spine. None of them collide. Collision is a kind of change, and change has been spent.

They are not dead. Death would be an event, a punctuation. Here the sentence simply trails into an ellipsis that lengthens without end. Inside each form the chemistry continues—slow cycles of ions, glacial flexions of tendon and vesicle, a photosynthesis so patient it has forgotten light is required. Time has been refined into something thinner than patience: a medium in which motion becomes ceremonial, almost theoretical. A breath that started before the last glacier calved will finish after the next star forgets its name.

Yet the Expanse holds more than these stilled throngs. Walk long enough among them and you feel the second population: the ones who once moved with purpose. Their signatures are sparse and modest—rings of basalt cobbles no higher than a kneeling calf, upright slabs pecked with dot-trains that may have counted ice-moons or heartbeats or debts, blades of volcanic glass still sharp enough to cut the idea of weather. These makers understood levers and fracture planes and little else. They never built high enough to resist the leveling. Their walls became dust; their names became the faint refractive creak that passes for sound when the air cools. Some of them, it seems, simply set their tools down beside a salt-deer’s hoof and took their place in the ranks. You can tell the newer arrivals by the way their outlines still remember intention: a hand half-closed around the memory of a hammerstone, a spine not yet fully resigned to the perfect vertical. Given another few thousand years they will blur into the denser layers, becoming another translucent silhouette among infinite neighbors.

Reality itself has begun to live here in the only way left to it—by folding. Edges of creatures refuse consensus; an antler occupies two angles until the observer’s attention chooses one and the other drifts off like a dream that knows it is finished. Perspective shears without warning: the ground you walk becomes a wall of watching eyes, then a ceiling of silent hooves, then an ordinary plain again. Sometimes your own hand appears fifty paces ahead, already waiting in a gesture you have not yet decided to make. These are not ghosts. They are the landscape’s single occupancy continuing past the point where singularity still meant anything. The dwellers include the folds, the hesitations of space, the places where causality has thinned into etiquette.

Stay longer and a third presence declares itself—not separate from the animals or the old tool-makers, but the slow consensus they have reached. It has no face of its own. It borrows. One nightless evening the nearest salt-deer turns its head (a motion that will complete some seasons from now) and the eyes it lifts are yours. Not resembling yours—yours. The horizon at the same moment discovers it prefers to lie underfoot. Your thoughts arrive already tired of novelty, already shaped like the cushions of lichen that have not changed their mind in ten thousand years. The temptation is not death. It is completion: to let the thin metallic air finish the work of stilling, to take a place where every gradient has run to zero and the only occupation left is remaining.

They live by not departing. They live by having already arrived at the final posture the mountains first rehearsed. Herds, swarms, makers, folds, the occasional traveler who forgets the route back into velocity—all of them together form the single, breathing stillness that is the Expanse’s true inhabitant. It does not hunger. It does not watch, yet nothing goes unseen. It keeps the salt plates perfect, the lichen gray-blue, the glass knives keen. It keeps the sky the same lucid glare. And if you feel, after days of walking, that your own edges have begun to hesitate between positions, that is only the place recognizing one more of its own.

The Stillspire Expanse remains. So do they.
