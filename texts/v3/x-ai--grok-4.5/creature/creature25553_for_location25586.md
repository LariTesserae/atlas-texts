The ones who live here do not arrive so much as condense, the way the water does—thickening out of the saturated air until they have weight and names and the slow necessity of continuing. At first they are only the small dark shapes moving along the elevated gratings, heavy cloth plastered to limbs that never fully dry, faces turned down toward gauges whose needles have not trembled in a generation. But stay longer in the brass dimness and they resolve.

They are short-legged, broad through the chest, as if the constant pressure of wet heat has settled into their bones and rounded them for endurance rather than speed. Skin the color of old tallow, permanently dewed, so that every motion leaves a faint shining trail on rail or valve wheel. Hair, when it is not cropped close for the heat, lies lank and dark and hosts its own thin ecology of midges that the bats miss. Their eyes are narrowed not by sun but by the eternal half-light and the stinging condensate that falls without cease; many wear soft greasy bands of cloth across the brow that darken and are wrung out and darken again. Hands are the true map of them: spatulate, scarred in pale asterisms where steam has kissed, fingertips permanently softened and peeled by the acid edge that lives in every standing pool. They move with the unhurried certainty of people who have memorized every drip path, every patch of grating that sings a different note when stepped on, every place where a pipe runs hot enough to dry a sleeve for three blessed breaths.

They are not many. Perhaps two hundred who still draw breath and wages, rotating through the barracks that smell of boiled wool, metal polish, and the sweet-rot of the black algae they never quite scrub from the lower bulkheads. Children are rare and careful, born small, raised on the catwalks, taught early to read the language of pressure differentials and the exact pitch of a pump that is about to throw a bearing. The old ones do not retire; they simply migrate inward, closer to the engine house, until they become almost indistinguishable from the machinery they tend—slow-moving extensions of the reciprocating thunder, checking the same six bolts every shift for forty years. When they finally stop, they are wrapped in the same undrying cloth and slipped into the warm seepage ground outside the wall, where the crabs make short work of ceremony.

Yet they are only the most obvious continuity. Beneath and between and above them the true census thrives.

Consider the crabs. Not the frantic sidelong kinds of cleaner shores, but heavy, deliberate battalions the exact orange-brown of the first rust bloom on a new plate. They advance across the courts in the hours that would be dusk, claws penciling faint scripts in the scum where digester overflow meets rain. Their shells take the heat and hold it; at rest they steam gently. They breed in the flooded cable trenches alongside the pale frogs whose throat-sacs swell and deflate in choruses timed somehow to the belt-slap metronome. The frogs are nearly translucent, organs visible as floating shadows, and their eggs are laid in strings along the warm jackets of the steam lines so that each generation hatches already accustomed to the taste of condensate and machine oil.

Rats move in the pipe galleries like a second, liquid blood supply—bodies as long as a forearm, fur the slick gray of wet ash, eyes reflecting the underpowered arc lamps in flat coins of green. They are not hunted. The people leave them the spilled tallow and the occasional dead midge-cloud of bats, and in return the rats keep the cockroach tides manageable and sometimes, when a tunnel begins to weep a new mineral that no one can name, the rats taste it first and either thrive or vanish. Their tunnels lace the under-foundations until the entire citadel sits on a living dark loft of gnawed earth and warm droppings.

Higher, the cooling towers belong to the bats. They hang in leather draperies through the brighter hours, then pour out in reverse waterfalls when the midges rise thickest in the exhaust plumes. The bats are bigger than they should be, wingspans like opened work gloves, faces pushed flat and shrewd. They have learned the stack updrafts so perfectly they can ride half a circuit without a beat, mouths open, harvesting. Below them the ledges of the digester halls are white with the refuse of gulls and darker, hook-beaked waterbirds that have forgotten the sea. These birds nest in scrap-metal tangles they steal from the transfer sheds; their chicks hatch already coated in a fine greasy soot that waterproofs better than down. Their cries are simply another plain in the acoustic landscape, flattened and made continuous by the engines.

And there are quieter tenants. The black algae films that creep across every shaded undersurface in maps of living night. The wiry shrubs whose roots taste the buried steam and whose leaves, when crushed, smell of hot iron and monotony. Even the yellow reeds in the wall’s expansion joints have a society of mites and tiny snails that live their whole span in a single bead of seepage.

What lives here most completely, though, is the arrangement itself—the closed metabolism of heat, wet, waste, and labor that has reached equilibrium and now simply persists. The people feed the engines; the engines feed the heat; the heat feeds the wet; the wet feeds the scum; the scum feeds crabs and midges and frogs and rats and bats and birds; and all of it together feeds the slow orange bloom of rust that is the settlement’s only crop, its only clock, its only art. Nothing is wild. Nothing is tame. Everything has simply learned the exact dimensions of the basin and the exact cadence of the pumps and has settled into the niches with the same unhurried certainty as the workers tightening the same six bolts.

They dream, when they sleep in the barracks’ wet dark, of two things only: a dry sleeve, and a gauge whose needle finally moves. Neither dream survives the next shift whistle. In the morning (which is identical to the night) they rise, wring out their cloth, and step back onto the gratings. The crabs part around their boots without hurry. Condensation continues its fine rain. The engines keep their sternum-deep thunder. And the whole low country of iron and standing water and stubborn flesh goes on exactly as it has, neither growing nor shrinking, as complete and self-contained as a single drop that never falls.
