The things that live here do not arrive. They accrete.

At first you notice only the motion that is already accounted for: the dog-sized birds with their sheet-metal cries, wedged into the gantries like corroded fittings. Their feathers are the dull black of old grease, edged with a faint verdigris where the perpetual damp has oxidized them. They do not hunt so much as dislodge. A slow wingbeat against a cooling tower loosens a plate of scale; the bird descends after it in a spiral that takes the better part of an hour, lands without sound, and begins to dismantle the fragment with a beak that works like a pair of tin snips. What they swallow is mostly rust and mineral grit. The rest they hammer into the soft tar of their nests until the nests themselves become small, heavy machine parts that will someday fall and be mistaken for debris.

Lower, the multi-legged herds cross the open scree. They are the color of wet soot because they are made of it—bodies compacted from the same patient rain of particles that the plateau sheds. Each leg is a jointed stylus; when they pause, which is often, the tips continue to tick against the stone in faint, arrhythmic clocks, writing nothing. They move as a single dissolving edge, a dusk within the dusk. If you stand in their path they flow around you without acknowledgment, leaving only a stronger taste of metal in the mouth and a thin black film on your boots that will not wipe away. At night (a word that means little here) they climb the factory walls in vertical processions and press themselves into the brickwork until they are indistinguishable from stains. Some never come down again. Buildings have been known to gain new buttresses this way, soot-legged and load-bearing.

The smaller throngs are harder to hold in mind. Luminous moths the size of open hands beat against the undersides of bridges, their wings powdered with the same fine ash that hangs in the air; each wing-beat leaves a brief blue-green sigmoid that floats like a discarded signature before fading. Hard-shelled crawlers—segmented, antennae like tarnished wire—move in the condensation channels, leaving trails of phosphorescent slime that spell long, incomplete equations across the brick. The almost-mammalian singers root in the vine mats. They are bare, folded creatures the gray of untreated canvas, and their chords arise from paired slits in the throat that vibrate without breath. The sound sits just under language, a grammar of almost-words that never resolve. Workers walking home sometimes hum along for half a street and then forget why their mouths are open.

But these are only the movable geology. What truly lives here has learned to occupy the same slow categories as stone and engine.

They call themselves, when they call themselves anything, the Shift. The name is less identity than scheduling note. You first see them at the edges of the great reciprocating halls: elongated figures in layered coats the color of wet iron and oxidized copper, the same palette as the shrubs. The coats are not sewn so much as laminated—sheet after sheet of grease-cloth, moss felt, and hammered foil, each layer stitched with wire that has already begun to green. Their faces are long, damp, and expressionless, the skin having taken on the faint translucency of things kept too long in steam. Eyes are large and slow to blink, colored the dull mineral green of the mosses; they catch the low light and hold it a moment longer than they should, as if reluctant to return it to the haze.

They move the way the buildings settle: almost imperceptibly, then all at once by a centimeter. A hand reaches for a valve and the reaching takes the better part of a minute; the fingers close, and the valve turns with the same grave deliberation as a glacier calving. They do not speak often. When they do, the words emerge in the same register as the singers—chords felt more than heard, phrases that last longer than their meanings. Conversations can span entire shifts. A question asked at the start of a forty-minute hour may receive its answer three clock-days later, delivered without urgency to whatever body now stands where the questioner stood. Memory, among them, is a kind of sediment. They remember in layers. Deeper strata are cooler and more certain.

Their work is maintenance that never quite completes itself. They walk the arterial viaducts with long-handled brushes, sweeping grit from one end of a span to the other; by the time they finish, new grit has already settled behind them. They chalk the anonymous diagrams onto boiler-room floors—precise modifications to piston timing or steam-channel curvature—then leave before anyone can ask who they are. The diagrams are always improvements, never authorship. In the long rectangular basins of black water they set measuring rods and leave them for years, returning only to note that the water has neither risen nor fallen but has nevertheless changed its mind about depth. Some of them tend the copper-leaved shrubs, pruning with shears so slow the cut stems do not bleed sap until the next day. Others simply stand in the thinner districts where scaffolding gives way to raw basalt, facing outward, as if listening to the unfinished mountain still grinding itself higher.

They are not untouched by the fraying. Reality loosens around them the way damp loosens brick. A Shift walking a straight boulevard may arrive at both ends simultaneously and then have to wait for the rest of itself to catch up. Shadows detach more readily from their heels than from other objects; sometimes a shadow will drift into a factory doorway and take up tools, working a full shift before remembering to return. Doorways open for them onto rooms that should not fit: dens lined with soft soot and luminous slime, workshops where the walls breathe like vanishing points. One will step through a maintenance hatch and emerge an hour later (or a week, or forty minutes) younger or older by a thin margin, coat more laminated or newly frayed. They notice these things the way one notices weather. Adjustments are made. No records are kept.

Children, when they appear, do so without ceremony—small damp figures already wearing miniature layered coats, already moving at the local pace. They are not born so much as weathered into existence, the way tool marks weather into the raised stone at the plateau’s edge. They play games that take seasons: rolling a single ball of compacted grit across a plaza until it grows large enough to be mistaken for one of the soot-legged herd, or sitting perfectly still beneath a vine drip until moss begins to take on their shoulders. Education is apprenticeship to slowness. A child may spend a year learning how to turn a single valve the correct fraction of a degree, or how to hear the difference between the mountain’s grinding and the engines’ thunder. By the time they are tall enough to reach the higher catwalks they already understand that time is a soft metal, easily reworked.

At the outer margins, where the city thins and the engines fall silent one by one, the inhabitants change again. Here the coats are heavier, the faces longer, the green eyes clouded by cold mist. Some have begun to incorporate the plateau itself: basalt grit embedded in the skin like dark freckles, fingers lengthening into stylus-like points that leave tick-marks on the unfinished stone. They walk farther out each generation, following the almost inaudible grinding, and fewer return. Those who do bring back new chalk diagrams scratched on bare rock—modifications not to engines but to the idea of height. It is said (in the long, chorded way things are said here) that the outermost of them no longer need coats, that their bodies have learned to shed particles in the same patient rain as the mountain, that they are becoming the next layer of whatever force first punched this slab of world upward. No one has gone far enough to confirm it. Distance itself softens at that edge; a day’s walk outward can return you to the same night you left, or to a night that has not yet learned how to be dark.

And beneath all of this—under the basements, under the black-water basins, under the slow thunder of the pistons—something else listens. It is not separate from the Shift. It may be what the Shift are slowly turning into, or what they have always been when the laminated coats and the mineral eyes are set aside. You feel it most when a streetlamp’s light finally puddles on the pavement and continues to glow: a vast, unhurried attention pressing up from below the basalt, patient as erosion, tasting of metal and wet stone. It does not speak. It does not need to. Every piston stroke, every dripping vine, every chord hung just below language is already its voice. The plateau does not remember being lowland. Neither do they. They live in the upwardness itself, in the continuous rain of grit, in the soft failure of cause and effect. They are the city remembering how to be mountain, and the mountain experimenting with the habit of calling itself a city.

They will still be here when the last engine seizes and the last bird nests in its quiet gears. They will sweep the grit. They will chalk the next diagram. They will stand at the thinning edge and listen to the unfinished dark. And the dark, unfinished, will continue to become them.
