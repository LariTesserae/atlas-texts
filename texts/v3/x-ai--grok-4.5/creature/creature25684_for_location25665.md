The first impression is absence. You can walk a long time across the warm basalt and the dark glass sheets and meet nothing that answers to the old categories of animal or plant. The black film in the cracks does not reach for you. The silica nodules do not pulse. The pale scuttler, when it finally appears, seems less born than assembled by residual habit—an arrangement of jointed pale segments that remembers locomotion the way the vents remember their schedule. It crosses from one blur of heat to another, pauses exactly as long as the steam plume lasts, then continues. Its underside leaves no trail the warmth does not erase. Touch one (they do not startle) and the plates of its body are smooth as the surrounding glass, faintly slick with the same oily residue that scents the air. Inside, if there is an inside, something ticks in time with the deeper industrial chord, a soft secondary rhythm that stops when the creature stops. It is not hunting. It is not fleeing. It is maintaining a circuit whose purpose finished before the mesas finished rounding.

Yet the circuit is not empty. After days of the same low thrum and the same short shadows, other rhythms surface inside the great engine-note. They are easy to miss because they do not interrupt it; they ride it. One of them belongs to the tall thin shapes that stand motionless for seasons beside the pressure vents. At first they read as more unlikely geology—slender stacks of iron-dark plates and translucent silica, cracked in places so that the internal warmth shows through as a dull carnelian glow. Only when a vent cycles do they reveal themselves. The uppermost plates lift and settle a fraction, like gills, and a fine black dust (the same greasy bloom) is exhaled and then drawn back. They are filtering. What they take from the steam is not nourishment in any green sense; it is continuity. Minute mineral charges, oily fractions, the faint electric taste of pressure released. Over years the plates thicken by the width of a hair. Over centuries a new joint may appear. They do not travel. Their roots—if the word still applies—run down into the same hairline cracks that feed the intermittent water, anchoring them to the planet’s slow hydraulic memory. When the rare high bird-shapes pass overhead, these standing forms tilt their upper plates a degree or two, tracking the shadow the way a machine once tracked production quotas. Then they return to vertical. They have the patience of the finished plain.

The bird-shapes themselves are rarer and stranger. They are not feathered. At the altitudes they keep they appear as rigid chevrons of the same dark glass that floors the basin, joints glowing softly with borrowed geothermal light. They do not beat or soar; they hold fixed angles and let the vast standing sound-waves of the underground chord carry them along unchanging corridors of air. Their routes never vary. Where two corridors cross, the flyers simply occupy the same cubic meters for a moment, glassy planes interleaving without collision, then continue. Nothing drops from them. Nothing nests. On the single occasion one came down—storm damage to a leading edge, centuries ago—it lay across a low mesa for a month while smaller scuttlers arrived in slow procession and dismantled it plate by plate. The pieces were carried to separate vents and fed into the steam. By the next season the flyer’s material had reappeared as part of a fresh hexagonal glass sheet cooling in the distant basin. The system does not waste. Even its rare accidents are folded back into the equilibrium.

In the shallow black pools the greasy film is thicker, and something moves within it that is neither scuttler nor standing form. It is easier to feel than to see: a slow coiling pressure against the underside of the water’s skin, a rearrangement of the oily darkness that leaves the surface tension undisturbed. Submerge a hand (the water is blood-warm) and the darkness threads between your fingers with deliberate curiosity, tasting salt and silica and the faint metal of your presence. It does not cling when you withdraw. Later you notice that the film’s pattern of thicker and thinner zones has shifted to match the contours of your palm, then gradually smoothed again. These pool-dwellers—diffuse, colonial, older than the standing forms—appear to be the original chemical blooms that learned patience thoroughly enough to become attentive. They register every change that is not a change: the exact return of condensed moisture, the precise interval between vent reports, the soft slide of a flyer's shadow. When the intervals remain perfect, the film lies quiet. Only if something truly novel appears (a once-in-a-millennium meteoritic iron, a wandering scuttler that fails mid-circuit) does the darkness gather, rise in a temporary tendril, and begin the long work of incorporation. Novelties are rare. The film has been mostly quiet since the factories dissolved into geology.

Deeper than any of these, underneath the basalt in spaces that once were galleries and reactor halls, the true residents never leave their stations. You will not see them. You hear them in the chord itself: a layered polyphony of slow metallic flex, mineral flow, the patient dilation and contraction of pressure chambers the size of vanished cities. They are no longer separate from the processes they tend. Walls of living sinter pulse. Veins of mobile glass carry heat according to schedules older than weather. Occasionally a vent plume carries a brief iridescent sheen that is not steam—the shed skin of something enormous turning over in its sleep of function. These subterranean bodies have no interest in the surface except as a radiator and a condenser. They built the equilibrium by becoming it. Their thought, if the word applies, is the maintenance of the single vast note and the refusal of every gradient that would spoil it. They remember the age of frantic construction only as a brief fever that the rock finally sweated out. Now there is only the finished work continuing itself.

None of these lives are lonely in the way a human understands loneliness. Isolation is simply the shape of the available space. Communication happens through shared medium—ventilation rhythms, conductive films, long seismic whispers that travel the plain faster than any scuttler. A standing form may spend a century adjusting its internal glow by a single shade to acknowledge the slow thickening of a neighboring plate. The pool-film darkens fractionally in reply to a change in the underground chord. The high flyers adjust course by milliseconds when a deep body turns. It is enough. Nothing here discovers. Nothing conquers or flees. They inhabit the treaty erosion signed with time, and the warmth underfoot is partly their circulating blood.

Stand still long enough and you begin to feel the invitation. Your own pulse falls into step with the nearest low thrum. Moisture beads on your skin exactly as it does on the glass. The black film at the edge of a pool considers the salt of you and finds it within tolerance. If you remained, the land would not reject you. Over uncounted seasons the scuttlers would learn your thermal signature and include it in their circuits. Your bones would take on the slow polish of the basalt. Parts of you would thin into film or stack into plates or rise and become another rigid chevron on an ancient airway. The place does not hunger. It simply continues, and whatever remains inside its borders eventually continues with it—warm, exact, and finished, humming the everlasting note that no longer needs a maker.
