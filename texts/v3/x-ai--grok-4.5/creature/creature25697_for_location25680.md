The ones who live here are not one kind of thing, and they do not agree among themselves what they are. In the cool mineral dark their outlines stay provisional a long time, as if the pocket has not finished deciding which shapes it will keep. Begin with the motion itself: the slow boil of bodies through the mesh of ladders and underbellies, the near-constant glancing contact of shoulder to bulkhead, claw to rung, wing to warm duct. Everything that belongs here has learned to occupy the same volumes at once without claim or apology.

There are the millers first, because they are everywhere and always. Pale, segmented, hard as kiln-fired slip, they move in loose constellations across every surface that holds dust. Their mouths are patient rasps; they graze the rock flour that sifts forever from the still-settling walls, the metallic bloom that flakes from gear teeth, the fine ash that collects in the elbows of pneumatic lines. When they have fed they leave polished tracks that catch the caged yellow light like snail-silver, and sometimes—especially on the wider basalt shelves or the quiet backs of sealed cisterns—they pause and arrange their dry excreta into faint concentric rings. No one sweeps the rings away. The rings persist for days or weeks, rearranging themselves by minute degrees, until a millimeter slip of the mountain erases them or a new generation begins again. The millers do not seem to mind. Their eyes (if the dark pits beneath the cephalic shield are eyes) hold no urgency. They simply continue, denser after every blast of the stamp-presses, thinning only when the dynamos idle.

Above them and among them move the cliff-hounds. Soft-footed, cat-sized, coat the color of cooled clinker, they nest in the residual warmth of motor housings and the hollow spines of old conveyor frames. Their teeth click when the electrical pitch changes—an involuntary metronome that the workers have learned to trust more than the official gauges. A cliff-hound will press its narrow skull against a vibrating rail and listen with its whole body; if the frequency is wrong it will yiprrow once, softly, and the nearest crew will already be reaching for tools. They travel the vertical city the way smoke travels, taking the thermals, slipping through gaps no wider than a wrist. At rest they coil into the same spaces the millers polish, and neither species displaces the other. Sometimes a hound will nose a miller ring with grave attention, then leave it intact. Their paws never quite leave prints; the dust they disturb resettles in patterns that look almost deliberate.

The singer-mites are less body than weather. In the low, directionless light they appear first as a thickening of the air, a granular pressure against the eardrums, a faint itch at the corners of vision. Only when a shaft of borrowed daylight—rare, sealed, quickly shuttered—cuts through a loft do you see the wings: translucent, veined like cooled slag, beating in thermal sheets rather than individual strokes. They ride the exhaust of the salt-loops and the leak-heat of the arc lamps. Their song is not sound so much as texture; it settles into the felt lagging and the soft cast of the fracture faces until the entire pocket seems to hum at the edge of hearing. Workers moving through a dense cloud will pause, turn an ear, and continue with slightly altered step, as if the mites have just revised the floor plan by a few degrees. The mites do not land. They do not feed in any way that leaves residue twice. They simply are the air’s way of remembering that it is crowded.

Lower, along the basal ledges where the fault lens thickens into darker, slower stone, the slag-things migrate. They are larger, slower, less like animals than like collapsed furnaces that decided to keep walking. Their hides are crusted, vesicular, warm to the touch long after the surrounding metal has gone cool. They sort. With patient, shovel-like forelimbs they gather the rejected ore scraps, the sheared rivets, the spent flux powders, and they arrange these into neat, rejected piles that the assay crews later find uncannily well-graded. No one ordered the sorting. The slag-things simply arrive at the end of a shift, work through the night in near silence, and by the time the next bell vibrates through the railings the ledges are clearer and the piles stand ready. When the mountain shrugs and a new shelf drops into usefulness, the slag-things are often already there, as if they felt the slip coming in their slow mineral blood.

And then there are the others—the ones who build and unbolt and write the shifting quotas in grease pencil on bulkhead plates. They are bipedal, narrow through the shoulder from generations of turning sideways in galleries that never widen. Their skin has taken the tone of the rock flour, a pale ashy gray that never quite flushes. Eyes are large, dark-adapted, reflecting the caged filaments in small steady points. They dress in layered felt and ash-cloth, soft-soled, every edge bound against snagging; their pockets are many and flat, filled with folding rules, stub pencils, capsules of powdered reagent, spare fuses wrapped in waxed linen. They speak close, mouths nearly at ears, because loudness is waste and because the pocket’s strange acoustics already carry enough. Their hands are scavenged of softness: callus, small burn scars from molten salt that never wet them, the crescent bruises of torque wrenches used in precipitous places. They sleep in the pressed cubes wedged between machine halls, six or eight to a bulkhead nest, hammocks staggered so that the sway of one becomes the lullaby of the next. Children (there are children) learn the ladders before they finish learning speech; they move through the vertical mesh like the cliff-hounds, silent, proprietary, already listening for the dynamos’ pitch.

They do not name themselves as a people separate from the pocket. They say “we” and the word includes the millers’ rings, the hounds’ clicks, the mites’ pressure, the slag-things’ neat piles. When a catwalk drops half a degree in the night they are already moving—crablike teams unbolting entire suites, walking machines to new shelves under lamps that sway like slow pendulums. Scaffold is temporary by doctrine; every surface carries the scars of previous attachments, bolt-holes peened flush, weld beads left like pale lichen. They keep the brass entry plate polished not out of reverence but because it is always slightly warmer than it should be, and warmth is data. They leave the compass needles to their preferred bulkheads. They watch the dust hang a fraction too long in the rare daylight shafts and simply adjust their timing, as if the pocket’s mild strangeness were another production variable.

At the highest loft, where the crystal-lined blind end pinches the fault closed, a few of them sometimes stand watch at the sealed ports. When the exterior shutters open (rarely, under procedure thick as liturgy) they look out at the knife-edged ridges and the thin sky and the absolute absence of green. They do not linger. The shutters close; the dense quiet reasserts itself—cool air, soft machine-song, the constant nearness of every other life that has decided this wound is home. In that closeness something unfinished continues to live: a city that is still deciding its own edges, a people still taking shape inside the mountain’s slow, unfinished tear. They are the motion that does not stop, the soft thunder kept private, the ones who turned a place that should have been only fracture into a thronged and working dark.
