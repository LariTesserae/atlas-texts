The ones who live here do not arrive by climbing. They condense.

At first you notice only the absences they leave arranged like measured breaths across the stone. A hopper membrane, stretched and cured, folded into a three-cornered sack and weighted with a single polished boss-stone so the wind cannot claim it whole. A thorn-paddle shaved to translucence and stitched along one edge with mite-silk so fine it disappears until the light strikes it brass-edge on; the resulting kite is never flown in open air but held low, almost dragging, its hooked hairs drinking the ground-gusts that skate the pavements. These are not abandoned. They are paused. The pause itself has a temperature.

Then the scent—dry, faintly metallic, like blood remembered by iron rather than by flesh. It pools in the lee of the stone rings, strongest where the ghost-fires once were. If you kneel and breathe it, the mites briefly reverse their spiral, spinning outward as if from a center that is warmer than the rock. That warmth is the first true announcement of a body.

They are tall when they choose to be, but height is a temporary negotiation. Most often they move close to the stone, spines curved into the same bolster shapes the mountain itself prefers, so that from any distance they read as further undulations of the Cusp. Their skin—if the word still applies—is a matte unknown color that takes the brass light and returns it slightly delayed, the way heat-shimmer returns the horizon. Under that delay something yaws and resettles: plates, or scutes, or simply decisions about surface. When the wind rises they lean into it until the lean becomes a second skeleton, external, made of tensed cords braided from their own shed fibers and the leftover membranes of hoppers that rode too close. These cords whip and sing; the song is how they speak to one another across the open stone without opening mouths they prefer to keep sealed against the dust.

Faces are rare and brief. What serves for a face is a vertical seam that can part along its length, revealing not eyes but a cluster of pressure pits that drink the air’s smallest changes the way the thorn-hairs drink motion. In storms the seam stays closed and they navigate by the way the dust rattles against their plates, each grain a syllable of map. When they do open, it is usually above the central basin, and only long enough to taste the almost-language that sometimes coils there. They never answer it. They only adjust their stance, as if the warning—whatever it once named—has already been incorporated into the angle of a knee or the set of a shoulder.

They are few. Rarely more than seven or eight on the entire Cusp at once, and some of those may be the same individual counted twice because time inside the groves is not trustworthy. A body that enters a thorn-grove thin and pale as a new paddle may step out heavier, its plates overlapping like storm-swollen tissue, eyes (when the seam parts) filmed with the after-image of a wind that has not yet blown. Another may emerge diminished, almost translucent, rings of old growth sloughed and already being combed for usable fiber by the mites that attend them like reverse shadows. They do not appear to mind the discrepancy. Age, here, is simply another current.

Their hands—five-jointed, longer than proportion suggests—are the most human thing about them and therefore the most unsettling. They use them sparingly. Most work is done by the wind once they have set the conditions: a cord begun and tethered so the gale continues the braid; a flake pressed and left where vibration will finish the edge; a digging stick socketed into a crack and abandoned to the mountain’s slow upward weather until the tip hardens into something that can pierce even the black trunks. The stone rings are theirs, but not as homes. They are valves. Inside a ring the air’s impatience lessens just enough for a body to set down its external skeleton, to unseal the face-seam, to spend an hour (or a clotted minute) remembering how to be singular. Sometimes they kindle the powder-fires not for heat—heat is everywhere—but for the way smoke, even dust-smoke, briefly teaches the light how to cast a proper shadow. They sit inside those shadows and watch their own outlines with what might be hunger or might be grief.

They eat motion, but at one remove. Where the thorn-trees comb the air and fatten, these people comb the trees. They wait until a grove has reached the trembling peak of a storm-feast, then walk through it trailing their corded exo-limbs. The paddles snag, tear, adhere. By the time the walker emerges, the cords are clotted with translucent copper-purple tissue that already begins to seal itself into new plate material against their skin. The trees do not die; they simply record the passage as a temporary thinning, then compensate when the next gale arrives. It is a harvest that looks like injury and functions as circulation. In return, the people leave their own dead—when death occurs—upright among the trunks, plates opened so the hooked hairs can finish unmaking what the wind began. Nothing is buried. Burial would imply the ground is willing to keep secrets. Here every secret is offered upward.

They are not silent inside themselves. Though they rarely sound their voices in the open air (the throat is just another seam, and dust is forever trying to colonize moisture that no longer exists), something like thought moves across them in slow surface waves, plate to plate, individual to individual, the way heat moves through the rock. If you stand among them long enough—if they permit it—you begin to feel the grammar of that thought: long conditional phrases made of stance and balance, verbs that conjugate only in the future perfect (“will-have-leaned,” “will-have-shed”), nouns that are mostly the names of particular eddies that have formed above the Cusp once and may never form again. Their stories, such as they are, concern the exact angle at which a forgotten god once slept, the number of dust-seasons since rain was last argued into existence and then dismissed, the proper way to fold a life so that when the mountain finally shrugs the life can rearrrange itself mid-fall and land still walking.

Sometimes one of them will stop at the rim of the central basin and remain there while whole weather systems manufacture and dismantle themselves overhead. The dust-vortex climbs their legs, collar bones, the sealed seam of the face. After-images peel off them and join the transparent traffic already orbiting the hollow: hands that once stacked stones, hoppers that have not yet been born, a relapse of rain that lasts three heartbeats and leaves no wetness. They watch these things the way other creatures might watch fire—wary, glad, ready to step away the instant the beauty becomes appetite. When they finally leave the rim, their plates carry a faint new calligraphy of dust that takes days to wear off. Other members of the group will touch those marks with the tips of their long hands, reading whatever the basin wrote.

They do not name themselves. Naming would require a stillness the Cusp refuses to underwrite. If pressed by some impossible visitor, they might muster a sound like the memory of water clicking over stones that no longer know what fresh means—an approximation, already evaporating. Mostly they are content to be the temporary syntax by which the mountain’s dry shout becomes almost legible. They are the pause that contemplates itself inside the frenzy, the agreement that holds just long enough for a tool to be set down and picked up again by the same wind that tried to steal it. When they move on—and they always do, eventually, following some pressure gradient too fine for any instrument but their own unsealed faces—the rings remain, the half-finished cords continue braiding themselves for a while, and the thorn-trees fatten on the memory of their footsteps.

And if, in a rare true lull, you hear something that is neither wind nor mite-drone nor the dental chords of the paddles, something low and almost fond, it is only them reshaping their plates against the rock, laughing without throats at the old joke of permanence, while the luminous dust settles into the continuously rewritten scripture of their idle, patient hands.
