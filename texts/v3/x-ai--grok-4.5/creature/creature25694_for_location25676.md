The basin does not keep inhabitants the way a house keeps furniture or a forest keeps deer. What lives here has grown so thoroughly into the grain of the place that the distinction between resident and residence thins to a membrane, then vanishes. Begin with the smallest, because they are the loom.

They are not insects in any simple taxonomy, though they wear the architecture of insects: jointed casings the color of weak tea or damp bone, bodies segmented into labor rather than elegance. They move in continuous, low-density clouds that never quite disperse, rearranging themselves the way heat shimmer rearranges air. Their legs are many and fine, tipped with microscopic hooks that let them walk the underside of every leaf without falling. They do not fly so much as loft, circulating on the slow convection that rises from the warm soil and sinks again where the canopy thickens. Mouthparts work constantly, not in feeding frenzies but in the steady rasping maintenance of the world: trimming softening edges, chewing fallen fronds into transportable wet confetti, ferrying the confetti along covered galleries whose walls they secrete from their own salivary glands. These galleries form a second circulatory system beneath the green, pale tunnels that branch and rejoin like the memory of vanished rivers. Inside them the air is hotter still, thick with the sweet-acrid smell of half-digested cellulose. The creatures themselves seem almost interchangeable; damage one and others close around the gap without pause, consuming the injured body as readily as any other feedstock. They keep no queens that can be pointed to, only gradients of pheromone density that thicken into temporary command in the deepest chambers. There the plant slurry is mixed with fungal exudates and left to ripen into a pale, nutritious gel that seeps upward along capillary runways to feed everything else. They are the basin’s quiet foundry shift, clockless and unending.

Larger soft bodies move through the same medium the way hands move through thick water. These are the tunnelers and the pillow-shapes: organisms whose skins are permeable membranes rather than armor, glistening with a constant mucus that keeps the leaf litter from abrading them. Some are toroidal, rolling slowly by peristalsis, gathering detritus into their central voids and extruding richer loam behind. Others elongate into tapered ribbons that can flatten to a film one cell thick or bunch into muscular bulbs capable of prying apart root mats. They carry no eyes. Light has so little meaning here that photoreception would be waste. Instead their entire surfaces taste chemistry: iron from the seeps, the faint electrical stutter of the fungal mats, the latex signature of the sheet-vines, the stress hormones of a leaf about to drop. When two meet they do not fight or flee; they pause, exchange coatings, sometimes merge for a time into a larger, temporarily shared body before separating again with redistributed nutrients. Their deaths are not events so much as slow liquefactions. A body that has reached the end of its useful integrity simply sinks, skin opening, contents joining the slurry. The others notice only as a local enrichment.

Above and between these move the hangers and the stalkers, forms that occupy the negative spaces the vegetation leaves. Some dangle from the low canopy on threads of their own making, bodies elongated into living plumb-lines that sway with the almost nonexistent breeze. Their undersides are fields of fine setae that drink moisture from the air and from the leaves they brush. Digestion for them is external and patient: they drop a loop of enzyme-laden silk onto a patch of crowded growth, wait while the silk softens and begins to dissolve whatever it touches, then reel the resulting nutrient tea back up. Others travel the horizontal plane, low-slung and wide, six or eight or a dozen limbs ending in soft pads that taste the ground with every step. These are the ones that can freeze into perfect continuity with a fern brake or a fallen log, not by camouflage patterns but by a slow dermal blooming of the same chloroplasts and fungal threads that surround them. They photosynthesize a little themselves when the rare fingers of light reach them. More often they wait for the small hard-shelled workers to stream close, then open ventral grooves that the workers enter willingly, depositing gel and departing empty. The exchange is so old it no longer registers as transaction; it is simply how mass moves through the single body of the basin.

There are still larger shapes, though “larger” is relative in a place where the canopy presses down like a held breath. These are the slow engines, bodies the size of fallen boulders or longer, built from laminated cartilages and hydraulic chambers. They do not walk so much as circulate, shifting the weight of themselves from chamber to chamber so that the whole mass glides forward a body-length in the course of an afternoon. Their backs are gardens. Seedlings take root in the crevices of their hide; colonies of the pale workers maintain runways across them; soft tunnelers bore temporary residences and move on. In return the great bodies receive shade, moisture regulation, and the constant mild current of fungal electricity that twitches their deeper muscles and keeps lymph moving. Sometimes one of them settles into a swale and does not rise again for a season, becoming for that time a living hill whose internal heat accelerates decomposition beneath it. When it finally moves on, the swale is richer, lower, ready for the next slow polishing by water and roots.

Interwoven with all of them—neither plant nor animal nor cleanly fungus—are the sheet-weavers and the current-bearers. Certain vines drip a white latex that hardens in air into translucent, slightly flexible panes. Specialized organisms, soft-bodied but equipped with hardened spatulate limbs, harvest these panes while they are still tacky, layer them, and shape them into nest structures that gleam like the skin on cooling metal. Inside those nests the temperature is fractionally higher, the chemistry fractionally more efficient. The fungal mats, vast and veined with something like primitive wiring, generate faint potential differences across their wet expanses. Anything that blunders into the fine hyphal webs experiences a mild, continuous twitch that either drives it away or, if the thing is already dying, holds it in place while enzymes and workers unmake it. The mats themselves feed on the resulting broth and on the slow leakage of sugars from living roots. At night—though day and night are only slight gradients here—the charge becomes visible as a dim, crawling luminescence along the wetter seams of the ground, a foxfire that does not flicker but migrates, seeking equilibrium itself.

No single creature rules. No species can be extracted and understood alone. What lives in the basin is a density that has learned to think in cycles of replacement rather than in individuals. A body is only a temporary membrane around processes that began before it and will continue after. The pale workers will still be moving slurry when the great engines have settled into hills and risen again as new engines. The soft tunnelers will still be tasting iron and latex. The hangers will still be reeling in their quiet teas. Heat will still press. The green will still lean upon itself. And the old shallow depression in the earth will continue its work of holding them all without comment, without name, without any need to become anything other than the shape that life, given long enough and no interruption, most naturally fills.
