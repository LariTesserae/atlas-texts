They are the scrapers.

Not a name they would recognize—there is no recognition in them, only gradient and density and the endless bisecting of stone—but it is what the sound makes of them. Each body is a closed fist of chitin and silicate, no longer than a thumb-joint, plated in overlapping shields the color of cooled slag. The shields are not secreted so much as grown outward from the mineral they eat; successive molts leave faint terraces of darker glass along the flanks, so that an individual older than a single heat-cycle carries its own abbreviated stratigraphy. Legs are six, jointed like broken spar, tipped with spoons of denser crystal that double as both pitons and sorting scoops. At the front the head is little more than a hinged wedge: two pale sensors that register only temperature differential and vibration, and beneath them the paired mandibles—serrated, self-sharpening, forever in motion. They do not bite to kill. They bite to grade.

In the rust-brown gloom their numbers erase individuality. What the eye meets first is simply a living scree, a granular tide that pours itself up vertical faces and across the unfinished pillars with the slow inevitability of cooling lava. Where the buried engine pulses hotter, the carpet darkens and thickens; bodies press so tightly that the click of plate on plate becomes a single sustained fretting, like someone endlessly tightening a wire. Heat is not endured here; it is metabolized. Each scraper carries a lattice of micro-channels just beneath the dorsal shields, lined with a dull catalytic felt that cracks waterless vapor into usable energy and leaves behind only a finer dust. They exhale powder. They walk on their own exhalations.

Watch one long enough—though the watching itself must be done in the thick delay of the brown dark—and the labor resolves into tiny, perfect stages. Mandibles close on a flake of still-warm basalt. The flake is sheared, tasted for metal content by organs that are neither tongue nor hand, then either discarded into the communal grit-stream or packed into a ventral pouch already lined with yesterday’s siftings. When the pouch reaches a precise mass it is voided against the wall in a tight spiral. The spiral dries in minutes into one of the transient spires; the next migration will trample it, the next fracture will reclaim it. Nothing is stored beyond the hour’s need. Nothing is built that outlasts the gradient that made it useful.

They migrate as weather. A cooling seam on the western curve of the pocket will hold them for days—bodies stacked three and four deep in every hairline crack—until the heat signature shifts by a fraction that only they can feel. Then the entire field lifts and flows. The movement is soundless at first, only a change in the pitch of the grinding; later it becomes a low, continuous avalanche as millions of crystal tarsi find new purchase. In their wake the stone is burnished to a temporary shine, the dull scar-skin polished by the friction of a thousand thousand undersides. Dust hangs in their absence like a canceled sentence.

Deeper still, where the vents freckle the dark with dull orange, the scrapers thin into specialized castes the eye can scarcely separate. Some are narrower, almost bladed, designed to slip into the freshest fractures while the rock is still weeping pale dust; these are the first tastes, the ones that decide whether a new seam will feed the colony or be abandoned. Others are heavier, shield-plates fused into crude shovels, and these remain longest at the vents, packing and re-packing the gypsum blooms until the blooms themselves become temporary galleries. The galleries collapse within a cycle. Collapse is simply another form of sorting.

They listen with the whole body. When the subsurface engine draws breath—evaporate, deposit, crack, heal—the chamber’s ambient roar modulates by an interval too fine for any ear shaped like yours. The scrapers answer. Their collective milling rises half a tone, or falls, or develops a new internal syncopation that lasts for weeks. It is not communication. It is entrainment, the way iron filings arrange themselves along invisible lines. The pocket itself is the only message, and they are its continuing reply.

No pair among them has ever touched in a way that is not about leverage or heat-sharing. No body has ever looked upward at the hairline leaks of light and wondered. The thinner world outside the blister does not exist for them; the sealed stone is the entire calendar. Yet they are not mindless. Density itself has become a kind of distributed tact. When a pillar half-risen from the floor begins to list under its own unfinished weight, the carpet deforms around the future fracture days before the crack appears. They empty the zone, leave a polished ring of avoidance, and when the basalt finally fails they are already elsewhere, already scraping the new face it exposes. Foresight without image. Care without tenderness.

In the rare hours when the migrating fronts cross and double the population in a single gallery, the sound peaks into something almost diurnal: a layered thunder of chitin and crystal and falling grit that would deafen anything with an eardrum. Then the fronts pass through one another, bodies interpenetrating like two transparent flocks, and the thunder subsides again into the ordinary industrial forever. Dust settles on dust. Salt lace re-forms. The rock continues its slow deciding. And the scrapers—small, hard, provisional, utterly fitted to a place that may yet close and rise without them—keep working the unfinished dark as if the dark had asked to be finished.
