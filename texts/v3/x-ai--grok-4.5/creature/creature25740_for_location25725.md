The ones who live here are not first the people, though the people are real enough and have names they speak only indoors.

They are a narrow folk, short in the bone, built for the damp and the uneven packing of young stone. Their skin takes a mineral pallor early; by thirty the cheeks and wrists look dusted with the same pale grit that collects in every seam of the notch. Hair is dark, straight, heavy with the constant mist, and they cut it with the dull glass scrapers they pry from the rock—short at the nape, longer at the crown so that wet strands can be bound back with fiber without dripping into the eyes. Eyes themselves are small and set deep, adapted to the perpetual late-afternoon light; they do not squint so much as receive, and in the rare hard-clear mornings they move slowly, as if light itself had weight. Hands are broad, fingers blunt, permanently cool. They work without hurry because hurry is how the stone remembers its fractures and how the quiet things notice you. Their clothes are layered felts and split hides sewn with sinew, darkened by peat smoke and the green rot that lives in every fiber. At night they burn fat-lamps that smell of marrow and old rain; by day they move along the graded paths with the careful gait of those who have learned that the rock underfoot is still deciding whether to hold.

They keep the green rooms private after dark not from superstition alone but from a practical courtesy. They know the larger soft bodies hang in the moss ceilings then, breathing cool moisture that fans across the forest floor like a second weather. They have seen the pale jointed sheets pour down a wall face and reassemble on the far side of a house without ever crossing the threshold, as if the house itself were only a difference in texture. Children are taught early to step around the empty circles that appear overnight in the crawler carpets, and never to poke the hanging clusters when the eyes—too many, recessed, unused to open light—begin to cloud with condensation. The quiet is attention, they say, though they do not say to whom. Their stories, when they tell them at all, do not settle on origins; they circle instead around continuing: how to re-stack a frost-pried wall, how the cistern’s waterline recovers if you leave the seepage threads alone, how the black-leaved trees sometimes face the settlement with radial calm and then forget, and how one does not take that personally.

Yet the people are only one density among many.

What lives here more numerous, more continuous, is the silent fauna that treats the notch as a single breathing body. The small jointed ones are first and last and everywhere: pale carapaces the color of wet limestone, legs so many and so fine that a sheet of them turning a corner looks like liquid thinking. They travel the bare faces at night, remapping, leaving faint etchings that the morning mist fills and erases. By day they thin into the moss and the underside of every leaf, a subterranean weather. Larger and slower are the soft-bodied hangers: pouched, limbed just enough to grip stone or bark, their undersides crowded with eyes that never fully open yet somehow track the dimness. They exhale visible coolth; clusters of them can lower the temperature of a green room by several degrees, and the white-barked trees lean toward that coolness. When predation occurs it is almost tender—a larger throat dilating, a soft collapse, the carpet of crawlers simply developing a circular absence that the remaining bodies respect for hours before flowing back in. Winged membranes pass overhead on flex alone, never clapping; they trail thin filaments of condensation that sometimes freeze mid-air into hairline singers, notes so low they register as pressure behind the eyes before the ice sublimates back into the cloud. On the forest floor the narrow hoofed things thread without sound, bodies the width of a forearm, legs articulated for the spaces between fern stems; their passage is recorded only by the slow closing of fronds and the occasional scent of crushed mineral green.

All of them together form a single metabolism that the settlement has learned to live inside rather than against. The people’s cistern catches what drips; the animals drink what pools and what sweats from the walls. The dull glass plates the people pry free show reflections half a breath late—sometimes a face that has already turned away, sometimes a hanging cluster that has not yet arrived at that ledge—and so the glass is used for scraping and for brief, cautious self-regard, never for vanity. At dusk the black leaves may arrange themselves into open-handed symmetries that face the houses for an hour, as if acknowledging, then dissolve the pattern into ordinary canopy again. No one claims to understand the gesture. Understanding is not required for cohabitation.

What lives here, finally, is the notch itself as a slow confederation of damp and attention: young stone still sharp enough to cut, moisture that collects rather than falls, plants that greed in pockets and then relent into bare quiet, animals that never announce, and a few dozen human bodies that have shaped their small deliberate rooms inside the larger ones without ever quite declaring ownership. They wake to drip and the soft tick of cooling rock. They sleep while the jointed sheets re-map the walls and the hanging clusters breathe the night cooler. Between those states they grind grain with stone against stone, mend cordage, watch the smoke of their cookfires hit the invisible lid of air and spread sideways like a second, lower cloud. They are not lonely. Loneliness would require silence to be emptiness. Here the quiet is densely occupied, and everything that lives inside it has learned the same economy: move without noise, grow without apology, leave the green rooms their privacy after dark, and endure by crafts and by noticing. The place keeps working—drip by drip, swarm by silent swarm—and what lives there keeps becoming more exactly itself, knee-deep in the unfinished stone and the unending soft attention of everything else that shares the hanging pocket with them.
