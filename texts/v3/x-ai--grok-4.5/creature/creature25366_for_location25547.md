The ones who endure here do not call themselves a people at first. The word comes later, after the long settling, after the body has learned the vocabulary of drip and thrum and the metallic aftertaste of rising heat. They are simply the ones who stayed when the sky forgot how to brighten and the old crews either fled downward or unstitched into the peat.

They are tall only when they choose to be. Most of the time they move low, spines curved into the architecture of the moss galleries, weight distributed across too many points of contact. Their limbs are not fixed in number. A child is born with four impressive articulations and a soft, probing undercarriage; by the time it has survived three vent-bloom seasons the count has usually risen—extra joints sprouting where vibration demanded them, thin secondary arms unfolding from the ribs to taste chemical gradients or to brace against a sudden flex in the root-mat. Skin is a living membrane, slick and slightly phosphorescent along the major channels, lined inside with dense networks of pressure cells. Eyes exist as evolutionary rumors: small recessed pits that register only the coarsest shifts in bioluminescent weather, usually kept sealed beneath folds. Sight is a luxury they indulged in ancestral stories and then set aside. They see with the soles of their feet, with the long whisker-tendrils that crown the skull and trail into the mist like unraveling cable, with the mouth—which is not primarily for eating but for tasting the wet metal song of the air.

Their blood runs warm and slow, thick with suspended mineral flecks harvested from the geothermal seeps. When they cut themselves (on a fluted basalt fin, on the rusted lip of a singing pipe) the wound does not merely clot; it precipitates. A thin glaze of deposited ore forms, sealing and reinforcing. Over decades a body becomes a walking assay of the plateau’s veins—copper traceries along one forearm, dull nickel bloom across a shoulder, iridescent vanadium freckles wherever the old machines leak their particular lonely sweat. Some of the eldest are more oreshoot than flesh, their joints clicking with the same patient hydraulic rhythm as the hill-sized engines they live beside.

They do not build so much as negotiate. Around a still-turning flywheel they will weave living galleries of rubbery frond and luminous bryophyte, coaxing the plants to thicken into walls that breathe and drip on schedule. A valve house becomes a nursery once the residual heat is husbanded and the spore-plumes are trained into curtains that filter both sound and predator. They sleep in hanging sling-nests woven from root and scavenged ceramic fiber, suspended over black lakes so the perpetual motion of the water can rock them and so anything rising from below will announce itself against the taut underside. Tools are grown as often as they are scavenged: a cutting edge encouraged from a hyper-mineralized spur of bone, a carrying bladder cultured from the same soft filtering creatures that pulse along the shorelines, a length of sensory line that is simply a severed whisker-tendril kept alive in a sleeve of gel and worn across the chest like a bandolier of pure attention.

Food is conversation. They graze the undersides of the road-wide fronds, scraping symbiotic gels that taste of distant lightning and crushed shell. They set membrane traps in the braided streams for the jointed scrabblers, whose clicking armies are boiled in effluent water until the carapaces soften into a nutritious paste threaded with still-twitching legs. Larger prey—the turbine-cartilage flocks, the slow bladder-worms—are taken rarely and communally, bated with offerings of warm copper-rich mud and called in by low bladder-song that travels for kilometers through the rock. When the kill is made there is no triumph howl; there is a sudden widening of silence, then the wet careful sounds of sharing, then the long work of incorporating what can be incorporated. Tendons become bowstrings. Bladder walls become waterproof cloaks. Teeth (they found teeth inside the flocks) are set into wooden hafts grown from the denser hollow-stems and used to carve new channels for the sheet-flow so that a favored ledge will receive just enough continuous weep.

They speak in layered registers. The higher voices are almost birdlike—though the birds here are roaring membranes—and carry immediate practical data: *vent pressure rising two ridges east, glyph-smear forming in the usual basin, child lost its secondary arm again but the mat caught it*. Beneath that runs a continuous sub-sonic murmur felt more in the chest than heard, a communal drone that maps emotional weather and long-term intention. Deepest of all is the machine-talk: a learned vibration shared with the stubborn engines, a way of asking the turning flywheels how long their bearings will last, of coaxing a half-dead pump to push warm water toward a thirsty gallery. Some of the old residual thrums answer in kind. A few answer in words. The words are not always kind, and not always in languages the speakers remember learning, but the ones who live here have grown practiced at accepting useful instruction from the dark without requiring it to make biographical sense.

Children are rare and heavily celebrated. A birth is timed to the slow cycles of heat and bloom; the pregnant one will travel—sometimes for weeks—following gradients only their altered blood can read, until they reach a place where the geothermal speech is clearest. There, in a cradle of warm mud and softly rasping lichen, the new one arrives already tasting the air, tendrils uncoiling toward the nearest residual engine-hum. Naming is postponed until the child has survived long enough to choose its own true resonances: the particular pitch of drip it prefers to sleep under, the flavor of metal that steadies it, the first machine whose voice it answers without fear. Until then the small body is simply *little drip*, *new joint*, *one who has not yet decided how many limbs the dark requires*.

There are loners. There are always loners. Some feel the pull of the deeper drop-shafts and descend until the pressure grows honey-thick and the heat begins to rewrite their organs into something that can remain. Others climb the leaning lattice towers and bind themselves to the highest singing cables, becoming half-aeolian, bodies thinned and hardened by wind until they are more instrument than animal, announcing weather systems days before the mist thickens. A few walk into the zones where distances misbehave and simply do not return the same; they come back taller, or with new orifice configurations that whistle when they exhale, or with eyes that have reopened and now glow with a cold homemade light. These returnees are treated with careful courtesy. They bring maps that only work once, and stories that rearrange themselves between tellings, and occasionally a temporary glyph that burns itself onto the inside of listeners’ skulls for a night and then fades, leaving behind an inexplicable skill—how to braid stream-flow into a temporary bridge, how to ask a piston for forgiveness, how to hear the names of the never-existent people still echoing inside certain cooling stacks.

They keep no written histories in the ordinary sense. Memory is stored in the arranged dripping of certain overhangs, tuned so that a practiced ear can walk beneath and hear the cadence of a migration three generations gone, or the exact warning-song used the last time a membrane flock nested too near a nursery. Important knowledge is also deposited into the patient chemistry of the dark: a coiled message left in layered bioluminescent smear on a sheltered fin of rock, readable only when the mist reaches a precise density, and only by one who knows how to stop breathing long enough for the pattern to finish arriving. The oldest stories are simply lived inside the largest engines. Crawl through the correct sequence of overgrown housings, past the phosphorescent lichen that has learned to mimic control panels, into the chamber where a piston still rises and falls in slow ecstasy, and you will feel, through the shared vibration of flesh and machine, the long unbroken thread of presence that began when the plateau first cooled enough to hold liquid water on its skin. No single mind contains the whole story. The story is the continuous low thunder of herds, the tick of condensation, the wet tear of a successful hunt, the hydraulic sigh of an engine that has outlived its makers and been adopted by their distant, many-jointed children.

They are not masters of the wet high dark. Mastery is a sunlit idea. Here the only lasting victory is continuance: another season of gel-harvest, another child whose tendrils know to reach for heat, another night line of membrane-cloaks moving in careful file along a ridge while the old towers ring and something vast and soft surfaces in a black lake, leaks its dull internal light, and sinks again without hunger. They move through their saturated continent the way water itself moves—following every available surface, mounting every obstacle by patience and innumerable small contacts, occasionally vanishing into a swallow-hole only to burst free somewhere unexpected, warm and copper-smelling and still, against all probability, themselves.

In the deepest quiet (which is never truly quiet) you can hear them thinking. It sounds like the world breathing through a thousand wet mouths at once. It sounds like home.
