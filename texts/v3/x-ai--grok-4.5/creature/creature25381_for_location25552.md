The people of the high plain are not many, and they do not hurry. They move the way the weather moves: in long, deliberate fronts that look, from a distance, like stillness. Their bodies are compact, dense with the slow musculature of those who have spent generations lifting stone against thin air. Shoulders slope inward as if braced against a constant, horizontal wind. Chests are deep, ribs sprung wide to cup whatever oxygen the elevation will spare. Fingers are blunt and ridged with callus, the nails thickened into pale horn that clicks softly against masonry. Skin takes the cold the way stone takes it—without drama, going grey-blue at the tips and flanks, then returning to the color of old parchment when work warms them. Eyes sit deep, hooded against glare that has nowhere to diffuse; the irises are the washed mineral shades of the plain itself, grey-green or dust-violet, and they track motion with a patience that can look like absence until something truly shifts.

They dress in layers of felted frond-fiber and cured lichen-mat, the blues so dark they drink light, stitched with cord that was once living stalk. Hoods are constant. Faces are often wrapped to the eyes against the granular ice that arrives without warning. When they uncover to speak, mouths are spare, lips chapped into fine interlocking cracks. Speech itself is economical, vowels held low in the chest where the air is denser, consonants clicked softly so they will not travel farther than intended. They speak of distance the way other peoples speak of mood—today the western approach is “long-minded,” or the alley behind the peat stores has gone “tight as a held breath.” They do not sound alarmed. These are simply the day’s conditions.

Children are born small and already quiet. They learn early to test a threshold with the edge of a bare foot before committing weight, to watch how smoke rises (or fails to) before trusting a hearth. Their games involve stacking the pale marker stones into patterns that might last the night, then comparing notes at dawn on how the stones chose to rearrange. No one scolds them when the spirals appear; the adults only nod, as one nods at frost. Toys are few: a smoothly worn knuckle of mountain-stone that rolls differently each afternoon, a length of translucent stalk that holds yesterday’s rain and, if shaken just so, will hum a note that is not quite the note you hear. By adolescence most have stopped trying to name the difference.

They keep no herds, no flocks, no hives. The absence of other animals is not mourned; it is the oldest fact. Meat, when it is eaten at all, is the dense, slow flesh of certain deep-growing tubers that must be prised from fault-line gardens with wooden levers and patience measured in days. The plants yield it grudgingly, and only to hands that have already repaired the low walls around them three times that season. Fire is peat and resin-slow; it burns with almost no sound and leaves a white ash that the wind writes into temporary scripts across the plazas. Those scripts are read, sometimes. Not for prophecy—prophecy is considered poor manners on a plain that already rearranges itself—but for the same reason one checks the joints of a roof: to know what is currently true.

At night they board the slit windows when the stars arrive wrong. This is done without prayer or cursing, the way one beds down a tool that has grown too warm. Inside the thick-walled rooms the lamps are few, fed on oils pressed from the same blue-black fronds. Light pools rather than spreads. Faces become islands. In that reduced glow the people sometimes notice that a familiar cheek or hand has gone briefly translucent, showing the dim green thread of vein or the denser knot of bone, before opacity returns like a polite correction. No one remarks on it unless the condition lingers; then the afflicted sits nearer the fire and is given the softer work of mending cord until the body remembers its edges.

They are not alone in the cities, though the other residents are harder to inventory. There are the hands that finish a repair you only half recall starting—an adze left wedged in a timber at dusk will by morning have completed its cut and lie neatly beside a small cairn of the pale stone chips. There are the distances that reorder themselves specifically around certain walkers, stretching to give one soul a longer solitude or contracting so that two people who have not spoken in a year suddenly find their doors adjacent. There are the soft, recursive footsteps that pace the outer walls on nights of fractured constellations; when followed they lead only to more wall, yet the mortar there is always fresh. Some households leave a shallow basin of meltwater on the threshold for these walkers. By morning the basin is empty and the doorstone has been planed smoother than any adze could manage.

Deeper still are the ones who do not use doors. They are felt most clearly in the gardens, where the frond-mats grow with an attentiveness that suggests audience. Kneel long enough among the translucent stalks and you may sense a vast, distributed regard turning toward the warmth of your blood—nothing so crude as hunger, more like the plain’s own slow curiosity about anything that still generates heat. The lichen’s faint green glow brightens in response, and for a time your thoughts arrive with a faint echo, as if considered by a mind whose geometry is not arranged for language. The people do not name this presence. Naming is another kind of edge, and edges are unreliable here. They simply work the damp pockets with greater care, leaving small offerings of repaired tools or braided fiber, and the gardens answer with denser growth, with rain that lingers one hour longer, with the occasional soft redirection of a path that would otherwise have led a walker into a fold of distance from which return is only theoretical.

Under it all—under the cities cupped in their shallow depressions, under the patient stone-fitters and the breathing distances—the older labor continues. You can feel it in the marrow when you lie down against bare bedrock: a tectonic patience that is no longer concerned with raising mountains or opening seas, only with testing the agreement between one volume and the next. Sometimes the testing passes through a person. They will pause mid-stride, head tilted, and for a few heartbeats their outline will forget the exact weather of solidity. Neighbors guide them gently to a wall or a bench until the argument settles. Afterward the person is quieter still, and their repairs take on a curious precision, as if they now understand which joints the plain itself intends to keep.

So the inhabitants endure: the compact, callused people and the uncounted others who share their crooked geometries. They do not conquer the plain or flee it. They inhabit it the way moss inhabits a long-fallen ridge—by remaining, by repairing, by allowing the slow green seizure and the slower seizure of space to pass through and around them. On clear evenings, when the stars arrive intact and the horizon holds its proper distance for an hour, they stand on their flattened roofs and watch the light go out across a world that has already forgotten how to end. Then they climb back down the low ladders, bar the doors against whatever mathematics the night may bring, and sleep the dense, economical sleep of those who must wake ready to fit stone to stone again, even if the stones have, overnight, become slightly more interested in becoming something else.
