The ones who live here are not one kind of thing.

They begin as pressure and damp. A child is born already knowing the weight of wet air on brand-new lungs; the first cry is shaped by it, lower and wetter than the cries of drier ages. Skin learns early to stay open, to exchange. Pores loosen. Nails thicken at the edges where constant contact with railings and wet stone would otherwise fray them. Many grow a fine, almost invisible nap of keratin and conductive filament along the forearms and the nape—nothing dramatic, just enough to wick moisture and to carry the tight-beam skin-comms that replace most raised voices. You see them in the markets: two people facing each other in the roar, mouths closed, hands still, while their forearm nap glints with exchange. Afterwards they smell faintly of ozone and each other.

They are not all the same age of flesh. Some bloodlines have lived in the Soak for so many stacked generations that their bones remember previous street levels the way trees once remembered drought rings. Their inner ears are dense, tuned to the subsonic throb of the pumps; they grow uneasy in true silence. Others arrived later—descendants of technicians who came to service the gravity lenses and never left, or of bargers who married into the floating stalls, or of the slow upward creep of people from the underwater districts where the light is blue-black and the speech is mostly hand-sign and pressure-tap. These newer lines still startle when a whole plaza lifts three centimeters overnight. The older lines only check their balance and keep walking.

Bodies are tools first. Joints are often reinforced early—carbon-thread sleeves grown under the skin around knees and wrists because the city never stops offering new heights to climb or new gaps to jump. Some replace an eye with a wetware cluster that can read the residual harmonics in a wall and tell you what configuration this corridor held seventy years ago. Others keep both eyes and simply listen harder; the walls speak if you lean your damp temple against them long enough. Teeth are commonly sintered with mineral drawn from the same runoff that feeds the calcite veils; they gleam dull white and never quite warm to the touch. When people smile in the low brass light the effect is companionable and slightly geological.

They move in currents. No one owns a route the way drier cities once owned streets. You join a flow heading toward the mid-level protein vats or the upper quiet belts and you become part of its logic for a while. Shoulders touch continuously. The contact is not intimacy and not aggression; it is navigation. Children learn to read the micro-tensing of a stranger’s deltoid the way other children once learned alphabet. A sudden stillness in the crowd means a spar is about to grind or a fogbank is spilling down; the body knows before the mind names it. Laughing is short, punched out on the exhale so it cuts through the white roar. Grief is usually silent and expressed by leaning harder into the nearest load-bearing surface until the surface leans back.

They keep animals the way the city keeps them—abundantly, utilitariously, affectionately. The needle-fish are half pets, half civic instrumentation. Children scoop the luminous ones into clear polymer bowls and carry them through the hanging markets; the fish’s capacitative scales flicker in patterns that the child’s own skin-nap answers, a private conversation of light. When the bowl is finally tipped back into the canal the fish stays close for a few days, a bright comma in the dark water, before the larger data-shoal reclaims it. The swivel-winged gliders nest in the acoustic baffles and steal folded protein sheets from unattended stalls. People curse them and also leave offerings on high ledges—scraps of conductive foil, beads of purified fat—so the flocks will keep riding the updrafts that vent the worst of the heat. Everyone knows the gliders are part drone, part bat- Remnant, part something the city grew because empty air longed to be crossed. Knowing does not reduce the pleasure of watching a flock turn as one silver knife-edge between towers.

Deeper, in the service strata most surface-dwellers visit only when a crawler must be bribed or a drain pleaded with, the soft-bodied things live. They are pale, segmented, the length of a transit car, and they taste the world through the entire length of their ventral surface. Their work is endless and perfect: they keep the black water moving. People who work alongside them for years begin to share a private dialect of taps and mucus-slick gestures. Some say the crawlers dream about the upper rain. No one has proof. The heavy shelled tramplers are quieter company—living tugs, patient as stone, their shells colonized by the same mineral veils that hang between towers. Children climb them. Old people sleep against their warm flanks when the habitat blocks are being jacked and the whole district shudders. The tramplers do not seem to mind. Their eyes are small and kind and permanently wet.

There are others who are harder to name. In the fused mid-level skybridges where daylight is only a rumor of milked silver, communities form that have given up ordinary verticality. They live sideways and upside-down, using the same gravity lenses that keep the decks from shearing, riding the faint taste of copper in the air like a tide. Their bones thin in one direction and densify in another. They speak less with hands and more with the lengthening and shortening of their own shadows. Surface people find them beautiful the way a calcification pattern inside an old pipe is beautiful—inevitable, intricate, slightly haunted. Trade happens through baskets lowered on cables. What comes back up is always useful and always slightly damp.

And then there are the ones who have stopped being singular.

Sometimes a person spends too long listening to the walls, or works too many shifts inside the nanoscale circulatory nets that knit the load-bearing bolsters, and the boundary thins. They begin to leave impressions that last after they have walked on: a palm print that keeps sweating long after the hand is gone, a laugh that rebounds in the residual harmonics for hours. Eventually some of them settle into a favorite junction or spar and simply… continue. Their bodies mineralize in elegant stages, joining the deliberate veils of calcite, until what remains is a thinking thickening in the stone, a warmth that is not industrial. People still greet them. They leave offerings of sweet resin and news. The settled ones answer by changing the way the water sheets across their new surfaces—patterns that can be read if you know how. It is not death. It is a change of address inside the same endless building.

The rich (those who have clawed their way into the thin belts where the air is dry enough to let silk last more than a week) keep themselves deliberately unfinished. They change skins the way others change districts, printing new dermis that repels the perpetual fine rain for a season and then peeling it off again so the damp can reclaim them and prove they still belong. Their children are born in sealed cores and introduced carefully, week by week, to the full roar and wet, as if the city were a sea that might drown the unacclimated. Some of those children never fully forgive the delay. At the first real opportunity they shed every filter and disappear down-stack into the thicker, louder currents, returning only as occasional polished messages routed through the walls.

No one is unaccompanied. Even the solitary ones who walk the farthest edges where the calcite veils thicken into cliffs of frozen rain move inside a cloud of the city’s own exhalations—micro-swarms that taste like metal and old sweetness, drawing maps of their passage so the Soak can remember who has been where. Loneliness here is not absence; it is a particular quality of presence, the feeling of being one more patient process among millions.

At night (the blue-white and amber kind of night the city makes for itself) you can stand on a gantry and watch them all at once: the glowing commas of the fish in the canals, the darker commas of people crossing skybridges, the gliders stitching the steam, the slow relocation of a trampler three levels down, the faint bioluminescent code flickering along a crawler’s flank far below, the settled ones gleaming wetly as the vertical rivers run over their surfaces, the children chasing a momentary gravity flicker so that their laughter rises and falls like something thrown and caught. The whole mass breathes. It shifts. It continues.

They are the Soak thinking about itself in flesh and filament and borrowed mineral. They are unfinished because the city is unfinished. They live by staying in motion even when standing still, by letting the damp write its slow signatures across them, by answering the roar with whatever voice or light or pressure they have left. When they finally stop—when someone lets the calcite take them all the way, or slides under the wide dark water at the continental rim, or simply dissolves into the maintenance nets and becomes another quiet pulse in the walls—the city does not mark the absence with silence. It simply routes the currents a little differently, grows a new baffle, raises a new generation of needle-fish with scale patterns that no one has seen before, and keeps breathing upward through the packed foundations.

That is who lives here. Not inhabitants exactly. Continuations.
