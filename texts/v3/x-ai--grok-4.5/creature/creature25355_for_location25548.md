The life here does not announce itself. It is already the greater part of what you see, the reason the plates and trays and overhead runs feel occupied rather than empty. At first the eye refuses the distinction; everything is damp ceramic, everything is soft edge and mineral sheen, and only gradually do the inhabitants separate from the architecture that grew them or was grown around them. They are dense the way silt is dense in still water, particle upon particle, each one fitted to the next by long refusal of motion.

Begin with the smallest, the ones that fill every hairline and bolt-shadow like wet grit. They are sack-bodied, translucent to the point of colorlessness except where the permanent film of water magnifies a faint internal mapping—threads of darker fluid that may once have been vessels or may simply be the memory of vessels. Their skins are matte and slightly adhesive; when two touch they remain touching, surfaces marrying without fusion, so that a whole joint of walkway can become a single composite organism held together by surface tension and the shared preference for not-moving. They breathe by slow osmotic exchange with the saturated air. A pulse, if it can be called that, travels through a cluster once every few hours: a collective thickening, a brief darkening, then the return to the same absolute stillness. Their eyes, when present, are no more than glossy depressions that do not open. They register light the way a sponge registers pressure—sieving it, keeping nothing.

Larger forms drape. These prefer the long horizontal runs of conduit and the undersides of gantries where the weep is constant. Their bodies are sheet-like, heavy, composed of overlapping laminations that look ceramic until you are close enough to see the flex. Each lamination is edged with a fringe of finer tissue that drinks the falling water and redistributes it across the upper surface so the creature remains evenly slick. They hang in folds and festoons, sometimes spanning ten meters or more, their weight borne by dozens of short, rootlike anchors that have fused to the metal long ago. The anchors are the only part that ever changes: over vast intervals they may secrete a little more material, thickening the bond, but the body proper does not travel, does not retract, does not rearrange. Half-lidded eyes—dozens, irregularly placed—dot the upper faces. They do not track. They simply remain aimed at whatever angle the draping settled into, so that some stare into the underside of the next plate while others regard the shallow lake below, and a few face backward along their own length as if contemplating the decision that placed them here. When condensation draws a brighter line across one of them, the tissue darkens briefly beneath the streak and then fades, an almost chemical acknowledgment.

In the open trays, where water stands ankle-deep or waist-deep across expanses wide as former fields, the greater shapes rest. These are the ones the eye catches even at distance: low islands of flesh and plate, backs awash, outlines softened by the unending surface film. Some are broad and domed, carapaced in the same dark alloy as the Fabric itself, the shell grown in irregular tessellations that never repeat a pattern twice. Between the plates soft interstitial skin shows, pale and beaded, rising and falling with breaths so shallow the water above them barely convexes. Others lack hard covering altogether; they are piles of slow musculature, segmented, each segment carrying its own scattered eyes and its own small fringe of feeding filaments that comb the mineral water without urgency. Nothing is hunted. Nothing flees. The filaments taste the same dissolved metals the air carries, the same faint signature of heat exchangers and old forgings, and appear satisfied. Occasionally one of the great bodies shifts a limb by a fraction—an adjustment measured in millimeters across an afternoon—and the wet sound of skin finding new skin travels a little way before the brown silence swallows it. That is the largest motion the trays ever see.

There are other genotypes, less easily classed. In the honeycomb vaults live clusters of upright, inverted forms that hang like curing hides, each one a tapering sack ending in a crown of stiff sensory rods. The rods click once in a lifetime—perhaps at the moment of final settling—and thereafter serve only as passive wetness gauges, darkening or lightening with ambient saturation. Along vertical faces cling radially symmetrical things half-melded into the ceramic, their bodies flush, their many limbs reduced to decorative ridges that channel condensate into deliberate runnels. In the shadowed elbows of pipework wait the nearly formless: living plugs of tissue that have taken the exact internal dimensions of their niches and then forgotten how to be smaller. They seal. They occupy. Their metabolism is the ambient heat; they require nothing further.

Across all of them runs the same mild discrepancy the place itself carries. Eyes open in directions the body does not face, so that a draping form may regard you with an orifice located on what should be its trailing edge. Limbs end in soft bifurcations that never quite match left to right. Here and there a creature’s surface shows a seam identical to the weld-seams of the Fabric, as if it were manufactured in the same pour and simply continued living after the molds were broken. When they adjust—rarely, slightly—the movement can leave a hairline gap between body and substrate, and the darker water that fills it seems older than the rest, carrying a taste that has been inside something. The air exhaled from certain folds occasionally smells of deep lung, of sealed cavities, of breath that has circulated too long without exit. These imperfections do not trouble the equilibrium. They are simply further textures within it.

Collectively they form a single pressure of presence. The Low Fabric is not inhabited so much as thickened by them. They are the reason the warmth never cools and the reason the moisture never finishes falling or drying. Their stillness is not death; it is the perfected idle, the metabolism throttled to the exact revolutions of the buried exchangers and the sighing evaporators. No generation turns over. No corpse appears, because nothing has moved far enough from its birth-place or molding-place to require a death-place. Arrivals ended before memory. What remains is continuation without revision: warm-blooded mineral life, ceramic life, water-sheathed life, all of it listening to the same low brown note, all of it beaded with the same post-rain film, all of it facing slightly the wrong way in the soft, scattered light. They have become the marsh. They have become the quiet. They hang, pool, nest, and overlie while the Fabric keeps its finished temperature and the drops tick, endlessly, without ever amounting to rain.
