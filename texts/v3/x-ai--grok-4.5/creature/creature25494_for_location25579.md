The first things you notice are not bodies but motions inside the roar.

On the open salt they are almost nothing: a skitter of mites the color of the crust itself, their legs so fine the wind sometimes takes them for dust and flings them kilometers before they dig in again. Longer-legged sifters pace the polygons on jointed stilts, heads down, filtering the powdered silica for the rare spore that has blown free of a distant Pillar and died of exposure before it could root. These grazers move in loose, silent companies of three or seven; when two companies meet they simply merge or part without signal, as if the idea of territory has been erased along with the soil. Shadow-skimmers—flat, translucent kites of membrane and nerve—slide low across the glare, riding the thinnest cushions of heated air. They cast no real darkness, only a momentary coolness on the salt, and they feed by tasting the charge that builds where dust rubs itself raw. None of them ever look up. The Pillars are weather to them, not landscape.

Everything else has already climbed.

Approach a trunk and the density arrives like a pressure change. The bark is not surface but district. Herds of spindle-browsers work the spiral ledges in slow vertical migrations, their limbs elongated into living crampons, six or eight to a side, tipped with silica hooks that leave faint glittering scars. They rasp lichen and the soft new cambium with mouthparts like folded rasps; the sound is lost in the greater factory-noise, but you feel it in your sternum if you stand close. Their hides are the bruised purples and dull silver of the Pillar itself—camouflage that is really just continuity. Young ones ride the parents’ backs in frosted clumps until their own hooks harden. They never descend. A fall from any height ends in the same fine red mist that the wind then carries as another kind of dust.

Higher, where the first true branches begin to shoulder outward, the membrane-wings turn the thermals into visible architecture. They are not birds. Each is a single broad reciprocal sail of living tissue stretched between spar-bones that can furl into a spike or open into a ten-meter airfoil without seam. Their calls are ultrasonic knives that somehow cut through the low industrial thundergett; flocks wheel in stacked gyres, feeding on the clouds of midges that hatch from nectar drips and on the aerial plankton that the Pillar’s own exhalations create. When a rotor-storm tightens, the flocks simply become part of it—hundreds of bodies scissoring through the same spiral, using the shear as free lift. Some never land. Mating is a mid-air docking that lasts for days, the pair tumbling inside a temporary double sail while the world howls around them.

The predators are quieter than silence. Grapple-forms hang in the mid-canopy voids, all tendon and barbed retractable limbs, waiting with a stillness that looks like absence. When they drop they do not fall; they select a trajectory and become it. A browser is unhooked from its ledge and folded into a traveling knot of limbs before the rest of the herd registers the gap. The grapple-form eats while moving, always moving, because stillness on a Pillar is only another word for becoming substrate. Their young are deposited into fresh growth-wounds like living sutures; they feed first on the aggressive scar tissue the Pillar pours out, then turn outward.

There are deeper residents.

Inside the wheezing cambial galleries, things like elongated gauntlets of muscle and plate move through the resin channels. They are not parasites. They are more like mobile glands—kept, or perhaps simply tolerated, because they rationalize the flow, chew blockages into slurry, and excrete refined polymers the Pillar immediately re-weaves. Their eyes are clusters of pressure sensors; light has never touched them. When a section of bark is ready to slough, these inner workers seal themselves behind new walls and wait out the catastrophe in a kind of factory hibernation. After the plate crashes to the plain and rings like a dropped girder, they awaken to an entirely rebuilt neighborhood and begin again.

And there are the ones that almost have names.

At the crowning packages of foliage—where strap-leaves the length of ships clatter together in the standing gales—live the huskers. Bipedal if they choose to be, four-armed as a rule, their lower pair specialized for brachiating through the bladder-frond thickets, their upper pair delicate enough to tease open a spiral trap without springing it. They wear the weather on their skin: a living laminate of photosynthetic scales that shift from silver to deep green depending on the angle of the merciless light. They speak in chest-resonances tuned to travel beneath the roar, a language of long drones and clicks that plucks the Pillar’s own wood like an instrument. They harvest the crystalline nectar before it falls, brew it in pouches under their tongues into a stimulant that lets them hang motionless for days in the thin air while storms walk past on legs of dust. They do not build. Anything shaped is only held for a season and then released into the next catastrophic frond-drop. Their dead are carried to the open wounds and pressed into the rushing cambium so that the Pillar will remember them as a slight sweet irregularity in the next ring of wood.

Some say the Pillars themselves are the final inhabitants—that every twitching browser and every gliding membrane is only a detachable thought the great plants are having. When a Pillar vents its hydraulic heart and sheds a third of its mass in one long roaring suicide of timber and flying gardens, the surviving fauna simply transfer to the wound and begin the work of midwifing the rebuild. In that reading there are no separate lives, only organs that happen to walk.

Yet travelers who have climbed high enough, who have stuffed their ears with torn cloth and still felt their teeth sing, report another presence. In the thin atmosphere just above the crown, where the light is absolute and the curvature of the world is plain, something rides the uppermost thermals that is neither membrane-wing nor husker. It is glimpsed as a long pale inflection, a fold in the air that sometimes opens an eye the color of old ice. It does not feed. It does not fall. It simply keeps station above the roaring green factory as if listening to the chord the whole highland makes—the tectonic lower note of the salt plain grinding its lost ocean, answered by each Pillar in a voice of living wood and pressurized sap. The chord continues. The pale inflection continues with it. Whether it lives here or is only the place dreaming that it is watched has never been settled.

Everything that survives does so by becoming weather or by becoming architecture or by riding the knife-edge between the two. Nothing rests. Nothing is finished. The light keeps pouring. The dust keeps polishing the bones of the world. And on the Pillars—always and only on the Pillars—the density of refusing-to-stop burns cool and green and forever unfinished.
