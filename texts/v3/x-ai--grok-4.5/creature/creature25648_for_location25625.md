The first things you meet are not the largest.

They arrive as weather. The metallic-green beetles do not so much fly as revise the air: ordered vortices that tighten and loosen like living smoke-rings, each wing-case a tiny hinged mirror clacking against its neighbor. Up close a single beetle is almost nothing—an emerald thumbnail with legs like fine wire and a head that pivots in clockwork increments—but together they become a single restless membrane, a mobile stained-glass window that flocks between buttresses and then dissolves into individuals again the moment a shadow passes. They drink nectar that has not yet decided whether it is liquid or light. Their larvae live inside the damp heartwood of the older trunks, chewing galleries that later become the organ-pipes for the wind. When a beetle dies mid-air its body does not fall; the green unhooks from the chitin and hangs a moment longer, a color still remembering flight, before the husk spirals down to be unmade by softer mouths.

Above them, and through them, move the fox-faced ones.

They are no larger than house-cats stretched thin by hunger and curiosity. Fur the brown of wet leaf-litter, faces tapered to black leather points, eyes too large and set too far forward, as if the skull itself were trying to become a pair of listening hands. They travel the liana bridges in soft explosions of number: one moment a solitary scout tasting the air with a tongue the color of peeled ginger, the next a continuous ribbon of bodies, each screaming a note half a tone higher or lower than its neighbor so the whole troop becomes a single gliding chord that Doppler-shifts as it rounds a corner of living wall. Their hands are clever without being cruel. They prune. They pollinate by accident. They steal starchy pith from the same cavities the beetles later colonize. At dusk they press themselves into the hollow elbows of strangler figs and become, for a few hours, part of the architecture—warm knots that breathe and dream in high, thin whistles. Sometimes a color detaches from one’s shoulder and continues a little way without it, a cinnamon ghost that the next animal walks through without notice.

Lower, where the light pools honey-thick on the root-plates, the slate birds drum.

Flightless, heavy-boned, their plumage the exact non-color of wet stone under tree-shadow. They walk with the deliberate rocking gait of creatures that have never needed to hurry and have forgotten how. Each bird claims a resonant buttress for minutes at a time, cocking its head to listen to the trunk’s internal weather before striking with a beak like a polished adze. The sound is not territorial so much as conversational: a deep woody knock that travels the xylem and is answered, after a pause just long enough to feel intentional, by another bird three chambers away. When two claimants meet they do not fight; they drum in overlapping rhythms until one rhythm absorbs the other and the loser simply wanders off to find a quieter plate. Their nests are scrapes lined with shed liana-skin and the iridescent wing-cases of beetles that flew too close to the wrong flower. Chicks hatch already knowing the hollow grammar of the place; their first cries are perfect minor thirds that slot into the continuous subsonic choir without friction.

But these are only the bright threads.

Beneath and between runs the quieter population, the ones that never announce. Pale amphibia the length of a finger live their entire lives in the water cups of the tank-bromeliads, swimming in tight circles through tea-colored despised rain, eating mosquito larvae that themselves never survive long enough to bite anything larger. Velvet worms the color of old bruises spill sticky lassos across the night side of leaves, looping springtails and the soft newly-hatched. Colonies of translucent isopods migrate up and down the blackwater filament like living dew, farming the fungal felts that grow wherever remaindered light meets remaindered moisture. There are moths whose wings are indistinguishable from the lichen maps on the high bark until they choose to move, and then the map peels itself free and flutters, leaving behind a bare oval of younger green that will take a season to heal. Everything eats and is eaten in loops so tight they feel like respiration.

And still the place is not full. The hollowness insists.

What lives in the emptiness itself is harder to name because it does not always agree to be singular.

Sometimes, when a corridor folds and returns you to your own footprints, you realize the footprints are already occupied—by a presence that is mostly pressure, a density in the damp air that has learned the shape of attention. It has no face, yet it regards. It tastes of crushed mint and the ozone left after impossible annexes collapse. Anchored nowhere, it drifts the way a held breath drifts through a sleeping body. The fox-faced troops fall silent when it passes; the slate birds miss a beat in their drumming; the beetle vortices tighten into defensive spheres and then spar out again once the pressure has moved on. It does not hunt. It simply occupies the same volume you do for a little while, and in that overlap you feel your own edges grow uncertain, as if you too might detach a color or leave a sound behind as a glistening thread.

Deeper still—or higher, or sideways, the directions here being negotiable—there are the ones who grew from the dreams the Congeries had after it finished dreaming cities.

They have no fixed morphology. One may appear as a slow vertical procession of leaves that have decided to walk, each leaf a step, the whole procession the color of shade under water. Another may be a braid of air-roots that has taught itself to gesture, forming temporary hands that pluck luminous pollen out of drifting sun-shafts and press it into new configurations that last only as long as the light angle allows. A third may be nothing but a mobile reluctance in the geometry: a place where distance fighters itself so that whatever tries to cross arrives younger, or older, or flowering. These beings do not build and do not tool; they edit. They prune possibilities the way the primates prune vines. Where they linger, a thoroughfare may quietly become a memory of a thoroughfare, complete with the ghost-scent of traffic that never quite happened. Where they depart, ordinary causality seeps back in like ground-water, ashamed of its own rigidity.

At the center of the largest undercroft, where the dust-motes turn in their private weather and the nearest green is twenty paces of pure waiting air, something older sits.

It is not plant and not animal. It is the settled negotiation itself, given just enough continuity to notice its own continuance. Vast without size, warm without fever, it has the patient geometry of the walls raised to the power of awareness. It does not move; motion happens around it the way blood moves around a heart that has decided to rest for a century or two. When the soft vertical rains begin, the ten thousand throats answer, and the emptiness cups the noise and gives it back louder; the something at the center receives that returned sound the way lungs receive a second breath. It does not speak. It has never needed to. Every creak of adjusting timber, every clack of beetle-case, every descending leaf that takes an hour or a heartbeat is already one of its long, braided thoughts becoming audible.

Live here long enough—though “long” is another elastic—and you begin to suspect that nothing merely inhabits the Hollow Congeries.

Everything is a local focus the place is briefly using to feel itself.

The beetles are how it tastes spectrum.

The primates are how it braids voice into motion.

The slate birds are how it knocks on its own bones to confirm they still resonate.

The pale things in the water-cups are how it keeps its memory wet.

The pressure that regards is how it wonders what it would be like to be looked at from inside a smaller, warmer skull.

And the slow leaf-processions, the gesturing roots, the reluctant geometries—those are the Congeries experimenting with what else a city can still become after it has already become everything necessary.

Stay until the next rain.

Feel the plants lean in by the width of a breath.

Notice that your own throat has begun, without instruction, to answer the falling water in a pitch you did not know you contained.

You are not visiting.

You are one more hollow the green has decided to frame, one more emptiness invited to sing.
