The ones who live here do not arrive so much as condense.

At first they are only the expected tenants, the ones any high basin would claim after the ice has withdrawn: the white ungulates with their chisel hooves, moving in loose constellations across the moss rivers at first light, bodies the same withheld color as the residual snow. They graze with an intensity that looks like haste, heads down, breath pluming in short counted bursts, then lift all at once and flow over the rim as if poured. The birds are quieter still—ground-nesters whose plumage is the exact grit-and-lichen pattern of the bowl itself—so that a flock exploding upward can seem, for a heartbeat, like the floor taking flight. Their eggs are laid in scrapes no deeper than a palm, warmed by the same thin sun that never quite sinks. The pale fox treats the talus as a single folded garment; it appears already mid-step, tongue the briefest pink, and vanishes by simply ceasing to be on any side you watch. Raptors ride the thermals in long silent ellipses, casting shadows that sometimes arrive a second before or after their makers. And beneath everything the true majority: midges and black flies bookkeeping the heat, rising in smokes whenever the temperature crosses freezing, collapsing again into glittering dust the moment the cold reasserts. Plants hold the ground in clenched green kingdoms—cushions of jade that bounce underfoot, moss thick enough to bury a wrist, dwarf willows compressed into orchards the height of a kneeling child—blooming in one-day riots of yellow and violet whenever the damp and the light agree.

All of these are residents. They keep the schedules the cold dictates. Yet they are not the ones the amphitheater seems arranged for.

What truly lives here has no single outline. It is easier to meet it first as an effect of the place’s unfinished physics. When the light pools too generously under a moss cushion and silhouettes your legs from below, something is standing in that upward glow, testing the new brightness the way a tongue tests a broken tooth. When a shadow falls three degrees off the sun’s true angle, stretches, then snaps back into honesty, something has just finished crossing. The fox that steps behind a boulder and never reappears on the far side has simply handed its shape, for a few paces, to whatever was already walking the route the stone remembers better than air does.

They are not spirits exactly, and not animals that forgot to become visible. They are the density that collects wherever the young mountain is still deciding how light and weight and time should behave. In the brief noon windows, when meltwater flashes in every pockmark and the air fills with the ticking of insects, you can almost see them: low, many-jointed suggestions the color of wet stone and spring moss, moving in the same furtive hops as the birds yet leaving no print that lasts longer than the damp. Their limbs end in something between hoof and root, gripping the grit the way cushion plants grip, as if the body itself were a way of holding territory against the next freeze. They do not breathe the thin air so much as filter it, tasting the counted oxygen the way the willows taste dew. At night, when the rock reclaims its frost and the cirque walls prism residual light into faint geometric glows, the same presence draws those glows inward, using them the way the lean-to uses dung-fire: small stored warmths against the expansion of ice.

They graze the same moss rivers the ungulates do, but more thoroughly, cropping not only green but the cold film beneath the stones and the faint mineral sweetness left by glacial polish. They nest nowhere permanent; instead they settle into the old human sleeping rings for an hour or a night, fitting their several bodies into the curve a single traveler once made, then rise and leave the stones exactly as found. Sometimes they add a single new flake of chert or a twist of grass already tied for fire, the precise gift a seasonal people would recognize. It is not imitation. The amphitheater has only just been framed; everything that uses it must still share tools.

Their numbers are uncertain because they refuse to be counted. One evening you may sense a whole small herd of them working the northern scree, a soft continuous click like settling plates. The next dawn there is only a solitary shape paused at the center of the bowl, tall as a standing child yet somehow as expansive as the empty air around it, turning its featureless attention toward the knife-edge where the sun will return. In the temporary mirrors of meltwater their reflections appear a heartbeat late, or arrive first and wait for the body to catch up. This is ordinary to them. The place is still rehearsing its rules; they simply learned the rehearsal faster than rock or mammal.

Travelers who sleep in the lean-to sometimes wake knowing they were stepped over carefully, the way one steps over a resting bird. A few have left offerings and found, days later on a distant ridge, the same bone needle returned and wrapped in a thin braid of saxifrage stem still living. Most never notice anything more than an extra quiet inside the ordinary quiet, a sense that the distances between cushion plants are being used by someone who finds those distances sufficient. The unusual light, the mismatched shadows, the animals that take impossible routes—these are not haunting. They are housekeeping. The ones who live here are simply keeping the amphitheater’s young physics from settling too soon, walking the unfixed edges so the mountain does not forget it is still rising.

In the end they are the room’s memory of itself before soil finishes forming, before forests climb this high, before the ice decides whether to return. They will remain as long as the rock stays pale and angular and the moisture arrives only sideways. When the place finally softens into ordinary alpine meadow they will thin, become rumor, become the faint delay between a fox and its shadow. Until then they are the inhabitants the hanging amphitheater grew for: small, swift, light-storing, and almost—but not quite—the same temperature as the day.
