The first thing that lives here is the damp itself—an intelligence older than lungs, threaded through every sponge and stoma. It has no single body, only local thickenings that sometimes lift free of the peat and move. These thickenings are the dwellers most native to the high country: the ones the ungulates step carefully around without ever quite seeing, the ones the long-legged birds thread their nests beside as if beside kin.

They stand, when they stand at all, between the height of a kneeling fern and the height of a young horsetail. Their limbs are jointed loosely, more suggestion than bone, wrapped in living nap—sphagnum that greens and browns with the hour’s light, filmy liverworts that pulse open and shut across the ribs. What might once have been skin has become a continuous capillary field. Water does not wet them; it completes them. When they walk, each footfall is a slow transfusion: the black paste of the soil climbs a little way up the ankle, trades minerals, and settles again. They leave no lasting print, only a slightly deeper green that fades before the next fog beads and drops.

Their faces—if the word still applies—are smooth planes of the same damp tissue, broken only by a pair of long, horizontal slits that serve for both breath and sight. Through those slits the world arrives already filtered aqua-gray, edges softened, metals gleaming like half-forgotten purposes. They do not blink. Moisture forms its own lenses and refreshes them. Inside the chest cavity, if one were to listen with a palm pressed gently (they do not object; objection is a drier climate’s habit), there is no single heart but a distributed tide: the same slow hydraulic rhythm that forces mineral water through the basalt labyrinths beneath. When the larger seeps pulse, the dwellers pulse with them. Their blood, if blood is the word, runs ochre and malachite by turns, staining the moss of their wrists in the same concentric smithings the rock itself wears.

They feed by stillness more often than by motion. A dweller will settle cross-legged or simply collapse into a moss-mound beside a working seep and open the permeable skin of the inner arms. Metal ions migrate inward; sugars migrate outward. The exchange is courteous and unhurried. Sometimes a carabid beetle, iridescent and purposeful, will quarry a free edge of crust while the dweller rests; the beetle is not driven off. Everything is already inside the same metabolism. At rarer intervals they browse the fruiting cushions of the bryophytes, nipping the spore-caps with small, keratinous plates that serve for teeth. The taste is of cold copper and chlorophyll and something faintly sweet, like syrup left too long in mist. They need little. The land is already digesting itself for them.

Reproduction is a weather. When a front stalls long enough for the hanging valleys to fill with an unbroken, warmish rain, two or more dwellers incline toward one another until their mosses interlock. Threads of mycelium—always present, always white and questing—braid across the joined surfaces. Over hours or days a new thickening swells between them, taking the minerals offered by both and the water offered by the storm. When the weather shifts, the newcomer simply walks away on whatever number of limbs it has grown, already fluently damp, already fluent in the low-frequency songs that the slate-colored frogs teach without teaching. There is no childhood distinct from the plateau. There is only smaller patience becoming larger patience.

They make almost nothing that lasts, yet their industry is constant. Along the seeps they encourage certain flanges with light scrapes of a fingernail (the nail itself a thin plate of deposited manganese) or discourage others by shading them with a palmful of transplanted cushion-moss. The resulting chambers and baffles alter the flow by fractions of a finger’s width, enough to change the taste of a stream two ridges distant or to lengthen the warm exhalation of a thermal seep by half an hour each dusk. These adjustments accumulate across centuries the way peat accumulates: unnoticed, decisive. When a dweller dies—when the hydraulic tide finally stills—the body does not fall so much as settle. Within a season it is indistinguishable from any other moss hummock, except that the metal content is higher and the next generation of clubmoss grows a deeper green. The mycelium remembers the shape for a while longer, then relinquishes even that.

Communication among them is mostly tactile and chemical. A trailing fingertip drawn along another’s forearm leaves a temporary track of exchanged ions that the recipient tastes for news: which valley is running clearer this month, which col has thickened its fog into a standing lake, which herd of soft-footed ungulates has shifted its moss-path by three body-lengths to avoid a new quaking lawn. The low singing of the frogs is also theirs; they amplify it by opening the chest slits a fraction wider, sending the vibration through the peat so that a listener three ridgetops away feels it first as a warmth in the wrist bones. Actual voiced sound is rare and always brief—a single descending note, almost identical to the bird that nests inside hollow horsetails. It means, roughly, *here*, or *still here*, or simply *yes*.

On the rare clear days when the hard blue sky presses down and the metal seeps glint like abandoned tools, the dwellers grow briefly more visible to themselves. They stand a little taller, moss steaming faintly. Some tilt their faces upward as if testing a rumor of dryness. None of them leave. Leaving would require becoming someone who could bear the memory of thirst. Instead they wait for the haze to close again, for the continent of green to reassert its self-contained weather, and for the quiet foundry of the uplands to resume the only work it has ever known: turning more of itself into more of itself, with these permeable thickenings as both witnesses and instruments.

There are other lives, of course—the pearled ungulates moving single-file, the long-legged birds, the industrial salamanders, the crane-fly mists—but they all move inside the same patient breath. The dwellers are simply the places where that breath has decided, for a while, to grow eyes and walk. They are not guardians. They are not spirits. They are the high country, briefly concentrated, looking out through horizontal slits at the endless, unsloping skin of stone and saturated green that has forgotten every sea but its own.
