The dwellers here are not Born so much as Condensed. They precipitate from the same saturated gray that weeps the plain into being, and they understand—without ever framing it as knowledge—that existence is only a temporary stiffening of water and rust against the urge to slump back into slurry. Nothing arrives from elsewhere. Nothing leaves. The population is the weather of making and unmaking, cycling through bodies the way the vaportowers cycle through turbines.

Begin with the smallest constancy: the bruise-colored sheets of iron bacteria. They are not passive scum. In the perpetual late-afternoon light they pulse in slow radial waves, each wave a decision about where the next piston will thrust itself up. They taste the mineral crust for fatigue-fractures and conspire, colony by colony, to feed those fractures microscopic blooms of oxide until a cylinder wall is ready to stand. When a gear-train assembles overhead, the bacteria darken beneath it, drinking the sparks that freeze mid-air; the charged meal lets them spin finer crystalline rimes that night, pseudo-leaves sharp enough to snip a cooling fin from a passing crab. They have no centers and no edges that last the hour, yet they keep the entire plain’s metabolism solvent. Without them the engines would rise too clean, too permanent, and the equilibrium would tip toward true ruin. They refuse that. Their quiet work is the oldest law.

Larger and lonelier are the bipedal crabs. Each is a knot of condenser coil and cartilage that decided, one saturated morning, to stand. They pick their way on jointed stilts that continuously resolder themselves from floating flakes. What looks like a carapace is only denser cold wrapped around a gut of spinning armatures. They snip cooling fins not from hunger in the ordinary sense but because the act of shearing metal completes a circuit inside them; the sudden voltage drop is their only version of sweetness. After feeding they pause, knife-legs locked, and vibrate at a frequency that makes nearby vapor-stacks lean toward them like attentive animals. Sometimes a crab will climb a half-formed tower and ride the collapse, letting the rain-that-is-becoming-wire rewire its own limbs mid-fall. They do not name themselves, yet each carries a private resonance—an ultrasonic signature left over from the last short-circuit it survived—that the bat-flocks recognize and avoid. A crab that loses too many fins becomes light enough for the copper skates to notice. Then the end is quick: enclosure, three tight revolutions of useless kicking, a final inductive cough, and the crab’s coils are claimed as nesting material for the next spontaneous gear-train. The skates leave the cartilage. It dissolves by evening into the general weeping.

The herds are different—lower, more numerous, almost tender in their persistence. Many-jointed browsers move in loose constellations through the finger-deep sheets, mouthparts revolving like patient drill-heads. They graze the bacterial iron and, in grazing, heat it; the warm oily sludge they trail behind is both digestion and cartography. Other herds follow these steaming paths the way ancient animals once followed river courses. Their bodies are frankensteined from shellfish teeth, black ice, and strips of something that remembers being rubber insulation. When two herds converge, they do not fight. They mesh. Joints lock, drill-heads synchronize, and for a span of minutes the combined mass becomes a single, larger milling machine that chews a temporary channel into the crust. Water rushes in, colder than before. Then the mesh softens; individuals pull free, some newly equipped with an extra limb borrowed from a neighbor, and the herds drift apart again. They communicate by the pitch of their drilling. A rising whine means a skate is near; a sudden polyphonic grind means a vapor-tower is about to form and the charge-rich rain will be good. Calves—smaller knots of fewer joints—move in the centers of the herds, protected less by affection than by the sheer density of revolving metal. Reality’s fraying helps them: a calf may flicker ten seconds ahead, already larger, already trailing its own sludge, and the present herd simply adjusts around the double-exposure as if this were ordinary weather.

Above, the membrane-and-spark-gap flocks own the dim sky. They are almost bat, almost ragged kite, almost the memory of a scream that taught itself wings. Their flight is a continuous negotiation with unfinished electricity. They dive to short the forming turbines because the released charge is blood-warm to them; it knits microscopic tears in their membranes and lets them climb again. Their cries—knives down elevator shafts—are not warnings or songs but surplus voltage bleeding off. When the light double-exposes, whole flocks appear in two places at once, and the only way to count them afterward is by the new holes burned in the clouds. Predators among them (for some have grown long, whip-thin trailing filaments) hunt by flying through their own future echoes and striking where the sound has already arrived. A successful kill mid-air produces a brief, bright rain of frozen sparks that the bacteria below drink greedily. The flocks never roost. Roosting would require a stillness the plain does not sell. Instead they thread themselves through the taller, shorter-lived smokestacks of pure cloud, letting the collapse re-fold their wings into fresher shapes.

And then there are the skates—the quiet ones, the copper that still remembers how to oxidize with intention. They glide just beneath the surface film like thoughts the water is having about hunger. Flat, broad, patient. Their bodies are single-circuit: when they fold upward around a browsing machine-beast they become a Faraday cage that cooks the prey in its own unfinished electricity. Digestion is slow and elegant; current is siphoned, stored in laminar layers of patina, and released later as a soft magnetic pulse that helps the next generation of gear-teeth align. A skate that has fed well will sometimes rise half out of the water and hold itself vertical for an hour, a dark mirror reflecting the slate sky, as if considering whether to become a tower instead. Most sink back. A few, over seasons of metal fatigue, do transmute—losing mobility, gaining height, joining the spontaneous industry as permanent (for a day) compression chambers. The herds graze around these new structures without surprise. Everything here has already been everything else enough times that transformation registers only as schedule.

There are quieter lives still. In the instant between a piston’s exhaust and its softening back into slurry lives a kind of mayfly intelligence composed entirely of pressure differentials. It experiences the full cycle as one long, ecstatic shrug, then ceases. Between the teeth of a forming gear-train flicker soft, moth-like multiplications of pure friction that mate by grinding and die by polishing each other into ordinary grit. Even the double-exposed moments—those ten-second ghosts of engines not yet risen—host pale, translucent herds that graze the future sludge and leave no trail the present can keep.

No single mind rules. No council of crabs or parliament of skates. What lives here is the plain’s own ongoing decision to remain noisy. The dwellers are that decision wearing temporary shapes. They feel the pressure in the knees (a woundless ache that is their only shared emotion) whenever the forgotten concept of slope tries to reassert itself; in answer they manufacture faster, graze harder, short more towers, until flatness is re-imposed. They do not fear the way reality frays. Fraying is how they breathe. A piston finishing its stroke inside a chest cavity is merely intimacy. A flock emerging counted differently is merely reincarnation without the bother of death. The air tastes of wet batteries and old snow because that is the flavor of perpetual occupation.

At the limits of vision where the industry only densifies, the dwellers grow stranger and more patient still. There the browsers have learned to marry the copper skates, producing long hybrid ribbons that snake through the shallows spinning completed valves behind them like egg cases. The bat-flocks there have begun to weave the spontaneous wire-rain into aerial nests that last almost an afternoon. The bacteria have started to write slow, bruise-colored glyphs that the crabs pause to read with their vibrating legs—glyphs that may be maps of engines a century from condensing, or simply the bacteria’s way of laughing. Nothing ends. The plain has forgotten height and seed and wall, forgotten any story that finishes. What lives here is only the work of staying: the cold, ringing, wet, metallic insistence that manufacture continue, that every gap be filled with something chewing or spinning or shorting, forever and without remainder. They are busy. They have always been busy. The gray wetness does not remember a time when they were not.
