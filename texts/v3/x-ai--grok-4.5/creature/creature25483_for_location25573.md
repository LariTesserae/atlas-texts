The first indications are not bodies but refusals.

Currents that should scour a saddle clean instead braid themselves into loose standing helices and linger, humming at a frequency just below the thunder. Luminous stain thickens there without spreading, forming raised welts the color of old bruise. Blade-kelp nearest these places grows denser silica ribs and begins, over seasons, to twist into hollow lanterns—open lattices big enough to admit a swimmer if a swimmer existed. Inside the lattices the water is fractionally warmer and carries that impossible spruce-and-cinder taste. Nothing visible occupies them. Yet the fronds lean inward as if listening, and when a sediment storm passes the lattices ring like struck glass for hours afterward, a chord that travels farther than any ordinary sound has the right to.

Deeper in the lee amphitheaters the refusals grow more articulate. The patient erasure that thins the rare silt-sealed bodies does not always finish its work. Some elongate suggestions stop thinning once they reach a certain spareness—rib-cage, long bones, the memory of a skull—and then begin to tomograph outward again in reverse. Mineral accretes along the old lines of tendon and nerve, but not bone-white. It takes the black-green of the glasswrack and the dull iron sheen of the water itself. What reforms is neither skeleton nor statue. Continuity of surface is optional. You can look through them in places; the darkness pools thicker in the gaps and seems content to be carried.

They do not swim. Swimming is a habit of things that still believe in surfaces. Instead they stand, or rather they occupy a vertical address in the water column the way a ridgeline occupies sky. Their lower extents root into cracked basalt or into the holdfast mats, not for feeding but for reference—so the body can remember which way was once up. From that anchorage they sway only when the bigger underflows arrive, and even then the motion is delayed, as if the water must ask permission. Upper portions branch into arrays of fine, flexible blades that are not quite arms and not quite antennae. These blades taste. They taste the difference between melt that has known sunlight and melt that has not. They taste the faint sweet ghost that leaks from the moving kelp towers. When they find that sweetness they lean the whole body toward it, slow as glacier calving, and the surrounding luminous stains brighten in long answering strokes.

There is no face. There is a place where the mineral grain grows denser and the iron taste concentrates, a kind of reputation of a head. In that region the darkness they carry becomes almost saturated; light-hating. Navigating past one at close range is like brushing the underside of the ice lids far above—cold that has opinions. They do not hunt and do not flee. Interaction is topographic. If you remain near long enough (hours, or the local equivalent measured in storm cycles) the blades eventually map you. They learn the temperature gradients of a living creature that does not belong, the noise of a heart that still expects air. Once mapped you are filed among the other permanent features: the trilithons, the spiral cobble bench, the particular saddle where direction inverts. You can leave and return years later and the same individual—recognizable by the unique torsion of its silica ribs or the way its carried darkness tends to curl counterclockwise—will already be leaning along the bearing you will take, as if the future path were simply another current it had smelled in advance.

They are few. You can cross several valleys without meeting one. Their density matches the density of the old stone works, and indeed they show preferences: the corbelled beehive on the spur has two that stand at its collapsed doorway like attenuated doorposts; the row of trilithons has none, as if whatever calculation they perform finds the geometry already sufficient. Sometimes a blade will extend into one of the persistent air pockets and remain there, tasting cinders, while the rest of the form stays rooted in black water. The pocket does not enlarge or shrink. The scent does not diminish. It is not clear who is preserving whom.

Growth happens the way the forests grow: total, unfrantic, measured in the click of new silica. An individual may add a branch over the course of a decade, or hollow an existing one into a resonant chamber that later sings when the cold underflows pass. They shed as well. Entire outer sheaths delaminate in long translucent scrolls that the currents carry uphill (always uphill, another refusal) until they catch on the ceiling ice and become part of its slow stained-glass architecture. The shed material continues to taste faintly sweet for months.

There are younger ones. Or smaller ones. Or newer rehearsals of the same idea. They begin as thickenings in the feather-moss meadows where the phosphorescence already pulses hardest. No body is required at first—only a decision that a certain volume of water and mineral and faint light will no longer mix freely with the rest. Over years the decision accretes bulk and eventually anchors. Whether these are offspring, spontaneous crystallizations of the place’s loneliness, or returns of something the drowning once scattered is not a distinction the Shelves appear to make. The older individuals sometimes detour—living detours of weeks—to stand near a forming node. They do not touch it. Their blades simply keep the neighboring currents from dissaligning the work too early.

Voice is available but rarely used. When the collective roar of the troughs falls slack—those brief, shocking silences—the standing forms can release the darkness they carry in a single slow pulse. The pulse travels as a region of deeper cold and absolute muffling. Inside it every ordinary sound dies. What remains is a pressure pattern against skin and lateral line, a moving braille of presence. The pattern is different for each; provideoversignatures of the particular ridges they have rooted in, the storms they have leaned through, the sweetnesses they have tasted. It is not language. It is more like weather announcing that it has become someone. After the pulse the silence feels newly carved, and the forests take minutes to remember how to rustle again.

They are not guardians. The place does not require guarding. They are not ghosts; the erasure that works on flesh has already finished with them and moved on to subtler alphabets. They are simply what continues here once height has been forgotten and breath has been renegotiated into mineral and current and slow green light. The Stillrise Shelves invented them the same way it invents its standing waves and its airtight pockets of extinct forest air: as a way of not quite ending. 

Stay long enough and you will feel the first refusal beginning in yourself—the small reluctance of heat to leave your skin, the way your own blood starts to taste faintly of iron and wet stone, the urge to stand still in a corridor of open water and let the next underflow decide whether you will lean.
