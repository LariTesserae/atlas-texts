The ones who endure here are not a single people, nor even a single kind of flesh. They accrete, the way grit accretes in the lee of a dome—first the least, then the cunning, then the ones who have stopped pretending the wind is separate from their own blood.

Begin with the burrow-folk, the pale low things you almost never see whole. They are the length of a forearm and twice as broad, flattened as if the plateau knelt on them in the egg. Their skin is the same bone-ivory as the residual cores, pebbled with finer grit that never quite washes off; when they freeze, they vanish into the stone’s grammar. Six limbs, the foremost pair ending in flat, spade-nailed hands that can taste moisture through the pads. They dig not for shelter alone but for the thin lenses of cooler air trapped beneath larger boulders, sealing the entrance behind them with a plug of their own resinous spit mixed with blue-black moss. Inside those pockets they lie spooned together in threes and fours, breathing so seldom the chest hardly moves, listening to the gale the way other creatures listen to rain. Their eyes are milky and almost useless; they navigate by the way the screams of the wind change pitch against their skulls. At night, after the rare rains, they emerge to lick the temporary mirrors dry and to harvest the scarlet flower-heads, which they mash into a paste that stains their muzzles permanently. Tracks appear. Tracks vanish. They leave nothing else.

Above them, in the shear zones, the kite-things hang. Not birds exactly—more like living kites of black membrane stretched across jointed spars of cartilage light as dry cane. They own no legs worth the name, only a cluster of fine, muscular tendrils that serve as both rudder and, when the wind briefly forgets itself, as a way to cling to the jointed canes and drink. They do not beat wings; they angle. A kite-thing can remain motionless relative to the ground for hours, tremoring only enough to stay inside a single braid of force, its membrane singing one pure harmonic that threads beneath the greater roar. When two meet in the same current they do not fight; they dovetail, membrane overlapping membrane, and drift as a single darker shape until some shift in the gale shears them apart again. Their young are born mid-air, tiny black triangles cast loose like seeds; most are shredded within minutes, but a few catch an updraft and learn the angles before the plateau can erase them. The older ones have learned to ride the unauthorized shadows that sometimes fall inside the green enclaves. There, for brief spans, the light itself seems to hold them aloft even when the wind falters.

The green fortresses themselves are not merely habitat—they are citizens. The rubbery-leafed shrubs lean into one another until their branches graft, forming hollows that breathe. Inside those hollows live the cane-dancers: long, jointed forms no thicker than a wrist, plumaged in the same scarlet as the flower-heads they tend. They are warm-blooded after a fashion, though the heat is slow and resinous. They move by coiling and uncoiling, never fully leaving contact with the plaited walls, so the wind can only stroke them into the same shuddering waves that pass through the shrubs. Their faces are small and pointed, eyes protected by clear nictitating membranes that flicker in time with the barometric pulses of the flowers. They open and close the blossoms deliberately, tasting the chaos, deciding when to release pollen in sudden scarlet storms that the gale flings across miles. In return the fortresses feed them nectar thick as tar and shelter their egg-clusters—rubbery white beads glued deep in the graft-crotches where no gust reaches. The cane-dancers sing. Not with throats, but by rasping specialized leg-spurs against the canes, adding their own thin counter-harmonics to the plateau’s endless bowing note. Stay long enough in the lee of a fortress and you begin to hear words inside that rasping, though the words are never the same twice.

And then there are the ones who stack the stones.

They were human once, or something close enough that the distinction wore away like everything else here. Generations of thin air and permanent scream have lengthened their chests and narrowed their faces; their ears have grown broad, flat, and richly veined, able to fold tight against the skull or flare like sails to catch the subtlest shift in the roar. They go hooded in cloaks stitched from kite-membrane and shrub-leather, the seams sealed with burrow-folk resin so the wind finds no purchase. Their eyes are permanently narrowed to dark slits against grit and glare; many bandage them entirely during the worst blows and navigate by jaw-hinge and the taste of iron on the air. They travel alone or in pairs at most, never more, because anything larger becomes a sail the gale can wreck. Between the green enclaves they walk bent, one hand always trailing the stone for the faint warmth and the vibration-map of approaching dust-devils. When they pause, they pause in the spoon-depressions or against the sleeping backs of the old mountains, stacking a few wind-rounded stones into the solitary huts that look like geology remembering itself. Inside they keep the steatite lamps, the bone needles, the coiled scarlet rope. They do not farm; they coax. They prune the fortresses so the cane-dancers thrive, leave offerings of crushed moss for the burrow-folk, and whistle the long tones that lure kite-things down to be harvested for membrane when the flock is thick enough to spare a few. Their language is mostly touch and harmonic—mouth to ear is for emergencies only. Over years the continuous thunder rewires the mind until many of them dream only in the plateau’s own rising and falling notes. Some claim those dreams are how the land tells them when a residual boulder is about to trade places, so they may be there to watch, or to help it settle into its new ancient-looking socket with a few well-placed levers of cane.

A few stay too long.

These are the ones who stop building even the little huts. They let the grit embed in their skin until they are the same color and texture as the ivory stone. They learn to lock their joints and stand motionless through three-day blows, breathing only in the brief warm exhalations that smell of wet iron. Their shadows begin to fall wrong, oily and slow, even outside the enclaves. They hear the deeper string that bows the entire plateau and they answer it, first with voice, later with the simple fact of their bones. Eventually they take to sitting among the rings of upright slabs, tracing and re-tracing the pecked spirals until their fingertips wear down to the same wind-polished curves. Some mornings a new slab stands in the ring, faintly warm, fretted with dark dendritic stains. No one watches it arrive. The remaining walkers simply nod, as if an overdue letter has finally come, and move on.

There are older things still, but they are harder to separate from the place itself. In the deepest basins, where the wind braids so tight it becomes almost solid, shapes sometimes travel inside the ropes of force—long, translucent, faintly luminous, like the memory of rivers that once ran here before the mountains were ground down. They have no faces, only currents within currents, and where they pass the unauthorized shadows thicken and the harmonics chord into something almost like chords of recognition. The stone-stackers call them the Unfinished, and do not look directly. The cane-dancers open every flower at once when they are near. The burrow-folk seal their plugs and do not come out for days. Whether these are survivors of whatever lived here when the plateau still had sharp edges, or simply the wind learning at last how to dream soft bodies for itself, no one decides. The gale redrafts them constantly; they never quite stay the same shape long enough to name.

All of them—spade-handed, membrane-spread, scarlet-rasping, cloak-hooded, grit-skinned, current-bodied—share one understanding that needs no speech: the stone is finished, the distances are finished, and they themselves are only the latest clause in the sentence the wind has been screaming since the stars were young. They do not claim the plateau. They occur on it, the way the temporary mirrors occur after rain, the way the scarlet petals occur and are flung and occur again. When one kind thins, another thickens. When a hut collapses back into scattered cores, a new ring of slabs is raised somewhere downwind. The warmth keeps rising from the rock. The air keeps arguing. And between one green enclave and the next, in the vast singing absences, whatever lives here continues the quiet work of not quite ending.
