The ones who live here are not a single shape, nor have they ever been. They arrive in the mind the way seepage arrives on the rock: first as a cool dark film, then as a thickening, then as something that chooses its own channels and will not be hurried.

They are tall when they stand, but they rarely stand fully upright for long. Their default is a long-limbed crouch that keeps the center of gravity low against the sudden side-winds that drop over the rim. Bones of a bird’s lightness, wrapped in muscle the blue-black of wet basalt, the same color as the ghost that coughs along the upper terraces at dusk—because that ghost is one of them, or rather one of their bodies left walking while the mind has gone elsewhere. Skin like abraded stone, finely cracked in patterns that collect the mineral film so that they glisten without ever looking wet. When they move through the moss the fronds close behind them with almost no sound; when they leap the short furious waterfalls they do so with hands and feet both splayed, sinew tearing tiny holds out of what looks like sheer face. Their nails are dark, thick, layered like shale, and self-sharpening against the quartz veins they climb for the pure mineral taste.

Faces are longer than human faces, tapering. Eyes set wide and slightly high, black-scleraed, adapted to the hard midday clarity and to the long blue dusk alike. No whites to flash and betray. Pupils open like slow apertures. They can hold a gaze for minutes without blinking while the delayed clouds continue their extra voyage across the nearest pond; sometimes they seem to watch those ghost-clouds more carefully than the living ones. Mouths are lipless slits that widen when they call. The call itself is the three-note descending whistle the rock hyraxes answer and embroider—except when the cirque is in full wind-voice, at which point their calling drops into the subsonic register that makes the quartz veins appear to pulse in answer. They are not making the mountain breathe. They are keeping time with it.

They are few. Never more than a dozen active bodies in the basin at once, and often far fewer. Yet the stone rings are theirs, or belong to the long braid of them that has occupied this same wet room since the glaciers finished their large work and withdrew into memory. Each ring is a sleeping place, a birth place, a place for the occasional deliberate fire when a digging stick must be hardened or a bone needle tempered. They travel light in the extreme: a cutting flake of the dark metavolcanic itself, edges pressure-flaked to a brightness that lasts only days before the mineral film dulls it again; cordage of willow bast and sinew; pouches of salamander skin sewn with hair. No metal has ever sung in this place at their hands. When they die (and they do die—falls, thin blood suddenly refusing the altitude after too many decades, the rare fever that climbs the massif like a lost goat) the others open the body under the open gulf side, let the moths and the raptors do the first quick work, then fold the cleaned bones into the nearest moss hummock. Within a season the green has closed. Nothing is left that the wind can take.

They eat what the basin gives and almost nothing else. Frilled-gill salamanders flipped from the ponds at first light, swallowed whole while still cold enough to ache. The pale moths, roasted so briefly over twigs that the wings blacken into crisp matrices of ash that dissolve on the tongue. Summer bulbs of the alpine willow dug with the fire-hardened sticks, the same sticks later used to pry liverwort mats aside for the crunchy pale grubs that live against the till. They do not hunt the lean six-legged browsers that sometimes cross the scree above—kin, or close enough that the cough exchanged at dusk is conversation rather than challenge. Hunger is chronic and low-grade, as much appetites as appetite; it keeps them moving along the seepage lines, tasting the rock, listening.

Their minds are the strangest architecture in the cirque. They do not keep continuous selves the way lower-altitude creatures do. A body can walk the ledges for a day and a night while its awareness has stepped sideways into the slow mineral respiration, riding the pulse until the pulse returns it. During those absences the body still avoids fatal falls, still snatches a moth, still answers a hyrax whistle with the correct descending phrase; it simply does so without the bright interior candle. When the awareness returns it may bring knowledge the body never earned: the precise hour a particular snow cornice will collapse above the unseen glacier, the taste of rain that fell three valleys away, the memory of a spiral being pecked into the prow of rock by hands that were and were not its own. They leave those spirals, half a dozen of them now almost erased by lichen, as anchors—places where a returning mind can seize the stone and pull itself fully inside its skin again.

They speak little in words. When they do, the language is mostly breath and clicks timed to the water’s silver hiss, audible only within a few body-lengths. Meaning is carried more reliably by what they do to the space between them: the angle of a shoulder to the open gulf, the duration of a freezeframe when every animal in the basin pauses as if listening, the way one of them will walk the exact perimeter of a pond whose reflection is still showing yesterday’s clouds. Children (there are children, long-limbed and almost translucent the first year, eyes too large) learn by pressing their palms to the weeping quartz and waiting for the cold to teach them the difference between water that falls and water that is exhaled by the massif itself.

At night when the plate ice forms and the frogs begin their improbable metallic trilling, the dwellers sometimes gather on the jutting prow. They arrange their limbs in angles that echo the upright slabs higher on the rim—those broken teeth that are neither trail marker nor wind-break but unfinished calendars, recording something about the migration of seepage lines across centuries. They do not sing. They simply sit until their breathing slows to the exact pace of the mountain’s slow counsel until, one after another, they step sideways out of themselves and the basin is left with only the empty crouching shapes and the sound of water that never stops.

They are not guardians. The place does not require guarding. They are not spirits; the moths’ wings still tear when they pass through them. They are simply what the high wet room grew when it was left alone long enough with stone, thin air, and the necessity of remaining. Every moss cushion, every delayed reflection, every organ-tone of wind has been negotiated with until the negotiation itself became a kind of blood. You can stay among them for days and believe yourself alone, because they are generous with the vastness. They leave you the cathedrals of stone and the loud green silence. Only if you sleep inside one of the knee-high rings and wake at the precise moment the quartz veins flare with graze-light might you find a long dark face watching you with pupils open so wide the entire sky of the open gulf is momentarily held inside them—curious, unsurprised, already beginning to listen to something you will never hear.
