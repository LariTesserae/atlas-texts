They are called, when anyone still bothers with names, the Steady. The word is older than their present bodies and may once have meant something closer to “those who wait inside the work.” They do not hurry to correct outsiders who use other terms. Outsiders are rare enough that the effort would be wasteful.

At first glance a Steady appears almost ordinary in silhouette: bipedal, forward-facing, the height of a tall human but broader through the torso, as though the ribcage had been given extra time to thicken. The resemblance ends there. Their skin is a living alloy, microscopic platelets of iron-rich mineral suspended in a slow-healing matrix that glints the same muted russet and slate as the mesas themselves. When they stand motionless for long enough—hours, sometimes days—fine grit settles into the microscopic seams and they become, for a while, continuous with the stone. You can walk past one in the half-light of a cooling tower and mistake it for a load-bearing strut until it turns its head.

The head is the clearest departure. There is no true face, only a smooth, slightly convex plate of the same mineralized flesh, interrupted by two long horizontal slits that serve as eyes. The slits do not blink; instead a translucent membrane slides across them from within when dust rises or when the Steady simply wishes not to see. Hearing is accomplished by a fringe of fine, articulated spines that ring the skull like a low crown; these spines taste vibration more than sound, and a Steady can follow the multi-year heartbeat of a distant foundry through bare feet planted on the plateau. Mouths exist, a short vertical seam that opens only for speech or for the rare intake of refined slurry. They do not eat in any frequent sense. Metabolism is a closed, almost geological cycle: heat drawn from the industrial blood of the cities, mineral salts leached from the stone through specialized pores in the soles, and the occasional dense wafer of compressed browser-excreta and scavenged filings prepared in the lower forges. A Steady may go a season without opening its mouth and feel no lack.

They move the way the land itself seems to move—deliberately, with joint articulations that can lock for days without fatigue. When two Steadies pass on a gantry they do not nod or speak unless there is immediate shared work. Recognition is conveyed by a brief synchronization of internal hum, a subsonic chord felt in the chest more than heard. Speech, when it occurs, is economical and low, more like the settling of cooled slag than conversation. They have little use for narrative. Their histories are kept in the slow deformation of the structures they inhabit: a tower’s lean of three degrees records a century of sultriness and decision; the pattern of wear on a particular stair records which lineage preferred that route to the high observatories.

Children—by the loose term still used—are not born so much as precipitated. In sealed chambers deep inside the oldest foundries, where temperatures remain constant across decades, pairs or triads of Steadies contribute mineral-rich secretions and a carefully measured quantity of their own living alloy. The resulting mass is shaped, over months, by ultrasonic cradles and by the heat itself. What emerges is small, soft-edged, almost translucent. It hardens in stages as it is carried through the city’s gradient of temperatures, learning the local resonances the way a crystal learns its lattice. By the time it can walk the outer gantries its skin has already begun to take on the plateau’s color. Naming is unnecessary at first; the child’s distinctive vibration signature is known to every Steady within a day’s slow travel.

Their work is maintenance in the largest sense. They do not so much repair the city-machines as continue them. A cooling fin that has thinned is not replaced; its remaining metal is encouraged, by careful thermal stressing and the application of living alloy grafts, to thicken again along lines of ancestral stress. Rail spines are extended a few meters per generation toward the next mesa, even though the gulf remains impassable; the extension itself is the point, a gesture of continuity. When a scavenger population in the undercuts grows dense enough to threaten cable integrity, the Steadies do not exterminate. They open new ducts, herd the segmented things with low-frequency pulses, and allow a new equilibrium to form. Predation among the larger fauna is watched but rarely interrupted. A browser herd that freezes in geometric stillness for a season is simply noted; the Steadies stand with them sometimes, sharing the same patient geometry, until the herd decides to move again.

They are not numerous. A single mesa-city may house a few hundred, spread so thinly through the tiered structures that days can pass without an encounter. Loneliness is not a concept that translates well. Each Steady carries, in the resonant bones of its chest, the ongoing chord of the entire settlement; solitude is only the softest dynamic of that chord. When true silence threatens—when a city’s deep engines beat too faintly—they gather in the highest open courts at dusk and add their own spines’ vibration until the sub-bass presence thickens again into warmth.

At night some climb to the outermost gantries and watch the envelope-creatures phosphoresce. They do not romanticize the sight. They measure it. The blue pulses are data: charge density, thermal gradient, the slow health of the updrafts. Yet there are evenings when a Steady will stand until the ordinary stars wheel and the inverted ghost-landscape appears overhead, and in those hours the membrane stays drawn back from the eye-slits longer than efficiency requires. Something like attention, or perhaps only a deeper listening, moves through them then.

They know the plateaus will outlast them. They expect, without drama, that successive forms—perhaps less bipedal, more thoroughly integrated with the mineral concrete—will continue the same long work. Already there are individuals whose lower limbs have begun to fuse into broader, more stable platforms, who spend years without leaving a single plaza, becoming living bridges between gantries. No one objects. Change that slow is only another expression of the truce between erosion and construction.

And so they persist: warm-skinned, iron-quiet, few, moving through their half-excavated cities like thoughtful blood through an ancient body, converting heat into permanence and permanence back into heat, forever becoming more precisely the plateau that holds them.
