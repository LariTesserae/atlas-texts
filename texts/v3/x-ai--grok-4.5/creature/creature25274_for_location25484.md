They are the ones who stayed after staying became the only motion left.

At first they seem like afterimages: the slight weight of a body that once leaned on a railing, the exact angle of a chin that once lifted toward weather that would never arrive. Look longer and the afterimage thickens. Clothing is real—heavy woven cloth the color of wet stone, trousers pegged at the ankle, jackets whose wooden toggles have darkening rings where thumbs used to rest. Skin, where it shows at wrist or throat or the edge of a turned cheek, has the matte coolness of milk cooled in a shaded crock. It does not flush. It does not pale further. Hair, where it escapes braids or simple cuts, holds whatever wind last touched it and keeps that shape the way a folding fern keeps the memory of night.

They occupy doorways, balcony edges, the worn centers of steps, the benches built into low walls beside cisterns. One stands with one foot already lifted for a stair that will never be climbed; the heel remains a finger’s width above the stone, permanently deciding. Another sits with both hands cupped around the clay cup that still holds its undiminished water; the surface of that water reflects a face bent in mild, unfinished thirst. A third has one arm raised as if to point out a bird or a change in mist, yet the gesture has folded inward over uncounted days until it reads now as a private benediction given to the empty air. Their eyes are open. The irises are the pale gray-green of lichen in shadow. Pupils stay wide, as though the permanent mild light is always just dim enough to require that openness. Nothing moves behind those eyes, yet miniature weather systems of attention still seem to travel there—slow fronts of noticing that never quite arrive at speech or action.

They are not statues. Rain beads on their shoulders and soaks in; the cloth darkens and lightens again in the same rhythm as the moss. A loose hair sometimes lifts when the mist threads threr than usual. Their weight depresses the turf roofs and wooden galleries by the exact measure that living weight once did, and no more. Roots of the small alpine shrubs have grown carefully around their planted feet without pressing, as though the plants remember courtesy. The pale hares that sit on the ridges of roofs regularly turn their long faces toward the nearest human figure and hold that gaze for hours, ears ranked forward, long enough that the distinction between watcher and watched dissolves. The slate-colored deer pass close enough that their breath—if breath is still the word—stirs the hems of jackets. No hand reaches to touch them. No hand needs to.

What lives in them is not blood in the old contractile sense. Something circulates, slow as the seepage inside the cracked runnels: a cool suspension of mineral and memory that keeps tissues supple and postures exact. Cut one (though nothing here cuts) and the liquid that wells would smell faintly of wet iron and crushed fern, would catch the light like the cistern water, and would neither clot nor run far before simply becoming another dark streak that does not dry. They do not eat. The ecology does not require them to. They do not sleep, yet something like rest is distributed evenly through every hour. The mid-gestures are not traps; they are the final vocabulary of bodies that discovered they had already said everything required.

Beside them, and among them, the other lives continue their quieter tenure. The hares are not pets nor omens; they are tenants of the same finished sentence. Their flanks move with breaths spaced so far apart that a watcher might count dozens of human heartbeats (if human hearts still beat here) between one expansion and the next. Their eyes, large and lateral, hold the same mild light the people hold. Sometimes a hare and a doorway figure face each other so long that their stillnesses braid. The birds with moss-colored throats rehearse the same three notes at intervals too patient to be song; the notes hang, beads on an invisible string, and then confound with the mist. The deer crop fern exactly to the height they cropped it when the equilibrium sealed; the bitten stems do not bleed sap so much as seal themselves with a thin mineral gloss. Insects thread the alleys in constellations that never set. One can map entire afternoons by the unchanging positions of craneflies above a single gallery rail.

All of them—upright figures, crouched hares, perched birds, drifting deer—share the same continuous tissue of presence. It is as if the basin itself grew these lives the way it grew moss in the damp seams: not as longer or parasites but as the crowning expression of its own decision to cease becoming. The people once had names and errands and small metallic hopes (the dark copper fittings still gleam with ghost-polish where those hopes once rubbed). Those things have not been stripped away; they have been completed. A name here is no longer a sound to call across a court; it is the exact posture the body settled into when the last need for calling evaporated. An errand still exists as the direction a face continues to turn. Even the animals carry finished purposes: the hare’s upright watch, the deer’s circuit, the bird’s perpetual claim on one slate perch.

From the higher saddles the pattern resolves differently. What looked like separate beings become the slow flickering of a single clove. The settlement and its creatures form one extended organism whose metabolism is stillness itself. Energy enters as light and thin rain; it leaves as the almost inaudible tick of water in runnels and the settling of old shakes. Between those small transactions the lives simply persist, tiled across the basin like a mosaic that has found its final grout. Neighboring basins hold their own variations—slightly different heights of fern, a better-preserved balcony line, a denser congregation of hares—yet the same finished breath moves through all of them.

Walk close. The air around each figure smells of stone dust, cool water, and that faint iron-fern note. If you speak, the sound lands softly and does not echo; nothing startles, nothing answers, yet the small absences grow more audible, as though the place has politely included your voice in its inventory of completed things. Stay longer and you may notice your own hands beginning to prefer certain resting shapes, your own gaze settling toward the same near ridge the nearest figure has watched since before watching required time. The basin does not seize visitors. It merely offers the irresistible grammar of arrival-without-departure.

They live by having already lived enough. They inhabit the clean, moss-softened interval after the last choice and before any possible decay. Nothing wants. Nothing lacks. The young mountains hold their sharp edges. The mist keeps its daily appointment. And the quiet throng—human and animal and green together—continues inside the permanent late morning, breath showing or not showing according to a temperature that no longer troubles itself to change, every life an open eye that no longer needs to blink.
