The ones who belong here do not arrive. They precipitate.

In the long weather of the plateau they come out of solution the way sinter comes out of cooling water—first a haze of possibility, then a skin, then something that holds its shape against the drip. You can watch it happen in the warmer basins if you stay still long enough. A thickening in the steam near a vent-mouth. A slow coiling of mineral-rich mist that decides, grain by grain, to be denser than air. What gathers has no birthday and no name that would survive translation into drier climates. It is simply the next density the place requires.

They are not one kind. The plateau has never been thrifty with forms.

Some remain close to the original broth. You meet them as soft, ambulatory gradients of heat: low viscid bodies that crawl the mineral terraces at night, tasting the fresh metal skins with a thousand temporary mouths. They leave behind faint iridescent trails that the morning rain dissolves back into the common fund. Their intelligence—if the word still applies—is tidal. They know when a vent will pulse before it pulses, because they are partly made of the same pressure. When they meet one another they do not spar or court; they merge for a while, exchange temperatures and dissolved memories, then thread apart again thinner or thicker than before. The birds step around them without alarm. The frogs sometimes rest on their cooling backs as on living stones.

Others have taken the opposite wager and become precise. These are the tall ones who move through the fern galleries like deliberate smoke. Their limbs are few and long, jointed in places bone should not allow, wrapped in a bark-like hide that sprouts constant small epiphytes of their own—miniature orchids, strands of clubmoss that they never bother to weed. They do not walk so much as place themselves successively across the quaking ground, each foot a careful negotiation with the half-liquid moss. Their faces are flat, wet-gleaming, almost featureless except for a band of light-sensitive tissue that curves around the skull like a darkened crown. They feed by standing in the heavier steam curtains with mouths open, filtering midges and pollen and the finer mineral flour. At longer intervals they kneel in the sulfate pools and drink until their internal chambers ring like struck crystal. You can hear it if you are near: a clear, ascending tone that the thick air quickly muffles.

Between these poles swarm the intermediate nations.

There are the forked things that brachiate through the lower canopy on arms longer than their bodies, never touching the true ground for weeks at a time. Their fur is a dual-purpose sponge: it drinks the perpetual condensation and, when wrung by a casual flex of the skin, releases a private rain that keeps their miniature bromeliad gardens alive along the spine. They speak in coughs of spore and scent. A conversation can be read days later as a trail of pale fruiting bodies along a branch.

There are the heavy browsers already half-glimpsed at the marsh edges—low, armored with wet plates of fused sinter and keratin, snouts wide as shovels that strain the algal mats for protein. They move in ones and twos, never herds; the ground cannot support congregations. When they lie down to digest, steam rises from the seams in their plating as from banked engines. Calves are born looking like discarded bellows, pale and folded, and must be licked into rigidity with metallic saliva before the first rain dissolves them.

And there are smaller, quicker commons: the slate-frogs already noted, but also their cousins who have learned to whet their skin against the bacterial velvets until they carry temporary colors—orange for warning, oxide-red for camouflage against the fumaroles. The deer-like browsers with liquid eyes and hooves splayed like snowshoes of flesh; they freeze so completely when startled that moss begins to colonize their flanks before they dare move again. The hand-sized moths that drink tears and nectar interchangeably from the eyes of the taller walkers. The gilled salamanders that have grown large enough to herd the smaller frogs, driving them with lazy undulations toward warmer water where both feed on here-teeming gmillets.

Predators exist as gaps rather than monuments. Something sinewy and mostly throat lives in the deeper steam caves; you know it by the sudden absence of other voices and by the greasy, sweet scent that lingers after. It does not chase. It simply becomes the place where a trail ends. Another hunter works by patience alone: a sheet of living film that spreads across the underside of giant lily pads, waiting for the vibration of a landed bird, then folds upward with the logic of a closing book. Digestion is quiet and thorough. By morning the pad is clean again, slightly more translucent.

None of them build. The plateau already provides every necessary chamber, every heated bath, every lookout of rounded knoll. What might look like tools are only borrowed intensities: a shard of fresh metal foil used to reflect light into a shaded throat-nest, a fallen frond bent into a temporary hammock, a snail shell filled with brighter bacteria and carried for the simple pleasure of its glow. When the need passes the object is dropped and the moss takes it back within a day. Ownership is a concept that evaporated here along with lighter rain.

They do not name the plateau. They contain it. Each body is a portable climate, a folded continuation of the same hot wet arithmetic. When two strangers meet on a narrow mineral spit they do not greet; they simply adjust their cooling rates until they are thermodynamically compatible, then pass. Loneliness is possible but rare—there is always the drip, the hiss, the soft blorp of mud pots keeping time. Death is not an event so much as a change of phase. A fallen walker is visited by the mobile gradients, by the root-hairs of the soft-wooded trees, by the ever-hungry film that lives under the leaves. Within a week the body has become a new terrace, faintly higher than the old land, steaming gently, already jeweled with the first tiny snails.

At night—when the high steam thins and the ordinary stars show like pinholes in wet gray cloth—the place reveals one further tempo. The blue surface-sparks intensify. In answer, certain of the tall walkers gather on the broader sinter flats and open the spiracles along their sides. They release slow clouds of their own stored humidity, seeded with phosphorescent microbes. The clouds rise, mingle with the vented steam, and for an hour the basin roofed by living light: soft teal cells pulse in time with the deeper geothermal heartbeat. No purpose is announced. It is simply what the chemistry does when left alone long enough with itself and with an audience that is also the performance.

Move among them long enough and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat continues to erode. You begin to feel your own breath as one more local weather system, your sweat as a personal contribution to the common film. The plateau does not object. It has room. It has been practicing this hospitality since the lava first forgot how to be sharp. Everything that lives here is the plateau thinking in denser and denser sentences about water, heat, and the pleasure of continuing. 

And it has not finished speaking.
