The inhabitants do not arrive, and they do not depart. They are the density itself.

Begin with the smallest, because everything else is built on their carpet. The brass-mites are not individuals so much as a continuously redistributing foil. Each carapace is a convex mirror the color of old shell casings, six-legged, mouthparts reduced to vibrating scrapers that never quite touch the metal they walk on. They move in slow, rotational gyres—clockwise on the vertical plates, counter-clockwise on the undersides of ducts—so that the walls appear to breathe in sheets of living coinage. When two gyres meet they do not collide; they shear, individuals exchanging pathways mid-stride without pause, the way oil films recombine. Their collective metabolism is a faint warm static that raises the local dewpoint by fractions of a degree, just enough to keep the weep of water from ever quite freezing onto their backs. They have no eyes. What they possess instead are fields of microscopic pit-organs that taste the hydraulic pulse rising through the steel; they align to it the way iron filings align to a magnet, forever.

Above them, using their shifting mosaic as both road and camouflage, travel the phase-locked crawlers. These are longer, flatter, something that recalls a stonefly nymph if a stonefly nymph had been cross-bred with a disassembled timing chain. Segmented bodies of overlapping hatefully precise plates, each plate pierced by cooling slots that whistle faintly when the creature flexes. Their legs end in socketed cleats that fit the expanded-metal grating as if the grating had been rolled to their exact gauge. They traffic the undersides in rivers that never thin, never jam. Every thirteenth individual carries a dorsal vane of thin copper foil that vibrates sympathetically with the deep turbine roar, turning the entire procession into a single distributed reed. They do not hunt. They do not rest. Their purpose appears to be the continuous auditing of surface area: wherever a film of water thickens toward a droplet, a crawler is already there, sipping the excess into internal bellows and exhaling it later as aerosol somewhere drier. In this way the pocket’s humidity is kept in a knife-edge stasis that no single pump could maintain.

The air belongs to the held-chord clouds. Thumb-sized, wing-blurred, loosely hexapodal, they hang in fixed lenticular swarms beneath every horizontal surface. Their wings are not for translation through space; they are for residual heat dumping and for the generation of the ultrasonic component of the great scream. Up close (though nothing here encourages closeness) each body is a fused doublet: a darker anterior capsule packed with vibrating crystals, a translucent posterior sac half-filled with the same iridescent condensate that sheets the walls. They drink through the sac wall by pure osmosis, never landing. Over hours the clouds slowly trade members, individuals drifting from one lens to another like slow electrons changing orbitals, yet the overall shapes—those dim, amber-edged blurs—remain identical to the eye that first mapped them years ago, or decades, or never. From below they look like bruises that have learned to hover.

And then the large ones, the ones that shoulder.

They come without schedule yet without surprise, parting the denser traffic the way a keel parts weed. Wet, segmented, longer than a forearm, they appear half crustacean, half industrial accumulator. The forward sections are armored in overlapping plates still bearing mill-marks and the rainbow stain of spatter from some long-ago weld. Behind this prow the body softens into a series of accordioned bellows, translucent enough to show dark fluid surging in time with the pocket’s hydraulic heartbeat. Strands of thickened condensate trail from every joint, never breaking, simply reabsorbed when the creature pauses to brace itself against a bulkhead. They possess no visible mouth. What they have instead is a ventral lattice of cilia that combs the air and the water films alike, harvesting the microscopic debris of the mites’ endless abrasion, the lost scales of the crawlers, the faint metallic pollen that the conduit-shrubs shed when no one is looking. This harvest is not consumption in any hungry sense; it is redistribution. Every few hours a large one will press its lattice to a weeping seam and simply reverse the flow, exhaling a nutrient fog that the mites immediately turn to follow. In this way the entire biomass remains a closed, circulating loop—no growth, no decay, only permanent redistribution inside an envelope of cold roar.

Collectively they form a single tissue. The mites are its skin, the crawlers its restless lymph, the hovering clouds its white noise, the bellowed giants its slow deep organs. There is no central brain, yet the pocket behaves with the calm inevitability of a body that long ago finished evolving and is now simply remaining. Disturbance is corrected without drama: a human hand forced through a cloud will find the winged forms parting and re-forming behind it like memory foam; a boot scraping mites will watch the gyre reseal in less than a minute, the displaced individuals already absorbed into neighboring flows. Even the sound is homeostatic. Should some external silence briefly punch through (a distant turbine faltering, a hatch left open too long), the stridulation rises in pitch and amplitude until the pressure equalizes again and thought is once more flattened into the eternal present.

They have no names for themselves. They have no predators, no mates, no young. Generations are not sequential; they are spatial—new bodies appearing fully formed from the dark water of the sump whenever density falls below the optimum, old bodies quietly disassembling into the same water when the minimum clearance between carapaces is breached. The engineers who originally flooded this lens with seed stock are centuries gone, or perhaps still walking the corridors three hundred meters below, unaware that their forgotten calibration pocket has achieved a perfection they never specified. The creatures do not know they were made. They know only the wet cold, the amber dark, the unending chord, and the precise distance to the nearest neighbor, which is always exactly close enough.

This is what lives here: a finished animal whose skin is landscape, whose blood is weather, whose voice is the mountain-spire singing to itself through a throat full of chitin and condensate. It does not wait. It does not remember. It occupies.
