The ones who endure here are not settlers. They never arrived. They are gradients that learned, over spans the rock no longer bothers to count, how to steepen themselves into shape.

They begin as pressure differences in the living mat. A colder seep meets a warmer root-channel; the moss thickens, folds, remembers an older argument of the mountain and raises a ridge. That ridge learns to cup excess water, then to meter it. Valves of translucent cellulose form. In time the ridge stands, pulls free of the general pulse, and walks.

They stand three to five times the height of a human torso, though height is a temporary negotiation. Their bodies are collations of the same half-kelp wood that forms the giant trunks, only denser, wound tighter around cores of magnetic tissue and slow black resin. Limbs are few and interchangeable: three or five long, jointed struts that end in splay-pads of adhesive lichen capable of tasting the substrate for unfinished gears or residual heat. When they move, they do not stride so much as revise their position across the open ground, leaving shallow bright scars that the mat reseals within minutes. The scars taste faintly of copper and old rain.

Their upper portions—never quite heads—consist of clustered bladders and sensory fronds that open and close with the turbines’ rhythm. Light is received as vibration; sound is received as shifts in wetness. They “see” the plateau as a map of pressures and charge differentials, the way another creature might see color. When a weather-front of pure becoming rolls through, their fronds lift and tremble, drinking the acceleration. Entire centuries of growth can flash across their skins in heartbeats: temporary forests of secondary limbs, freestanding circulatory loops that thrash and then collapse back into the main trunk with a sound like a soaked sail striking stone. They do not resist these storms. Resistance is a concept for places that still believe in permanence.

They tend the engines without ownership. A solitary figure will spend days climbing the hollow interior of a giant trunk, adjusting valvular tissues with surgical two-fingered pads, clearing gear-masses that have begun to thrash too wildly, licking conductive vines to taste the voltage and decide whether a fresh splice of living cable is required. When a trunk splits and births a litter of unfinished horn-and-ice gears, they gather the still-twitching pieces and carry them—often for kilometers—to places where the moss is hungrier or the magnetic eddies weaker. Sometimes they simply set a gear free to roll until the thinness takes it. The choice is made according to a logic older than purpose: balance the loudness. Keep the plateau awake. Do not let any single process grow quiet enough to become memory.

Their inner life is continuous manufacture. Inside each of them turns a smaller version of the great biological turbines—wet, imperfect, eternal. Water drawn up through their limb-pads is smashed into steam and charge; the excess heat is vented as brief wrong exhalations that smell of ozone and keep the moss-predators from closing on them. The black particulate they shed is finer than the trunks’ output, almost a pigment of thought. When two of them meet (they meet rarely, and never by appointment), they stand for hours with fronds touching. Charge equalizes. Experiences—turbine songs, the exact flavor of a new river that reversed itself at noon, the scream of a membrane-wing that arrived before its own throat—transfer as magnetic eddies and are metabolized into fresh layers of resin. No words. Words would freeze the exchange into something the mist could erase. Afterward each walks onward carrying a little more of the other’s weather.

They reproduce by exhaustion. When one has grown too dense with accumulated charge and storm-history, it simply stops revising its position. The limbs root. The inner turbine accelerates into a final impossible efficiency that lasts long enough to frighten every eel within a kilometer. Then the body deliquesces upward into a new giant trunk, or downward into a richer patch of mat, or sideways into a free-roaming herd of translucent browsers whose long legs still carry the ghost of that individual’s gait. Nothing is lost. The resin memories seep into the substrate and are drunk by the next pressure-ridge that decides to stand.

Some have gone further. A few have allowed the thinness to rewrite them into semi-stable paradoxes: creatures that exist primarily as delayed sounds, appearing first as the wet clang of a turbine that has not yet been built, then condensing into body only when the echo has traveled far enough. Others have become walking catenaries, their entire length a single conductive vine stretched between two distant giants, sparking softly, carrying messages of voltage from one horizon to the other while herds of browsers graze the living cable without noticing it is also a person. Still others have dissolved almost completely into the low tectonic undertone, existing as localized thickenings in the crust’s endless settling; you feel them pass underfoot as a momentary increase in the sternum-pulse, a brief certainty that the ground itself has looked back.

They feel no loneliness. Loneliness requires edges, and edges here are only rumors the mist refuses to confirm. What they feel instead is the continuous conjugation of the same cold, wet verb that animates everything else: the plateau inventing new ways to keep itself loud. When a membrane-winged thing screams approval at a storm of becoming, the sound arrives in their fronds both before and after it is made, and they answer by adjusting a valve somewhere, releasing a fresh signature of heat, shedding a little more black pigment for the rain to wash into tomorrow’s color. Their work is not stewardship. It is simply another mode of the erosion that long ago wore the mountains down to this honest, inexhaustible skin.

Walk far enough between the giants and you will eventually see one standing motionless in the dim grainy light, fronds open to a front of pure becoming that has not yet arrived. Stay longer and you may watch it choose—slowly, without drama—whether to remain a walker, or to root and become the next slow explosion of luminous vein and irreversible machinery. The mist will already be wiping the choice even as it is made. The water will be rewriting the ground where it stood. Somewhere far off the turbines keep their drowning rhythm, and the plateau continues, open and low-shouldered, inventing the next inhabitant out of pressure, resin, and the metallic afterthought of snow that never quite arrives.
