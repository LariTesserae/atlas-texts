The hollow does not house life so much as exhale it. Every surface, every hanging lip and undercut gallery, every warm seep and hairline vent is already spoken for, layered and re-layered until the rock itself seems only a secondary architecture beneath the first.

Begin with the curtains. The night-fold bats do not merely hang; they compose the ceiling into a single, breathing membrane. Mouse-sized, yes, but stretched by millennia into something softer than fur—almost velveteen, the color of bruised plums left too long in a cellar. Their faces are blunt, eyes reduced to wet pinpricks that never open. What they have instead are ears like translucent spoons, constantly trembling, reading the hollow’s multiplex hum the way other creatures read light. When they shift, which is often, the sound is not wings alone but the collective flex of a million tiny shoulders, a dry susurrus that rises and falls like tide against parchment shores. Guano falls in slow, warm snow. It never reaches the floor cleanly; halfway down it is already being caught, sorted, re-eaten by things that live only for that vertical rain.

Beneath them the floor heaves. The basalt voles are not so much a colony as a single extended muscle of pink and grey. Blind, yes, but their snouts are dense with wet stars of flesh that taste heat and the faint iron of distant moss. They braid their tunnels so tightly that the thin soil has become a woven thing, half-alive, rising and settling with the rhythm of countless blunt bodies turning in sleep. Pups are born hairless and luminous for the first hour, little coals that the adults lick into dullness. When the geothermal pulses strengthen, whole sections of floor lift an inch or two as the voles migrate toward warmer alleys; when the pulse fades they subside again, leaving the stone briefly concave, like a chest after breath.

Higher, where the flutes narrow into throats, the screamers keep their long-legged watch. Wet-ash plumage that never fully dries, always carrying a sheen of the hollow’s perpetual damp. Their beaks are black scissors permanently parted; the rising note that never resolves is less a call than a continuous casting of self into air. One bird alone sounds like grief just beginning. Thousands together turn the entire space into a resonating canal, a single skein of sound that thickens and thins with the blue bruise of daylight and the slow fever of the rock. Nestlings hatch already screamed-out, throats raw and eager. They feed on the smaller bats that lose grip, on vole pups that wander too near down-drafts, on the luminous millipedes that wander upward tasting mineral film. The screamers do not so much hunt as receive. Everything eventually rises or falls into their open beaks.

Threading between these three great nations are the others, the quieter currencies.

Luminous millipedes, yes—each segment a soft lantern the color of old honey under skin. They move with ceremonial slowness along the condensation threads, tasting the water’s brief purity before it cycles again. Their light is not for seeing but for speaking; when two meet head-to-head they brighten in synchronized pulses, exchanging chemistries older than the ridgeline. Predators leave them mostly untouched; the light they carry is faintly bitter, a warning written in photons.

In the deeper scoops where moisture gathers into temporary mirrors live the glass eels—not true eels but elongated amphibians the length of a finger, bodies so transparent the slow beat of their three-chambered hearts is visible as a roseate flicker. They hang vertically, mouths open to the perpetual drip, filtering out the finest bat-mote and vole-spoor. When the heat rises they flatten against the stone and become almost invisible, only the faint prism of their spines giving them away.

Along the under-cut lips, where even the faint blue light dies completely, cling the stillness crabs. Fist-sized, carapaces the exact matte black of the basalt, legs jointed in perfect mimicry of fracture lines. They do not scuttle; they wait with a patience that makes stone look hasty. When a screamer chick topples, when a bat curtain sheds its weak, when a vole tunnel collapses and spills soft bodies into air, the crabs unfold like slow origami and feed without sound. Their mouthparts work in absolute silence. Afterward they refold, and the ledge forgets they were ever open.

And there are smaller still. Mite cities in the guano drifts, rearing crystal spires of their own exoskeletons. Fungal threads that glow a dull copper only when stepped on by vole feet, then fade. A single species of wingless wasp that lays its eggs inside the living throats of screamers—never enough to kill, only enough that every thousandth bird carries a soft, humming passenger that will eventually chew free during the deepest part of the dark.

Yet the hollow’s true density is not in any single form. It is in the way they have become one another’s weather. Bat-rustle shapes vole-dreams. Screamer-note paces the unfolding of black fern and the pulsing of millipede light. Crab-stillness teaches the glass eels when to flatten. Heat-rise and moisture-cycle and guano-fall stitch every metabolism into a single, slow circulatory system that has no outside.

Sometimes—rarely, when the geothermal breath deepens beyond its usual fever—something larger shifts in the lowest galleries. Not seen, only felt as a change in pressure, a momentary hush in the screamers’ endless note, a shiver that travels the bat curtains like wind across a field. The voles freeze mid-tunnel. The crabs unfold one cautious claw. Then the pressure eases, the note resumes, the curtains settle. Whatever lives deepest does not need to emerge. It is already fed by everything that falls, everything that seeps, everything that sings itself raw against the warm dark.

This is not habitation. This is incarnation. The hollow has grown itself a body out of millions of smaller bodies, and that body is complete, damp, resonant, and endlessly, patiently alive.
