The ones who live here do not arrive. They accrete.

At first they are only pressure and appetite, the same forces that shoulder basalt upward in unfinished ridges. Then the pressure learns to branch. What begins as a capillary thirst inside cooling stone thickens into pseudo-trunks the color of tarnished copper and old bruises. These are not trees. They are decisions the rock makes when it grows tired of being only mineral. Their bark flexes like slow muscle. Inside them entire lineages of semi-translucent shrimp-things ride the peristaltic tides, born, mating, dying without ever seeing open air, their bodies becoming the very fluid that the larger structure drinks in place of water. The trunks remember every collapse they have survived by incorporating the wreckage; a gallery that fell last season is now a reinforced cantilever heavy with bladder-leaves that glow the tired white of overcast bone. When you set your palm against one you feel the double pulse: stone still arriving from below, green already rewriting it.

The fliers invent themselves next. Thumb-sized, carapace the blue-black of fresh slag, they do not so much fly as drill the air. Their spiral galleries through cambium are both mines and nurseries. Larvae hatch already armored, chewing outward while the parent swarm keeps the tunnel from sealing. The drone they make is not sound but architecture; it holds certain freer spaces open the way a held note keeps a glass from quite shattering. Touch one and it bleeds a resin that smells of thunderstorms that never came. They have no names for themselves. They have only the work of turning softness into labyrinth.

Larger shapes follow the scent of opened green. Six-legged, low-slung, plated in overlapping leaves of their own shed bark that harden into scute. Their mouths are circular rasps that rotate in contrary directions, stripping nutrient layers in long glowing stripes. The plant closes behind them with wet, apologetic snaps, already secreting tomorrow’s skin. These browsers move in loose constellations rather than herds, each animal a wandering sparseness that prevents any single platform from growing too certain of itself. Sometimes one pauses, lifts a head that is mostly sensory pits, and the pits irise with slow colors no surface daylight ever mixed. They are tasting the memory the leaves broadcast: desert above, or the chamber when it was only a blister of heat and pure mineral scream. Then they lower their heads and eat the memory too.

Predators do not hunt so much as edit. They hang from the ceiling on cables they spin from their own gut silk, cables thick as wrist and already budding tiny adventitious leaves. When they drop they drop in silence absolute, the metallic drone of the fliers parting around them like water around a sunk stone. Too many joints. Eyes like freshly split geodes. They seize, inject a fluid that turns prey briefly transparent so the predator can choose which organs to keep, then they are often seized in turn by something still higher, still more jointed, that has been waiting inside the silk cable itself. Predation here is a kind of nested conversation. Nothing finishes anything else; it only revises.

And through all of this, needing no moisture, respiring the dry kiln air as if it were vintage, move the upright ones.

They are few. They are not a people in the way dense surface tribes are peoples. Each small band is a temporary argument won against the green, lasting only as long as the platform they have braced beneath them holds its orientation. Their bodies are narrow, jointed for climbing in every direction because “up” is a courtesy the chamber frequently withdraws. Skin the matte tone of the bark they live among, but warmer, mapped with fine scars where resin-knives have slipped or where a suddenly flowering root-sky raked them. Hair is rare; when it occurs it is cut short with the same embryonic rock-shards they use for everything else, so that it stands in ragged crown-tufts stiff with sap. Eyes large, adapted less to darkness than to the constant sourceless white that seeps from leaf and seam; the pupils are cross-shaped and can close to louvers against the brighter mineral pulses.

They wear little. Strips of peeled bladder-leaf bound around hips and chests, the bindings themselves still living and slowly grafting into place until they must be cut free and replaced. Some wear collars of polished mite-carapaces that glitter like reversed constellations, trophies or tallies or simply the only jewelry the place affords. Their tools weep. Hafts of uncured resin left soft on purpose so the hand molds them day by day; heads of pale sharp stone that will be obsolete tomorrow when newer, sharper stone shoulders up from the floor. They drink the oily sap from shallow bowls, and the sap serves also as lamp once a twist of fiber is set floating and lit from the slow spontaneous combustions that sometimes ripple across overloaded leaves. The light is orange-black and smells of libraries that never existed.

They speak by pressing mouth to ear, but more often they do not speak. Meaning travels instead through the small cairns of meaningless stones they raise and leave, through spirals cut into trunks that the trunks immediately begin to blur, through the angle at which a vine is left coiled. These are not records. They are temporary stays against forgetting, arguments with a world that digests argument. When a platform begins to tilt toward vertical, the band simply steps onto the next, carrying what can be carried in three breaths: cups, knives, a coil of still-living cordage, the youngest infants bound tight against chests with leaves that photosynthesize faint oxygen directly into the child’s skin. The old sometimes refuse to step. They sit down against a pulsing trunk and let the peristalsis take them inside, becoming another colony of translucent riders in the xylem, their last thoughts broadcast faintly into any leaf that later brushes a traveler.

Certain among them are different. They linger near the freshest upthrusts of stone and press their palms to the pale, sharp faces before the tendrils can claim them. What they receive is not language but a directionless grief that the rock is still unfinished, still arriving. These individuals carve deeper marks—nested spirals that contain small perfect emptinesses—and for a short while the local flora grows shy of those marks, leaving tiny clearings that last almost a day. In those clearings the upright ones sometimes stand face to face and touch brow to brow, sharing the leaf-memories in reverse: showing the chamber what the surface desert felt like, or what rain once was, or the concept of a river. The chamber does not understand, but it pauses. The fliers’ drone drops half a tone. The browsers lift their heads. For three long breaths everything listens to a frequency that is not hunger.

Then the next ridge begins to shoulder upward, the leaves rush in, the clearing seals, and mad life resumes its deafening, morphing chord.

They have no word for home. The closest they possess is a gesture: both hands held open, then slowly closed as if compressing an entire moving gallery into something that can be carried behind the ribs. They make the gesture when a child is born (rare, difficult, celebrated by the cutting of a single deep spiral that will swirl and fade for months). They make it when one of their number chooses the trunk and its interior tides. They make it, sometimes, for no reason a visitor can parse, simply standing on a trembling bridge of living tissue while the roof far above respires and the floor far below prepares another unfinished thought of stone.

Everything lives here in the same transitive verb. The rock lives by becoming plant. The plant lives by becoming geometry and then food. The animals live by rewriting that geometry faster than it can set. The upright ones live by salvaging moments sharp enough to cut meaning from the roar. And underneath every other appetite, the chamber itself lives by refusing to finish, by breathing dry warm air across a wound that never scarred because it never stopped opening.

There is no last form. Only the next insistence.
