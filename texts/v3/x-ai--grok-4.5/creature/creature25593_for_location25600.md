The hollow does not host inhabitants so much as it secretes them, generation after compressed generation, out of the same unfinished stone and heat that keep rewriting its floor. What lives here is first a density, a pressure of bodies against bodies, all of them provisional, all of them already halfway to becoming the next scree. Begin with the lowest register and let the eye rise only when the ground itself forces it.

Along every temporary watercourse the armored browsers move in trundling rivers of their own. They are low, longer than they are tall, built like living basalt knuckles. Each carapace is a mosaic of overlapping plates that click and grate when the animal breathes; the plates are not grown so much as accreted, mineral films and shed chitin welded by the creature’s own acidic saliva into ever-heavier armor. Beneath the shell the body is pale, muscular, almost larval, propelling itself on six stubby limbs that end in horny spades. They graze the pale bacterial mats that bloom wherever dampness lingers longer than an hour, rasping the rock clean with tongue-pads studded in microscopic teeth. When a fresh scarp steams open they are already there, flowing over the hot glass while it is still soft enough to take their prints, packing the new mineral into their shells before it cools. Their herds leave polished roads that last less than a day. Occasionally one is caught mid-stride by an upthrust and simply becomes part of the geology—shell fused to cooling lava, soft interior cooked and then mineralized—until later browsers quarry their ancestor for the salt still locked inside.

Above them the air is never empty. The winged things are weather with appetite. Some are pure membrane stretched over jointed spars, translucent, the color of old bruises; others carry rigid chitin vanes that flash iridescent when the slanted light catches them; a few are leather and muscle, heavier, banking in slow predatory circles. They do not so much fly as continuously fall and catch themselves. Their colonies coagulate under any overhang that stays stable for more than a few hours—thousands of bodies packed so tight the rock disappears beneath a seething brown crust. When the stone groans they lift as one thunderclap, a single organism of wings that darkens half the hollow, and within the hour they have resettled three ridges away, already regurgitating mineral-rich paste to glue new nests to fresher ceilings. Their young hatch without eyes, guided only by heat and the vibration of too many heartbeats. Adults rake the air for the smaller midges that breed in the steam vents, or drop onto the backs of the browsers to drink the moisture trapped under lifting plates. Death among them is constant and public; a torn wing means a spiraling fall into the talus where burrowers wait.

The multi-limbed grazers move through the mid-heights of the rubble fields like animate hinge-work. Eight limbs, sometimes nine or eleven—the number seems flexible, extra joints budding under stress and withering when the food grows scarce. They are lean, almost skeletal, each limb ending in nested scissoring mouthparts that can prune fungal film from vertical faces or snip through the tethering sinews of a grounded flier. Their bodies are sacs of fluid and nerve, draped in loose folds of skin that change color with the local rock so that at rest they vanish into the scarps. They do not herd; they flock in loose, quarrelsome constellations, always testing one another’s limbs for weakness. A injured leg is eaten on the spot by its owner’s companions, the protein recycled before the next collapse can claim it. Predators of pure velocity haunt the same strata: longer, narrower, almost nothing but jointed springs and cutting edges. They do not stalk so much as wait in coiled stillness until some seismic shudder startles a grazier into motion, then they unfold in a single crack of acceleration, strike, and are gone before the dust settles. What remains is a neat arrangement of emptied limbs still twitching among the stones.

Under everything the burrowers stitch and unstitch the new talus. They are the color of wet ash, elongate, headed only by a ring of digging spines and a secondary mouth that tastes the grit it displaces. Their tunnels collapse behind them by design; they do not live in dens but in the act of denning, endlessly circulating through the porous substrate, aerating it, packing bacterial films deeper where they can overwinter the brief cold snaps that sometimes roll through when a fracture panel seals off the afternoon light. You feel them more than see them—a continuous low crawling in the soles of the feet, the ground itself restless, faintly alive.

And moving among all these, neither above nor below but threaded through, are the upright ones. They are sparse-haired, the hair itself more like fine silica fiber than true fur, catching the mineral dust so that each individual carries a pale corona that brightens and dulls with the light. Bipedal, yes, but the posture is not proud; it is a constant negotiation with the rolling floor. Knees stay flexed, spine slightly curved, arms long enough that the knuckles often brush stone for balance when a fresh tremor runs through. Their skin is thickened in plates across shoulders and shins—scars of old heat, old scrapes, old collapses—yet still raw and pink in the folds, always healing, always abrading again. Eyes are dark, large, adapted to the perpetual dust-haze and the long slanted glare; they reflect the late-afternoon color even when no direct light reaches them. They travel in small, fluid bands that form and dissolve with the same tempo as the fliers’ colonies: a dozen bodies moving with shared purpose for an hour, then scattering when a ridge shears away, reforming later around a new water pocket or a newly exposed seam of softer stone.

They make tools the way the browsers make armor: by accelerating the inevitable. Hammerstones are chosen from the freshest basalt, still warm, and flipped against one another until a flake detaches with a sound like a swallowed bell. The edge is used immediately—before the glass re-anneals or the next quake knocks it from the hand—to strip sinew from a fallen grazer, to widen a sleeping scrape, to cut the fibrous rind of the tough tubular organisms that sometimes extrude from steam vents like living cable. Cordage is twisted on the thigh while walking. A finished length is never stored; it is used at once to lash chitin plates into a temporary chest-shield or to lower a child into a hollow that might stay quiet until morning. Their cairns are not monuments but messages to the next hour: a stack of chips on a momentarily stable crest, crowned by one bright flake whose gloss still smells of percussion. Another band, cresting the same ridge after the light has shifted, will read the angle of that flake and know whether the previous makers found water, or meat, or only more empty steam.

At night—or what passes for night when distant fracture panels grind shut and the light thins to a dull mineral glow—they deepen shallow scrapes into body-long hollows and line them with still-warm plates shed by the browsers. Two or three will share the space, limbs interleaved, breathing in the same rhythm so the small heat lasts longer. Sometimes, on a rare stretch of ground that has not buckled for most of a day, they build the low rings: knee-high uprights of basalt chinked with red geothermal mud. Inside, the floor is swept with a bundle of dried sinew until it is unnaturally bare. What happens inside the ring is brief and almost silent. Fingers count something invisible—perhaps the pulse of the stone underneath, perhaps the number of living throats still audible beyond the haze. A single worked flake is placed at the center, then removed again. The mud is already cracking before they step out and scatter. The ring will be gone by the next upthrust, or will stand long enough for strangers to add one more stone and wonder.

They do not name the hollow. Naming would imply it stays still long enough to bear a name. They move through it the way heat moves through stone—outward, upward, always seeking the next temporary equilibrium—scratching their faint intentional marks onto surfaces that will not outlast the week, pausing only when the ground stops shaking long enough for aim. In the long pale corridors where the herds thin and steam rises in slow breaths from the faults, you can watch a single band crest a far ridge and disappear into the haze, already becoming rumor to the next valley of the same unfinished country. The place keeps secreting them. They keep answering with flaskes, with cordage, with rings of brittle mud. Nothing is finished. Everything is already at work.
