The ones who endure here are not a single people so much as a gradient of bodies that have all, in different registers, memorized the same wet arithmetic.

At the scale you first notice, they are the dark-furred runners. Low-slung, longer than a human torso and built like compressions of otter and millipede, they pour through the moss channels in single-file ropes that split and rejoin without signal. Their pelts are not fur exactly but overlapping plates of keratin slicked with a living mucus that sheds ice before it can bond. Six limbs, the middle pair shorter and edged like digging scoops; the outer four end in splayed, frost-laced pads that leave almost no print on the sphagnum. They do not so much hunt as continuously metabolize the valley’s overflow of insects and amphibian eggs, filtering the black water through fringed mouthparts that click softly under the larger roar. When one of the pale tree-stands collapses under sudden melt, the runners arrive in a glittering black sheet and strip the fallen wood of every soft inhabitant before the moss has finished reclaiming the trunk. They are numerous enough that their collective passage is one of the low thrashing sounds you take for granted.

Above them, half-holding to the lichen-furred trunks, move the jointed ones. These are taller when they choose to be—extendable, telescoping arrangements of pale cartilaginous spars sheathed in translucent cuticle that takes on the gray-blue of the prevailing light. At rest they fold into angular knots no larger than a crouched child and become nearly indistinguishable from the broken basalt. In motion they are decisive and brief: a limb unfolds with a wet telescoping sound, hooks a trunk or a hanging filament, and the whole body slings itself ten meters in a single contraction. They possess too many elbows. Some configurations show nine, others fourteen; the number seems to increase when they are agitated or when one of the light-lattices nearby reconfigures. Their heads are not distinct; sensory fans of feathery cilia ring the upper joints and taste the iron-and-ozone air continuously. They do not vocalize in the avian register. Instead they produce the long sliding calls by forcing air through internal resonators while vibrating against the tree bark, so that each cry is half voice and half wood. They are curious about the manufactured frames. Small groups will hang motionless for hours around a humming triangle, cilia outspread, then suddenly erupt into a storm of limbs when the lattice unfolds into its temporary pavilion. Whether they worship, feed, or simply calibrate themselves by the alien geometries is unclear; after the pavilion collapses they leave behind small cairns of polished basalt pebbles and strips of their own shed cuticle, arranged in patterns that echo the lattice’s last shape.

In the colder pools the larger amphibians hang like drowned thoughts. Dog-sized is an understatement for the mature ones; some back arcs rise higher than a kneeling human when they shift. Their skin is a mottled charcoal and sickly jade, patterned with moisture-retaining pits that double as chemical sensors. Throats work in slow peristalsis even when the mouth is shut, tasting dissolved metals and the faint electromagnetic tingle that leaks from the alloy ribs. They are patient ambushers. A runner that misjudges a moss bridge over a pool may vanish in a single elastic surge, the water barely disturbed. Yet the amphibians also tolerate—perhaps cultivate—colonies of glowing midges that nest in the folds of their flanks; at night the pools become constellations of pale green fire that pulse in time with the deeper ground-roar. When one of the alloy structures discharges and the air briefly stinks of ozone, the amphibians rise higher, throats ballooning, and release clouds of fertilized eggs that the midges immediately begin to shepherd into the safer sphagnum mats.

There are others, smaller and more provisional. Clouds of the glittering insects are not merely swarm; certain generations develop elongated legs and begin to walk the hanging filaments like tightropes, harvesting the water sheets that climb on command. Minute crustaceans live inside the pitcher-leaved plants and have started to secrete mineral shells that look suspiciously like fragments of the black alloy. Even the moss itself harbors writhing thread-life that contracts in unison when the wind-howl reaches a particular frequency, turning whole carpets briefly rigid as if listening.

And then there are the ones who may not be native, or may have become native by staying too long. You glimpse them only at the edge of identity: upright silhouettes half-lost in the blue-gray murk, their outlines wrong in subtle ways—shoulders too fluid, heads that seem to carry residual lattices of light behind the eyes. They move with the slow deliberation of those who have learned the valley’s stratified weathers and can step from the roar of water into the slightly different roar of machine resonance without flinching. Their garments, if garments they are, grow moss on the leeward side and leave thin ice films when they pass the alloy spikes. Sometimes one will pause beside a frame and rest a hand (or hand-equivalent) against the humming surface; the light lattice brightens in acknowledgment, folding through a few extra geometries before settling. They do not speak where you can hear them. They leave new filaments behind them, tighter and more deliberately placed than the abandoned silk, and the pools obey these new threads with greater precision. Whether they are caretakers, remnants, or simply another organism the valley has invented out of whatever raw material drifted here, they belong now. The cold wet dark has written itself into their posture.

All of them together keep the overcrowding intact. No niche stays empty long enough to cool. Birth and collapse and reconfiguration happen at every scale, from the midges exploding out of an amphibian’s flank-nest to the sudden calving of a basalt shoulder that buries a runner trail and is already sprouting fern crowns by the next dim afternoon. The advanced objects punctuate but do not rule; they have been metabolized into the same restless grammar. The runners drink meltwater that has sheeted across alloy. The jointed ones tune their cries to the upper harmonics of the singing filaments. The upright silhouettes walk routes that avoid the newest peat channels yet somehow always arrive when a lattice is about to unfold.

Stay long enough and you begin to feel the gradient in your own nerves: the way the deafening sound separates into layers you could almost inhabit, the way your eyes stop expecting full daylight and start reading meaning in the gleam of wet cuticle or the afterimage of a folding plane. The valley does not care if you understand the rules. It only keeps producing more life that already knows them, bodies wired for persistent chill, filtered light, continuous vibration, and the strange indifferent music of things that were built and then forgotten by everything except the moss and the rain.
