The ones who live here are not hurried.

They move through the pale damp like weather that has learned to prefer certain paths. At first you notice only the animals—the long-legged browsers whose coats never dry, beaded forever with the same fine condensate that sheets every rock face. They drift in loose, unhurried constellations across the scree, cropping the gray-green cushions with soft tearing sounds that the wet air swallows within a few body-lengths. Their eyes are large and dark and almost without shine, adapted to the thinned light; when they look at you there is neither fear nor curiosity, only a slow acknowledgment that you too are made of temporary water and mineral. Overhead the wide-winged forms bank in near silence, riding thermals so weak they seem imaginary. Their feathers or membranes—hard to tell which—carry the same perpetual sheen of moisture. They do not cry out. Nothing here raises its voice.

Smaller lives stitch the ground: arthropods the color of wet ash that cling to weeping stone and drink, hares that become indistinguishable from broken scree the instant a shadow crosses them, burrowers whose round tunnels exhale faint warmer breath into the cold. Predators are brief geometric events—an unfolding of limbs or a drop from a ledge—then absence again. The whole animal nation runs on fog-time, on the barely perceptible pulse of seasons that never trouble themselves with drama. They are perfectly of the place: abundant, unafraid, quiet as the drip of water onto stone that did not exist when the older ones among them were born.

But the cities hold others.

You do not see them at once. Approaches last days, and the towers and terraces reveal themselves slowly, extruded from the same young rock, laminted with lattices that breathe. Only when you stand on the crowed outer ways, where the responsive foundations make constant microscopic adjustments so that streets remain true while the highland continues its long unfinished rising, do you begin to meet them. They are tall, or they can be. Their bodies are long-boned and lightly built, as if gravity here has always been a suggestion rather than a law. Skin or hide or living membrane—again hard to separate—holds the damp without shedding it; they gleam faintly, the way the rock gleams. Some wear the condensate like a second garment. Others seem to drink it inward, so that on colder days thin clear ice flowers briefly at their joints and collarbones before being reabsorbed.

Their faces are calm and elongated, eyes protected by nictitating layers that give them a permanent soft focus, as though they are always looking partly through mist even when the air is clearest. They do not blink often. Mouths are narrow; speech, when it happens, is low and local, carried only a few paces before the perpetual moisture takes it and turns it into more silence. They prefer not to speak in open air. Inside the capillary architecture, where water moves in controlled hush through walls and underfoot, conversation is almost tactile—a matter of shared breath and the faint pressure changes that their lattices can translate.

They are old in craft and young in geology. Every structure they inhabit is a negotiated truce with a planet still deciding its shape. Foundations lengthen or contract by hair-widths overnight; the inhabitants feel these shifts the way other peoples feel wind, and they answer with small postural changes, a flexing of weight, a hand briefly pressed to a wall that is also a living calculation. Water is their medium and their quiet engine. They pull vapor from the air, store it, release it as ice or pure liquid or soft controlled mist that cools a chamber or powers a deeper, soundless gradient. Light they hoard without greed: at true night their cities glow like banks of cool coals because each dwelling exhales stored day in thrifty pulses. No glare. No waste.

They are not numerous inside any single city. Density would create noise, and noise here feels like a kind of violence against the scale of the place. Instead they live in measured constellations of their own—households or ateliers or contemplative clusters spaced along terraces so that each can hear the water in the walls and the occasional soft chime of a load-adjusting spar, but little else. Children (they do have children) move even more slowly than the adults, pausing often to touch stone that has newly risen, to taste the mineral freshness with ungloved fingertips. Education is largely ambulatory: long walks between cities lasting weeks, sleeping on ledges that may gently tilt by morning, learning the difference between a scarp that will become mountain and one that will sink back into marsh. By the time they are grown they carry internal maps of fold and saddle that no fixed cartography could hold, because the highland will not stay fixed.

Their thought is like the light—thinned, far-traveled, unwilling to glare. They build no monuments that fight the cold or the wet; everything incorporates. A bridge is a thought stretched across empty air on cables thinner than reason, and birds roost there because the cables also hum, very softly, with boring data about stress and temperature that the birds somehow find companionable. Tools are rarely held; more often they are grown or persuaded out of the same responsive materials, used, then allowed to rejoin a wall or floor. Violence among them is almost unknown—not from saintliness, but because the sheer work of remaining in conversation with an unfinished continent leaves little surplus heat for rage. When conflict arises it is settled by stillness: two or more will simply stand together on a high terrace through a long damp day and a longer night, sharing the same fog memory until the disagreement has been rained out of them.

They know the animals as neighbors rather than resources. Sometimes a browser herd will move through a city’s lower skirts without haste or hindrance; the inhabitants step aside or stand quiet, watching the beaded coats and the neat dark droppings that steam their brief ghosts. Predators are granted the same courtesy of indifference. Death happens, of course—falls from new scarps, rare illnesses of the damp lung, the efficient sudden ends the carnivores bring—yet the dead are returned to the water cycle with little ceremony. Bodies are laid on porous ledges where the weeping rock can take them slowly back into mineral and mist. What remains is memory held in the lattices, faint thermal signatures that later walkers sometimes feel as a pocket of inexplicable warmth under the hand.

Walk far enough, past the animals, past one city and its cool-coal glow at night, into the next emptiness of bare wet stone, and you begin to suspect that the highland itself is the primary inhabitant. The pale angular rock, still tasting of its own pressures, weeping constantly, rising in places by hand-spans while travelers sleep—this is the oldest life. The cities and the browsers and the tall calm people are simply its more mobile expressions, temporary arrangements of water and attention. The people know this. You see it in the way they pause when a new scarp surfaces, edged with fresh shine: not surprise, not ownership, only recognition. They place a palm against the infant stone and wait, listening with skin, until the rock’s small decisions become part of their own longer ones.

They have no collective name they bother to speak aloud. Among themselves they use short resonant calls that the damp shortens further, names that mean things like “she-who-flexes-with-the-east-saddle” or “the-quiet-interval-between-two-mists.” Outsiders, on the rare occasions outsiders reach this far, have tried to label them—glacier-folk, plateau minds, the damp ones, the slow engineers. None of the labels stick. They themselves seem gently amused by the attempt, the way they are amused by any noise that does not last. What lives here is simply what continues: breath hanging in thinned light, footstep on living stone, the soft drip from a ledge onto rock younger than memory, and, everywhere, the sense that the work of becoming is still underway at continental scale, patient, unfinished, and already enough.
