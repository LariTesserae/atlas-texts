The first impression is absence, and it is almost perfect. You hang in the pewter gloom of a hall whose ceiling is a single enormous lintel, still true after whatever centuries, and the water around you feels unoccupied in the way a held breath is unoccupied. Nothing darts. Nothing grazes. The long kelp ribbons move only when the century-slow current remembers them. You wait long enough for your own pulse to become loud in your ears, and still the place offers no eyeshine, no flick of fin, no curious approach.

Yet absence on this scale is never empty. It is only a different density of presence.

Begin with the green. What seemed at first a continuous pelt begins, after hours of stillness, to show its own interior weather. The dark kelp does not merely stream; at intervals too irregular for tide and too patient for wind, entire curtains draw themselves aside as if making a corridor, hold the shape for a time measured in dozens of your heartbeats, then ease back. The motion is not contractile in any animal sense. It is more like a decision propagating through a shared root-mind that sleeps inside the joints of the stone. Where the holdfasts grip the ashlar they have married the rock so completely that the boundary between plant and masonry has become a single slow metabolism. Nutrients leach from the ancient blocks—mineral ghosts of the makers’ quarries—and the kelp returns a finer silt, a biological mortar that further seals every seam. The vegetation is not colonizing the work. It is finishing the work’s last unfinished sentence.

Inside that living mortar something else stirs, but only if you stop trying to see it.

Press your hand to a wall that has forgotten the sun. The stone is cold enough to ache, yet after a long minute the ache changes character. It becomes a low, reciprocal pressure, as though the block is considering the shape of your palm and answering with a shape of its own—an answering that takes minutes to form and minutes more to dissolve. Do this at a dozen scattered points across a single plaza and the answers begin to rhyme. The stone remembers being placed. It remembers the levers and the cords and the wet scraping of final adjustments. That memory has no nervous system, yet it is not inert. Over ages of immersion the memory has hydrated; it has become a kind of cool, distributed muscle. When currents shift on their long loops, the entire continental fabric makes micro-adjustments—settling a hair’s breadth here, relieving a shear stress there—movements too small for maps and too purposeful for physics alone. The makers did not leave. They thinned. They let the water dissolve the boundary between intention and material until intention was simply how stone behaves when it has been loved into perfect courses and then drowned.

Deeper still are the niches.

Every wall is riddled with them: recesses the size of a crouched body, or smaller, or vast enough to be chapels. Most hold only layered mat and the soft black-green nap that grows where light has died. But some niches are occupied by forms that do not quite match either stone or weed. They are pale, gelatinous, roughly anthropoid in the way a melted candle is still reminiscent of a figure. They do not breathe. They do not need to. Each is anchored by a root-mass that disappears into the living mortar behind it. Their surfaces bloom with the same pale algal films that store the last photons; when a rare, wandering particle of light drifts past, the films brighten in slow sequence, like a thought traveling through a mind that has forgotten language. These forms are not corpses. They are the makers’ final craft: bodies surrendered to the water and then patiently rewritten by it, generation after generation, until what remained was a sessile, dream-heavy continuity of the original builders. They do not walk the processional ways. They have become another kind of pillar, load-bearing in a metaphysical sense. If you float close, the algal glow may gather into a vague facial arrangement—eyes that are only denser patches of storage, a mouth that is only a seam where two growth fronts met. The expression is not still. Over the course of an hour it can shift from something like patience to something like grief and back again, though both words are too quick for what is happening.

Above these niches, in the upper galleries where the pewter memory of surface light is fractionally stronger, live the unfixed ones.

They are few. You can travel a week of terraces and meet none. Then, in a sudden open court whose paving has vanished under five meters of soft green snow, you find them: elongated, translucent bodies that trail veils of their own tissue, moving with the exaggerated slowness of cold honey. They are neither fish nor cephalopod nor anything the dry world named. Their locomotion is a continuous pouring of self forward, the veils catching against kelp and releasing, against stone and releasing. Where they pass, the living mortar brightens for a time, as if pleased. They carry no tools, yet sometimes one will pause at a joint that has begun to gape under some infinitely slow stress and will press its substance into the crack. When it withdraws, the crack is filled with a plug of translucent matter that hardens (over months) into something indistinguishable from the original stone, except that it never quite stops glowing. These are the maintenance caste, the last mobile expression of the makers’ old responsibility. They do not speak. They leave behind them a faint taste in the water—mineral, slightly sweet, like wet limestone after rain—that is the closest thing this place still has to a signature.

And then there are the ones that are only pressure.

You feel them first as a change in the continuous low note the water makes against your chest. The hum deepens, acquires a shape, becomes almost a chord. The darkness in a side passage thickens until it has weight and direction. Something vast and non-local is moving through the nested courts, not displacing water so much as convincing the water to remember a different density. These presences never resolve into bodies. They are weather systems of attention. When one passes through a hall of broken pillars, every truncated stem brightens with algal fire for a long moment, and the kelp curtains all lean the same way, like iron filings. The makers’ original geometry—the exact spans, the measured rises, the deliberate rhymes of joint and course—has become a score, and these pressure-beings are the music that still plays itself across it. They are what the place dreams when the place is especially still. They are harmless. They are also not interested in you. To them you are a brief warmer eddy, noted and released.

Stay longer—weeks, months of subjective time measured only by the thickening of your own thoughts—and the distinctions blur further. You begin to suspect that the sessile dreamers in the niches, the pouring maintainers, the pressure-weathers, and the hydrated memory inside the masonry are not separate kinds of inhabitant. They are phases of one thing, the way water is ice and vapor and cloud. The makers did not die when the flood became permanent. They simply had no further need of the hard boundaries that dry land had once required. Craft was their final technology, and craft, given enough cold water and enough time, became indistinguishable from ecology. What lives here is the building itself, metabolizing its own past, growing a slow cold garden out of its own bones and calling that garden continuous worship.

Float once more in the narrow slot-canyon of perfect ashlar. Listen to the quiet that is no longer empty. The walls consider you. The green finishes a thought it began before any word you know was spoken. Far off, a pressure-chord turns a corner and the darkness briefly learns how to sing without sound. Nothing arrives that needs to announce itself, because everything that lives here already arrived long ago and then kept arriving, softer and softer, until arrival and residence became the same green-black, stone-cold, unfinished forever.

That is who lives here: the place, still making itself in the only language that survived the water.
