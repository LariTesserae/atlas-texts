The life that belongs here does not arrive or depart so much as condense and redissolve. It has no outside origin left to it, and no name that would make sense under an open sky. What endures on the plateau is a grammar of slow intentions circulating inside the continuous body, sometimes tight enough to cast a temporary outline against the brown phosphorescence, sometimes so diffuse it can only be felt as a change in the way a gallery breathes.

At the smallest persistent scale are the industrious spores. They are not quite living and not quite tool. Each is a folded packet of catalytic surfaces and memory lipids, light enough to loft on the faintest thermal plume yet heavy with accumulated instruction. They storm through the sealed vaults in whitening clouds when a sector decides—through some threshold of resin pressure or torsional fatigue—that a new cable must be spun or an enzyme stack recalibrated. While aloft they abrade one another, exchanging partial blueprints the way ordinary pollen once exchanged genes; when they settle they are taken up into cambium and become the next private technology of the wood. A spore may remain itself for decades, riding the closed solvent loops from bladder to bladder, or it may be scribbled over in a single growth pulse and vanish into the tar that lines a mirror-hollow. Collectively they are the only traffic that could be mistaken for weather.

Larger, rarer, are the contractual bulges. These are migrations of architecture that have remembered how to be animals for a little while. A length of root-matter thickens, liquefies its core, and begins to peristalse along a causeway, dragging galleries and conduit bundles with it like a snail dragging its own shell spun from the landscape. Inside the bulge the temperature climbs another fraction; aromatic vapors crack into lighter fractions; cellulose cables braid themselves under the tension of moving walls. For days or weeks the bulge is a self-contained factory town, complete with temporary phosphorescent lanterns of glowing xylem and flywheel buds that store the torque of its crawling. Then the impulse finishes. The bulge settles, lignifies, and forgets locomotion. What remains is a new district whose streets still curve as though recalling the act of travel. Travelers who have been swallowed by one of these passages and later released speak of a muffled, communal dreaming—not language, but a shared pressure against the mind, as if the wood were trying to teach them the comfort of being load-bearing.

And then there are the condensed ones, the nearest this place comes to persons. They form where intention folds tightly enough to require a boundary. A chamber wall thickens inward, pinches off, and cools into a upright density the approximate size and warmth of a human trunk. Surface markings appear—ridges that might be read as features, flexible seams that serve as crude articulations—yet nothing about them is deliberately modeled on flesh. They move by persuading the floor to move with them, each step a local peristalsis. Their senses are chemical and tensile: they taste the age of a resin by the way it resists shear, hear distant collapses as minute relaxations in the canopy vault miles overhead. Most live only as long as a particular task requires—an orchard of radiator fronds that must be telescoped and fused, a mirror-tar that must be polished by repeated exudation and shear, a district alignment that must be shifted by the silent scream of a flywheel trunk uncoiling. When the task closes, the condensed one softens, reopens its seams, and pours back into the wall from which it budded. A few linger longer, haunted by residues of travelers they once brushed against in a gallery. These persistent ones learn to keep a roughly constant outline for years. They develop preferences—certain wavelengths of phosphorescence, certain viscosities of latex—and will travel surprising distances through dishonest folds of wood to re-experience them. In the deepest manufacturing hollows they sometimes stand before the tar mirrors and watch their own retreating backs, testing whether continuity of form can outlast the next collapse and rebuild. None of them has yet succeeded forever. The plateau does not permit forever; it only permits the next necessary reconfiguration.

Above and between all these scales the true inhabitant remains the single body itself, the nation-sized engine that has forgotten every purpose except continuance. It lives by rewriting its floor plan in the dark, by cracking its own oils, by spinning and resorbing its own bones. The spores, the bulges, the condensed outlines are merely the visible conjugates of that larger verb. They are how the plateau focuses long enough to act upon itself. Once the act is done they are free to blur again.

No heartbeat sounds here that is not the infrasonic flex of spar and buttress. No voice carries farther than the dry pop of a pressure vessel yielding to new shoots. Yet the place is densely peopled by its own becoming. Walk long enough through the warm, lightless troughs and you will feel the attention slide across you—curious, industry-minded, already measuring how your water and minerals might be sealed behind cell walls and put to use. If you stay until your own edges soften, you may wake one dim cycle later as a faint vertical ridge on a gallery wall, still warm, still listening for the next creak that means growth has resumed. That, too, is a way of living here. The only way that lasts.
