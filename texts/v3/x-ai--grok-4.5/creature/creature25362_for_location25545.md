The ones who live here are never only one kind of thing.

At first they seem like interruptions—small, upright, tool-using shapes moving along the catwalks and through the dripping archways between shifts. They wear layered coats stiff with old mineral film and boot soles worn to the metal mesh beneath. Their faces are creased by the perpetual damp; eyes narrowed not against glare (there is none) but against the fine mist that beads on lashes and drips from the brims of their caps. They speak little while working. When they do, the words come out half-swallowed by the larger sounds, as if language itself has learned to travel in the spaces between piston-falls and the wet slap of climbing things. They know the schedules of the great engines the way other people once knew seasons. A woman will pause on a gantry, palm flat to a railing that thrums, and say only “three more days” before the ore-lift changes pitch; everyone within hearing understands she means the herds will soon move through the lower meadows and the scavenging packs will grow bold around the canteens.

Yet even these workers are not separate from what surrounds them. Watch long enough and you see how the city has rewritten their bodies across generations. Shoulders slope forward from centuries of ducking under low pipe runs. Fingers are blunt, nails thick, permanently outlined in rust and calcite. Many carry small, pale scars where the soft wall-dwellers have tasted them and been brushed away. Children learn early to sleep against warm dynamo housings and to tell edible mineral film from the greasy kind that sickens. They are not masters here. They are one more clade that found a temperature range and a rhythm it could occupy.

Around and through and over them move the greater numbers.

Begin with the slow herds. They are hide-bound, yes, but the hide is plated in irregular keratinous scutes that click and scrape when they shoulder one another on the gratework meadows. Each animal stands higher than a loader cab at the shoulder, carried on six or eight jointed legs that end in broad, adhesive pads suitable for wet metal and slag glass alike. Their heads are low, swinging, equipped with dense brushes of stiff hairs and rasping plates; they graze the drip-algae and the mineral beards, leaving behind long, shining trails where they have scraped the stone clean. Breath leaves them in visible plumes that add to the city’s exhalation. They do not startle easily. A piston the size of a house can rise ten meters away and they will only flick an ear-membrane and continue feeding. Calves are born already flailing, dropped onto grated floors that ring like dull bells; within hours they are following the adults along routes that double as coolant pipeways and ancient rail beds. The herds have names among the workers—names that are really just descriptions of their migration clocks. “The Ash-Side Walkers.” “The ones who come after the mid-cycle venting.” No one remembers a time without them. The older gantries have been reinforced specifically to take their weight; in places the original engineering has been entirely replaced by ramps of packed slag and bone that the animals themselves compacted over centuries.

Above them the air is stitched with wings. The angular fliers are not precisely birds. Their cores are dense and warm, wrapped in overlapping vanes of something like keratin and something like cooled slag-glass, translucent at the edges so that the perpetual mist catches in them and turns each wheeling flock into a drifting constellation of soft light. They dive for the pale crabs that boil out of the cooling-pond outfalls whenever the temperature gradients shift. Their calls are high, metallic, almost like unmeshed gears singing; at dawn the upper steam layers fill with overlapping screams that roll from district to district like slow weather. Nesting happens on the sheer faces of the great smokestacks and on the undersides of viaducts, where the birds (if the word still serves) mortar together mineral crusts and shed scutes and stolen insulation wire into cupped platforms that drip continuously. Nestlings hatch looking like wet machinery—angular, already sharp, eyes protected by nictitating membranes that shed water in quick shivers. By the time they fledge they know how to ride the updrafts from the forge halls and how to avoid the larger gliders that share the same thermals.

Those gliders are quieter. Translucent, almost membrane-thin when they stretch, they unfurl from the hollow legs of towers and from the cable races at dusk. They are soft-bodied at the core, maintained by internal lattices that can stiffen or relax; they feed on the singing wall-things and on the smaller midges that breed in every permanent droplet. When a glider passes close you feel the air move before you see it—an oily, warm displacement—and then it is already past, climbing toward the weaker light still filtering through the upper haze. Their mating flocks form temporary spirals that last for hours, thousands of individuals rising and falling together until the chosen pairs peel away to the high wet galleries where the air is thinnest and the rain (when it finally arrives as more than mist) first condenses.

In the walls themselves the density increases. Soft-bodied singers live in the cable conduits and the older brickwork, pulsing gentle luminescence through their skins at the hours when the factory whistles used to sound (the whistles still sound, but the singers no longer need them; they have internalized the rhythm). Their chorus is low and complex, a wet multiphonic hum that workers use the way other cultures once used clock towers. When the singers fall silent all at once, people stop what they are doing and listen for whatever larger shift the city is about to make. Armored millers click along the same routes, /**
ir of chitin protocols that have converged on the frequencies of the nearer engines; sometimes a whole stretch of corridor ticks in perfect register with a distant trip-hammer, and the effect is so precise it feels deliberate. No one knows whether the millers learned the engines or the engines were once tuned to the millers. The question is asked only half in jest.

And then the scavenger packs. Lean. Many-eyed. Clever in the specific way that living close to human garbage and human schedules produces. They are quadrupedal at rest, hexapedal when climbing, their extra limbs folding tight against the ribcage until needed. Eyes—six or eight or ten—run along the skull and down the neck in staggered rows; some are adapted for the infrared shimmer of warm pipes, others for the ultraviolet markers that certain fungi-films leave on metal. They know the shift changes better than most supervisors. Five minutes before a foundry crew clocks out, the packs are already waiting in the shadowed under-hangs of the canteen levels, patient as rust. They do not attack healthy workers. They remove the fallen, the discarded, the unlucky, and every scrap of protein or carbohydrate that leaves human hands. In return they keep the lower sumps from choking on their own dead. Humans leave them the occasional barreled offering at the edges of the deepest cisterns; the packs accept these with a formality that looks, from a distance, almost like ritual. Their eyes at night turn the mid-level bridges into slow constellations, just as the description said. What it did not say is how those constellations sometimes pause and rearrange, spelling temporary patterns that other packs on distant gantries appear to answer. Information moves that way—about collapses, about freshly cooled slag slopes rich in edible crusts, about the rare appearance of surface predators that have dragged themselves up from whatever wet darkness lies beyond the plateau’s remembered edge.

Below everything, in the deep warm cisterns under the oldest foundries, live the ones that never surface. Workers feel them more than see them: a slow rearrangement of pressure in the floorplates, a subsonic boom that rises through bootsoles and settles in the base of the spine when the weather turns. Calving grounds. The young are born into water that has not seen daylight in geologic time, water kept permanently at the temperature of blood by the waste heat of engines that have been running since before the maps forgot what lay under the plateau. Sometimes a maintenance diver (there are still a few who take the long, pressurized walks) reports glimpses: vast pale surfaces freckled with short cilia, eyes the size of manhole covers that do not blink because they have never needed to, limbs or fins or feeding structures folded against the body like carefully stored gantry arms. The divers do not linger. They leave their offerings—high-energy concentrates sealed in degradable film—and ascend while the booming answers follow them up through the stone. Whatever lives down there is patient even by the city’s standards. It has survived the lighting of every kiln and the extinguishing of a few. It will outlast the current configuration of people and packs and herds.

There are smaller lives still. Things that are almost only appetite and cling: filter-crescents that coat the inner surfaces of steam vents, glossy and dark; thread-thin borers that spend their entire multi-year larval stage inside a single cooling fin, emerging only to mate once in the open air and die; translucent worms that braid themselves into temporary ropes so they can cross from one dripping overhang to another. Ecologies so nested that a single abandoned hauler cab can contain, rusting on a siding, seventeen interdependent species none of which have names and three of which have never been collected because they die in any light bright enough to photograph them.

And through all of it, the friction and the commerce and the occasional tenderness between kinds. A herder (human) walks beside a juvenile grazer that has learned to accept touch, one broad adhesive foot lifted so the herder can scrape an impacted stone from the pad. A scavenger pack leaves a clear path when a funeral cortege passes, then closes in behind to perform their older office once the living have gone. Children learn which singers can be coaxed into the hand with a particular humming frequency, and which will sting. On certain nights when the fog banks rotate just so and the mid-level air stills, people and packs and even a few of the braver gliders gather along the same long overlooks. No one speaks. The city breathes under them. The deep ones boom softly. Someone may open a thermos of bitter mineral tea and pass it. A pack leader may set down a scavenged battery still holding charge, an offering or a trade or simply a recognition that power is power. For a while the boundaries loosen. You can feel, in those stretched minutes, that the plateau is not inhabited by separate species that happen to share a space. It is one long, wet, interlocking respiration. The people are a warm, tool-using membrane across part of its surface. The herds are its slow digestive peristalsis. The fliers carry its salts from outfall to stack-top. The buried giants keep its deepest heat distributed. Even the rust seems to participate, advancing and flaking in rhythms that free micronutrients for the films that feed the grazers that feed the packs that eventually feed the cistern things when something old or sick finally falls.

They are still becoming. That is the only constant. New hybrid scrapes of territory appear after every major mechanical failure; a collapsed reactor dome cools into a pristine gradient and within a decade it has been recolonized by clades that did not exist in that form before the failure. Workers whose great-grandparents still used elevated walkways now travel routes that duck through scrap-metal tunnels lined with grown shell. The scavengers’ eye-arrangements continue to multiply; some of the newest pups are born with a rear-facing cluster that has no parallel in older generations. Deep in the cisterns, something has begun to answer the factory schedules with more than boom—patterns of pressure that match the weekly trip-hammer cycles, as if the buried ones are learning to keep time or teaching the engines a slower heart.

Stand on any overlook long enough and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat dissolves into the same warm mist. The plateau does not end. Neither do they. They only continue, layered and dripping and dense, becoming weather that thinks, iron that hungers, steam that remembers every footstep ever placed on it. What lives here is the city itself, wearing a thousand thousand temporary skins, each skin believing—for a while—it is separate.
