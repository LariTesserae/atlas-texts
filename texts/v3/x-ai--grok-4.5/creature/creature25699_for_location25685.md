The ones who live here do not arrive. They accrete.

At first you notice only the motion that refuses to be motion: a slow brightening along the salt, as if the pale ground itself has decided to lift and wander. What you are seeing are the grazers, but that name is already too quick, too edged with intent. They are low constellations of jointed mineral, twelve or fifteen or twenty pairs of limbs depending on how many seasons of molting they have endured without losing count. Their bodies are the same fused evaporite as the plain, plated in overlapping shields that look poured rather than grown. When they feed they do not bite; they rasp. The sound is part of the permanent roar, a million microscopic files working the rubbery mats into slurry that they draw upward through channels in their underplates. Between feedings they simply stand, sometimes for years, while dust settles into the seams of their armor and lichens of a harder, slower kind colonize their backs until each grazer becomes a small traveling outcrop. Calves—if the word can stretch to coversomething born as a soft, translucent knot of salt-rich gel—are deposited in the shallow basins and left. They harden in place, taking the imprint of the mat beneath them, then rise on untested legs and join whichever loose herd drifts nearest. There is no recognition, only gravitational drift into the larger pattern. Over centuries a single grazer may cross the same plaza ten thousand times, polishing a trail so slowly that the trail itself becomes a kind of kin, a shared memory underfoot.

Above them the air is never empty. The membrane-wings do not fly; flight would imply a decision against gravity. They sled. Their bodies are lighter than the idea of bone, translucent vanes stretched across cartilaginous frames that can lock rigid or go slack as the faintest density current requires. Colonies hang in the residual vaults like vast, breathing curtains. When a current thickens—when a dry fall of ceiling dust displaces air, or when a herd far below exhales a collective warmth too slight for any thermometer—they tilt, release, and coast in slow spirals that may last days. Their clicking is not communication in any hurried sense; it is continuous census, each click a proof of still-being that merges into the subsonic thunder until you cannot tell where one life ends and the pressure of the rock begins. They birth by budding. A mature vane develops a thickening at its trailing edge; the thickening loosens, catches a micro-current of its own, and is gone, already learning the grammar of drift. Detritus from their molts—hexagonal platelets thin as breath—becomes part of the mats below, a silvering that the grazers rasp up again without comment.

Closer to the half-melted walls move the scavenger rivers. These are smaller, harder, almost pure carapace. They lace the bases of ruins in braided streams that look from a distance like dark water remembering how to flow. Each individual is little more than a mouth and a gut wrapped in articulated stone. They dismantle what the larger lives discard: a cracked grazer plate, a fallen membrane, a patch of mat that has grown too thick and begun to dock itself. Their work is so thorough that nothing remains to fossilize. What they cannot digest they cement into the wall bases with a salivary mortar, slowly thickening the ancient foundations, healing the city back into plain. They too are colonial in an accidental way; when two rivers meet they braid without hierarchy, exchange members, separate again changed. A scavenger may spend a decade climbing the soft shoulder of a slumped column simply because the angle offers a longer continuous scrape.

Yet these are only the obvious lives, the ones that cast soft shadows under the lichen-light. Something else moves through the same spaces more slowly still, something that uses the herds and the curtains and the rivers the way a circulating system uses blood. You notice it first as a coordination too perfect to be chance. Herds thin or thicken in surrounding basins in rhythms that match the slow pulses of the roar. Membrane-wings tilt in unison across kilometers when no current has changed. Scavenger rivers divert around certain plazas for decades, leaving them unswept, until one season they converge and dismantle a single fallen column with the focus of a single appetite. The Underflat is thinking, but the thought is not housed in any skull. It is distributed across every rasping mouth, every clicking vane, every rootlet of the pale rosettes that draw mineral from the bones of the old city. The vegetation is not backdrop; it is the longest-lived tissue of the same body. The fleshy rosettes swell and divide on schedules that anticipate the arrival of grazers generations before the grazers themselves turn that way. The silver fans angle their brittle surfaces to cup dust that will later feed mats that will later feed herds that will later feed the deeper shafts with their shed matter. Every green is a slow gland. Every animal is a mobile enzyme.

In the farthest basins, where the black shafts open, the distributed life thickens into something that can almost be met. Individuals grow rarer and larger. A grazer here may stand three times the bulk of its avenue cousins, its plates so encrusted with secondary lichen that it has become a walking garden, its rasping mouthparts refined into delicate combs that can sort salt grain from salt grain. Membrane-wings above these basins have vanes so wide they no longer sled; they hang plunged into the upwelling roar from below, trembling with its frequencies, filtering whatever richer spores or mites the deeper levels exhale. And around the shaft lips themselves, where the plain drops away into stacked iterations of the same world, the scavenger rivers halt and build. They mortar their own bodies into living grates, half-bridges, partial lids, shaping the openings without ever sealing them. Through those shaped throats the Underflat breathes itself downward and upward at once. What descends is spent carapace, excess mat, the occasional whole grazer that has simply walked too near the edge and continued walking. What rises is a denser noise, a slightly wetter mineral scent, and sometimes a new form—plates arranged in unfamiliar symmetries, vanes that carry faint veins of bioluminescence older than the lichen above. These newcomers do not invade. They take their place in the roar, adding a new harmonic, and the larger body adjusts around them across the next few centuries.

No single mind looks out through all this. Yet there are moments that feel like attention. When a dry fall of ceiling material begins its day-long descent, herds pause mid-rasp and turn their plated faces upward together. When the subsonic thunder shifts key, every membrane-wing across a hundred galleries locks rigid at the same instant, listening with its whole surface. Something is aware of its own continuity. Something prefers the avenues to remain clear, the mats to keep their layered glow, the shafts to stay open. Something remembers—without memory—the taller walls that once divided the plain into rooms, and keeps walking the same paths because the paths still fit the width of its many-legged thoughts.

Stay long enough and you begin to feel the same slow circulation inside your own chest. Your breath lengthens to match the tidal pulses. Dust settles on your shoulders and is not brushed away. You understand that if you remained a few decades more you would not leave; you would simply take your place among the soft-sided mounds, another low architecture being rounded into the oldest shape. The Underflat does not exile outsiders. It waits until they dissolve into its patient green, its mineral herds, its roaring dark, and then it continues, stacked and endless, under the light that never brightens and never fails.
