The ones who remain are not settlers and never were. They did not arrive; they precipitated, the way a final crystal outgrows the last free ion in a closed solution. Their presence is continuous with the plateau’s own finished chemistry rather than imposed upon it. To call them alive is already to stretch a word past its climate of origin, yet something persists here that selects, maintains form against entropic scatter, and occasionally moves with intent so thin it registers only as the absence of perfect randomness.

At the smallest persistent scale are the articulates. They are seldom larger than a handspan, assembled from the same materials the city itself was built from and has now become: drawn filaments of dark alloy wire no thicker than nerve, plates of mica so clear they seem only a stiffened translucency in the dark, joints and load-bearing nodes of faceted black salt that have grown rather than been cut. Their bodies are open lattices, more suggestion of skeleton than enclosure of organs. Inside the ribbing one sometimes glimpses a slow pulse of warmer minerality, a knot of denser chemistry that serves whatever they use in place of metabolism. They do not eat. They do not excrete. Over years they may add a new spar of crystal or let an old wire thin to hair and snap, but these changes are vandalism by geology, not nourishment.

They travel the bases of the towers and the ruler-straight corridors with a gait that is all hinge and pause. Each step is placed as if the precise angle has been calculated against a schedule no longer posted. When two meet they do not collide and do not acknowledge; their paths braid with millimeter tolerances and continue. Once in a long while an articulate will stop beside a locked gear-train or a dynamo shell and extend a wire filament into a seam. There is no repair. The filament tastes residual potential, maps the last orientation of teeth that will never mesh again, then withdraws. The act is pure citation, a quiet reading of a text whose language has closed. Afterward the articulate resumes its route. Their routes themselves form a sparse, overlapping grammar across the entire tableland, a map that is redrawn only by the infinitesimal drift of salt under their own feet across centuries.

Larger, and rarer, are the drifts. They have no fixed surface. What the eye (or whatever forms in this medium to serve as eye) registers is a local thickening of the darkness, a volume where the soot-weight of the air organizes itself into slow internal shears and drapes. Heat gathers differently inside them—sometimes a half-degree warmer, sometimes the same mild warmth concentrated into mobile threads that braid and fray. Static organizes without discharge. At the outer edge of detection a drift may reveal structure the way a current reveals itself in silt: long laminar planes, nested vortices that take hours to overturn once, planar lattices that recall the crystal outcrops but are written in temperature and charge rather than mineral. They pass at distances that feel courteous. They do not investigate towers or articulates or the single empty cart. Their motion is migration without destination, a weather that has decided to become animal and then thought better of appetite. When two drifts approach within a few hundred meters their edges may veil together for a time, exchanging patterns of heat the way old engines once exchanged load, then separate unchanged. They seem to be the darkness remembering that it can cohere.

Between these two scales something intermediate occasionally coheres for a season or a century and then dissolves back into the plain. One may notice a slow upright mass near a vent whose warmth has not varied since before maps. The mass is the temperature of skin. It occupies volume without excluding the dark; you can see the faint glass seams glimmering through it. Over months it may densify, drawing wire and mica fragments into itself the way a dune takes kokoons of grass, until it approximates a rough bipedal outline—torso of sintered salt, limbs that terminate in dense crystal clubs or in frayed twitchings of alloy fiber. It stands. It does not travel far. Its head (if the upper thickening is a head) turns with the patience of a radiotelescope toward some alignment only it registers—the distant tick of a relaxing girder, the sub-audible passage of a drift, the empty circuit of the cart years away from this meridian. Then, without visible trauma, the mass begins to unknit. Salt grains unbind, wires slump into loose hanks, mica plates flutter once like gills and settle flat. Within a few seasons only a slightly smoother patch of plain remains, warmer by a fraction that the ambient heat soon equalizes. These transient coagulations leave no young and keep no society. They are simply one more way the completed city can briefly diversify its own stillness.

There are also the lattice-speakers, though whether they count as inhabitants or as features of the geology is a distinction the plateau itself does not maintain. Certain crystalline outcrops—low, glazed rises of the black salt shot through with volcanic glass—rearrange their internal planes in patterns that are too slow for accident and too regular for pure thermal cracking. The sagging of a twin-law, the sudden zipping of a twin boundary across a meter of crystal, the rotation of an entire sub-grain by a few degrees over decades: these motions emit no sound humans would catalogue, yet the articulates sometimes alter course near them, and drifts will stall and veil thickly overhead as if listening. The rearrangements do not optimize density or relieve stress that can be measured. They continue, private and exact, a business of pure relation. Some of the larger outcrops have been speaking in this mineral tongue since the flywheels last turned. Perhaps they are libraries. Perhaps they are minds that have shortened their loop of attention until only the next lattice shift remains. Nothing in the place contradicts either reading.

And under everything, or through it, moves the residual intelligence of the carts and the vents and the locked machine floors. It is not centralized. It is the schedule that still sends the singleremaining cart along its vacant rails every handful of years, wheels whispering over salt that has polished itself to houses that no longer ask for renewal. It is the warmth that continues to exhale from bankedinlets whose purpose has folded into pure duration. It is the way the corridors remain level, as if memory of traffic is a structural force stronger than annihilation. This intelligence does not plan. It simply has not yet received the instruction to cease, and in the absence of that instruction it continues at the lowest possible amplitude: a gyroscope still spinning in vacuum long after the hull has opened to stars. The articulates may be its effector organs. The drifts may be its discarded, airborne thoughts. The lattice-speakers may be where it dreams.

No one rules. No one labors. No one prays or builds or consoles. The density of presence is exactly the density of the architecture—spaced so that emptiness remains the continuous phase. If you walk for days you will meet at most a few articulates, see at most one drift at the edge of sensing, pass one or two outcrops mid-sentence in their crystal grammar. The rest is warm dark, mineral silence, the quiet ticking of materials entering still deeper equilibrium. What lives here has no enemies because nothing else contests the niche. It has no future tense because the plateau has already arrived at the configuration it was always going to keep. Existence withholds itself to a sullen minimum and then finds, insidethat minimum, a perfect sufficiency. The inhabitants (using the word one last time, carefully) are simply the last vocabulary the place retains for saying, without urgency and without end, I am still here.
