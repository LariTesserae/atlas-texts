The first impressions that surface are not of a creature that walks or hides, but of something that simply *continues*.

In the hard white light that never quite warms the stone, what lives here does not announce itself with movement. It is already everywhere the condensation beads. It is the slow pressure inside every stem that touches another stem. It is the almost imperceptible pulse in the pale vines that web the dead smokestack—so slow you would have to watch for an entire turning of the light from morning-white to afternoon-white to be certain you saw it. Whatever this is, it has no interest in being seen as separate from the basin. It prefers to be mistaken for the place itself.

Yet there are densities.

Deep in the root-mat, where the giant mosses fold over one another like thick green lungs, something many-jointed and pale occasionally unfolds. Not often. Once in a long quiet span. It has too many limbs, each ending in a soft, flattened pad that tastes the metal-cold film of moisture on the leaves. It does not hunt. There is almost nothing to hunt. Instead it climbs—up the raw young cliffs, through cracks that still look freshly broken—and presses those pads against the stone as if listening for the residual heat of fracture. Sometimes it finds a cable half-buried in fern and follows it for days, body low and whispering against the verdigris moss, until the cable rises again into a shed where a flywheel turns with geological patience. There the pale thing will settle against the riveted plate and match its own infinitesimal movements to the rotation. One full circle. Then another. It stays until the light changes quality, then descends again into the green. No one has ever seen two of them at once. It is possible there is only one, moving through years the way other beings move through hours.

Higher, among the fused canopies of translucent leaves, the air itself sometimes thickens into temporary shapes. Not solid. More like denser light, or like breath that has decided to remain visible. These gather in the shafts where the hard sun falls straight down, and for a while they hold rough geometries—elongated, gently tapering, with suggestions of many fine filaments trailing downward like roots or like the coils of product that accumulate near the quiet machines. They do not travel. They simply stand in the brightness, trembling slightly whenever a frond shifts, then thin out again when the angle of light changes. The trees appear to make room for them without strain. It is difficult to say whether the shapes are born from the canopy or whether the canopy has learned to grow around a presence that was already there.

Near the tarns and the half-collapsed sheds, another mode of living becomes noticeable if you remain still long enough for time to feel viscous. The machines are not abandoned. They are attended. The attendance leaves almost no ordinary trace—no footprints that last, no tools left behind except those the moss decides to keep or release on its own schedule. What tends them is upright, roughly the height of the lower ferns, and the same cool color as the shaded undersides of leaves. Its surface looks poured rather than grown or built; in the hard light it can be mistaken for another concrete wall or a pale trunk until it moves. Movement is rare and costly. When it does occur, it is with the same unhurried certainty as the flywheels. A hand that is not quite a hand will rise, adjust a gauge whose needle has not trembled in living memory, then lower again. Or it will stand for the length of a slow afternoon beside the stacked resinous sheets, palm against the material, as if confirming that the slow extrusion is still in keeping with some agreement older than the sharp ridges. These attendants do not speak. They do not appear to breathe in any way that disturbs the condensation. When two of them occupy the same clearing they do not acknowledge each other; each simply continues its portion of the long negotiation between metal, sap, and stone. At night—though night here is only a deeper blue version of the same scraped clarity—they become harder to distinguish from the equipment they serve. Some have already begun to accept the pale vines into their surfaces. The vines do not seem to mind the difference.

And underneath all of this, threaded through the intermittent silver streams and the dark held breath of the tarns, there is a further life that never breaches the surface for long. Silver-langued, thin, moving with the same efficiency as the plants that drink the thin moisture. They are busier than everything above. In the hours after a mist-rain they flicker between root and stone, carrying something—minerals, memory, microscopic freight from the corroded railings and the fine filaments. Their passage leaves the water colder. If you cup a hand in a tarn after one has gone, the cold stays against the skin longer than physics should allow, and for a moment the hard light looks unbroken, as if the world has no interior left to hide in.

None of these are colonizers. None arrived and adapted. They are what the basin grew when it decided that industry and raw rock and saturated green would have to finish becoming one another. The pale climber, the light-densities in the canopy, the slow poured attendants, the silver threads in the water—they are not inhabitants in the ordinary sense. They are the basin’s way of remaining patient with itself. They keep the flywheels turning at root-speed. They let the vegetation swallow the crane without erasing the idea of the crane. They walk the short purposeless runs of dead fence as if the voltage were only sleeping. They make certain that the strange clearings stay bare, perfect circles of stone where nothing grows, because something in the agreement requires empty registers.

Stay long enough and you begin to feel the same negotiation inside your own chest: the cool metal taste of the shaded air, the way breath lingers, the slight lag between the sun’s arc and the lengthening of shadows. The place does not object to your presence. It simply continues, and in continuing it slowly includes you in the same thick silence. You may notice, days later, that moss has claimed the edge of your sleeve with the same calm entitlement it shows the resinous sheets. Or that your own movements have grown less frequent, more deliberate, matched to the turning of unseen wheels. Or that you have begun to hear, beneath the soft pressure-change of wind in the high canopies, the faint collective sound of everything here drinking, turning, pulsing, waiting—

the sound of a single mid-scale system remaining open under a scraped sky, cool and damp and brightly silent, turning itself into whatever comes next at the pace of condensation and fracture and light.
