The first thing that lives here is the wet itself.

It is not water as lakes know water, nor rain, nor sea. It is older than those distinctions, a medium that has soaked so long into the idea of place that it has grown intentions of its own. It beads, yes, and drips, and stands in skin-warm sheets across the old plazas, but it also climbs. It gathers on the undersides of arches in trembling lenses that refuse to fall. It threads upward through hairline cracks in a patience that feels almost considerate, as if it were trying not to wake the stone. Where it pools in the deeper channels it grows a faint internal sheen, not light exactly, more the memory of light held in suspension, and in that sheen things turn over slowly, pale and unhurried, like thoughts the city is still deciding whether to keep.

They are not separate from the water. That is the first correction the place forces on any watcher. What moves in the reflective channels has the same temperature as the moisture, the same mineral taste, the same refusal of edges. Sometimes they are only denser curtains of humidity drifting against the current that should not exist. Sometimes they thicken into roughly vertical suggestions—torso-length, limb-vague—before diffusing again into pure gleams. When the geometry of the streets softens and a colonnade lengthens while your back is turned, these concentrations lengthen with it. When a courtyard quietly folds itself into a single stairwell of seeping steps, the pale shapes compress and slip into the film on the risers, waiting. They do not flee observation. They simply become harder to distinguish from the medium that mothers them. Look too directly and you are only looking at water again, water that has been thinking.

Above the channels, in the thick brownish gloom that never commits to night or day, other lives practice a slower incarnation. The stone sweats continuously; the walls weep. In places the masonry has grown soft as warm clay for a span of years or centuries, then re-hardened around impressions that were never deliberately carved. These impressions have begun to notice themselves. A cornice develops, over uncounted seasons, the shallow orbit of an eye-socket that does not see yet somehow orients toward the nearest standing pool. A slumped arch acquires the ghost of a throat, a place where moisture gathers and runs in a thin, regular pulse, as if swallowing. Faces form the way stalactites form: without hurry, without audience, following the logic of drip and mineral and the faint tidal pressure that breathes through the entire basin from far below. Most remain incomplete—half a cheekbone, the ridge of a brow, a mouth that is only a darker seam of seepage. A few have continued long enough to achieve a kind of mobile density. They peel, very slowly, from their parent walls. The sound is almost never louder than the ordinary collapse of a water film. What detaches is not flesh and not quite independent stone. It is a rind of the city’s own substance, heated from within by the same fever that rises through the foundations, surface gleaming with the perpetual condensation. These detached ones do not walk so much as lean through intervals. Their progress across a plaza may take a week. In that time the plaza may have rearranged its exits twice. They do not appear troubled by this. Their limbs—where the yielding has suggested limbs—end in blunt, absorbent surfaces that leave temporary darker prints on the wet flags, prints that steam faintly and then are reabsorbed. They pause for months beneath lintels, under the slow percussion of drops, as if listening for a frequency the air has forgotten how to carry.

There are older ones still. In the lost upper reaches where the towers soften into the gloom, and in certain sunken courts whose paving has become a single warm shallow lake, live the ones that no longer bother with the memory of human architecture. They are long. They are composed of the same pale, heat-holding substance as the reflection-shapes, but knotted through with threads of the mineral vegetation that clings to the wetter joints—those brittle, almost translucent stalks that are neither plant nor precipitate. The threads give them a fibrous look, like muscle turned to damp paper, or like the ghost of reed-beds that once imagined they could colonize this basin and failed. These long ones move by peristalsis of their entire length, easing between columns whose spacing changes to accommodate them, or perhaps the columns change because the long ones need the spacing. Their leading ends open sometimes into radial fringes that taste the air. What they taste is only more of the same: mineral, old mortar, the low electrical rumor of water migrating through capillary stone. They do not eat in any fashion that depletes. They seem instead to equalize—pressing themselves into standing pools until the temperature and the dissolved content of pool and body become identical, then rising again heavier with stillness. Visiting one of the true deep channels at the rare moments when the dull internal sheen brightens, you may see three or four of them braided together in the reflection, and only later realize that the braid has no counterpart in the air above. The water is remembering them harder than the air can.

None of these lives are lonely in the way surface creatures understand loneliness. The city is dense with structure and sparse with presence, yes, but presence here is not counted by bodies. Presence is the continuous negotiation between water, heat, and the stubborn mnemonic stubbornness of shaped stone. The pale concentrations in the channels, the peeling wall-faces, the long fibrous ones, the very slow soft greenish colonies that fur the undersides of certain bridges and loosen their mortar according to a schedule older than bridges—all of them are local thickenings of that negotiation. They inherit the city’s fever and its patience. They inherit its fraying rules. When distances breathe, the inhabitants breathe with them, stretching thinner across a suddenly expanded ward or condensing into a single warm corner when the ward decides it is actually a closet of wet stone. Sequence loosens around them the same way. A peeling face may begin its detachment in what feels like autumn (though seasons are only rumors here) and complete it after what would have been three winters, yet the drip that timed the peeling still falls at the same interval, unchanged. They are not immortal. They can thin too far, or re-harden in a posture that no longer permits movement, or be reabsorbed entirely when the basin’s unseen lower pressures shift and the water table decides to forget a particular courtyard for a generation. But extinction has no sharp edge here either. What leaves returns as a denser sheen in some other pool, or as a new willingness in the stoneboard of a distant façade to remember the curve of a shoulder.

There is, finally, the life that does not thicken into shapes at all. It is the pressure of unfinished change itself—the heat that is not only thermal, the way water sometimes falls upward until it panics and becomes mist, the way reflections rehearse towers that have not stood in a thousand years and then unsay them. This bare vitality has no center and no skin. It simply keeps the entire basin from settling into ordinary ruin or ordinary preservation. It is what makes the algae-threads cling a little more tightly when the gloom thickens. It is what lets the detached stone-rinds lean forward another finger’s width each decade. It is the reason the quiet is never perfect: somewhere, always, a film of moisture is deciding to run, or a submerged course is groaning through a shift no thicker than a breath, or a pale length is tasting a column and finding that the column now tastes of it in return. This life does not need witnesses. It does not need names. It has been becoming itself in the dark for longer than the idea of visitors, and it will go on—water rising through stone, distance folding, heat blooming from nowhere—while any description of it peels away, softens, and runs down some leaning wall into the warm black pools that have already begun to recreate it.
