The ones who live here do not arrive. They are already inside the work, the way moisture is already inside the courses. You notice them first as interruptions in the permanent dusk: a denser darkness that shifts, a place where the slow sheets of air abruptly eddy, the wet slap of clay gaining a second, softer rhythm that is breath. Then the voices—short, practiced, never wasted—measuring spans or warning of a shift in the packing. Only later, when your eyes have borrowed enough of the mineral glow or the smear of a fat-lamp, do the bodies resolve.

They are compact. Shoulders and hands dominate; the rest of the frame seems arranged to serve those two zones of force. Skin the color of the damp brick they handle all day, mottled where older scars have taken on the green-black of copper salts or the pale rind of constant wet. It is not quite flesh as other places know it. Pressed and abraded from birth against grit and slurry, it has grown a fine mineral grain. Under the fingertips it feels cooler than blood should allow, and slightly particulate, as if a thin slurry of the place has replaced the usual oils. Hair is sparse, plastered in dark commas against scalp and forearms, or burned away in patches near the rare hearths. Eyes sit deep and small, lenses adjusted to layered grays; they do not shine, but in very low light a faint chalky reflection sometimes answers the phosphors, like the inside of a shell. When they look at you the gaze is steady and unhurried, already calculating load-bearing angles.

They move in single file along the braided passages or in small hinged teams at the open courts. Everything is done at the speed of muscle and wet material. A line of them will pass baskets of still-plastic clay hand to hand up a rising wall while another group, lower down, rams the previous course with mauls whose heads are river stones bound in scavenged sinew. The work has no clock. It pauses when the fat-lamps gutter or when a cold-sink court grows too numb to grip tools, then resumes without discussion. Children (there are children) appear first as smaller silhouettes carrying water or sorting shards; their hands are already thickened, their speech already reduced to the necessary phrases. No one marks age in years. Bodies are read by the density of scar and the particular list of a spine that has lifted too many green-copper plates.

They eat what the place reluctantly yields. The pale worm-things are gathered when they uncoil from weep-holes, snapped, and pressed into cakes with a binder of ground bone and the colorless mineral gels that thicken on north faces. Larger cavern-bodied creatures are rare windfalls; every part is taken—fat for the lamps, sinew for bindings, bone for the reinforcing courses that keep the deeper vaults from settling too fast. Sometimes the simplest films arrange themselves into raised patterns that look briefly like script or map; the builders step around these, as they step around the cold-sinks, but they do not harvest them. To touch is to dissolve the pattern, and something in the quiet etiquette of the work forbids it. Hunger is real but paced to the same slow metabolism as the masonry. They sleep in short, overlapping shifts in the half-roofed basins where the black water keeps a steadier temperature, bodies curled against one another for the meager shared warmth, skin to skin already coated in the same fine dust.

Their dead are not removed. When breath stops, the body is stripped of anything still useful—tools, the last soft fats, any copper ornaments hammered thin as nail-parings—then broken at the major joints and set into the next course of wall or floor. The bones vanish into the fabric as deliberate aggregate, exactly as the description of the place has always stated. No ceremony beyond the practical: a short call that the span is ready for packing, the wet slap of fresh clay closing over femur and rib. In this way the lowlands remember every generation as compression strength. Dig and you find them, older and deeper, the marrow long since replaced by denser ramming. The living do not speak of this as sacrifice or continuum. It is simply how the work remains upright.

They are not uniform. Some districts favor taller vaults and produce builders with longer reach and a characteristic lean; others, low and water-heavy, breed shorter frames and nearly webbed toes for the constant cisterns. A few individuals carry the oily scent of the lamps more strongly than others and are left to tend the phosphorescent clays, nursing the faint glows that seep from certain fired faces. These lamp-tenders move more slowly, speak even less, and sometimes stare into the mineral light until their eyes film over with the same chalky reflection. Others excel at hearing the deep granular complaints of settling stone and can predict a shift half a day before the grinding begins; they walk ahead of the teams, bare palms on the walls, reading vibration. Strangeness is noted but not pursued. When a corridor walked twice yields different lengths, they simply choose the length that fits the clay they have mixed that day. When sounds arrive from directions that have no openings, they lower their voices and wait until the stone finishes speaking. They treat the place the way the place treats them: as ongoing fact.

Language is local and dense with measurements. A single syllable can mean “the runnel needs a deeper cut before the next vertical curtain” or “the timber brace is failing two levels above.” Names, when used, are short and attached to function or scar: Drift, Ram, Weep, Copper-knee. They do not tell stories of origin. The fabric has never been otherwise, so the question does not arise. Yet in the cool dark between work bouts, when the water percussion and the wind-reeds fill every joint, some of them sit with their backs to unfinished arches and let their hands move idly in the dust, tracing geometrical figures that match no current plan. These figures are left for the next shift to pack over or ignore. Occasionally the mineral gels form similar figures later, unbidden. No one claims the correspondence.

They keep extending the lowlands because the lowlands keep extending them. One pressed brick, one fitted slab, one careful channel at a time, bodies and walls exchanging substance until it is no longer clear which is scaffold and which is flesh. In the permanent dusk their eyes adjust, or they do not; either way they continue, listening to the loud conversation of water and stone and labor, adding themselves to it without remainder.
