The ones who belong here do not arrive. They precipitate.

They begin as a thickness in the water itself, a slight reluctance of the mineral film to stay inert. In the shallowest pans, where the exudation is newest and the glare most absolute, the film darkens by fractions—no green, never green, but a smoky quartz clouding that gathers into translucent thumbnails. These are the first bodies: crustaceans only by the convenience of an older tongue. Each is a living lens of chitin and salt, flexed around a gut that is mostly filter and crystal lattice. They graze by lying open. The fresh sinter settles across their backs; they close, poach the minerals in a brief internal fever, crack the depleted shell outward, and glitter once as they sink again. Generations turn over in the time it takes a droplet to form and fall. Their cast skins sink into the porcelain layers and become the next grazing surface. Nothing of them is wasted except the brief flash that gives them away.

From this constant harvest larger anatomies condense. The form-walkers are herds only in the loosest sense—more like slow weather fronts of appetite. Low, many-legged, their plating is not grown so much as recruited: the same ochre and iron-black crusts that armor the ledges are persuaded, year by year, to take the shape of a back, a flank, a grinding palate. Inside the armor the true animal is a long, segmented pressure of muscle and memory, cooler than the rock by careful degrees. They move by deciding which legs will soften their mineral sleeves enough to bend. The sound is the sound of cool metal thinking about warmth. Mouthparts work like slow mills, scraping thicker crusts into paste, mixing it with a secretion that keeps the paste from sintering again until it is safely inside. They travel the wet highways in loose constellations, never hurrying, never quite stopping. When two herds meet they do not contest; they interpenetrate, individuals swapping places like thoughts crossing in a single mind, then continue, each body carrying a slightly altered ratio of salts. Over decades a single walker may become mostly geological, its living core reduced to a dark, hot thread that still remembers direction.

In the deeper, hotter pools the bellows live. They are less animals than slow events—membranous chambers the color of old milk glass, anchored by root-like mineral tendrils that drink both heat and dissolved metal. A bellows inhales the superheated water through a lattice of fine tubes, holds it in a central volume where symbiotic crusts and threads perform an occult chemistry, then exhales a cooler, enriched stream. The exhalation is deliberate, almost musical in its restraint: a long diminishing pressure that other lives read as current and follow. Whole processions of walkers will alter course by a few degrees to walk the cooler plume. Winged things stall above it, tasting the altered mist. The bellows themselves never leave their pools; they enlarge or dwindle according to the seasons of heat that rise from the rock beneath. Some have grown so large their upper surfaces break the water and become new ledges, already silvered with the first biocrusts. Engineers of the city have learned to set their intake pipes near mature bellows; the creatures do not seem to mind, or perhaps they have long since incorporated the pipes into their own circulatory logic.

Higher, where the mist thins into glare, the winged hang. They are the closest thing the basin has to suddenness, and even they refuse it. Bodies like elongated spearheads, partly metallic, faceted in ways that scatter the white light into brief prismatic wounds. Wings—if they are wings—are vanes of sintered foil and stretched membrane that catch thermals too subtle for any instrument. They can remain motionless for hours, each one a dark asterisk against the glow, then fold and fall in a silence so complete it feels like the air forgetting them. What they seek on the descent is not flesh but gradient: the momentary difference between one mineral breath and the next. They scrape a fine dust from the backs of walkers, from the rims of bellows, from the cooling towers of the factories, and carry it upward again to process in the thinner upper mist. Their droppings are dense black flakes that spiral down and become the iron-dark strata of tomorrow’s ledges. No one has ever seen them mate or die. It is possible they are not many individuals but one dispersed attention, sampling the basin the way a tongue samples a tooth.

And then there are the suited ones—the residual people.

They move through the hush like afterthoughts of an earlier ambition. Heat-reflective suits the color of the brighter sinter, faces hidden behind polarized slits that turn the glare into a manageable gray. They speak in short bursts over short-range links; the thick air eats longer sentences. Their work is endless and gentle: measuring the undercutting of stilts, driving new ceramic piles, scraping salt blooms from turbine housings before the blooms become load-bearing, mapping the slow migration of the form-walker herds so that slurry channels can be rerouted without offense. Some have been here so long the suits are no longer purely external. Mineral threads find the seams. Cooling circuits begin to look like twin circulatory systems. In certain older workers the distinction between person and apparatus softens until a raised hand leaves a faint white print of evaporite on the air, quickly reabsorbed. They do not raise children here. The heat and the silence make new lives feel like interruptions. Instead they maintain, and in maintaining they are themselves maintained by the same paced economy that governs every other metabolism.

Yet the true inhabitants may be none of these separately.

Walk the graded ways at the hour when a steam-front drifts down from the caldera rim and the hard light softens. The factories tick and flex. The pans mirror nothing because there is nothing above them but denser white. In that contracted world it becomes possible to feel the single continuous life that uses crustacean and walker and bellows and wing and suited engineer as temporary organs. The city is not built upon the basin; the basin is dreaming a city as one more mineral form—districts like larger hermit-crab shells, canals that are simply deeper seep-lines disciplined by concrete for a while, power houses that teach the rock’s own heat to turn in useful circles before rejoining the mist. When a wall becomes half masonry and half sinter, when a pipe vanishes into a bellows and emerges as pure current, when a walker’s plating begins to show the faint regular geometry of an access hatch left unattended too many seasons, the dream is simply continuing.

At night—though night is only a cooler white—the basin glows with its own slow fever. The suited crews retreat to pressurized dormitories cut into the higher cliffs. The herds settle into still constellations. The winged become unmoving points of darker silver. And the water keeps writing soft additions onto every surface, the way a body under fever writes new versions of itself without ever waking. What lives here is that writing. Everything else is handwriting.
