The Underskip does not host a single people. It hosts a gradient of hungers that have learned the lattice the way lungs learn air.

Begin with the silver fusiforms, because they are the weather that eats. They are not true fish. Their bodies are tapering sleeves of living foil, each one a continuous muscle wrapped around a cartilaginous pressure spine. Scales are absent; instead the skin is a microscopic bristle field that traps a thin laminar sheet of water and rides it, so that an entire school can change vector without turbulence, turning like a single thought. Their eyes are simple black pits rimmed with bioluminescent rings that flare only when the school decides to panic. In the flat white of the industrial lamps they look almost decorative—sheets of living mercury pouring between struts—until a larger shadow crosses them. Then the rings ignite in cascading waves and the school folds itself into a dense, glittering knot that races down an access trunk faster than any unassisted swimmer. They feed on the bacterial film that sheets every surface, rasping it free with ribbon tongues that leave bright metal scars. They have no individual names; the lattice’s residual machine tongues call them simply “shear.”

Hunting them are the greys. Torpedo bodies roughly three meters long, matte hull plating that still carries faded chevrons and hazard striping, as though factory paint crystallized into scale. Their jaws are not bone but nested reciprocating shears scavenged, long ago, from the same industrial vocabulary that built the place. They do not chase so much as intercept. A grey will hang motionless in a shear zone for hours, camouflage patterning blanked to the exact albedo of the local ferrocrete, until a school’s leading edge commits to a corridor. Then the grey’s lateral thrusters—fleshy nozzles that vent superheated water—kick once, and it is already inside the school, jaws cycling. The leftover silver confetti drifts for days. Greys are solitary except when mating; then two will press vent-to-vent along a warm effluent pipe and remain locked for a tidal cycle while smaller scavengers clean the blood from their flanks. Their young are born live and already striped, expelled as miniature versions that immediately seek breaker boxes or turbine housings to claim as dens.

Larger still are the vehicle-crabs. The lattice has no true benthos, so these creatures carry their own. Each is a dome of chitin-alloy laminate six to eight meters across, propelled by twelve jointed walking limbs whose tips end in magnetic claws. They graze the same bacterial pastures as the fusiforms, but where the schools skim, the crabs scuttle and scrape, leaving behind polished metal that gleams like new welds for a few days before the film returns. Their mouths are ventral grinders that can pulp filter-tubes, old cable, even the softer alloy struts if the crab is desperate. They communicate by tapping: complex foghorn rhythms conducted through the lattice itself, so that a conversation begun in one cell can still be heard, attenuated, three cells away. When two crabs meet at a contested heat exchanger they rear, display the fluorescent breeding patches under their limbs, and either withdraw or lock claws until one is levered into open water and must swim—awkwardly, furiously—to the next spar. Empty carapaces are prized dens for other things.

The filter-tubes themselves are barely animal. Pale, rubbery, sparsely branched, they hang like neglected wiring under every horizontal member. Each tube is a colony of polyps that have surrendered individuality to a shared hydrostatic skeleton. They taste copper and current; when an effluent plume rich in metal salts passes, the whole forest inflates and feeds, pulsing slowly. They are the closest thing the Underskip has to trees, and like trees they are mostly ignored until something larger decides to harvest them for nesting material or emergency calorie.

Then there are the things that use the dark between lamps.

An eel-form longer than a freight keel will sometimes pass through a major void, its body a continuous undulating ribbon of muscle sheathed in light-absorbent skin. It does not hunt the greys; it hunts the greys’ hunting grounds, inhaling entire schools in a single peristaltic wave. Its passage is announced minutes in advance by the sudden silence of every smaller creature and by the deep flex of the lattice as displacement pressure rolls ahead of it. No one has seen its head clearly. Survivors report only a vertical split that keeps opening.

Rarer are the membrane-jellies: colonial intelligences that have fused thousands of bells into a single pulsing organ fifty meters across. They glow with a cold, shifting auroral light generated by symbiotic bacteria living in their mesoglea. The light is not for attraction; it is a distributed nervous system, patterns of color carrying computational weight. A membrane-jelly can solve the local current maps faster than any residual machine core, and it uses that knowledge to station itself at the mouths of still-active pump trunks, filtering the outrush for anything edible—including the occasional greys caught mid-acceleration. When two membranes meet they do not fight; they briefly overlap, exchange genetics and memory in a ripple of impossible colors, then drift apart larger and slightly wiser.

And then the scavengers that wear the dead machines.

In the half-flooded manufactory nodules, luminous shrimp the length of a forearm nest inside breaker boxes, their bodies staged with stolen fiber-optic filaments so that they spark and stutter like failing indicator lights. They are not mimicking the machines; they have simply discovered that looking like a fault draws fewer predators. Deeper in the same bays, adult greys have been known to sleep inside the barrels of silent turbines, perfectly circular, waiting. Automated arms still try to weld, their last gestures now brushing useless sparks across crab carapaces that have claimed the welding jigs as thrones.

There are others, less frequent, less classifiable. A drifting constellation of hard-shelled mites that harvest rivets and leave behind perfectly sized holes. A soft, transparent predation sack that inflates around a school and digests it in place, then deflates into a glass-like film that becomes someone else’s nest lining. Once, something that looked like a length of flexible trackway uncoiled from a ballast tunnel, revealed ranks of grinding teeth along its inner surface, and re-coiled around an entire vehicle-crab before the crab could finish tapping an alarm.

No single intelligence claims the Underskip. What lives here is a living critique of the unfinished build: everything that can metabolize cold water, metal salts, and the low thunder of engines that will not quite die. They do not name themselves. They do not need to. The lattice registers their masses, their sounds, their heat blooms against the residual sensors that still function. In the machine tongues that occasionally flicker across dead displays, they appear only as density anomalies, as vibration signatures, as the reason the pumps must sometimes surge to clear a clogged intake.

They are the Underskip’s true weather now—the part that swims, and clicks, and waits with jaws that once were tools.
