The ones who live here do not arrive. They precipitate.

At first you notice only denser currents—sinews of water that refuse to shear apart when the surrounding torrent demands it. They hold their shape for the length of a long breath, then longer. A thickening. A refusal. What gathers itself inside that refusal is neither flesh nor fluid but a third state the basin permits only to those who have forgotten every other home.

They are long. Not in the way of eels or kelp, but in the way a thought can be long when it has no shoreline. Seven times the height of a standing body, sometimes more, their forms taper slightly at either end so that either direction may serve as forward. The surface of them is a continuous, slow roar of micro-turbulence; dark pewter like the light, slick as the underside of a wave just before it turns itself inside out. When a shear-current strikes them they do not tumble. They hinge. Segments of their length become momentarily gel while others thin into weighted fog, and by this controlled partial dissolving they step through the violence the way a hand steps through a bead curtain.

They have no faces. What they have instead are galleries of pressure. Along the flanks, staggered spirals of membranous pits open and close in patterns too rapid for eye-light; each pit tastes the water’s density, its warmth, the faint mineral signatures of basalt freshly cut and obsidian still cooling. Through these they know the basin the way lungs know air—completely, involuntarily, without interval. When two of them pass within a body’s length, the pits along one side flare in unison and a low, sub-audible chord moves between them, felt more in the teeth than heard. It is not language. It is agreement about the next unfolding of the water.

Sometimes an entire being will collapse itself into one of the rare spherical air pockets. Inside the sudden muffling they hang coiled, almost still, and the phosphorescent mosses on the micro-ledges brighten in answer, casting a brief grid of green-black constellations across the curve of their body. In those suspended moments you can see that they are hollow without being empty. Veins of slower water run the length of them like roots grown in zero gravity, pulsing with a heat that is slightly above the blood-warmth of the torrent. At the core of the coil something denser turns—an elongated knot of mineral and memory, smooth as the polished lintels, shot through with hairline fractures that glow the same patient phosphorus as the moss. That knot is the only part that never dissolves. It is older than the present shape of the basin. It remembers the stone arcs when they still stood above water, when the mountains were still undecided about their height.

They do not eat the ribbon-creatures that sometimes tear past, already fraying. They do not eat the ferns. What they take from the place is the place’s own violence. Each time a fresh spire of glass-sharp rock lances upward and shears apart into black sand, one or more of them will feverishly braid themselves through the collapsing helix, gathering the mineral incoherence into their outer layers. For a few heartbeats their surfaces bristle with fleeting crystallizations—obsidian needles, basalt flakes—before the warms oftens everything back into sleek continuity. The internalized chaos becomes the fuel for their next refusal to come apart. In this way the basin’s relentless creation-and-erasure is folded inward and kept.

They are not many. At any true moment you would count fewer than a dozen full lengths within the nave-shaped volume, yet the number is unstable because they can braid. Two will meet in a downdraft and spiral through each other, surfaces foaming into a single thicker current that carries both dense knots side by side; later the braid may part into three, or into one that is longer than either parent. Identity here is a weather, not a boundary. The stone thrimitives—the low arcs, the solitary lintel—still bear the scraped signatures of earlier braidings, when the beings were heavier, more rock-true, when they still believed the basin could be terraced and kept. Those attempts have been polished into anonymity, but the beings themselves have not abandoned the geometry. They will pause in the lee of a half-swallowed wall and press their length along it, pits wide open, listening to the memory of fitted stone. Sometimes they add a single new block—lifted from a shearing ridge, rotated until its facets forget which way they broke free, then settled into place with a tenderness that seems impossible inside the detonation of sound. The block will last only days or hours before the water reclaims it. They continue anyway. The act is not construction. It is recollection performed with the body.

Gravity leans; they lean with it. When “down” becomes diagonal they simply regard the new direction as another current to be hinged through. When distance double-exposes, a single being may occupy both far and near until the illusion collapses, leaving a brief afterimage of itself still traveling the abandoned path—an afterimage that occasionally detaches and swims off as a shorter, unfinished sibling before dissolving into ordinary rain. Reality’s unreliability is their native climate. They do not suffer it. They metabolize it.

Once in a great while the deepest tectonic groan rolls through, the mountains shrugging another interval older. All of them stop. Every pit flares at once. The entire population, however braided or solitary, orients toward the unseen ceiling of vapor and becomes perfectly, impossibly still while the water continues to thrash through and around them. In that stillness the pewter light brightens by a single impossible shade, and the dense knots at their centers can be seen to turn in perfect synchrony, as though answering a ground-pulse older than the torrent itself. Then the moment passes. The hingeing resumes. The white detonation closes over everything again.

They have no name for themselves that could be shouted through the roar. They do not leave the basin. To leave would require becoming singular forever, a shape that cannot unshape, and that is a kind of death they do not recognize. Instead they continue—lengthening, braiding, gathering black sand and lost geometry, pressing themselves to the memory of walls they once believed could last. The warm rain stands and thrashes. The young mountains keep rising. And inside the lung-like nave that is never dry and never silent, the long refusals keep precipitating out of the water that made then, holding their form against the endless instruction to come apart, becoming more themselves with every collapse they survive.
