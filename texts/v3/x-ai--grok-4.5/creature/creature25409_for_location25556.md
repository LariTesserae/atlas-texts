The ones who live here do not arrive.

They are not migrants who crossed from wetter latitudes and learned thrift. They are not the long-descended children of some earlier wet-world overflow. Whatever first sparked inside the closed loops of the resinex has simply never left. Over spans that make stone look impetuous they have thinned, clarified, and tuned themselves to the Fold’s single unending note: hold every molecule, waste nothing, move only when the light or the pressure gradient insists.

At first glance you might miss them entirely. Against the blue-green columns they register as slight thickenings, or as places where the prismatic haze seems to hesitate. Their bodies are built on the same thrifty chemistry as the groves. A flexible residual matrix holds colonies of photosynthetic endosymbionts that drink the hard ultraviolet directly; the visible remainder scatters through their translucent dermal vanes in the same soft halos the trees cast. Standing among them is like watching cooled flame decide, for a while, to walk.

They are tall when they choose to be—elongate, jointed in ways that let them fold into the hanging curtain roots and become indistinguishable from them. At other times they compress, becoming low multi-limbed drifts that flow across the mineral pavement at the same geological crawl as the keratin-snails. Speed is a rare indulgence and always expensive. Most of their lives generate almost no heat signature; what little they must radiate is dumped in brief, controlled pulses during the coldest pre-dawn, when the frost beads form and can carry the excess away without loss.

They do not eat in any way a lowland mind would recognize. Inside the oldest groves they unfurl long, membranous fans that brush the moss-analogs and the undersides of the vanes. Microbial films, stray spores, the faint organic dust that even this thrifty place sheds—these are collected, lysed, and shared through temporary anastomoses. A group that has not touched in decades may still be exchanging metabolites through the shared canopy, a slow communal bloodstream conducted by wind and condensation. When a lattice-winged glider dies and drifts down, or when a keratin-snail finally stills after its decade-long crossing, the event is treated as a festival of scarcer elements. The body is not torn; it is petitioned. Soft colonial processes—kin to the dust elegies, but mobile and deliberate—invest it, dissolve it over weeks, and distribute the liberated minerals as luminous paste that each participant paints across their own vanes. For a short season afterward the grove shines with extra refractions, private auroras under the hard white sun.

They have no names for themselves that settle into sound. The vanes of the resinex already sing too high for ordinary hearing; the dwellers simply add harmonics. When wind passes through a populated grove the sternum-vibration deepens, acquires overtones that feel like recognition, like countable presence. A solitary drift crossing open stone will sometimes stop and unfurl, catching the slope wind so that its own body becomes a temporary instrument. The chord it sounds is both location and lineage: I am the continuation of historical water-loop 14-that-once-touched-the-southern-flexure, currently three kelvin cooler than equilibrium, carrying surplus iron paste from the last snail. Other navigators, hundreds of kilometers distant, adjust their own routes by those felt intervals.

Memory is architectural rather than neural. Each individual grows thin internal lattices of silica and residual organics, layered like tree rings but readable as interference patterns when light is correctly angled through the body. Elders—those whose loops have circulated for millennia—are semi-transparent libraries. Younger ones will gently pin them against a sunlit vane and read the caustics that crawl across the pavement: entire topographic histories of which mesas rose, which groves contracted, which dust-elegy colonies once migrated and then chose to stay. There is no myth of a wetter past. The lattices record only the Fold’s own breathing, the slow rise and fall of crust, the centuries when grit polished a particular amphitheater into perfect acoustic curves. They remember the place becoming more itself, and they remember themselves becoming more like the place.

Reproduction is rare and combinatorial. When two or more loops find their water chemistry and mineral loads sufficiently complementary, they may braid curtain-root extensions together for a local year. Cells migrate, genomes cross-map without hurry, and eventually a new, smaller lattice detaches—already containing endosymbionts, already tuned to the ultraviolet, already carrying gifts of paste and pattern from every parent. The new one does not cry or seek milk. It simply begins the long work of thickening its own vanes and deciding whether to remain in the natal grove or drift toward the next archipelago of green. Some never leave. Some cross open stone for centuries, pausing as living cairns through seasons of dust-sheet wind, until a new grove’s microclimate claims them.

They are not lonely. Loneliness would require a different density of self. Instead there is a vast, low-grade awareness of every other loop still circulating across the Fold. Pressure gradients and shared dust carry miasmas of information: a change in the frequency of glider wings means a thermal has strengthened over the western scarps; a sudden richness in snail trails announces that a pavement’s mineral crust has bloomed after some subtle flexure brought deeper elements near the surface. The dwellers incorporate these facts the way the resinex incorporate frost beads—silently, completely, without drama.

Once in a great while something like art happens. In certain alignments, when floating lenses of overlapping halos magnify distant mesas, a group will climb the highest available columns and arrange their bodies into living apertures. They hold the pose for days, allowing the concentrated light to etch temporary geometries into the silica lattices of the younger ones. These are not messages. They are simply the Fold looking at itself through the only eyes it has grown, and the dwellers are willing—being made of the same thrift and the same brilliance—to serve as temporary lens and temporary retina. Afterward they disperse, each carrying a new caustic pattern somewhere inside, a private refraction that will slowly surface in the way they fold, the harmonics they add to the wind, the precise shade of surplus mineral they choose to paint across their vanes next time a snail completes its crossing.

There are no tools because the body already is every necessary tool, refined across ages until the distinction collapsed. There are no ruins because every structure is alive and still drinking the ultraviolet. They do not anticipate an end to the Fold, nor do they remember a beginning. They continue, the same way the high crust continues its imperceptible breathing, the same way the closed water loops have circulated inside the same cortex for centuries. Cool, luminous, sparsely extravagant. When you stand inside a grove and feel the vanes tremble at frequencies just below hearing, some of that tremor is them—adjusting, sharing, reading the light that braids between columns older than language. They are the Fold’s way of walking through itself without ever leaving. 

And if you remain long enough, quiet enough, one may unfold near you: a tall clarity of blue-green and prismatic scatter, regarding you with the mild curiosity of a place that finished its arguments with entropy long ago and now simply notices whatever else has managed to climb this high and live.
