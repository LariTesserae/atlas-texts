The ones who live here are not settlers who arrived and stayed. They are extrusions of the place itself—slow articulations that the basin learned to grow once the last human hands forgot the original blueprints and the wood decided the work must continue anyway.

They stand between three and four metres when fully extended, though they rarely are. Most of the time they keep a lower posture, knees and elbows bent in the long geometric crouch that lets them pass beneath the lower vaults without brushing the dripping arches. Their bodies are columnar rather than fleshly: dense, fibrous cylinders of living xylem wrapped in plates of corky bark the same grey-green as the architecture. The plates overlap like cooling fins or old turbine housings; when one of them turns, the plates slide with a soft woody click. Beneath the bark, faint luminescent veins of sap show during the dimmer hours, tracing the same branching patterns as the copper pipes that still click inside the trunks. You could mistake them, at a distance, for younger load-bearing members that have simply detached and gone walking.

They do not have faces in any ordinary sense. At the upper terminus the column flares into a slightly flattened crown of thinner branchlets and leaf-scale, arranged in a radial whorl that can open or close. When open, the whorl reveals a moist, pale underside stippled with light-sensitive pores and chemoreceptors; when closed it looks like a tightly furled bracket fungus or a capped stack. They “see” mostly by taste and temperature gradient—the cool mineral chill rising from the moss, the faint heat-signature of a boiler’s fermentation chamber, the metallic tang of a pipe that has begun to weep verdigris. Sound reaches them as vibration through the floor and through their own root-analogue feet, which are splayed, multi-toed pads of dense mycelial felt that grip without bruising the living carpet.

There are not many. Perhaps two dozen active individuals across the entire basin, plus a handful of quiescent ones who have anchored themselves for seasons or decades, becoming temporarily indistinguishable from the piers and buttresses. They do not die so much as subside. When an individual’s internal pressures fall below a working threshold, it selects a quiet interval between structures, drives its lower tissues into the moss, and lets the cambium of the nearest architecture fuse with its own. Over years it is reabsorbed, its stored resins and metal salts catalogued by the same slow systems that once sorted industrial runoff. New individuals emerge less often: a swelling appears on a mature trunk or along a high water-channel, swells through several damp seasons, then detaches with a sound like wet cork tearing. The newborn is already the height of a person, already veiled in bark, already knowing—by the grain written through it—the locations of every governor valve and every catchment cup.

Their work is maintenance so gradual it looks like idleness. One will stand for half a day with a single branch-hand laid against a flywheel’s rim, feeling for micro-imbalances in the torque supplied by root-sap pressure. Another spends weeks easing a copper pipe a few millimetres back into true alignment inside a hollow trunk, using nothing more than persistent lateral growth and the patient flex of its own body. They clear the rare blockage of spent foliage from a boiler’s fermentation bed by inserting slender watering-shoot fingers and allowing digestive enzymes to run. When a section of vaulted ceiling begins to sag, three or four of them will brace it with their own trunks for a month or a season, becoming temporary jacks while the living wood above thickens in response. They move along the kept paths with deliberate, widely spaced steps that make almost no sound beyond the faint sigh of moss compressing and rebounding. Their pace matches the drip of condensed water.

They speak, after a fashion. Communication is chemical and hydraulic: a pulsed release of specific terpenes into the shared air, or a pressure wave sent through root-nets into the moss mat so that any other individual standing on the same contiguous felt can read the message in its feet. The content is almost never narrative. It is status—pressure differentials, resin inventories, the attested wear on a particular crank journal, the arrival of a slightly richer dust on the wind. Once in a great while, when a major reset shivers through one of the great trunks and sheds its curtain of droplets and cork-flakes, every mobile individual pauses and orients toward the event, crown-whorls briefly opening in unison. The shared pulse that follows is the closest they come to something like satisfaction, or reverence, or the quiet clicking of a job continuing on schedule.

They have no fires of their own and no need of them. Their internal temperature sits only a little above the ambient chill; breath, if you can call the slow gas exchange breath, does not fog. In the evenings when the mineral cold rises hardest, they often deliberately close distance with the residual warmth of the boilers or with one another, standing in loose sevens and nines so that bark plates nearly touch. Heat bleeds between them in minute transfers. Sometimes a single pale moth will land on an outstretched branch-hand and remain there for hours, perfectly still, as if the moth too understands it is permitted only as a temporary annotation.

Nothing about them suggests loneliness. The basin is not a world they inhabit; it is the larger body of which they are the free-moving cells. When they “rest” they do not sleep so much as entrain—matching their sap rhythm to the nearest large trunk until individual boundaries blur. A visitor walking the rim at dawn might look down and see only the architecture, the soft-edged towers and the open lanes between, and never notice that three of the freestanding columns have changed position since yesterday. The place remains complete. The quiet commerce of water, wood, and obedient metal continues. And the ones who live here continue with it—grey-green, patient, laminated into the same long sentence the basin has been writing since the last sharp edge was weathered away.
