The dwellers are never first seen entire.  

What registers, long before any outline hardens, is a slow rearrangement of the wet air itself: a denser column of heat that does not tremble quite the same way as the sheets around it, a place where the miniature rainbows bend and then hold a new, secondary spectrum for several breaths. Only after the eye has accepted that the light can fold does a silhouette begin to declare itself against the shadowless glare.

They are tall, but not by much—half again the height of the reeds when fully upright, which they seldom are. More often they keep a deep, deliberate crouch that brings their faces level with the permanent white cups of the flowers. Their skin, if the word still applies, is the same dense black as the basalt, yet it does not shine; moisture does not bead on them so much as sink in and travel beneath the surface in visible, branching capillaries of brighter wet. When they straighten, those channels catch the sourceless light and throw momentary prismatic flares that die as soon as the posture settles. The edges of their limbs remain knife-true, never abraded, as though whatever force regularly refuses weathering to the blocks has extended the same courtesy to them.

They move with the same refusal of hurry that governs the crabs and the hanging fish. A single step may take the length of many ordinary breaths; the foot—broad, toeless, the sole faintly cupped—descends onto the rubbery leaf mats without sound and without compression. Where bare stone is required, the foot leaves no print, yet the air above the place remembers the passage: a vertical smear of cooler moisture that hangs, handprint-like, before slowly re-absorbing into the general fever. Their hands are long, the fingers fused into two flexible lobes that can splay into a flat paddle or curl into a precise wedge. With these they adjust. That is the clearest verb. They do not build now; they adjust. A reed that has leaned a fraction out of its allotted circle is guided back. A joint between basalt blocks that has somehow admitted one extra molecule of drift is stroked until the seam forgets how. A silver fish that has drifted half its body-length too close to the surface is pressed gently downward again with the flat of a lobe. Each correction restores the previous perfection so completely that the correction itself becomes invisible a moment later.

Their faces are the last surfaces to resolve. There are no eyes in the expected places—only long, vertical seams that open and close with the same languid rhythm as the fishes’ gills. Along each seam runs a continuous row of pale, iridescent spots that catch the light and refract it inward. What they see is not light as we know it but the interruptions in the light’s constancy: the places where heat thickens, where a reflection has begun to show the underside of a block, where a vanishing point has started to argue with its neighbors. When two of them face one another the seams widen slightly and a low, velvety thrum passes between them—the same thrum that sometimes travels through the stone beds—before both seams narrow again. No mouth is visible. Moisture simply leaves their skin in the same slow slides that travel the vertical faces of the blocks and vanishes before reaching the next joint. They neither eat nor drink in any way that would disturb the balance; whatever sustenance they require appears to be taken directly from the permanent film of wetness and the unmoving heat, the way the reed roots drink without piercing.

They are not numerous. Across any given expanse of the plain one might count five, or seven, or none. Distance is unreliable here, and the non-Euclidean stubbornness of the channels means that the same figure may be encountered twice without either traveler having turned around. When two paths cross, the dwellers pause. Sometimes they touch lobe to lobe, and in that contact the air between them holds a more complex shape—an architectural ghost of platforms and courts that do not yet exist, or that have always existed, or that the stone will be persuaded to remember. Then they separate and continue their independent tours of adjustment.

At longer intervals something larger occurs. Several will converge on one of the sunken courts whose chisel marks remain perfectly parallel. They descend the dry-stacked steps that batter gently inward, and there, in the bright wet silence, they perform the only action that might still be called making. From the tools that lie exactly where they were last set down—bone levers swollen with permanent damp, flint adzes that have never dulled—they select what is needed. The work is almost motionless: a lever eased beneath a block that no eye could have sworn was out of true, a single tap of swollen mallet against stone that sounds more like a drop of water striking its own mirror. When they finish, the court is unchanged to any external measure, yet the light sits differently inside it, and the reflections in the surrounding pools now show, for a while, the court from angles that do not exist above the plain. Then the tools are returned to their precise former places, the dwellers climb out, and the quiet reseals.

They do not sleep. They do not age. The young edges of their own bodies never soften, just as the blocks never weather. Occasionally one of them will stand for a very long time on a cane platform beside the motionless pale-green herons, posture identical to the birds’, seams half-open to the glare. In those hours the thrum gathered from many throats at once may roll outward through the basalt for great distances, a single sustained note of contentment or of warning or of pure equilibrium. When it ceases, the fish hang a fraction deeper, the crabs freeze mid-click even more completely, and the flowers hold their condensate drops with renewed, crystalline attention.

What they are, beyond the sum of these actions, resists a single name. They are the memory of the crowbars and antler wedges that first dressed the basalt, still walking. They are the heat deciding to grow limbs and correct itself. They are the place’s refusal of time given slow, wet, black-skinned form. Stand long enough among the reed circles and you may feel one pass within an arm’s reach; the denser column of air brushes you, the handprint of cooler moisture rises from your skin and drifts upward without breeze, and for a short while your own edges feel unnervingly true, as though weathering has been postponed in you as well. Then the presence continues on its unhurried circuit, the rainbows realign, and the bright, impossible present closes over the moment as if it had never admitted interruption.

They will still be doing this when the horizon itself has been walked around and found to be only another straight channel leading back to the same grove, the same frozen crab, the same open white flower holding its single drop forever.
