They are the ones who stayed when the high country finished forgetting itself.

Not first people, not last—just the current thickness of bodies the crease can support. Generations have worn their shapes against the same stone the wind rounded above, so that standing among them you cannot always tell where a calf muscle ends and a polished foothold begins. Their skin holds the same mineral stain as the walls: a dull copper-olive that darkens in the humid months and pales to ash-salt when the fog forgets to come. Hair is kept short or bound tight against the constant soft assault of wings; what grows is wiry, the color of dry resin smoke, and often threaded with the same gray-green lace that hangs from the trunks, living ornaments that drink a little of the wearer’s vapor and give back a faint cooling.

They are small by the old measures of the plateau—compact torsos, long pulley-arms, feet whose soles have become half-callus, half-sense organ. Toes spread wider than the hand’s fingers, gripping bark and ledge with independent strength. Children are born with that spread already pronounced; the first games are hanging contests from the lowest trained arches while moths explode around them. Eyes sit deep, protected by heavy lids and a fine constant squint that never quite leaves even in sleep. Pupils drink the spent green light greedily; after a lifetime many no longer tolerate the raw slit of sky and move under woven leaf-shades when work forces them near the rim. Their voices are mid-pitched and quick, trained to cut through drone and boom without shouting. A whole conversation can happen in the clipped route-calls you hear rising through the terraces, each syllable timed to a foothold or a hand-pass of load.

They do not name themselves as a nation. When they speak of “we” it means the current density in the pocket—the living arrangement of people, jointed stems, amphibia pulse, fly-cloud, and the thin bright memory of water. Outsiders (rare, usually lost or trading copper scraps for resin and dried lace) are simply “thin ones” or “sky-eyed.” Kinship is reckoned by shared platforms and by who has breathed the same fog-night air in the netted alcoves. A child belongs first to the ledge it can cross alone, then to the work-group that feeds it, then to the wider weave of the crease. Old ones whose joints finally refuse the ladders become the slow heart of the lower terraces: they sit braiding fiber that never stops needing replacement, teaching the exact pressure that bruises a water-cortex, humming the amphibia’s pulse back to the mud so the young remember which songs are conversation and which are warning.

Bodies are mapped with the same thrift as the settlement. A woman’s hip may carry the pale scar-lattices of repeated resin burns from years of fire-tending; a man’s forearm will show the parallel nicks of vine-cutting and the smoother patches where a favorite six-limbed runner has learned to perch. Teeth are strong, slightly brown from chewing the waxy outer skin of the copper trunks for its slow sugars and for the way it stills thirst. Many have a permanently crooked left shoulder—the side that braces against the wall when two pass on a narrow root-bridge. Hands are identical in their roughness until you look closer: fiber-workers develop a glossy pad at the base of each finger; scoop-carriers grow a broad, shovel-like spread; the rare copper-workers (needles, edge-blades) keep one thumbnail pristine and wickedly tapered.

They dress in the pocket’s own cast-offs. Loincloths and brief chest wraps of beaten fiber softened by spit and fog, dyed with the dark juice of crushed epiphytes into mottled patterns that break up outline against leaf-shadow. Belts of seed-husk chain hold bone awls, resin cakes, the precious copper needle in its thorn-sheath. Children go naked until their skin toughens enough to need protection from the abrasive trunks; then they receive the first apron and the first netted sleeping sling in the same quiet ceremony of smoke and boom-signals. Jewelry is living or once-living: a mating-swarm wing iridescent and stiff, a polished amphibia tooth, a coil of the thin snake’s shed skin worn until it crumbles. No one owns more than they can carry up three terraces at a run when a platform sags or a fire slips.

Work is not separate from breath. At the first bead of condensation the walls bloom with people, scoops out, absorbent mats already unrolling like tongues. They move in the practiced single file even when fog-blind, calling the live roots, shifting weight so the cortex stays unbruised. Later the same hands beat fiber clean, graft new living braces into a failing arch, pick insects from the broad leaves for the midday paste, smash the hard-winged flies into oil that keeps the worse biters off. Everything edible is tasted first by someone whose belly is known to be wise; everything useful is tested against the oldest memory of what the crease will allow. They keep no herds—the six-limbed runners are too clever and too much cousins—but they maintain caches the runners half-respect, trading fruit for the glittering beetles the runners extract from deep bark. The amphibia are never eaten after the singing starts; beforehand, sometimes, if the fog has failed too long and the cistern stems ring hollow.

At dim-time the pocket draws inward. Netted alcoves bloom with quiet talk, the soft rhythmic work of repairing weave, the occasional low laugh when a moth finds its way inside and must be ushered out without tearing the dust-scales. Lovers share breath more carefully than liquid; every exhaled drop is noticed. Arguments happen in hiss and gesture so the larger noise is not troubled. When someone dies they are carried to the highest terrace the body can still grace and placed among the epiphytes with open hands; the crease finishes what the body began, turning it into the same dark working mass that feeds the rest. Names last only as long as living voices need them for stories; after that the person becomes another polished foothold, another trained arch that suddenly holds firmer.

They are not peaceful in the sentimental sense. Crowding makes tempers short; a spilled water mat or a bruised root can draw blood from knuckles. But the blood is always less important than the repair that follows, and the repair is performed by whoever is nearest, not by the wronged. Reputation is measured in how lightly one steps and how quickly one notices the sag before it fails. Heroes are almost invisible: the child who first feels the fog-tongue on their upper lip and strikes the cistern stem in the correct slow pattern; the elder who dreams the new graft that will take; the hunter who catches the transparent ambush-thing in a water-cup and moves it lower, where it will take more flies and fewer toes.

Stand long enough in the half-light and you begin to see them not as figures moving across the green but as the green’s own mobile density—another layer of waxy skin, another set of jointed stems that happen to sing in words, another flock that combs the air for what the air still carries. They have worn themselves into the most stable temporary arrangement that still allows growth. Above them the plateau continues its endless rounding. Inside the crease they keep deciding, loudly and together, what to do with the little they are given. And the little, for now, keeps answering.
