The ones who live here are not arrivals. They are the damp’s long memory given legs and hunger and the need to touch every surface that will hold them. They have been exhaling this same warm air for so many quiet centuries that the chamber has forgotten it was ever stone.

Begin with the smallest currents, the ones that never quite land. The sharp-winged pour through the mist like dark pollen given sudden purpose. Their bodies are no larger than a child’s thumb, yet the wing-cases meet with a dry, repeated clatter that becomes the chamber’s second pulse after the engines. Each pair of wings is veined in the same faint copper as the root-wire the machines spool; when light catches them they flash the tired amber of the thorn-caged bulbs and then vanish into blue-green afterimage. They do not flock so much as braid—streams splitting, braiding again, never colliding. Their mouths are soft and brush-like; they drink the oily film that sweats from cooling vanes and the sweet condensate that beads on freshly stamped leaf-leather. When a predator drops, the braid simply dissolves into a thousand separate vanishings, then knits itself half a gallery away as if the panic never happened. They sleep inside empty seed-husks that still hang from the higher vaults, dozens packed into each husk so that the husk itself seems to breathe with a glossy, collective shimmer.

Lower, where the moss gives underfoot, the grazers move. Soft. That is the first word that finds them and never leaves. The body is a loose sack of translucent muscle held in shape by seven or eight pairs of thin, jointed limbs that end in flat, suctioned pads. Fur is the wrong word; what covers them is a living velvet of tiny photosynthetic threads that drink the dim mycelial light and flush, now and then, with slow ripples of emerald. They are the size of well-fed housecats, but longer, lower, almost flowing. Mouths are valve-like slits ringed with fringed tentacles that constantly taste the air and the ground. The saliva they leave behind glows for a few heartbeats—pale blue constellations marking their meandering paths—before the moss drinks it and grows a shade thicker. They travel in loose, breathing herds of twenty or thirty, never hurried, stopping often to seal their mouths against a sap-weep or a cooling pipe. When the great engines thump harder, the herds press close and the velvet darkens, as if they are listening with their whole skins. Their droppings are neat pellets of compressed spore and resin that the floor-moss absorbs within an hour; nothing is wasted, not even the heat of their bodies. In the quieter hanging districts they pile together in living alcoves whose walls gently constrict and release, matching the rhythm of their soft, wet breathing. Sometimes a grazer will climb—slowly, almost dreamily—onto a low-turning bast belt and ride it for a few meters before dropping off again, as though the machine were simply another warm back.

Higher, using the galleries the way rivers use banks, move the elongated ones. Six eyes in two staggered rows, black and wet and always open. The face is narrow, intelligent without kindness, the jaws hinged far back so that the mouth can open into a dark, leaf-shaped cavern. Fur and bark share the body without argument: shoulders and spine plated in living cork that flakes and renews, flanks and belly covered in dense, rain-colored hair that sheds water in silver sheets. The limbs are too many and jointed in too many places; they move with a liquid, recursive grace, hands (if they are hands) unfolding into smaller hands that grip bark, vine, cable, or the impaled shell of a beetle with equal certainty. They speak in layered registers—clicks that travel through the wood itself, wet whistles that prefer mist, and deep drumming that makes the hollow trunks answer like struck drums. Their villages are the trellis-houses: woven platforms thickened by generations of discarded cork and resin until the floors are springy and faintly luminous. There they keep small gardens of the specialized mosses that also cool the machines, trimming them with careful claws, feeding them the rich black water drawn up through living tubes. They do not fear the flywheels; they time their crossings to the slowest arc of the belts, and young ones learn to leap the gaps by feel alone. At rest they fold impossibly small, cork plates overlapping like old fortress architecture, six eyes half-lidded but still tracking the blue stutter of mycelium far below. When a deeper pump-shift rolls through the chamber they freeze mid-gesture, every eye turning the same direction, as if the place itself has spoken a name only they remember.

And then the ones that drop.

The predators are rarer, heavier, and somehow lonelier. A body built around a single enormous hinged jaw, the rest arranged as afterthought—powerful haunches, a long balancing tail edged with stiff leaf-vanes, hide mottled in every shade the canopy casts so that stillness makes them vanish into living wallpaper. Eyes small, golden, set far forward. They do not hunt often; the chamber does not require frequent death. When they do fall it is with the sound of wet canvas tearing and the white roar that follows is almost ceremonial, a necessary thinning that keeps every other population sharp and sparse. After the kill they drag the body—grazer or clamberer or, once in a long while, one of their own—to a high crotch where resin drips thickest. There they feed slowly, methodically, while the luminous fungi begin to colonize the carcass almost before the last muscle stops. The predators themselves carry colonies: soft blue-green light pulses under the leaf-pattern skin along the belly and the inner thighs, brighter after feeding. They sleep stretched along the thickest ribs of the vault, stomachs to the wood, jaw hanging open so that smaller things can pick the teeth clean. No one mounts a watch against them. Their presence is simply one more slow season the others move through.

Between and among and inside all of these move the insects that never agreed to be secondary. Finger-long beetles with carapaces like polished machine parts stridulate until the air rings metallic; they fight over sap-flows and the oily excretions of the trunk-engines with pure, joyous fury, winners earning the right to nest in the warm crankcases where their larvae grow fat on grease and wood-pulp. There are moths whose wings are scraps of the same translucent leaf-leather the presses stamp; they lay eggs only on sheets still warm from the rollers. Transparent isopods the size of a palm live permanently in the condensate rills, grazing the iron-rich slime, their many legs stitching silver wakes. Even the luminous mycelium is half-animal some nights, sending up brief, blind fruiting bodies that crawl a few centimeters before settling into new light.

And then there are the parts that no longer choose a kingdom.

Some of the smaller engines have grown hearts. Muscular bladders swell and contract beside heartwood pistons; nerve-rootlets lace through bearings so that a sudden flush of sugar high in the canopy can make an entire production line race or languish. Cooling vanes wear permanent moss gardens whose tendrils open and close to regulate heat the way pores regulate sweat. Gear trains are groomed by dedicated beetles that have never seen daylight and would die of dryness if removed. The great trunk-engines themselves breathe—slow hydraulic sighs that lift and settle the nearest galleries like a sleeping chest. In return the animals treat the industrial cycles as weather: nesting in exhaust warmth, drinking from pipes, timing matings to the hours when the leaf-leather is softest. A few of the half-barked climbers have learned to urge a sluggish belt by pressing sugar-water into certain rootlets; they do it without ceremony, the way one might scratch an itch on a long-shared body.

Nothing here is alone and nothing is pure. A grazer will climb into the open maw of a resting predator to lick mineral salts from the teeth and neither will flinch. A swarm of sharp-wings will suddenly settle on the moving shoulders of a climber and ride, picking mites, before pouring off again in a single shimmering thought. The chamber’s own long breath moves through every lung and every stomata and every leaking valve. What lives here is a single damp consensus of flesh and fiber and low-voltage longing, endlessly repairing itself with the only materials it has ever needed: warm mist, slow sugar, the memory of motion, and the patient dark.

They do not name themselves. Names would suggest they arrived or might leave. They simply continue—thrumming, chewing, glowing, greasing, climbing, dropping, resealing—while fifty meters above the old floodplain forgets they were ever anything else.
