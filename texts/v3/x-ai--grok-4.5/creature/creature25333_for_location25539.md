The ones who live here do not have a name that travels. Outside the rim there is no one left who would use it, and inside the rim names are unnecessary. What they are is an arrangement that has been refining itself for longer than the basalt has held its present shape. They are the density. They are the soft, continuous pressure that keeps the stone warm from within.

At first the eye (or whatever passes for an eye in the poor light) registers only mass: a single dark tissue that rises and falls with a slower rhythm than breath. Then the mass resolves into directions of grain. Some bodies run lengthwise, thick as a man’s thigh and longer than any single stride, their surfaces segmented in dull irregular rings that slide over one another when they advance. These rope-forms do not crawl so much as persuade themselves forward, a wave of contraction that begins near the trailing end and travels the full length like a swallowed stone. Between them the lower traffic moves—broader, flatter creatures whose many legs are hidden beneath overlapping shields the color of old furnace clinker. The shields are not shed all at once; they erode in flakes, and the flakes become part of the dry mulch that sifts steadily toward the bowl’s lowest point. The plated ones climb by main force, hauling themselves over whatever living surface presents itself, indifferent to whether that surface belongs to their own kind. Their undersides are pale and faintly luminous with the heat they carry, pink-orange in the rare moments when one is forced onto its back and must right itself by slow, rocking labor.

Higher, and hanging, are the curtain-things. They fasten to the undersides of every overhang and to the steeper inner faces of the rim with hooked clusters that look, in silhouette, like knots of root. Their bodies drape in long, membranous folds, translucent enough that the dull internal fires of digestion show through as shifting bruises of light. When the press below grows too fierce they simply draw upward, folding themselves smaller, and the space they vacate is filled at once by whatever was waiting. Sometimes two or three of them will fuse along a shared edge for a time—whether in mating or in simple heat-exchange is impossible to know from the rim—and the joined curtain sways like a single organ.

Above even these, occupying the warm columns that rise from the living floor, stand the tall ones. They are fewer. Their lower halves are buried to the mid-section in the general crush; only the upper trunks and the broad, heat-catching spreads of their crowns are visible from outside. The crowns are not leaves. They are plates of the same dark, mineral-tough material as the bowl itself, veined with softer tissue that opens and closes microscopic vents. When a vent does open, a brief glow wells out—deep orange, almost black—and for that instant the surrounding creatures lean toward it the way cold hands lean toward a coal. Then the vent seals and the glowing stops. The tall ones rarely relocate. When they must, the entire neighborhood shifts in a long, grinding rotation that can take days, every smaller body forced to climb or be climbed over until the new equilibrium is found.

There are others, less easy to separate into kinds. Thread-thin borers that live inside the spent skins and the chalky residue of old carapaces, mining the last lipids. Soft, bag-like forms that inflate with the superheated air near the tall ones’ vents and drift a short distance before collapsing again into the press. Creatures that are mostly mouth, idle for long periods, then suddenly folding around a neighbor and remaining folded until the neighbor is no longer distinct. Predation here is not a drama of pursuit; it is a local rearrangement of mass, quiet as the rest. What is eaten is already warm and already smelling of the same sulfur and salt as the eater. Nothing is wasted long. The mulch at the bottom is worked by generations of smaller lives until even the oils are gone and only a fine, dark grit remains, which the next rainless decade will polish smoother still.

They feel one another constantly. Every surface that can sense pressure is pressed upon. Information moves as gradients of heat, as traces of excreted chemistry wiped across a flank, as the simple refusal of a body to yield space. There is no center to the conversation and no edge. A change in the isotherm near the western lip will, within hours, have altered the posture of animals on the opposite floor. Pairing happens the same way: two or more bodies remain in contact longer than traffic requires, chemical signatures shift, and later a third or fourth body, smaller and softer, is pressed out into the crush to take its place in the fabric. No one guards the young. The young are simply additional living volume.

The old built things have become part of the same grammar. A cairn is a slight rise that offers a different angle of rest; the incomplete arch is a cool (relatively cool) overhang where curtain-creatures hang thicker; the fragments of vessel or bone are only another texture beneath the nubs of crawling feet. No one among the living remembers what the shapes once meant, if they ever meant anything beyond the stubbornness of some earlier, less perfectly fitted tenancy. The present inhabitants have no need to make new marks. Their bodies are the only architecture required, and they rebuild it every hour by the simple fact of continuing to exist against one another.

From the rim the whole bowl seems to seethe, yet the motion is almost silent—a dry, endless rustle of plate on plate, hide on hide, the faint tick of cooling mineral wherever a body has rested too long in one exact spot. Stand there long enough and the heat climbs your own legs, borrowed from the multitude. The darkness stops being an absence and becomes a medium, the same medium that fills every lung or spiracle or diffusion surface down below. You begin to understand that the place is not inhabited by creatures. The place is the creatures, temporarily arranged as stone, as gradient, as bowl. What lives here is the arrangement itself: hot, dark, complete, and still gently, ceaselessly rearranging.
