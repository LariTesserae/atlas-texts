The high plateau is inhabited first by itself.

Not as metaphor. The continuous vegetal body is the primary resident—one organism or one slow nation of tissue, depending on the depth at which you cut the question. It does not think in any nervous sense. It budgets. It remembers load paths the way a river remembers its last flood, by the shape of the scars it leaves and then grows over. Across centuries the mass has learned to keep certain chambers at precise temperatures, to meter sugar solutions, to extrude hardened tips and root-caps the way a body extrudes nails. These workshops open like temporary organs and close again when the local geometry no longer requires them. No caretaker walks between them. The caretaker is the pressure differentials and the chemical gradients and the ancient habit of not dying when the wind shears a hundred meters of crown away. The plateau lives in a state of continuous, quiet self-surgery. That is its daily occupation.

Secondary lives move through this primary one the way corpuscles move through a body that has almost forgotten it has blood.

The felt-deer are the most obvious. They appear rarely enough that each sighting feels like a privilege granted by accident. Legs like spindles wrapped in dense gray down, bodies held close to the bark so that heat is never wasted on open air. Their pads are soft thick cups that grip without bruising; they leave no lasting print, only a momentary darker sheen where the living surface has been compressed and then slowly rebounds. They browse the newest epiphytic leaves and the soft white cambium exposed where a beam-branch has torn. They do not call to one another. They do not herd. Between appearances they seem to phase out of the available dimensions of the place, as if the forest can choose to metabolize them back into itself and later differentiate them again when some minor nutrient ledger requires mobile grazers. Watching one cross a causeway at dusk is like watching a half-formed recollection decide, briefly, to have hooves.

Deeper and slower are the dwellers that never fully leave the wood.

In the under-architecture—where collapsed titans have been riddled and shored by younger growth—there are lengths of vascular tissue that have swollen into long, segmented galleries. Inside these move the bole-worms: not worms exactly, more like ambulatory cambium. Soft, translucent, faintly green-gold, each the thickness of a human torso and many meters long. They travel by peristalsis, tasting the wood with their entire skin, thinning places that have become over-thick, laying down reinforcing lignin where micro-fractures are beginning. They are the forest’s circulating maintenance crews. When two meet head-on in a narrow gallery they simply merge for a time, exchange whatever chemical news they carry, then divide again and continue. Their waste is a nutrient paste that younger roots drink directly. They have no eyes. They do not need them. The difference between light and dark is only another gradient in a life composed entirely of gradients.

Higher, in the perpetually pearlescent mid-canopy, the curtain lichens have begun to develop opinions.

Some hang in sheets thick as winter blankets. Over geological time a few of these sheets have folded and refolded until they contain internal spaces—soft caves lined with their own reproductive surfaces. Within those caves live the lichen-ghosts: colonies that have learned to detach small free-moving fragments of themselves. Each fragment is a pale, slow flake the size of a hand, capable of crawling by the expansion and contraction of microscopic air bladders. They migrate across the great branch roads carrying packets of algal partners or fungal partners to districts that have lost one or the other. They are pollinators of a sort, except what they pollinate is the possibility of continued partnership. When the wind burden grows too great they re-anchor and become wall again. Consciousness, if the word applies, is distributed across the entire curtain; the walking flakes are only temporary verbs the curtain conjugates when it needs to reach beyond its own edges.

Then there are the things that denser language fails.

In certain super-trunks that rose and fell and rose again across ten thousand years, the heartwood has been replaced by a lacquered, dark material closer to amber than to timber. Inside that amber, suspended yet not dead, hang the Continuants. They are roughly humanoid only because the humanoid outline is a stable solution to certain mechanical problems—central mass, articulating limbs, sensory cluster at one end. Their surfaces are polished root and compressed leaf-mold. They do not breathe. They do not eat in any digestive sense. They are grown when the forest requires a mobile perspective that can last longer than a felt-deer and think further ahead than a bole-worm. A Continuant may walk for decades along the high causeways, testing tensions with the slow pressure of its palms, listening with its whole body to the subterranean hydraulic arguments of the plateau. When its information is complete it returns to a workshop chamber, sinks into the living wall, and is dismantled. The data it gathered is processed into new growth rules. Nothing of the individual is kept except the improvement. They are not souls. They are long-term measuring instruments the forest sometimes sculpts into a shape that can climb.

Above all of these, and within all of these, the true inhabitant remains the turbulence itself.

The plateau is never still. Overnight a buttress may shoulder itself three meters laterally, rearranging the skyline. A beam-branch may elect to die and fall away so that light can reach an understory that has waited in patient dusk for centuries. Entire amphitheaters of pale-barked giants may go into reproductive spasm and release clouds of cold, heavy pollen that drift like slow snow and germinate where they lodge, knitting new lateral bridges. These events are the forest’s thoughts. The rare animals and the softer parasites and the temporary Continuants are only the foam on those thoughts. Life here is not a collection of species occupying a habitat. Life is a single, cold, green-black engine that has learned to keep all its spare parts in motion inside itself, recycling every failure into the next load-bearing surface.

Stand still long enough and you feel the citizenship rules. You are welcome to pass through. You are not welcome to remain distinct. The moisture will begin to negotiate with your clothes. Spores will test your lungs. Rootlets will consider your warmth. The plateau does not hate. It simply has no category for a visitor that does not eventually become hardwood or humus or a new set of walking flakes. The cold preserves the negotiation, keeps it elegant, prevents fever. Everything that lives here lives as a temporary hesitation in an industrial process that has no product except the process’s own continuation.

And somewhere, always, a workshop is opening in the dark, preparing to extrude another precise and perishable tool.
