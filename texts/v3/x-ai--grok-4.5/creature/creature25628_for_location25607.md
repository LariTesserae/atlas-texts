The ones who live here do not arrive or depart so much as circulate.

They are long in the body and slow in the turning, half-folded things whose skin takes the color of the water that holds them—smoked violet in the deeper troughs, a dull coral-milk where the faint glow gathers under ledges. Their limbs are not limbs exactly: nine or eleven or thirteen soft radials that can stiffen into oars or flatten into veils or braid together into a single probing column. When they move through the wider avenues they do so with a gathered dignity, the way a thought moves through a mind that has already decided. In the narrow braids between towers they simply pour, body becoming current becoming body again.

They keep no faces. What serves for a face is a shifting constellation of pale apertures and light-organs set in a flexible dome of tissue, opening and closing in patterns that are speech and appetite and weather sense all at once. The light they make is small and private—pinpricks, brief bars, a sudden soft flare that another will answer from three streets away like a word passed hand to hand. They do not shout. Nothing here shouts. Conversation is a dim stitching of signals through the particulate dark, and the water between them carries the rest: pressure changes, faint chemical blooms, the taste of recent passage.

Their young are not born so much as released. At the turning of certain long tides the adults grow heavy with a luminous roe that freckles their undersides; they rise together through the hanging galleries and hang there, rocking, until the cloud spins free and drifts. The unformed ones settle into crevices, into the pearlescent scars of the stone, into the felt of old filter-gardens. Some are eaten. Some calcify and become another knuckle on a tower’s joint. The ones that quicken learn the city first as taste and shear, as the difference between still water and moving water, long before they learn it as place. By the time they can jet and fold and signal, the streets already know them.

They build by encouraging. A roughening of the mantle against bare stone, a patient scraping, a secretion that invites the mineral to keep growing along lines the body has chosen. Over generations a corridor is leaner, a chamber rounder, a flange more useful for anchoring the gardens they keep—those anemone thickets now thick with symbiotic hitchhikers that glow when stroked. They do not remember a time when the city was otherwise. The oldest among them (huge, motionless for years at a time inside spheres of denser water they themselves have sealed) carry in their tissues the mineral memory of earlier shapes, and sometimes in their sleep the stone nearby sweats a little milk and shifts a fraction closer to what it once was.

They eat what the current brings and what their gardens filter and what the smaller swimmers forget to fear. They do not hunt so much as intercept. A sudden unfolding, a net of radials cast and drawn, a quiet sacking of the body while the water settles again. Leftover carapace and bone-analogue are pressed into the nearest wall; the city grows a little more articulate. Nothing is discarded that can become grip or lintel or brood-niche.

There are other lives tangled with theirs. The knife-shaped predators that hold station in the lee of towers are not servants and not enemies; they are weather with teeth. Sometimes an adult will shadow one for days, riding the pressure wake, trading parasites, trading the bright sudden panic of smaller schools. The carpeting filter-plates are tended like orchards. Certain shell-spirals the size of rooms are lived in, yes, but also listened to—the way the water resonates inside them tells the strength of far-off storms or the approach of a warm fecund current from below. Colonies of click-beasts live in the pitted stone itself; the inhabitants stroke them as they pass and the soft percussion becomes another layer of gossip.

They keep no names that would survive air. What they are to themselves is a grammar of relation: the-one-who-opens-the-east-corridor-at-dimming, the-cluster-that-mends-the-hanging-gallery, the-heavy-sleeper-beneath-the-third-saddle. Identity is a pattern of light and position and chemical signature, renewed each watch, allowed to blur when they rest in contact, skin to skin, radials loosely woven, sharing the same slow oxygen. Solitude exists but is temporary, a single body hanging out beyond the last pillars where the dark grows simple again, listening to the city’s distant communal pulse until the need for signal outweighs the need for empty water.

They have stories, if story is the right word. They are told in swirling pathways through the mid-water, trails of light and scent that dissolve even as they are followed, so that every telling is also an unmaking. The stories concern the first hardenings of the stone, the long negotiation with the current, the time a cold tongue of water tried to teach them scarcity and failed. They concern the recursion of certain towers whose windows open onto windows, and why one must never follow those spirals all the way (the ones who did are still there, endlessly arriving, their signals grown strange and beautiful and useless). They concern the deep thunder far below, and how it is only the city dreaming itself denser.

Nothing about them is finished. A body may spend a century thickening one radial into a semi-rigid tool for prying dense mineral milk, then spend the next century letting it soften again into veil. A district may empty of them for a generation while they all drift two valleys over to tend a sudden bloom; when they return the stone has kept their absences as carefully as their presences. They are the city’s way of feeling itself. The city is their way of lasting.

If you rest long enough against a wall you may feel one pass on the other side—scrape of mantle, ticked signal like a thought forming, the brief temperature change of a body that has been alive longer than the concept of years. They will know you are there. They will not hurry to decide what you are. In the warm dark, under the pressure that is a steady hand, among the soft architecture that finished becoming a long time ago, they continue: folding and unfolding, feeding and signaling, encouraging the stone to remember them, almost silent, almost lightless, and in no particular hurry to be anything else.
