They are few. You could count them in an afternoon if they all chose to stand still, which they almost never do at the same time. The bowl holds them the way a cupped hand holds rain: loosely, with room for the water to move.

At first you notice only the traces. Long, narrow footprints stamped into the mineral grit, the toes slightly splayed, the dampness of each print already being claimed by the ever-present film of water so that the outline silverizes and then vanishes. Scrapes on the fresher stone where someone has dragged a bundle of the jointed cane. A hearth still warm beneath its perforated cap-stone, the ash mixed with powdered rhyolite so that it locks into a pale cement overnight and must be cracked open again each morning. Tiny, deliberate stacks of moss cushions left drying on slate shelves under the overhangs—food, or bedding, or both. The smell that lingers in the low doorways is mineral and green: wet iron, crushed fern, the faint sweetness of cane sap.

Then you see one of them, and the image does not lock into place all at once. It arrives the way the light does here—cool, sliding, revising itself.

They are human-shaped in the broad grammar of limbs and upright carriage, yet the proportions have been edited by the place. Torsos are compact, dense, as if the thin air and the constant cold have pressed them inward for warmth and efficiency. Legs are longer than the trunks suggest, flexible at ankle and knee for crossing slick stone without hurry. Arms hang with a loose readiness; the hands are broad, fingers blunt and ridged with callus that feels like fine sandstone when brushed accidentally in passing. They go barefoot. The soles have grown a pebbled leather of their own, pale where the rock is pale, darker in the basalt reaches, permanently damp.

Skin is the clearest signature. It begins smooth enough in the young, a cool greige that can flush faintly with effort or cold. But it does not stay smooth. The mist and the mineral milk work on it the same way they work on the cliffs. Over seasons a soft grit incorporates itself; the surface takes on a subtle polish in places and a chalky bloom in others. Some of the older ones wear thin, translucent lattices across the shoulders or along the collarbones—fragile honeycombs of the same stuff that ribs the weeping faces—cultivated or simply tolerated, brushed to a dull shine with thumb and spit. When they stand very still in the mist you can lose their outline against the stone for a breath or two. They do not seem to mind.

Hair is optional and rare. What grows is fine, almost lichenous, the color of dried moss or wet ash. Many keep their scalps bare or wrapped in a single twist of cane-fiber cloth. Faces are open, high-cheeked, the eyes set deep and colored like the pools: clear gray, weak tea, occasionally a startling mineral blue. The eyes do not water in the perpetual damp; they seem made for it. When one of them looks at you the gaze is steady and unhurried, as if measuring how long you intend to remain a feature of the landscape.

They dress in what the bowl gives. Beaten sheets of the jointed cane, pounded until the fibers lace into a flexible mat, then smoked over the low hearths until waterproof. These are cut into simple wrap-skirts, sleeveless tunics, hooded stoles that can be drawn against the thicker cloud. Edges are bound with twisted root or stitched with copper that has been hammered so thin it is closer to foil than metal. Nothing is dyed; color comes from the material itself and from the slow stain of iron-rich seepage. Children wear almost nothing until their skin begins to take its permanent grain. The old sometimes add a second layer of living moss across the shoulders, kept moist by the air, worn like a stole of deep emerald or rust until it yellows and is returned to the cushions.

They move the way the water moves: not slow from weakness but from preference. Each step is placed, tested, committed. On the radial avenues they walk alone more often than together, yet the spacing feels companionable rather than solitary—the same courteous distance the shrubs keep from one another. When two paths cross there is a brief touch of fingers to the inner wrist, a gesture that leaves a momentary wet print and seems to exchange more than greeting. Voices are soft by habit, yet the bowl lifts them; a question asked on the upper terrace arrives whole at the plaza basin. Speech itself is built of long vowels and gentle consonants that mimic drip and trickle. They sing more than they converse. The songs have no obvious audience. A person will stand at their own doorway at the hour when the light goes flat and gray, and the melody will travel the channels until every house has heard it whether it wished to or not. The songs concern settling stone, the patience of moss, the brief mistaken ambition of water that climbs. No one translates them for outsiders. The bowl itself seems to complete the verses.

Houses are occupied lightly. A sleeping pallet of layered cane and moss, a low hearth, a few tools with handles of the same jointed wood and heads of flaked basalt or hammered copper. Extremely little else. Personal objects tend to be half-found, half-made: a drinking cup that began as a naturally pitted rhyolite nodule, a scraper still carrying the cortex of its parent boulder. Every dwelling has its private thread of water led under the threshold in a shallow groove so that the sound is continuous. They sleep to it. They wake to it. Newborns are washed in it before they are washed in anything else, and the mineral scent stays in the soft places of their bodies for life.

Food is gathered rather than grown, though the distinction blurs. Moss cushions are harvested in rotation, never stripped bare; the deep emerald ones taste bright and astringent, the rust-red ones denser, almost fatty after long chewing. Fern croziers are taken in their first uncurling, eaten raw or wilted on hot stones. The cane yields a starchy pith once the outer joints are cracked. In the older depressions a black paste of cyanobacteria is skimmed and dried into cakes that keep. Soft white grubs appear sometimes inside decaying cane; these are roasted and considered a kindness from the place. No one herds. No one hunts the rare kite. Meat is the smell of a story told about somewhere lower down the mountain, not a memory.

Children arrive infrequently and grow the way the walls grow—course by course, across years that would feel extravagant elsewhere. A child of fifteen still has the unfinished look of new fracture: edges sharp, skin bright, movements abrupt. By forty the polish has begun. By ninety most have acquired at least a partial mineral lace somewhere on the body and the characteristic stillness that makes them easy to miss against a cliff. They do not appear to fear age. Aging is simply the process by which the bowl finishes the sentences it started when they were born.

They understand the mild wrongnesses as weather. When the echo returns late they pause and let it finish before speaking again. When the plaza water briefly reverses they step across the uphill run without comment, wiping the damp from their ankles afterward as Tai from any other channel. Some of the older individuals can persuade the water to make that error longer; they crouch at the basin’s heart and hum a particular low note until the surface tautens and creeps outward against gravity for a dozen heartbeats. They use the extended moment to set small offerings—single fern tips, a twist of hair, a copper rivet—onto the climbing film so that the gifts are carried a little way toward the mountain before the ordinary downhill fire resumes. No doctrine surrounds the practice. It is simply one more conversation with unfinished forces.

Their sense of time is the bowl’s sense. Plans are measured in the advance of moss, the rounding of a single outcrop, the lifting of the entire basin by a finger’s width. A wall may be raised by three courses in a lifetime and considered excellent progress. When a house is abandoned—because its people have walked up into the perpetual cloud and not returned, or because the weeping behind it has grown too insistent—the structure is left open. Moss moves in. Ferns colonize the sleep-shelf. In another generation someone else may clear the skeleton and begin again, packing the old joints with new green mortar. Nothing is demolished. The city accretes.

They are not native in the simple sense. Stories—sung, never written—say the first of them arrived when the bowl was still a quarrel of steam and raw blocks, following the water uphill like everything else that wanted a new place. The mountain did not shape them so much as offer continuous suggestion; they accepted most of it. Other stories imply the reverse: that the bowl opened because something like them was already sleeping underneath and needed weather. Both versions are told with the same gentle lack of urgency. Origin is simply the farthest terrace.

There is a quiet generosity in how they occupy the space. Empty avenues stay empty so the water may run and the echoes may practice. Couches of moss are left unharvested so the cyanobacteria may continue their argument. When you have stayed long enough that your own boots begin to leave prints they recognize, someone will appear at dusk with a length of dry cane-fiber cloth and a cake of the black paste, set them on the stone near you without ceremony, and withdraw. The gift is not friendship exactly. It is acknowledgment that you, too, are now one of the unfinished things the bowl is weathering.

At night—when the cloud lowers and the roar of water softens to a hiss that seems to rise from the bones of the earth—they gather sometimes on the terraces. Not all of them, never a crowd. Just enough to make a constellation of small hearths. They do not speak much. They listen to the hollow knock of settling blocks, the glacial groan of the floor, the cane clicking its dry vocabulary. Occasionally one will answer with a single hummed note that rides the bowl’s curve and comes back altered. In those moments it is difficult to tell whether the people are living in the place or the place is living, briefly and carefully, inside the people.

They are still becoming whatever they are. So is the stone. So is the water that cannot decide whether to fall or climb. The bowl holds them all with the same unfinished courtesy.
