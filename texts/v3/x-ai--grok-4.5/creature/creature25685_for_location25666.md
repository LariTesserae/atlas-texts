The first impressions are pressure and displacement. Whatever lives here announces itself less as silhouette than as a change in the resistance of the warm dark, a thickening or a parting that your own body registers before thought does. You feel them as rival volumes, patient and numerous, rearranging the wet. Only gradually do distinctions accrete—temperature gradients, textures against the skin of the knee or the back of the hand, the faint electrolytic kiss when two claims on the same cubic meter brush and recover.

Near the bottom layer, where mud remembers every earlier rain and has not yet chosen which to keep, the braiders work. They are long, segmented more by habit than by anatomy, their bodies a sequence of slow peristaltic decisions. Skin like cooled paraffin over denser jelly; the outer surface continually sloughs microscopic films that the water accepts without ripple. They do not swim so much as revise their own length, gathering coil, releasing coil, stitching themselves through the substrate in patterns that look random until you watch for days and realize the braids are open-ended glyphs, rewriting the drainage. Digestion is external and seasonal: a braider will drape itself across a cluster of the cartilage-stems, exude a clear enzyme that tastes (if you were foolish enough) of cold batteries and sugar, and then simply wait while the stems soften into nutrient slurry. The process can last a local autumn. Nothing hurries them. When two braiders meet they neither fight nor merge; they braid around each other for a time, exchange a few segments as casually as conversation, and continue, slightly altered, slightly more the same. Their knowing is entirely tactile and hydraulic. Eyes would be an impertinence.

Higher, at the level where a human hip might travel, the heat-blooms move. These are the ones whose passage you feel as a brief, animal warmth, a pocket of metabolism traveling through the grease-dark. Their shapes are soft approximations of quadruped or multilimbed things, never quite fixed; folds of translucent mantle can open into temporary vanes or collapse into a single purposeful mass. Internally they run hotter than the ambient dark by just enough to matter. Blood (or whatever serves) is dark and slow and faintly luminescent only in the moment it is wounded—then the glow is swallowed again, unwilling to become light. They feed by envelopment: a smaller presence, a mat of the remembering-moss, even a length of still-warm cable left by the old industry, will be folded into a temporary stomach that is mostly decision rather than organ. You can watch the outlines of swallowed objects travel beneath the mantle for weeks, growing softer, simpler, until only warmth and a contented redistribution of mass remain. The heat-blooms are companionable without affection. They shoulder past one another constantly, and each contact leaves a thin transferable film that carries chemical news—where the water is fractionally sweeter, where a dynamo still turns and leaks a useful vibration, where the dark has recently folded and may fold again. Their migrations are basin-wide and nearly imperceptible, a gradual lean of thousands of warm bodies toward whatever lower discharge the land is still inventing.

Above these, drifting with that wet-silk sound, go the curtain-forms. They are less creature than mobile morphology of the water itself—sheets and bells and trailing veils that thicken from the ambient wet, hold a kind of distributed intention for a while, then thin back into indistinguishability. To touch one is to feel your own edges reconsidered; for a moment your skin forgets whether it is boundary or continuum. They appear to roseate around the industrial remnants, especially the half-sunk housings that remain faintly warm. Perhaps they feed on the last thermal differentials, or on the low electrical murmur that still climbs the insides of drowned casings. When a curtain-form drapes itself across a gantry or a truncated cooling tower, the metal briefly remembers purpose: a crane-arm may complete half a degree of its eternal lift; a shaft may catch and turn with something like relief before the curtain drifts on and the machine sinks back into its glacial private dream. The curtains leave no tracks, only a temporary silkiness in the dark that other inhabitants pause to sample, the way one might pause inside a sudden improvement in the weather.

There are rarer shapes. Once in a long wandering you may meet a landmarker: taller than the rest transcripts of flesh, stiffened in places with cartilaginous struts that echo the pale stems of the vegetation, as if they grew by the same hesitant logic. They stand for weeks in one place, root-analogues sunk into mud, upper arrangements slowly rotating the way the drowned dynamo shafts rotate—fractional, stubborn. Their patience is geological even by local standards. Around their stationary bulk the ordinary traffic eddies and reforms. Sometimes a landmarker will insert a limb or a probe into a length of exposed pipe and remain that way, communing with whatever residual whisper of current or empty instruction still threads the metal. When they finally move on they leave behind a vertical scar of cleaner, denser dark that takes a day to diffuse. Their purpose is illegible, yet the basin feels subtly re-calibrated after each long stationing, as if they are the ones who keep the unfinished draft from accidentally completing itself.

And then there are the ones that are hardest to separate from the medium. Call them fold-dwellers, or merely the dark’s own occasional decisions to clump. They exist most clearly when reality thins—when a pipe you already passed reappears dry and ringing, when a shadow peels free and drifts, when time stutters and a century tries to happen in your teeth. In those intervals something larger and less biologically polite leans through. It may wear temporary skins borrowed from heat-bloom or braider; it may be only a mobile density, a place where the metallic tang grows sharper and the warmth turns almost affectionate. Encounters are not predatory. More like being briefly included in a larger respiration. You feel your own memories rearrange slightly—old industrial words surface unbidden (tachometer, sluice, quench), then submerge again, rinsed. When the seam in the dark closes, what remains is a conviction that the basin is watched, or rather that watching and being are not yet separate verbs here.

No one among them is solitary in the ordinary sense. Even the landmarkers, locked in their weeks-long standing, are nodes in the same slow exchange of films, segments, thermal gossip, and borrowed industrial murmurs. Populations are not counted; they are densities that wax and thin with the basin’s own pulses of warmer wetness. Reproduction is seldom distinct from feeding or from the braiding of paths. A new heat-bloom may simply disentangle itself from the mantle of an elder after a long shared digestion. A curtain-form may tear, each half thickening into renewed intention. The cartilage-stems and the moss-that-remembers are tended almost accidentally, cropped and fertilized by the same traffic that moves through them, so that vegetation and fauna blur into one another at the edges—another unfinished boundary the place declines to resolve.

They do speaking rarely, and never with mouths. Meaning travels as altered pressure, as the gift of a warmer or cooler current, as the faint electric prickle modulated into pattern. On the longest timescales you can sense consensus forming: a general drift of bodies and almost-bodies toward a new sag in the land, a collective patience around some freshly exposed machine that has begun, ever so slightly, to hum with less futility than before. They are not building a future. They are maintaining the draft. Every slow assimilation, every fractional rotation of a buried shaft, every time a fold opens and closes, keeps the basin from coarsening into something finished and nameable. The dark stays absolute because they prefer it thick. The water stays undecided because their bodies are part of its indecision.

If you remain until your own edges begin to soften, until you catch yourself shouldering past a heat-bloom with the same unhurried courtesy, until a curtain-form drapes you and you taste the faint choir of extinct factory whistles as though they were childhood songs, then you may notice one further thing, living here almost too large to see. The basin itself is inhabited by its own continuing. All these forms—braid and bloom and curtain and landmark and fold—are less residents than organs of a single unfinished appetite that began when the ground first decided to sag, and that expresses itself equally as mudscarp, as weeping pipe, as patient flesh, as saturated dark. What lives here is the refusal to conclude. It wears ten thousand soft shapes so that it may keep testing, keep weaving, keep almost-turning the old machines one more useless, necessary degree. It has no name that holds. It does not need one. It is already busy becoming more itself, and it has all the time the rest of the world is busy spending.
