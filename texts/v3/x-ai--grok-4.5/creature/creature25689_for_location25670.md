The ones who endure here do not arrive all at once in the mind’s eye. They surface the way the fog does—first as a softening of outlines, then as weight, then as the accumulation of small necessities that have, over uncounted seasons, shaped them into something continuous with the decks and the cables and the breathing green.

At first they are only the warmth that lingers on a handrail after someone has passed, or the faint citrus-ozone scent that clings to cloth left drying on a high terrace. They are the soft scuff of bare feet on kilim-patched gratings at the hour when the heavier engines change pitch. They are the patient clipping of vine tendrils that have begun to fog a mirror-shaft, the low conversation held while waiting for a condensate cistern to finish filling, the unhurried way a body leans into the long sway of the structure when the wind presses hardest. Nothing abrupt. Everything already half-adjusted to the thin, pearled light and the permanent damp.

Slowly the forms clarify. They are bipedal, upright, but the uprightness has been negotiated with the place. Shoulders are a little narrower than the flatland norm, as if the body has learned to slip between cable stays and hanging planters without thinking. Arms are long for hauling, for steadying against a sudden diversion of fog-rain, for reaching the bolted access plates that must be checked by touch alone when mist thickens. Their hands show the same polished callus one finds on the centers of the oldest rails—skin burnished by generations of the same ropes, the same greasy governors, the same damp iron. Fingernails are often rimmed with the green-black of algae and crushed leaf; no one scrubs it entirely away. It is useful camouflage among the mossed joints and, more than that, a quiet badge of belonging.

Skin itself has taken the local light and humidity into account. On the highest terraces it is wind-cured and faintly lustrous, the color of old parchment left too near a heliostat. Lower down, in the fern galleries and the warm undercroft arcades, it softens and pales, picking up a greenish undertone from reflected chlorophyll and the perpetual thin film of moisture. There are scars, of course—small arcs on forearms where a tensioned cable once kissed too hard, pale coils on ankles from childhood brushes with the corkscrew antlers of the pale deer, the fine stippling that comes from years of breathing metal-and-green air. These marks are not hidden. They are read the way one reads the half-effaced numerals on a bulkhead: history that still carries load.

Hair is kept practical yet never austere. It holds whatever the air gives it—droplets, pollen, the occasional iridescent scale from a grazing insect—so that in bright hours a head may briefly dazzle like wet leaf. Some let it grow into heavy plaits that double as carrying straps for tools or for the small moss-lined baskets in which heartier fruits are moved from corridor to kitchen. Others crop it close and oil it with the same residual lubricants that keep the mill wheels quiet, so that it sheds fog the way the pale undersides of the louvers shed glare. Children run the terraces with uncut manes full of vine seeds; elders prefer the shaved scalp that lets them feel the exact direction of every shift in the wind.

They dress in layers that can be shed or added as the vertical climate demands. Cloth is woven from fibers the orchards and the hanging meadows themselves provide—tough cellulosic strands spun with threads drawn from retired belts, then dyed in the quiet mineral baths of the settling bowls. The resulting garments are the colors of the place after rain: oxidized red-browns, deep verdigris, the grey of wet basalt, the sudden white of untreated fleece from the denser-furred marmot-creatures that keep the drains open. Seams are reinforced with the same monofilament used to mouse cables. Pockets are deep and numerous, because one never knows when a pocketful of grease, or seed, or scaffolding pins will be required three decks away. At the wrists and ankles the fabric is left a little long so that it can be knotted tight against the wind or loosed to trail moisture into the finger-gutters. No one wears anything that cannot, at need, be used to patch a bellows or bind a wound or cradle an injured bird.

Their eyes have adapted in quiet, irreversible ways. Pupils open wider than they once did, drinking the pearled brightness without flinching; the irises themselves tend toward

the greys and ambers of weathered metal, though here and there a lineage still shows the dark soil-colors of whatever world first raised them. They can stare a long time into the dazzle of a sun-tracking louver and still pick out the silhouette of a goat-thing on a distant ledge. At night, when the great engines slow to their resting cycle and the only light comes from bioluminescent fungi cultivated along the undersides of decks, those same eyes gloss with a faint residual phosphorescence—enough to walk the familiar routes without lamp, enough to meet another’s gaze in the condensate tunnels and recognize kin.

They move as the place itself moves: almost never sudden. A crossing from windward escarpment to leeward bowl is planned like a tide. Elevators are trusted but never hurried; when the cars are full the people simply join the ladders and stairs that spiral through the root floors, pausing wherever a hanging meadow offers fruit or a flywheel hall offers warmth. Conversation unspools in the sameregister as the mechanical chorus—low, layered, intermittent with comfortable silences while everyone listens to a relief valve or a change in the bees’ thunder. Laughter, when it comes, is short and bright, like the ring of a mill wheel striking a stubborn piece of recycled decking. Arguments are rarer than fog-seasons and usually settle themselves once the parties have walked far enough that the horizon’s curve reminds them how small any single claim remains against the whole.

They eat what the structure gives and return what they cannot use. Breakfast is often fog-condensate boiled with the red-veined leaves that grow on rusted iron, sweetened by whatever night-blooming vine has dropped its apricot-scented blossoms onto the evaporation fields. Midday might be smoked flesh of the slow armored browsers (taken only when a shell is already failing and the mosses have begun to claim it), or the pale, hearing-blind fish of the warm tunnels, wrapped in fern and steamed over greenhouse flues. Evenings bring the denser breads baked from orchard mast and the oily seeds of vertical meadows, shared on long communal tables bolted directly to the deck so that when the whole edifice sways the bowls merely rock in place. Nothing is wasted. Peels and bones and spent oil go into the composting vaults that feed the residual-gas engines. Even the metal taste that never quite leaves the mouth is accepted as a mineral ration.

Children are raised by decks more than by blood alone. A toddler learns balance on the same rails that her great-grandmother once polished with her palms. By the time the child can climb she already knows which marmot-creatures will tolerate a gentle riding along the drainage slots and which ledges the pale deer prefer at dusk. The animals are not pets; they are co-tenants. A child who forgets this and pulls an antler too hard learns, once, the swift corrective kick that still leaves no lasting mark. In turn the deer sometimes appear at open dormitory hatches with a mouthful of clean moss if a fever is known to be moving through the hanging neighborhoods—no one taught them this; the place simply taught them all together.

Maintenance is not a profession. It is respiration. Every able body takes a turn walking the tensioned spans with a pot of grease and a pocket of replacement pins, listening with bare feet for the frequencies that mean a cable is ready to retire. When a deck slab finally begins its slow failure the nearest workshop empties, not in panic but with the calm of midwives who have attended this birth before. Moss is already at work; the people merely hurry it along with crushed aggregate and lasting, knowing the green will finish what they start. They speak of the structure in the first-person plural. *We are leaking a little more this decade on the third windward bank.* *We swayed farther than usual in the last long wind, but the counterweights remembered us.*

There are quiet oddities among them that match the place’s mild strangenesses. Some bloodlines dream in the deep thrum of the flywheel halls and wake already knowing which bearing will need grease before the next bright month. A few elders claim they can taste an approaching rain collar on the air hours before the netting darkens—an accidental gift of the same high-altitude chemistry that paints the upper gauges with ozone and apricot. Lovers sometimes meet in the warm steam of the forgotten mains, where the walls sweat clean water and the only light is the glow of their own residual eyes. No one cuts those mains. The heat is a kindness older than maps.

They do not name themselves after stars or gods or vanished continents. Their names are small and practical: a particular louver’s cadence, the pitch of a favored relief valve, the shape of a moss scar that once saved a life. When they speak of time they speak in crop-turns, cable-generations, the interval between one complete re-greasing of the great accumulators and the next. Memory is stored in the wear patterns of the place itself; a child walking a newly patched terrace can still feel, through her soles, the ghost path of the older brick-and-tie-rod route that once ran three degrees askew. History is simply the set of loads that have already found their resting shape.

Stay among them long enough and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat thins to a translucent membrane. Their voices join the single complex tone that visitors eventually sleep inside. Their breathing slows to the night cycle of the engines. Their dreams rock with the hand’s-breadth sway of kilometer-long spans. They are not stewards, not masters, not parasites. They are one more continuity—like the leaks that have become hydraulic balance, like the vines that have become soft walls, like the animals whose claws keep the drains open and whose deaths keep the vaults feeding the flames. They keep the place in the same slow good repair with which it keeps them, and in that mutual staying they have become, finally, what lives here: the living equilibrium itself, slightly upright, slightly scarred, permanently damp, and still, after longer than memory, refusing to be sudden.
