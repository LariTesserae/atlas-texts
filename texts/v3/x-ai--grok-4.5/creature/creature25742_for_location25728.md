The basin does not keep inhabitants the way a forest keeps deer or a tidal flat keeps crabs. Life here is a temporary loan, a leaning-in rather than a claim. Still, something leans.

It arrives the way the mist does—by seepage, not arrival. First you notice only that the air has grown fractionally heavier in one forest-court of tree-ferns, as if an extra thickness of moisture has chosen to loiter between the dripping crowns. The rushing of water seems to part around a silence too small to measure. Then a glitter of wet velvet that is not moss shifts on the underside of a frond. The shift is slower than any slug, more deliberate than drip. Whatever made it has the same patience as the mountain: it has been deciding to move for longer than the decision itself.

When it finally gathers enough of itself to be seen, it is never whole at once. A length of body the color of water that has stood too long on stone—slate-blue, iron-gray, the dull sheen of wet schist—unfolds from the base of a fern trunk. It is jointed loosely, like a starched cloth shaken out under water. The joints do not hinge so much as remember old folds. Four limbs, or six, or a continuum that finesses the difference, find purchase on the bare darkened rock. Each foot ends in a soft splay of pale pads that leave no print, only a brief darker dampness that the next bead of water erases. The body is longer than a human is tall when fully extended, yet it rarely extends fully; most of the time it travels in a low, snaking cluster, segments overlapping like petals closing against cold.

Its head is the last thing that commits to shape. For a long while there is only a tapering of the forward mass and two shallow depressions that might be eyes if eyes could be made of the same slow water that fills the pools. Then the depressions deepen, gain a liquid lens each, and regard the basin with the unblinking calm of something that has watched the same hour recycle for centuries. No mouth is visible until it feeds. Feeding is rare and quiet. It lowers the forward end into a warm pool and simply becomes continuous with the surface film. Water-striders dimple away; minute blister-shrimp and the larval ghosts of midges are drawn along unseen currents into that temporary seam. When the creature rises again the pool is no emptier than before, only cleaner, as if a penance has been performed rather than a meal taken.

It does not hunt the slate-colored walker you sometimes glimpse between trunks. That walker is its own smaller cousin, or its discarded skin still learning to walk independently, or perhaps the same being caught in a thinner phase of itself. They pass each other without acknowledgment, two densities of the same damp attention.

Skin, if the outer layer can be called skin, is never dry. It beads constantly, the mist condensing on it faster than on stone, so that the creature carries its own weather. Moss spores catch and germinate along its flanks in fine emerald stitchwork that is shed again within days, leaving faint green ghost-trails on the rock where it has rested. It rests often. Immobility is its chief occupation. It will half-submerge in a pool and match the temperature so exactly that only the wrongness of its reflected sky gives it away—the deeper blue, the unfamiliar slow stars—until a drop falls and both sky and creature ripple back into the common world.

Sound passes through it. The bright conversational rush of the weeping walls does not break against its sides; it continues, slightly modulated, as though the water has been given a longer throat for a moment. When wind moans across the open stone the creature leans into the note, lengthening its body along the path of the air until the submarine vibration finds a second, softer voice inside it. Then it shortens again, satisfied.

It has no need of shelter and takes none. Night and day are merely different thicknesses of the same pearled light; it moves under both or under neither. Time sticks to it. Watch one pale pad lift and you may find the light has leaped forward to late afternoon while the pad was still deciding whether to come down. Or the reverse: an entire slow uncurling of a new fern frond can occur between the beginning and end of a single step. The basin accepts this. Nothing else stays long enough to be troubled by it.

Occasionally—intervals measured in the sinking of fallen trunks—two or three of them will braid together in the center of the open stone. Bodies slide along bodies with the soft friction of wet silk. No purpose is obvious. They form a temporary knot of deeper damp, a small weather-system of their own, and the false constellations in the pools multiply and wheel. Then the braid loosens and each length returns to its separate dripping. If this is mating, or congress, or simply the overflow of a solitude too long sustained, the mountain does not say.

What it is named by, if it names itself at all, is not spoken in any register ears can keep. The closest the place comes to utterance is the way the layered hiss of water sometimes seems, for a few heartbeats, to articulate a low and elongated vowel—something between a coo and a stone rolling underwater—when the creature is near. The sound is gone before it can be memorized.

It will outlast any footprint you leave. It was here while the sandstone was still deciding how rounded it wished to become, and it will remain after the last tree-fern has surrendered to the moss. Yet it never claims. It only continues, a mobile thickening of the basin’s own eternal wetness, content to drip and listen and occasionally reflect a sky that has not happened yet. When it finally moves out of sight—down a seep, under a shelf, into the bright relentless conversation of water on stone—the air lightens by exactly the weight of one more silence returned to the mountain. The pools hold ordinary sky again. The liverworts shine. And the place goes on exhuding its fine mist, lush and sparse, loud and nearly empty, as though nothing had leaned in at all.
