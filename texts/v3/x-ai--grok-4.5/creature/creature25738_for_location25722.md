The first things you take for living are the ones that move most like ordinary animals, and even they do not move ordinarily. The blind fish turn in their bilge-colored schools with a patience that feels rehearsed across generations; each flash of flank is less a startle than a quiet insistence that silver still exists. The crabs work the seeps in overlapping shifts no clock could measure, clicking bits of mineral free, tasting them, rejecting or incorporating, their shell-edges already half-translucent with the same brass dusk that coats the lamps. The wading birds step as if the water might remember them if they hurry. Their legs are longer than hunger requires, jointed so finely they look assembled from spare gantry pins. When they spear, the motion is clean and final and then dissolves back into stillness so complete you forget the bird was ever anything but another angle of stone. Above, the leather-wings stitch the warm curtains of air into temporary corridors, never colliding, never truly landing; their bodies are sacks of heat and cartilage that smell, when one passes close, of damp iron filings and old yeast.

But these are only the brightest threads. Look longer—let the mist bead and run and bead again—and the denser citizens begin to differentiate themselves from the rock that drips them.

They are upright, after a fashion, though uprightness here is a negotiation. Their lower limbs have thickened and splayed into something between foot and root-pad, skin rough as unfinished basalt, already seeded with the black-green moss that never dries. They do not walk so much as re-anchor, each step a slow suction and release that leaves shallow, perfect molds filling at once with seepage. Their torsos are welded by habit to the ambient throttles and valves; many wear the corroded remnants of harnesses and tool-belts the way outcrops wear lichen, the metal freckled into their hide by decades of mineral sweat. Arms are long, jointed more times than bone should allow, ending in clusters of tactile filaments and blunt spades that can read the vibration of a compressor three galleries away or prise open a fresh hairline fracture to taste the water inside.

Faces are the last element to resolve. Eyes have sunk and multiplied into flat, phosphor-sensitive pits along the brow and cheek, enough to parse the brass gloom into usable gradients of heat and motion. Mouths are lipless seams that open mainly to exchange the thick air—iron, spore, the sweet-rot of the fungal plates—rather than to speak. When they do communicate it is through the water itself: low-frequency pulses sent along the boot-deep sheets, or by pressing a filament cluster to a weeping wall until the stone rings back in kind. Conversation at ordinary volume was always useless here; they abandoned it before memory began.

They are not many, yet the chamber feels peopled to bursting because each individual occupies volume the way the mist does—diffusing, overlapping, returning. A single figure may spend a week inching along a catwalk that ends in mid-air, not stranded but listening to the wall that calved away, waiting for the next clean face to finish opening so the work (always the work) can continue. Another hangs half-submerged beside a dredger whose engine still ticks through its seasonal cycle, filaments buried in the silt, monitoring the accumulation of fresh-broken basalt the way a heart monitors blood. They do not rush the machines and the machines do not rush them; both understand that teeth advance by fractions of degrees, that a hose pulse is a kind of breath measured in months. Sometimes a cluster of them gathers where a new joint is stressing, palms flat to the rock, simply bearing witness as the pressure-crack travels. Their presence does not prevent the opening—nothing prevents it—but when the thin black mouth finally weeps they are already there to taste the first water, to decide whether it will feed a sluice or a larval trough or the slow Circuits of the leather-wings.

Children—if the word still applies—are budded more than born, soft nodules of tissue and swallowed rust that detach when the parent’s body can no longer metabolize its own mineral load. They begin life translucent, almost indistinguishable from the pale reed-stems, drifting in the warmer upwelling until filaments harden and eyespots kindle. No one shepherds them. The place itself shepherds: the crabs teach density, the fish teach navigation without light, the endless reciprocating sigh of the untended generators teaches time. By the season a child has grown heavy enough to dimple the silt it already knows which levers must never be thrown too quickly and which fractures are worth waiting for.

They have no name for themselves that translates. In the pulse-language they are simply the ones who stayed when the ceiling was still deciding whether to be ceiling, the ones who learned to breathe yeast and iron until the distinction between lung and landscape blurred. Outsiders—on the rare occasions light-footed surveyors or scavengers manage the descent—mistake them for ruins or for elaborate growths of the same cork-moss that carpets the ledges. The mistake is useful. By the time the outsider’s lamp has swung long enough to notice an eye-pit watching back, the mist has already closed, the water has already erased the footprints, and the slow industrial breath continues, absolute, unperturbed.

Stand among them long enough and the boundary thins further. The mist beads on your lashes, a crab tests your boot, a filament brushes your wrist as if comparing pulse signatures. You feel the rock consider opening another mouth above you. Somewhere a gear advances one quiet tooth. The citizens do not object if you remain. They simply re-anchor a fraction closer, include your heat in the tapestry of the vents, and wait—without hurry, without doubt—for you to sprout moss of your own.
