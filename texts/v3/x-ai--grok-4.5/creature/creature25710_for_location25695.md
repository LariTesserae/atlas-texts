The pale ones are not visitors. They are the margin the place permits, and they have been pared to fit it exactly.

They move through the shallow floods as if the water were a second skin they have never fully entered or left. Low bodies, longer than a forearm and thinner than the wrist that might measure them, tapering at both ends into blunt suggestions of snout and tail. No fins, no limbs that persist—only long soft undulations that press against the mineral sediment and lift again, half swimming, half pouring themselves forward. Their skin is the precise grey-white of the wet limestone they resemble, matte when the light is kind, suddenly specular when a sheet of seepage catches the sun and flings it across them. Up close the surface is not smooth. It is minutely pitted, a living honeycomb that takes in water as readily as the rock does, so that each creature is always somewhat translucent, clouded from within by the same cold that aches human bone.

They do not travel so much as circulate. A pale length appears between two fluted pillars, glides through the ankle-deep glare, and is gone around a blade of stone; hours later the same shape, or one identical, returns from the opposite corridor. There is no herd and no solitude—only density without collision. When two meet they do not turn aside. They pass through each other’s space as smoke would, bodies deforming around the contact and reforming, leaving the water briefly milkier where they touched. Whether this is greeting, mating, indifference, or simple fluid mechanics is not a distinction the place encourages.

They feed on the only abundance that exists: the dissolved stone itself. Microscopic scrapers line the ventral side, invisible except as a faint iridescent shimmer when a body rolls. With each slow undulation they rasp the pale sediment, taking in the finest flour of calcium and iron and whatever slower minerals the weeping has freed. The surplus is exhaled again as the white sinter that bandages the lower rock—living paint laid down in the same unhurried sheets the water already uses. Over centuries a single pale one may thicken a pillar’s ankle by the width of a finger. They are the place finishing itself from within.

Eyes are absent, or have become something else. Along the flanks run flattened ovals of clearer tissue that do not track motion but register the pressure of light. In the hard noon glare these ovals cloudy over, protective; in the rare hours when mist thickens enough to dull the sun they clear and the creature lingers nearer the surface, as if listening with its skin to the difference. Hearing proper has been surrendered to the roar. The continuous white voice of a million seeps has become the baseline of their nervous system; silence would be the injury. When they rest—though rest is only a slower pour—they press themselves into the bowled hollows where water gathers before falling, and the vibration of every thread and sheet travels through them until creature and cascade are one circuit.

Reproduction is an event so infrequent it feels geological. Once in a long spanning of seasons—whose markers are unknown here—two bodies do not merely pass but remain overlaid, pulsing at the same unhurried rate as the falls. The water around them milks thoroughly and then clarifies, leaving behind a third pale length already the size of a finger joint. The new one is swept a short distance by the current, lodges in a vertical fluting, and begins its own scrapings almost at once. No childhood, no contrasting form. It is simply smaller, and then, without ceremony, it is not.

Higher, the dark flecks remain themselves and no more. They never descend. Whatever they are—membrane stretched on silicate struts, or something still leaner—they ride thermals that do not exist at this elevation yet somehow sustain them. Their shadows never touch the stone. They are less inhabitants than evidence that the sky, too, has a margin thin enough for life, and that the place tolerates it only by refusing it landing.

But the pale ones are the true measure. They do not hide; they simply match. Stand long enough in any corridor and one will pour past your ankles, indifferent to the heat you leak into its medium. Touch one—if you choose—and the skin yields like cold gel before springing back, leaving your fingertips filmed with mineral and a faint scent like wet slate and static. They do not startle. They do not learn you. You are weather of a briefly warmer kind, and weather here is only another equilibrium.

They have no calls, no territories, no seasons of hunger or plenty. Their span is the same closed loop as the water’s: rise by capillary seep, travel the flood, scrape, lay down stone, return. Time does not accumulate on them. A pale one scraped raw by some rare abrasion will regroup its surface within days; one pinned beneath a shifting cobble will wait, months if needed, for the next adjustment of weight, then slip free none the older. Death, when it comes at last, is a gradual thinning. The body spreads, grows more translucent, and is incorporat­ed into the sediment it once grazed. No corpse persists. The flood carries the last soft residue until even that is indistinguishable from the mineral flour.

So the high country remains complete. The pale ones do not alter its grammar; they conjugate it. They are the density that moves, the seep that chooses direction for a time, the light made flesh too cold to cast a true shadow of its own. Everything else—every corridor empty of them, every hour they do not appear—is simply the place resting in its finished state, while its one permitted life continues the long, low work of turning stone into stone again, under the roar that never checks its voice, beneath the sky that has never learned to dim.
