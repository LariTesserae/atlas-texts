The throat does not keep a single tenant. What lives here is a layered congregation, an overlapping citizenry that has grown so gradually into the stone that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has softened past recovery. Begin anywhere and the lives interleave.

Highest, where the walls still catch a rumor of sky, the soft-winged ones pack the ledges in living sediment. They are pale to the point of translucence, bodies the length of a forearm, membranes so thin that heat and the faint mineral glow of the vents show through them like candlelight through oiled paper. By day they hang folded, a continuous soft drapery that breathes and rustles even in sleep; the stone beneath them is polished to a dull luster by centuries of their gripping feet. At the hour when the slit of sky finally blackens they begin to loosen. First a few, then hundreds, then the entire upper population peels free in slow spirals. Their wings do not beat so much as pulse—long, elastic strokes that generate a low, continuous thunder, the sound of surf trapped inside a narrow drum and forced to travel forever between the walls. They rise only a little way, never clearing the rim, circling inside the throat itself as if the open air above were an alien country. When they return they settle in slightly different configurations, so that over decades the colonies migrate a few body-lengths up or down according to the slow drift of warmth and seepage. They feed on the midges and on the fine rain of organic dust that the lower traffic constantly sheds. They are not silent: under the wing-thunder runs a softer vocabulary of clicks and wet flutters, information passed wing to wing about which vents are strengthening, which ledges have grown overcrowded, which of the velvet-throated callers has shifted its slot.

Below them the vertical cities of the jointed climbers begin. These are longer, narrower, built of many articulated segments the color of wet slate. They cling to every face, moving with deliberate, rippling gait, each limb ending in a pad that tastes the stone for the thin microbial films they graze. Their chitin is never still; it produces intricate overlapping rhythms—click-trains that run ahead of the body like scouts, answering click-trains that confirm the way is clear or warn of a heavier shell approaching. The largest of them reach the length of a full arm; the smallest are finger-long and travel in braided streams that look, from a distance, like dark water finding new channels. They are patient cartographers. A single individual may spend years traversing the same hundred vertical meters, learning every flute and overhang the way a hand learns a familiar tool. When two columns meet they do not fight; they negotiate with sound and with slow touching of antennae until one yields the preferred seam of moisture. Their cast skins remain for seasons, ghost-outlines that the lichens eventually claim.

In the warmer columns of air the midges keep their trembling cities. Iridescent, heat-drunk, they form vertical swarms so dense they look like smudges of bruised light. Their collective drone is the brightest thread in the throat’s sound: a high, insistent filament woven through the deeper wingbeat and the glissandi of the callers. Most of the time they simply hang, recycling the same cubic meters of warm breath. Then something larger passes—an ascending soft-wing, a drifting shell, a velvet body shifting in its slot—and the swarm collapses into a sudden dark tornado of hunger, a brief frenzy that lasts less than a minute before the column reforms, trembling again as if nothing had happened. They are the throat’s quickest pulse, yet even they bloom and fade on long cycles; entire seasons may pass in which the swarms stay thin, only to thicken again when the geothermal breath strengthens.

Across the scree the heavy-shelled grazers move like geology deciding to relocate. Each is the size of a sleeping child, dome of mineral-thick shell overgrown with the same black and rust lichens that map the walls, so that a motionless individual is indistinguishable from stone until it takes a step. Their progress is measured in days. A single crossing of the throat’s width may occupy a generation of midges. They feed by rasping the microbial crusts and the moss cushions, leaving faint pale trails that the jointed climbers later explore. Inside the shell the body is soft, pale, and almost motionless; they seem less like animals than like the rock’s own slow thoughts given temporary locomotion. When two shells meet they simply stop, sometimes for weeks, in mute conference or mere indifference, then one eventually continues. Their droppings are rich and rare; wherever they linger a slightly greener footnote of moss appears.

And then there are the callers. Half-seen, child-sized, coated in dense velvet fur the color of deep charcoal or old blood, they occupy the higher slots and overhangs—the places where the stone leans inward and creates shallow, protected chambers. They are rarely glimpsed whole. What one sees is a curve of shoulder, a long-fingered hand gripping a rim, the brief wet gleam of an eye. Their fur drinks light; even the faint phosphorescence of the vents clings to it only a moment before vanishing. What they give instead is voice. The calls are long glissandi that begin almost below hearing and rise in slow, unbroken slides before falling again, each phrase lasting half a minute or more. They overlap deliberately, answering across the throat, building temporary cathedrals of resonance that ricochet and re-ricochet until the stone itself seems to participate. Sometimes—without warning, without obvious cause—the overlapping voices lock into accidental harmonies so pure and sustained that the air feels braced by them, as if architecture had briefly become audible. The callers are the throat’s memory keepers. Their phrases carry information older than any single body: which vents have failed in past decades, which ledges collapsed, where the rare meltwater sheets once ran longest. A young caller may spend years listening before it adds its own voice to the long conversation. They move seldom. When they do, it is at night and with exquisite care, transferring from slot to slot along invisible routes known only to the furred. They leave no paths, only the faint, repeated grooves in one deep alcove—marks that might be claws or might be the first deliberate tally kept by anything in this place.

All of these lives share the same unhurried metabolism. Nothing here is frantic. The geothermal breath itself strengthens and softens across decades; the colonies adjust across generations. Even hunger is patient. A soft-wing may fast for weeks while waiting for the midges to thicken. A jointed climber will pause an entire season beside a drying seep rather than risk a longer traverse. The throat has taught them that almost everything necessary will return if one simply remains.

There are older, quieter tenants still. In the deepest fractures, where the dark is absolute and the heat rises most steadily, live the thin white threads that are neither plant nor animal in any comfortable sense—filamentous, faintly motile, tasting the stone from within. They glow with the same shy phosphorescence that sometimes rides the vents, and it is their light, leaking upward through hairline cracks, that briefly paints the undersides of wings and the wettest lichens. They have no voices and almost no movement measurable in human time. They simply persist, binding the warmth to the rock, making the long equilibrium possible.

No single mind rules the throat. What lives here is the dense, warm argument itself: wing-thunder answering glissando, click-train answering drone, the slow scrape of shell across grit answering the faintest drip of condensation. The place has already arrived at its own consensus of stone, moisture, darkness, and layered breath. Stand in its center when the sky-slit goes black and the sound is thickest; feel how little the thousand lives need to acknowledge you. You are weather that will pass. They are the climate that remains.
