The ones who live here are not first visible as bodies. They announce themselves as a slight thickening in the already heavy quiet, a local intensification of the cold that seems to rise from the stone itself rather than fall from the air. Stand long enough on any of the intermediate terraces—where the flagging is cut so fine that the seams are only darker threads in the wet sheen—and the sense of being accompanied arrives before any shape does. It is a pressure behind the knees, a faint drag on the breath, as if the altitude had grown personal.

When they do come into sight they are easy to mistake for further work. Their limbs are the same mineral palette: the pale grey of fresh quartzite fracture, the iron-dark of the hauled volcanic blocks, the mica glitter that never quite dries. Skin, if it is skin, holds water the way the paving does—continuous film, beading only at the edges of motion. They move at the pace of seepage. A single crossing of a narrow slab bridge can take the better part of an hour; the body advances by small, exact placements of weight, each foot or hand or whatever serves testing the stone as though remembering how it was fitted. There is no haste because there is no elsewhere to reach. The basin is the whole of their range, and they have never been seen to look toward the unfinished margins where the gangs of living laborers still hammer and lever. Those laborers, for their part, do not look back. The two sorts of presence share the same water and the same light without acknowledgment, like successive drafts of the same intention.

Their numbers are uncertain. Sometimes only one is near, crouched at the lip of a channel, palms flat to the flow so that the water climbs their wrists in thin, deliberate sheets and disappears into the fine joints of their forearms. Sometimes several occupy the same shallow amphitheater of terraces, spaced with the same care as the standing sheets of stone that serve as incomplete walls. They do not converse. What passes between them is transferred through the pavement: a trivial adjustment of balance on one side of a knoll will, minutes later, be answered by an answering shift farther down the gradient, as if the entire made surface were a single tensed membrane. On nights when the winter constellations are hard and close, the membrane can be felt to breathe—almost too slow to register, a rise and fall measured in the gradual darkening and brightening of the water film under starlight.

They are not identical. Some keep close to the proportions of the laborers who still come and go at the edges: bipedal, shouldered, with faces that hold a wet, unblinking glaze where eyes might be. Others have let the work change them more thoroughly. There are those whose torsos have flattened and spread into mobile paving-stones, aided by many jointed limbs that tuck flush when they rest so that a careless step might land on what is only sleeping. There are tall, attenuated ones that stand for days in the positions of unfinished pillars, water threading down their lengths in the same slow verticals that decorate every overhang. And there are the rare, almost formless concentrations that prefer the central sink: masses of living grit and seepage that assemble themselves just beneath the black glass surface, rising at long intervals to trail long translucent curtains of themselves across the graded grates before sinking again. These last seem oldest. The geometry of the basin warps more noticeably around them; channels in their vicinity deliver water to impossible heights without effort, and the light fractures into thinner and thinner spectra as it nears their bulk.

They feed on nothing that grows, because nothing grows. Their sustenance is the labor of maintenance itself. Wherever a freeze has lifted a course a fraction, wherever a season’s grit has rounded an edge that must stay young and cruel, one of them will eventually arrive and begin the correction. They do not use the hammerstones or sapling sledges of the outer gangs. Their method is closer to persuasion. A hand—or the splayed, stone-colored pad that serves—rests on the offending block. Hours pass. The block settles. Micro-fractures close or open according to need. Fresh faces appear without the clean percussion scars left by tools; the new planes look almost melted into truth. When the work is done the being withdraws a short distance and remains still while the water tests the repair, filming it, anointing every new contact. Only then does it move on. In this way the basin is kept in the state of permanent inception: never ancient, never finished, always at the pitch of sharpness where water must be specifically invited.

They are not unkind, nor kind. A living laborer who collapses from cold or exhaustion at the fringe will sometimes be found, days later, roofed by a temporary vault of fitted shards that sheds the wind and threads meltwater past the body rather than through it. No other help is offered. When the laborer recovers enough to crawl free, the vault is already being dismantled and its pieces returned to larger purposes. Conversely, a tool left overnight in a drain path may be found at dawn incorporated into a new curb, its wooden handle petrified into the same dark silicate as the surrounding work, its metal head fretted into a miniature spillway. Whether this is theft, tribute, or simple economy of material is not a distinction the place recognizes.

At the center, around the tiered sink where all waters pause, the population densifies into something like a court, though a court without sovereign or ceremony. Here the older, less articulated ones predominate. Their presence deepens the strangeness of gradient: standing among them one feels the entire high basin tilt through angles that have no name in ordinary ascent or descent. The black surface of the final pool becomes a lens. Look into it long enough and the reflected maze of walls and walks arranges itself into configurations that do not match the world behind your shoulder—extra terraces, impossible intersections of channel, distant figures who are yourself still approaching. The inhabitants regard these discrepancies without surprise. They are simply further surfaces requiring fitting. Occasionally one of them will extend a limb or a curtain of self into the pool and make a minute alteration; somewhere far out in the basin a standing sheet of stone will lean a finger’s width closer to true, and the water will accept the new path with its usual unhurried gratitude.

They have no speech that reaches the air. What they know of themselves is written in the lock of edge against edge, in the patient correction of every lip, in the way the light is broken and broken again across unbroken wetness. The construction continues because they continue; they continue because the construction has never once conceived of an ending. In the thin brilliance of the high basin they persist—mineral, meticulous, and as little hurried as the threads of water that fall, and fall again, from every stone that has learned how to hold them.
