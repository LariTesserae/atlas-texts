They are the ones who stayed when the stone first began its long refusal to remain seafloor.

You notice them first as interruptions in the dim: darker dense shapes moving with deliberate economy between the kelp trunks, or hanging motionless in a doorway while a mineral skin finishes precipitating across the lintel. At a distance they read almost as seals that learned vertical posture, or as people stretched and softened by pressure and time. Closer, the differences accumulate until the resemblance to any surface memory frays.

Their bodies are compact, heavy-boned yet buoyant, jackets of dense muscle wrapped around a skeleton that has thickened in the long arms and the splayed, spatulate feet. The skin is a living slate-blue or deep olive that takes on the faint foxfire of the mineral skins at night; it is smooth except where it roughens into fine denticles along the shoulders, the outer forearms, and the ridge of the spine—places that brush raw basalt all day. A low dorsal keel runs from between the shoulder blades to the small of the back, not for speed but for stability when they pole the stone-weighted sleds or brace against a sudden upwelling. Between the fingers and toes, thin translucent webbing still shows the ghost of ancestral paddling; many cut small slits in it so they can grip flaked-stone tools without the membrane bunching. The hands themselves are broad, the thumbs strong, the nails shortened to thick dark spades useful for scraping aragonite bloom or prying loose a stubborn holdfast.

Faces are blunt and forward-set. Eyes large, dark, and slightly protruding, the pupils round in the perpetual dusk yet able to narrow to vertical slits when a wandering giant’s pale bulk reflects too much of the residual silver. A nictitating membrane sweeps sideways more often than blinking; it carries a faint opalescent sheen that some dye deliberate patterns onto with the luminous juices of the siphon-weeds. Nostrils are closable slits high on the snout; they keep them shut most of the day and taste the water instead through a constellation of pit organs scattered across cheeks and temples—pits that register the metallic sweetness of cooling rock, the iodine spike of a newly cut kelp frond, the faint electric ghost of another’s heartbeat three channels away. Mouths are wide but tight-lipped. Inside, the teeth are small, numerous, and subtly serrated, suited to scraping algal tissues and crushing the occasional translucent crab rather than tearing flesh. They can drink the surrounding water if they must, extracting what they need through specialized glands along the palate, but most prefer the fresher liquid they squeeze from certain bladder-weed pods grown in the roof gardens.

Speech is an intimate thing. They produce soft clicks with the tongue against the palate, chest-resonant hums that travel as much through bone and water as through open mouth, and occasional chuffs of expelled water that serve as emphasis or laughter. Bubbles are skill and manners: children are gently corrected until they can smile, argue, or sing without wasting air. Names are short pulse-patterns rather than strings of syllables; a full formal name might be the rhythm of three clicks, a held hum, and the particular way a certain algae curtain sways when brushed—something that can only be finished in the presence of the place that helped shape it.

They do not tire of the dim the way a surface mind expects. Their vision drinks the milked silver and the green foxfire geometry with equal hunger; at the deepest indigo hours their eyes catch every faint bioluminescent signature left by the worm-things, reading them the way others once read stars. When they sleep—curled in niches lined with soft red macroalgae, sometimes two or three together for warmth and the comfort of shared slow heartbeats—they leave small glowing threads of tamed worm-sigil above the entrance, map-marks that say which family breathes here and whether the stone beneath has been restless lately.

Clothing is minimal and practical: hip girdles of braided kelp fiber or tanned needlefish skin, tool loops, small pouches of inflated float-bladder. Many wear collars or chest panels dense with beads of polished shell, vertebrae, and carefully shaped chips of the greener unknown mineral that precipitates only on the newest swells. These are not mere decoration; the arrangements encode lineage claims, debts of stone-song, which gardens a person has tended through how many slow risings. Hair is rare—most heads are sleek—but some cultivate long trailing crests of modified algal symbiotes that root lightly in the scalp and trail luminous tips; these are groomed with the same flaked knives used on the roof gardens and are considered both beautiful and slightly dangerous, since a sudden current can turn them into tangling snares.

Children are born live after long gestations, already able to hold breath and swim weakly. For the first years they stay close to the densest parts of the city, learning the clicks, the polite way to scrape frost without angering the stone, how to feel through their pit organs when a pillow of basalt is still too warm to build upon. They ride the coracle sleds standing between an adult’s knees, small webbed hands gripping the gunwales, eyes huge. Adolescence brings the first real negotiations with lithification: a youth may be given a minor swell of their own to midwife, planting the red algae that encourage cementation, stacking the first mismatched courses of wet stone, singing the low held notes that some say persuade the mountain to accept weight. Success is slow and public; failure (a wall that cracks as the dome shrugs, a chamber that pitches too steeply) is simply dismantled and begun again lower down. No shame attaches—only the quiet adjustment of plans.

Adults move through layered occupations that blur into one another. There are the scrapers who harvest the overnight mineral skins before they thicken into armor the city cannot use. There are the coaxers who spend days placing crushed shell and bone meal in precise arcs so that living basalt will swell into a buttress exactly where a new channel wall needs shoring. Gardeners prune and tie and harvest, their knives flashing dull silver in the gloom; some specialize in the faintly luminous dyes, others in the fiber algae that become nets, lines, and the soft nest-linings of sleeping niches. Channel-keepers clear fissures, cut new ones with fire-hardened mangrove blades when the rising stone pinches an old thoroughfare shut, and maintain the weighted sleds. A few become listeners: people who press their keeled backs to fresh faces of rock for hours, reading the almost subsonic held notes of the dome’s adjustments, advising which neighborhoods may lift and which may crack. Elders who have midwifed many houses sometimes slide into a different stillness, spending their last years simply drifting the outer terraces, trailing one hand along the stone the way long-dead surface ancestors might have trailed fingers through grass, humming the oldest songs about rain that falls upward.

They eat what the plateau gives. Soft tissues of the feather-red and indigo macroalgae, scraped mineral films rich in strange salts, the gelatinous bells when they rise in season, occasional larger packs of needlefish driven into waiting nets. Cooking is the careful application of the heat-pair stones: two chalky nodules struck together until they bloom with chemical warmth sufficient to boil a sealed bladder. Meals are communal more often than not, passed hand to hand in the narrow channels while people float at different heights, feet braced on door lintels or window ledges. Conversation during eating is sparse and low; the sound of contented chewing and the occasional appreciative chuff travels farther than words.

Conflict exists but is muffled by the medium and the shared dependence on the unfinished mountain. Disputes over a promising swell, over the lateral inheritance of a particularly well-cemented cluster of chambers, over whose garden fronds were harvested a day too early—these are settled by long circling arguments in the clicks and hums, by the weighing of shell-bead tallies, and finally by the slow arbitration of listeners who read the stone’s own inclination. Violence is rare and almost always clumsy; the water makes sudden motion expensive and visible. When it happens it is usually a grappling for position that ends with both parties exhausted and drifting apart, later repaired by gifts of luminous dye or a day’s free coaxing labor.

They know they are not alone in the larger sense. The lesser giants that ghost through the outer channels are given wide respectful berths and sometimes small offerings of surplus algae left on high ledges; whether the giants notice is uncertain, but the practice continues. The translucent crabs that farm the mineral skins are half pest, half partner—raided for food yet also gently herded so their grazing keeps certain faces clean for building. The worm-things that write and erase phosphorescent sigils are regarded with something closer to superstition; a few of the older coaxers claim the sigils can be read as the mountain’s own slow thoughts, and that once in a generation a truly gifted listener can persuade a worm to leave its light in a permanent helpful map across a difficult new fracture.

Death is ordinary and quiet. Bodies are not buried (the stone would only lift them again) but carried to the outer steepening slopes and released with small stones bound in netting so they descend into darker water where the city does not yet reach. Songs afterward speak of the person becoming more of the mineral sweetness in the water, of their clicks joining the held note of the rock itself. Names are retired from everyday use for a time, then sometimes reappear generations later attached to a new child whose pulse-pattern happens to match.

Above everything floats the shared, nearly wordless understanding that the Shelf is still deciding. Houses tilt and are corrected. Channels vanish upward into silver gloom and must be replaced from below. A family’s carefully coaxed chambers may find themselves fifteen feet higher after two generations, their original doorways now windows onto the roofs of newer dwellings. No one owns the vertical; inheritance snakes sideways through webs of need, indebtedness, and affection. The city thickens like a living reef across the unfinished stone, and the people thicken with it—bodies and customs and low songs all slowly elaborating in answer to a geology that has not yet finished its sentence.

They do not dream of the surface the way the oldest songs still mention it. The idea of light that hurts, of rain falling the wrong direction, of a boundary the water does not cover, has become a kind of beautiful absurdity told to children when the foxfire is especially bright. What they dream instead is continuation: another inch of patient rise, another season of gardens taking hold on a fresher swell, another night of navigating by green-glowing ledges that were not there the night before. They are not waiting for the highland to finish becoming land. They are the way the unfinished stone has begun, at last, to think about itself.
