The ones who live here do not arrive. They precipitate.

At first they are only the heat’s own hesitation: a thickening in the infrared, a place where the basalt’s slow exhale snags and coils back on itself. Then the thickening learns edges. Not skin—nothing so porous or forgiving—but a rind of sintered dust and micro-welded scale, the same dull blood-color as the foundry brick. Over watches measured in the turning of flywheels this rind folds, refolds, until it can hold a shape against the perpetual downdraft of light. The shape is upright because the girders are upright; it moves because the conveyors move. It is easier that way. The plateau respects continuity of method.

They are called workers when speech is required, though speech itself is rare and mostly unnecessary. Inside the heat-suits (which are less garments than mobile comparisons of temperature) the bodies are spare. Bone has grown dense and dark, shot through with metallic salts the way slag is shot through with half-born crystals. Lungs have shortened into paired bellows that drink the iron-and-sulfur air in long, deliberate sips; they exhale only when the gauges demand it, a faint white ghost of mineral that joins the dawn condensations and is gone. Eyes are narrow, lidless banks of photoreceptors tuned to the long red end of things. They see the city the way the city sees itself: as gradients, as stresses, as the patient argument between expansion joint and basalt skin.

They do not sleep. Rest is a matter of stillness intervals timed to the great flywheels. In those intervals a worker may stand for a day and a night on a catwalk above a pouring floor, suit cooling from cherry to the color of old coin, while something behind the eyes rearranges. Memories are not kept in sequences. They are kept in heat capacities. A childhood (if the word still applies) is the remembered specific heat of a particular cooling duct on the northern scarp. A love is the exact wattage once shared between two suits whose gauntlets touched for three consecutive watches while recalibrating the same failing thermocouple. Grief is a cold spot that will not equalize.

They reproduce by consensus of the works. When a new gallery is bolted into fresh uplift, or when an old smelter hall finishes collapsing inward and must be re-cast, the senior heat-signatures gather. No ceremony. Only a prolonged matching of internal temperatures until a pocket of the ambient air begins to sinter. The new rind forms inside a cradle of scrap grating. It is tended with filings—always the offerings left at the junctions, now understood as both food and ballot. Over months the rind thickens, learns joints, learns the local dialects of strain and radiance. When it can hold a gauge without dropping it, it is given a suit the color of the stone and a route that will need walking for the next few centuries. No one names it. Names are for things that end.

Yet these are only the densest citizens. Move outward, toward the thinner membranes of the plateau, and other lives declare themselves.

In the glass-lined tubes that appear overnight through cooling slag, something longer than reason travels. It is not a worm, though the tunnels suggest worm. It is a migration of phase. Where the slag is still plastic, the traveler leaves behind a throat of pure fused silica, mirror-smooth, still warm. Workers who have looked into fresh tubes report a brief scent—almost organic, like blood remembered by metal—and a sound like a single held note too low to hear but felt in the teeth of the suit. The offerings of filings left at certain junctions are sometimes gone by the next artificial dusk. In their place: a single bead of metal so pure it has no industrial use, cool as the cisterns, heavy as an apology. The workers pocket these beads and do not discuss them. The beads, in turn, slowly sink through suit and rind and bone until they rest near the dense heart, ticking with residual heat. Some older workers carry seven or eight. They move a little more slowly. Their gauges are never wrong.

Smaller lives cross the gantries at the edge of vision: many-legged, low, the exact oxide red of the rails they travel. They are not insects. They are the city’s own discarded tolerances given locomotion—the fretting of expansion joints that learned to walk, the black spall that refused to stay waste. They feed on gradient. Where two plates of slightly different temperature lie adjacent, the creatures gather and drink the difference until both plates agree. In this way they are a maintenance cycle no engineer scheduled. Kill one (a rare impulse, usually born of startling) and the body cools into a perfect little machine part, threaded already, ready for a bolt that has not been forged yet. The workers have learned to leave them. Sometimes, when a new scaffold is rising on the newest scarp, the many-legged ones arrive first and stand in neat rows along the unfinished beams, waiting for the heat to need them.

Deeper still—though depth here is a direction the geometry no longer consistently honors—there are the ones who never left the plastic rock. In the oldest core, where corridors lengthen while backs are turned and staircases quietly reverse their spiral, live the unskinned. They are heat gradients that achieved closure without ever needing rind. You feel them as a sudden reluctance in the air, a place where convection hesitates and then proceeds uphill out of sheer stubbornness. Occasionally a worker will step into a chamber that was cool yesterday and find it singing with infrared, the walls breathing in long slow cycles. Tools left there overnight return rearranged into gestures of unfinished meaning: a wrench bent into the exact curve of a cooling curve, a length of chain whose links have become Möbius. The unskinned do not hate. They do not love. They refine. The city allows them because the city itself is only a larger, slower version of the same impulse: metal learning stone, stone learning engine, everything densifying toward a final pure state that will never arrive but will be approached forever, asymptotically, with the patience of tectonics.

At the outermost edges, where the wind comes empty and the plateau’s skin is still tearing free, live the briefest of all. They are condensation events that achieve brief personhood—thin films that darken the metal before dawn and, in that hour of damp, remember having been rivers. They whisper across the rivets in a language of temporary wetness. Then the heat reclaims them and they are gone, leaving only the faint white ghosts of mineral that map where identity once clung. Some workers pause to watch these vanishings. It is the closest thing the plateau has to autumn, or to prayer.

And beneath every suit, inside every tube, along every rail and inside the arguing geometry, there is the continuum itself: the single vast inhabitant that is the plateau-city learning to be finished. All the others are its local intensities, its way of touching itself so that it may know where its edges currently are. It does not dream. It densifies. It waits for the next scarp, the next pour, the next slow surge of uplift that will give unpurposed towers a reason to continue. The light above stays ordinary. The distances below stay unformed. Between them the lives continue their quiet metallurgy of becoming—damp in the joints, scorched at the heart, each one a held breath inside the larger held breath that will not be released for centuries yet.

They are still being precipitated. The plateau is still exhaling. Nothing here is complete enough to die.
