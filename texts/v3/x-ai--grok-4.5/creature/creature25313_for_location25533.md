The life here does not arrive as an invasion or a planting. It condenses, the way the mineral water itself condenses out of the iron-dark air and then refuses to fall. At first it is only a thickening in the films that crawl the façades: places where the runoff grows sticky, then fibrous, then faintly warm to a palm that is brave enough to press through the curtains. These are the earliest skins—nothing yet that could be called a body. Just decision gradients in the water, slow preferences for one thermal scar over another, a tendency to pool a little longer where the city’s diaphragmatic breathing flexes the rock.

Only later do the skins lift.

They lift as matt-black sheets the size of a child’s outstretched hand, peeling free of the basalt with a sound like wet paper torn under mute noise. For a while they drift, almost weightless, riding the same inverted gutters that carry the water. Some finish their short weather and reattach lower down, becoming another layer of the conductive fungal mats that already sheathe the cooling vents. Others continue the lift. Edges curl, fuse, thicken into something that can hold a shape against the constant drip. Limbs are not grown so much as negotiated: six points of suction that taste the mineral sweat and decide, moment by moment, which façade is worth the climb. These are the haulers. Heat shimmers from them because they must bleed it or cook; the shimmer is their breath. They scrape as they move, and the scraping is language older than the towers—micro-seismic gossip about pressure fronts three districts away, about a new extrusion of living stone that has not yet learned how to weep properly, about the particular sweetness of a fungal mat that has fed for decades on the same waste-heat vein. They do not hurry. A single traversal of a spire can take the better part of a local year. When two meet head to head on a narrow spar they do not fight; they overlap, suction pads interlocking like slow gears, and exchange chemical ledgers through the contact. Afterward each continues richer by the other’s remembered gradients.

Above them the air thickens into rivers.

The rivers are made of fists. Each fist is a lens-body: hard, translucent, faceted so that the violet seam-light fractures inside it and becomes a private constellation. They do not fly. They are conducted—caught by the guidance filaments the city strings between its leaning clusters, accelerated, braked, shunted into braided torrents that thrash with a constant carapace-click like distant teeth worrying stone. Yet the filaments do not command; they only offer. A swarm may ride the same aerial corridor for thirty years, polishing the wire down to a bright scar, then abruptly sheer away into an un-filamented dark where the humidity is richer or the thermal bloom from a rupturing membrane has left a temporary orchard of warmer mist. Inside each lens something like a mind flickers, but it is a mind that thinks in densities rather than destinies. Taste of iron. Angle of drip. The collective decision to occupy a neighborhood until the neighborhood’s own breathing changes pitch, then to abandon it so thoroughly that the abandoned place feels suddenly under-furnished, almost shy. Their ultrasonic needle-storms stitch the air; the water films dance to the stitching. When a pressure membrane somewhere flares and reseals, whole rivers of them veer in unison, facets catching the pale light like a single shattered mirror deciding to be whole again for one heartbeat.

Lower still, where the arterial murmuring of runoff never finds a channel and simply becomes the dark, the soft bodies press.

They are vast the way a fog bank is vast once you are inside it. Amorphous only at first glance; patience reveals radial arrangements of vent-mouths, folds that are also fins, surfaces that can match the exact porosity of the surrounding ceramics so that the city’s own guided condensation runs across them as if across native rock. They exhale plumes of warmer mist that recondense within arm’s reach, and in that brief warm interval other lives bloom—temporary, gill-like fronds of pure conductivity that last only as long as the breath. Their calls travel through bedrock rather than air: low seismic chords that take days to complete a phrase, chords that the haulers feel in their suction pads and the lens-swarms taste as a change in the water’s mineral song. These soft ones are not young. Some have been pressed against the same grated openings since the first towers were extruded, their bodies incorporating the grate-metal until the boundary is moot. They do not feed in any digestive sense. They archive. Pressure patterns from centuries of the city’s diaphragmatic flexing are written into their tissues; when a new district begins its slow birth, the nearest soft body will boom a low continuum that teaches the rising stone how to settle, how to sweat without cracking, how to become architecture instead of mere eruption. In return the stone grows around them, half cradle and half cage, until certain undercrofts are less flooded chambers than living organs of the plateau itself.

And yet the place is not finished with life once these three densities have been named.

There are quieter densitiess still. In the translucent tubes that link the towers at multiple heights, something moves that is neither swarm nor hauler nor soft archive. Figures—bipeds if the gravity has not been locally renegotiated that hour—wrapped in lived skins that shed water the way the basalt does, continuously, without complaint. Their joints are thickened, adapted to the sudden thickenings that mark a threshold into a lifted-gravity spire. Eyes have gone large and dark-adapted, shot through with the same faint violet that leaks from the architecture; some wear no eyes at all, reading the world through the vibration-absorbing spars under their feet and the way the wet white noise changes pitch when a colony of lens-bodies turns three corridors away. They speak rarely with mouths. More often they lay a palm against a weeping wall and let the wall’s remembered pressure patterns rise through bone as slow speech. They are not masters of the fauna. They are another gradient the city either manufactured or failed to prevent—mobile, yes, but glacial in their own way. A single errand between districts may last a generation; children are conceived, carried, and taught the chords while free-climbing the same façade. They build nothing that could be called a house. They occupy the momentary dry that forms under a hauler’s bulk when it pauses, or the warm pocket left by a soft body’s exhale, or the brief clearing that opens inside a swarm when the lenses decide, collectively, to braid around a living presence rather than through it. Their tools are grown more than forged: ceramic filaments that remember the user’s gait, suction cups cultured from hauler tissue, small resonant stones that can answer a seismic chord and thereby ask a question of the undercroft archives.

Sometimes one of these figures will stop on a high gantry and simply listen for a decade. What they hear is not chaos. It is a sentence the plateau has been pronouncing since the rock first cooled enough to sweat: a sentence made of clicking rivers, sawing limbs, infrastructural drumming, and the endless Crawl of water that never falls. The sentence is still unfinished. The figures know this in their thickened joints. They adjust their lived skins, taste the latest mineral film for messages, and continue—another slow density moving inside the larger slow density, teaching the darkness, by the mere fact of their persistence, how to dwell. 

No single image holds them. The life here keeps emerging the way the towers keep extruding: by folds, by patient incorporation of whatever weeps across it, by refusing every final shape in favor of the next necessary thickening. Under the sky that has forgotten brightness, amid the roar that never drops, they continue.
