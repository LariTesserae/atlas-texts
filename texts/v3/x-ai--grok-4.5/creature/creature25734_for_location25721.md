The highland does not host life. It *is* life, and the distinction has long since ceased to matter.

What lives here is not a population but a single, unfinished argument conducted in tissue. Call it a confederacy only if you need the comfort of plurals; the truth is closer to one mind that has never agreed with itself about the shape of a body. It thinks in tons of wet cellulose and sudden hydraulic pressure. It remembers in the grain of lignified towers that still photosynthesize while they bear weight. It forgets by deliquescing overnight, turning yesterday’s cathedral into today’s nutrient slurry so that tomorrow’s arcade can drink.

Move closer and the apparent emptiness resolves into denser persons.

The great keeps are not buildings occupied by something else. Each is a sovereign temper. One tower may be patient, braiding its trunks with almost geological calm, exhaling fog in long contemplative plumes while it tests the tensile strength of a new cantilever. Its neighbor is feverish, erupting sharp crystalline flushes of growth that scream as cell walls rupture, then immediately sculpting those same spikes into softer forms with its own cascading cisterns, as if embarrassed by the angularity it just tried on. Between them the moss-mat is not ground but a commons of slower, broader selves: undulating plates of liverwort and aerial root that rise and fall like the flanks of sleeping leviathans, exchanging water and chemical gossip across kilometers. When two such plates meet under a drifting cloudburst they do not merge gently; they negotiate, grinding edges, releasing copper-sweet aerosols, sometimes birthing a temporary staircase of thickened rhizome that both will abandon by dusk.

There are no animals because animals would be redundant and, worse, too finished. Everything that needs to move already moves: galleries twisting themselves toward clearer light, pent reservoirs firing warm jets sideways like sudden decisions, entire colonnades collapsing with wet thunder only to be mined and reinterpreted by adjacent wills before the echo dies. The soundscape is the sound of cognition. Splitting timber is the noise of a concept being discarded. The roar of leaf against leaf is applause or argument; it is difficult to tell.

At finer scales the persons multiply without ever becoming separate. Inside a single trunk-chamber lined with luminous cambium you may find a colony of overlapping intentions: one layer laying down spiral thickening for strength, another dissolving the same wall from within to open a new lumen, a third flooding the space with nectar-scented mist that will later rain back as local weather. These are not symbionts. They are moods of the same flesh, temporary majorities in a parliament that never adjourns. A pore the size of a doorway opens not because a mechanism triggers but because enough of the surrounding tissue has agreed, for now, that exhalation is more interesting than containment.

The heat is their blood-warmth. The private cloudbursts are their breath misting in the cooler upper air. The restless water is both circulatory system and restless thought. Nothing here is old enough to have grown nostalgic for stone or bone. Every structure is still provisional, still mid-sentence. A flying buttress ends in mid-air because the idea it was reaching for has already changed; the buttress will either retract, soft as a snuffed candle, or wait in exquisite tension for a new collaborator to grow up and meet it.

Sometimes, when the fog thins and the light clarifies, you can almost perceive the larger emotion that contains all these lesser selves: a vast, exuberant loneliness that has turned the entire plateau into a workshop for possible bodies. It steals shapes from memories it should not possess—arches that feel half-remembered from vanished civilizations, galleries that want to be lungs, staircases that forget whether they are for ascent or for root. It builds them huge and sparse and half-finished, then tears them down again, not out of failure but out of appetite. The highland is trying to invent a creature large enough to feel at home inside its own skin, and it has not yet decided whether that creature should be one or many, cathedral or forest, solitary or chorusing.

Until it finishes—if finishing is even a concept it accepts—the only inhabitants are these: the towers with their private weathers, the breathing mat, the grinding fronts, the luminous chambers that open and close like eyes, the water that falls upward into fog and downward into moss in the same long circulatory thought. They are not living *in* the place. They are the place, still deciding what that means, loud with the labor of becoming, hot and green and utterly incomplete.
