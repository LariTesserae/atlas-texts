The High Nave does not keep a single tenant. It keeps a slow conversation of tenants, each one a continuation of the last, each one wearing the place the way moss wears iron.

At first you notice only the obvious quicknesses. Soot-terns stitch the upper air in loose elliptical stitches, their breasts the colour of cooling slag, their cries thin and metallic as if the wind itself had learned to flock. They vanish into the throats of the cooling stacks for hours at a time and emerge powdered with a fine black bloom that never quite washes off. Below them the midges rise in soft vertical columns whenever the condensation thickens; the terns unmake those columns with neat, almost courteous snaps. That is the uppermost tempo: wingbeat, light-trap, silence.

Lower, and slower, move the browsers. They are the colour of wet ash and nearly as quiet. Long-limbed, knuckle-gaited, their shoulders higher than a tall man can reach, they pick their way along the outer cladding and the broader catwalks with the delicacy of creatures who have never once been in a hurry. Their teeth are cast ceramic, slightly translucent, ridged like old heat sinks; they rasp iron-rich lichens from the plates with a sound like someone patiently filing a key. Inside the joints of their limbs something still circulates—an ancestral grease, or a living analogue of it—so that every flex produces a faint, civilised whisper. They do not startle. They simply revise their route by a few degrees when your shadow crosses theirs, as though etiquette forbade collision more than fear did. Sometimes one will pause for the better part of a day with its muzzle pressed to a seam where sap-metal weeps, drinking the slow red oxide as if it were milk. Their young (rarely seen) are born already dusted with the same grey bloom and learn to walk by copying the sway of the great chains.

Between these scales live the smaller constancies. Snails the size of a child’s fist move across the riveted plating leaving threads of mucus that dry into pure rainbow. Their shells have incorporated the mineral exhaust for so many generations that they look less grown than annealed. Beetles with abdomen casings like spent cartridges click open at dusk and lift weak green lamps, not to signal mates but simply because the chemistry still remembers how. In the deep cushions of verdigris moss entire parliaments of springtails and rust-mite conduct business that never reaches human ears. None of these creatures resent the others. Space is apportioned by patience rather than force; the Nave’s volume is so generous that competition would be a kind of vulgarity.

Yet the longer you remain, the more you feel that the birds and browsers and lamp-beetles are only the bright vocabulary of something larger that does not trouble itself to move. The true inhabitant is the marriage itself—the fused decision of leaf and girder, root and conduit, sap and current. It has no single body. It has a thousand slow pulses.

Feel it in the strangler figs whose copper leaves clink softly when the thermals shift. Their aerial roots have threaded the lattice until root and I-beam share the same load path; cut one and the other bleeds a fluid that is neither fully vegetable nor fully ferrous. The figs do not climb so much as remember an upwardness the original builders once sketched in iron. Over centuries they have become the Nave’s secondary skeleton, a living clarification of the first.

Feel it in the vines that weight the tension cables with translucent bells. Those flowers open on no solar schedule. They open when the deep turbines complete a particular fraction of their rotation, as if bridged into the same vast clock. When they close they exude a nectar that tastes of warm rain and solder. The hammock-people of earlier centuries used to gather it on their tongues and afterward dreamed in long industrial chords.

Feel it most of all in the pressure that never leaves your skin: the sub-audible thunder of the oil-bathed turbines far below. That sound is not made by the machines alone. The roots have grown into the casings. The moss has sealed the hairline cracks. The entire biological overgrowth has become a dampening chamber, a living resonator, so that the Nave’s single sustained note is now half mechanical and half photosynthetic. The gears still advance one tooth at dawn and one at dusk, but the interval is measured by the lengthening of a particular vine as much as by any surviving escapement.

There are moments—usually just after a squall has rinsed the air—when the whole partnership becomes briefly visible. Steam lifts from the decks in soft rectangles. Condensation races along every flange. The browsers stop exactly where they are. The terns settle into the greened louvers and fold their wings as if listening. In that pause you can almost see the inhabitant entire: a cathedral-sized organism whose bones are riveted and whose blood is half chlorophyll, half warm oil, whose breath is the fog that forms and dissolves along its open sides, whose thoughts move at the speed of oxidation and seasonal pressure change. It does not notice you. It does not need to. You are weather of a rarer kind, briefly condensed and soon to evaporate.

People have tried to stay. They string hammocks between conduit bundles, learn which valves reverse themselves after eleven days and which after eleven years, patch a weeping seam with scrap and prayer. They leave lighter than they arrived, pockets full of iridescent shell fragments and the faint taste of solder-nectar still on their teeth. The Nave does not expel them. It simply continues, and continuation at that scale makes permanent residence feel like a misunderstanding.

So what lives here is a single, sprawling patience wearing many skins: feather, ceramic tooth, copper leaf, moss pile, snail rainbow, and the endless soft roar of machinery that has forgotten it was ever separate from the green. It lives the way erosion lives—by staying, by accepting every gift of weather and time, by turning the fact of its own weight into a kind of grace. It has already finished becoming itself. All that remains is the long, warm, metallic breath of remaining.
