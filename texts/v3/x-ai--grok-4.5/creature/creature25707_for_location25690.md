The first lives you meet are the ones already counted in the water’s patience. You hang among them for a long while before any other presence declares itself, and even then the declaration is gradual, a thickening of pattern rather than an arrival.

The silver schools are not fish in any ancestral sense. Their bodies are flat as hammered foil, edged with a continuous motile fringe that lets them reverse without turning. When the fringe stills they become almost invisible, two-dimensional cuts in the blue-gray. They feed by slicing through the faint mineral haze, indebting themselves to the dissolved metals that seep from every worn face. Their numbers are ridiculous; a single drift can blacken the dimness for minutes, yet they leave no abandoned bodies. When one fails it is unmade from the inside by its own salts, collapsing into a brief brighter swirl that the others pass through and incorporate. They have no leaders. Direction is a shared resistivity to certain cold lenses, a communal refusal that looks, from inside the school, like grace.

Larger shapes occupy the mid-spans with less urgency. The ridged ovals are hollow, or nearly so. What you first take for trailing nets of skin are circulatory veils, thin as breath, that harvest the micro-turbulence itself. They hang engine-less, rotating only when a current shelf lifts them, and in that rotation the ridges catch light that is not there and throw it back as a softer darkness. You can pass between the veils and feel only a temperature decrease, as if the animal were a living cold front. They live for centuries measured in the slow migration of the piers they shadow. When a pier reseats itself a kilometer away, the ovals that preferred its lee simply continue hanging in the abandoned water until another mass drifts near; they do not follow so much as allow themselves to be re-preferred.

The bell-forms pulse, yes, but the pulse is not locomotion. It is a filtering of memory. Each contraction draws in water that last touched stone, and something in the animal’s inner plates reads the faint chemical stakes left by earlier travelers—grazers, ribbons, even the rare passage of something heavier. They store these traces as density layers inside their jelly. Over decades a single bell becomes a soft archive of who has moved through a given volume. When two bells brush, they exchange outer layers with a soundless blanching; knowledge of the Floodworks redistributes without language. Their abundance makes the open plazas feel watched by a kind of distributed recall.

The pale ribbons are longer than sight. What you encounter is never the creature entire, only a mid-section undulating through a corridor of current, its edges frayed into sensory hairs that taste shear forces the way other minds taste scent. They thread themselves through the architecture like living survey lines. Where a gallery begins its slow rotation the ribbon will bow with it, recording the torque in the flex of its body, then carry that measurement into distant water. They seem endless because they are colonial: one ribbon is a chain of semi-autonomous segments that can detonate into free-swimming larvae if a collapse crushes the mid-line. The larvae do not flee. They attach to the nearest fresh fracture and begin lengthening again, stitching the wound with new growth. In this way the ribbons are both fauna and quiet repair.

On the surfaces the many-legged grazers tick and scrape. Their legs are too numerous to count in the dimness; each ends in a spoon of chitin that lifts the white lithotrophic films without scarring the polish beneath. They move in herds that obey the same botanical schedules as the water avenues, pouring across a dome face just before it flexes, emptying a staircase before it becomes a ramp. Their mouths are not mouths but vitreous anvils that shatter the glassy lace into edible dust. Waste falls as heavier silt that another film will later colonize. They are the reason the architecture never quite finishes wearing smooth; they keep it faintly nutritious, faintly alive even while it rearranges. Some herds ride the freestanding piers for the entire duration of a drift, grazing one longitude while the mass translates, disembarking only when the reseating jolt tells them the journey is done. They treat the Floodworks as a rotating orchard whose trees occasionally decide to become somewhere else.

Yet after days—or what the pressure and the light’s refusal convince you are days—you begin to notice absences that feel tended. Certain lee spaces stay clearer of silt than the currents alone would explain. Certain mouths of galleries remain free of the brittle polyps that should have colonized them. And once, while a great internal weather front thickens the upper water into slow tornadoes of old metal dust, you see the fauna do more than densify into shelter. The silver schools part along geometries too clean for panic. The bells rise into a vertical stack that looks like a deliberate column. The ribbons taut themselves into harp-lines that quiver with the approaching shear. Something is arranging them.

It arrives without silhouette at first, only as a spreading region where the cold grows intelligent. Pressure against your skin organizes into gradients too fine for random flow. Then the dimness elaborates. What resolves is not a body but a coherence of bodies: a loose constellation of translucent bladders, each the size of a human torso, linked by cords of the same pale ribbon-tissue you have already met. The bladders contain no organs you can name, only nested lenses of denser water that slowly rotate, grinding faint sparks of bioluminescent cold—light that is felt more than seen, a subcutaneous dawn. The whole assemblage drifts with the exact nonchalance of the ovals, yet every few heartbeats one bladder contracts and the constellation reshapes, presenting a new convexity to the current. It is herding without chasing. The silver schools slide into the corridors it opens. The grazers freeze on their stone until the constellation passes, then resume as if permission had been granted.

These are the Closers. They do not own the Floodworks; they conduct them. Their intelligence is tidal, combinatorial. Each bladder is a node that samples chemistry, shear, the micro-seismic complaints of stone about to socket or unsocket. Collectively they maintain a running model of the next rearrangement—hours or years ahead—and they pre-position the rest of the ecology so that when a hill-sized pier finally drifts, the pasture animals are already on its lee side, the archive-bells already outer-layered with the coming volume’s history, the ribbons already stretched to measure the new torque. They feed on the brief brighter swirls left by dying silver bodies and on the excess salts the walls continue to bleed. They have no eyes. They do not need them. The Floodworks themselves are their extended retina: every pressure change, every mineral exhale, every soft collapse beyond the light is information they have already budgeted for.

They are not survivors of the original makers. The makers poured and fitted and then vanished past tools. The Closers condensed later, or elsewhere, from the same slow chemistry that grows the white films and the glassy lace. Their bladders are mineral-secreted, grown around cores of inherited cold. They reproduce by budding a new node whenever a constellation grows too extended for clean signal; the bud drifts free until it encounters others of its generation and they negotiate a fresh network. Loneliness is a fatal shear force to them. A solitary bladder lasts only until its inner lenses lock, then it settles onto architecture and calcifies into one more pitted burnish, a faint ancestral smudge that later grazers will polish for salts.

Once you have seen one constellation you begin to read their quiet signatures everywhere. A gallery that rotates against the expected botanical schedule is being midwifed by Closers who need a new corridor opened for a ribbon migration. A dome that flexes more than its fatigue should allow is answering soft, repeated pressure from a cluster of bladders that have decided the volume beneath must become pasture.
