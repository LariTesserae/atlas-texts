The ones who live here do not arrive so much as condense.

They form the way the fine mist does—gradually, out of the same damp that beads on every rail and leaf—until their outlines hold. At first they are only the sense of occupancy itself: a mild pressure in the alleys, a warmth that lingers inside the sleeved pipes after the night shift has gone, the quiet certainty that the freights and the ferns are being noticed by something that notices back. Then the pressure thickens into gait, into breath that tastes of cold iron, into hands that know without looking how to steady a scaffold against the slow upward nudge of new stone.

Most numerous are the pale jacketed figures who move between the mill-buildings and the stacked tenements as if the two were a single continuous interior. Their clothing is heavy, oil-dark or moss-stained, cut for endurance rather than display; the fabric itself seems half-living, taking on the bottle-green sheen of the surrounding vines after only a few seasons. Faces stay unhurried, eyes adjusted to the even tin light so that neither shadow nor glare startles them. They speak little. When they do, the words travel only a few meters before the moss and the moisture swallow the edges, leaving meaning intact but soft. Many have worked the same braid of steam lines and coolant pipes so long that their marrow has cooled to match the metal; on winter mornings you can see the subtle frost-ferns that form along their wrists when they pause too long in open air. They repair, retool, realign. They wake to find the jigs already altered and simply continue the new pattern, as though the factory and their own sleep had conferred overnight. Children among them learn early to step across the slight fresh tilts in the pavement without breaking stride, and to read the condensation maps that sometimes bloom on cold engine blocks—maps of districts that will exist next week, or next year, or only in the city’s dreaming.

Above them, and among them, move the smaller herds.

Short-legged, heavy-coated browsers the color of wet brick and soot pick their way along the vegetated roof ridges. Their hooves are soft, almost rubbery, so that even when they descend at dusk to crop the fern beds in the interstitial lots they make almost no sound. They do not startle. When a new low ridge of unweathered rock shoulders up through a courtyard, the animals simply adjust their trails the day before, as if the stone had already whispered its intention into the soles of their feet. Pale moths the size of open hands drift between the sodium lamps and the dripping undersides of elevated rails; their wings carry a faint metallic dust that catches the ordinary light and throws it back as brief, illegible sigils. In the cooling towers the oily dark birds nest in thick colonies, riding the warm exhaust in slow spirals, their calls reduced to soft clicks that blend with the baffled steam. Beneath everything—under the walkways, inside the weeping cellars, among the braided pipe runs—the metallic beetles clatter in low choruses when the temperature drops. Their carapaces look forged rather than grown. Sometimes a beetle will freeze mid-path and the condensation on its back will arrange itself into a tiny, perfect replica of the street grid three blocks over, complete with the newest spontaneous berm.

Then there are the others, the ones who occupy the deeper lots and the half-swallowed foundations.

They are larger, slower to resolve. At a distance they can be mistaken for abandoned machinery draped in glaucous vines, or for very old goats that have eaten nothing but moss and rust for generations. Closer, the distinction collapses. Joints show both cartilage and articulated housings. Breath leaves them as strong-smelling fog that smells of leaf-rot and hot oil. Some walk on four limbs, some on two, some on arrangements that change according to the footing the embryonic ground offers that hour. They do not work the factories in any ordinary sense, yet the quiet freighters seem to wait for them; a crate will be left unsecured until one of these shapes shoulders it into place with surprising delicacy. Their presence is the reason certain alleys can fold a walker several streets sideways without transition: the creatures carry pockets of rearranged space the way other animals carry burrs. Residents nod to them the way one nods to weather. Children leave small offerings of pressed cloth or cooled bolts at the edges of the deeper lots, not out of fear but out of the same practical courtesy they show the shifting stone.

All of them—jacketed workers, roof herds, moth-clouds, tower birds, beetle choruses, deep-lot walkers—share a single quality that is not quite language and not quite instinct. They treat the constant low-grade turbulence as ordinary climate. When a wall is swallowed overnight by fresh silt and inevitable moss, no one mourns the wall; someone simply begins the next necessary surface on top of the new contour. When geometry fails politely (a courtyard larger inside than out, a stair that returns you one story higher), they adjust their routes the way one buttons a coat against finer rain. The strangeness is not an intrusion. It is the medium they respire.

And underneath even these, so continuous with the place that it is easy to miss, lives the slower body of the plain itself.

It is not a single animal. It is the way the sediment keeps arriving without wind, the way the raw stone ridges advance a measured centimeter each day, the way the vines flower in brass fittings and the condensation briefly maps futures. It dreams in infrastructure and wakes as denser green. Every engine turning under its thick housing, every quiet realignment of rail and doorway, every soft clatter of beetles after dusk is a pulse in that larger circulation. The people and the near-people and the beasts are its mobile cells, its exploratory growths. They are not separate from the packed brick and the leathery canopy and the creaking concrete. They are how the plain continues to decide its shape—hushed, lush, industrious, and never finished—under the ordinary light of a sky the color of damp tin.

In the long view from any cooling-tower deck, they are simply the living texture of the horizon: dark green and damp metal and small deliberate motions that never quite stop, breathing the cold, rewriting the ground, becoming more densely themselves with each soft resettling of the world.
