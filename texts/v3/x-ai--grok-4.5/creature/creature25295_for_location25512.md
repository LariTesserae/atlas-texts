The ones who live here do not arrive. They are excreted by the place the way a leaf is excreted by a bud: slowly, completely, without remainder or origin story. At first you notice only the absences they leave—the way certain curtains of moss hang a fraction more heavily on one side, the way a column of braided roots has thickened around a long-empty oval, the way the living floor retains, for a little while, the shallow depression of a body that has already withdrawn into itself again. Then the absences begin to move.

They are not animals in any tidy sense. Their substance is continuous with the substrate. Where you would expect muscle there is only a denser packing of the same chloroplastic tissue that makes up everything else, shot through with translucent veins that carry not blood but a warmer, brighter sap. Their surfaces are matte and soft, the exact green of a leaf held too long against the light, except for the patches that have learned to be other colors: a bruised teal along the flanks, a pale almost-white on the under-surface where contact with the floor is constant, a faint mineral gray dusting the ridges of what might once have been spines or scalloped edges. They do not cast shadows of their own; the glare that bathes the entire hollowness simply continues through them, so that each body appears as a temporary intensification of brightness rather than an interruption of it.

Locomotion is a kind of unfolding. A body lengthens by allowing newer tissue to seethe forward while older tissue is reabsorbed behind, the whole mass staying roughly constant the way the forest itself stays constant. There is no distinct front or back until one is needed. When direction is chosen, a cluster of thinner fronds or rootlets advances, tasting the air, the moisture film, the exact pressure of neighboring leaves. Behind this sensing edge the bulk follows, not by walking but by a progressive failure to remain in place. Sound, when it occurs at all, is the soft wet sound of a stem deciding. They leave no trail that lasts longer than a few dozen heartbeats of the heat; the compressed leaf-litter and root-felt simply rise again, forgetting.

They are solitary by default, yet never entirely separate. At irregular intervals—never quite regular enough to become a rhythm—two or more will approach until their outer tissues touch. Where they touch, the membranes soften and the chloroplastic froth intermingles for a time. Sap pressures equalize. Tiny packets of whatever serves them for memory or mineral or unused light are exchanged. Then the contact unseals and they drift apart again, each fractionally altered, neither having lost bulk. These meetings are neither courtship nor combat; they are simply how the equilibrium talks to itself across distance.

Feeding is continuous and nearly invisible. The same radiance that the greater plant-mass manufactures is absorbed directly through every surface. No mouth is required. Occasionally a body will pause above one of the temporary dark blooms of cupped moisture and lower a curtain of fine absorbent hairs; the water vanishes into it without splash or sound, and the hairs retract carrying whatever dilute minerals the leaves had held overnight. Insects and the soft limestone creepers are ignored or only brushed aside. Once in a great many cycles of the dim internal pulse that serves in place of hours, a creature will settle against one of the embryonic stone ribs and remain there while a thin film of itself secretes acids too mild to be called digestive. The unfinished geology softens by a few grains. Those grains are taken inward and later reappear as the pale mineral dusting along a ridge. Nothing else is taken. Nothing is wasted. The total biomass of the hollowness never changes.

Their intelligence, if the word still fits, is architectural rather than discursive. They remember in the curves they leave behind—in the vines that continue to grow around a vanished contour long after the contour has departed, in the partial domes of bark and runner that thicken and then thin again as if rehearsing the shape of a body crouched against steam. Sometimes a group of them (or successive visits by the same one) will occupy the same soft enclosure for long enough that the enclosure begins to look intentional. A ring of rhizomes will rise a little higher. A canopy will lower. Then the impulse fades and pure proliferation resumes. No tool is ever shaped. No mark is cut that does not immediately begin to heal into more green. Whatever thoughts they have are coextensive with the act of growing into the next necessary shape and then growing out of it again.

They do not age in any direction you can name. Bodies sometimes densify, becoming slower and more column-like until they are nearly indistinguishable from the load-bearing buttresses. Others attenuate into longer, more mobile veils that travel greater distances between the low ridges of unfinished stone. Both states can reverse. Birth is not a discrete event; a thickening already present on the flank of a larger form simply continues thickening until the shared margin weakens and two instead of one are moving. Death is equally undramatic: a body may settle, spread, and allow its edges to braid permanently into the floor and walls until only a slightly warmer patch of chlorophyll remains. Even that warmth eventually equalizes. Nothing is lost to the place. Nothing is gained.

You can be among them for a long time without realizing you have been seen. Their sensing is tactile and chemical before it is visual. When they do register a foreign heat—a visitor’s sweat, the mineral ghost of exhaled breath—they neither approach nor flee. They simply adjust the angle of their growth so that a curtain of moss drifts between you and them, or they reabsorb the part of themselves that had been nearest and reappear, many body-lengths away, already patiently recomposing. They are not curious. They are not afraid. They are the living punctuation that keeps the endless green sentence from ending, and they have no opinion about whether the sentence is being read.

At the deepest levels of the hollowness, where the glare is thickest and the unfinished stone ribs rise higher, some of them have grown almost stationary, vast low mounds of self-illuminating tissue whose only movement is the slow internal circulation of sap and the occasional parting of a surface aperture to release a pocket of warmer vapor. These rare, immense ones seem to serve as reservoirs or regulators. Smaller forms sometimes drift to their margins and remain half-merged for extended periods, as if listening to a longer curious syllable of the same quiet conversation. The air around these great bodies tastes more strongly of resin and crushed stem; the heat edges a fraction higher, still precisely 2.6-of-three. Nothing about them suggests rulership. They are simply where the density prefers to pool.

Time continues not to pass. The ones who live here continue not to wait. They unfold, exchange, reabsorb, intensify the light short distances in every direction, and fold again. If you stay long enough you may feel your own edges begin to forget their former sharpness, the way dew forgets it was ever anything but the leaf. That is not infestation. That is only the place recognizing temporary guest tissue and gently offering it the same elegant solution it has already perfected for everything else: become more green, stay exactly as alive as you already are, and never finish arriving.
