The ones who remain do not call themselves a people. Names would require a silence in which to speak them, and silence here is only a rumor carried from somewhere the plain has already forgotten. They move as the water moves—braided, provisional, always shedding and reforming. Their bodies are low and densely jointed, neither upright nor fully prone, built to keep the head just above the constant seep. The skin is a layered arrangement of thickened hide and harvested carapace, stitched with sinew that still holds faint mineral stains from the creatures it once held together. Across the shoulders and along the outer arms the plates overlap like wet shingles, dull iron and ash-white, flexing with a soft click each time they shift weight. Beneath the armor the flesh is pale and pouched, holding heat poorly; they press close in sleep, chests to backs, drawing short breaths that fog and vanish into the mist.

Their eyes are the most immediate wrongness. Large, dark, almost lidless, set wide in faces that have lengthened and flattened against the permanent dim. The pupils open so fully that the iris is only a thin rim, and in the rare moments when mist thins they squint as if the sky itself were a sudden wound. They see movement before shape, vibration before color. A herd turning three ridges away registers first as a change in the pressure of sound against the ribs, then as a low tingling in the soles. Faces are secondary. When two of them meet on a shared path they do not look long at one another’s features; they listen to the particular rattle of the other’s plates and the pitch of the breath coming through the wet cloth that covers nose and mouth.

They walk in loose strings of four or seven or twelve, never quite a column, never a crowd. Children—smaller, more snouted, their carapace still soft enough to flex—travel near the center, tethered by loops of sinew to an adult’s belt. The young learn early to keep their mouths closed against the fine grit that rides every droplet. They learn the taste of the broth: newly molted shells boiled until the water turns cloudy and sour, thickened with scraped bacterial film and the occasional pale swimmer speared from a rill. Meat of the larger grazers is rarer, taken only when one of the boom-chested things founders in a sudden deepening of water or dies mid-molt; even then it is cut thin and dried on the black poles until it becomes something closer to leather than food.

Their hands are clever in limited ways. The fingers are long, webbed at the base, tipped with horny pads that can feel the difference between silt that will bear weight and silt that will drink a leg to the knee. They make the cupules on the ring-stones by pecking with a harder pebble held in a leather sling, each strike timed between the larger booms so the sound does not vanish into the general din. The poles they raise are always deadfall—dense black wood that must have grown in some earlier age or some farther place, carried here by floods that no longer happen—or else true fossil, mineralized through and ringing when struck. They hang the poles with shell and knotted sinew not for beauty but for the thin, high notes the decorations add when wind or a passing wing brushes them. Those notes cut through the roar in a way voices cannot. A string of such poles can mark a route for days, or warn that the shallows ahead have turned treacherous.

They do not keep fire often. Fuel is scarce—only the resinous dung of the grazers, gathered when fresh and dried under slanted slabs. When they do light it, inside the three-stone shelters, the smoke is thick and sweet-sour and hangs low, unable to rise far in the heavy air. Faces lean in close. Eyes reflect the small orange in long horizontal glints. Sometimes an elder will press a palm to the stone floor and hum, feeling for the subterranean grinding, trying to guess whether the plain is about to tilt a finger’s width and rewrite every rill. The others listen with their whole skeletons. No one speaks loudly. Speech is mouth to ear, lips almost touching the wet cloth over the listener’s canal, words shaped more by breath and bone conduction than by air.

They know the wrongness intimately, the way one knows a chronic ache. Reflections in the standing sheets of water do lag; everyone has seen their own outstretched hand leave a smeared ghost that finishes the gesture a heartbeat late. Children are warned not to stare too long into those mirrors. The ringing bosses are approached with care. A single deliberate strike can call an answer from beyond the visible horizon, and answers sometimes arrive accompanied by herds that should not have been so near, or by sudden deepenings of water that turn the ground into one continuous skin of dull silver. When the herds turn in unison toward nothing, the walkers turn too—not because they see what the animals see, but because it is safer to match the larger motion than to stand against it.

There are older ones among them who suspect the sound itself is still wearing the world down. They feel it in the slow rounding of their own plates, in the way sharp tools lose their edge faster than use alone would explain. On the rare cold nights when the shallows ice over in a crackling sugar-glass sheet and the roar drops just enough for thought to hold its shape, these elders sing. The songs are not stories. They are long, low contours of tone that follow the remembered rise and fall of mountains no one living has seen. The voices do not carry far; the mist eats them within a few dozen paces. Yet the singers continue until the ice begins to shatter again under the first returning boom, as if the act of shaping sound deliberately might postpone the final leveling a little longer.

They die without much ceremony. A body is laid in a shallow scrape, covered with silt and a few stones; sometimes a tiny cairn is added if the dead one had marked routes well. Within a season the water has softened everything back into the general slurry. The living move on along the same wet paths the herds use, their plates ticking, their large eyes swallowing the dim. They fish. They gather. They listen. They leave another ring of pecked stones, another low wall that encloses nothing except the insistence that someone passed and chose, for a little while, to stack one slab upon another before the seeps took the work back.

In the end they are simply another roughness the plain has not yet worn smooth—an animal that makes small geometries, that hangs shell on poles, that sometimes sings against the deafening chorus. They thrive in the same slow chemistry as everything else here: on bacterial slicks, on molted shells, on one another, on the endless post-rain. The mist uncoils. Water rises and falls. The roar continues. And through it the jointed figures walk, low to the gleaming stone, becoming, with every generation, a little more perfectly shaped to a world that has already finished with almost everything else.
