The ones who live here are long-boned and pale in the way of things grown without haste beneath a roof of water. Their skin holds a faint mineral sheen, as if the same dissolved stone that feeds the rhizomes has settled into them over generations; in the green-gold light it looks almost translucent at the wrists and throat, so that the slow blue threads of veins show like rootlets under bark. They move with a deliberateness that is not caution but courtesy toward the living timber that holds them. A sudden gesture can set a whole hanging gallery thrumming, and so their limbs have learned economy: elbows carry close, fingers trail rather than stab, and when they swim the short distances between platforms their bodies undulate once, twice, then glide, ribboning through the open water like slow eels made of shadow and pearl.

They are not many. A full count of the settlement would fit inside a single high-mountain cattle byre if such a thing still existed above, and most of them prefer to keep the clusters thin so that the organ-note of the currents remains clean. Families occupy separate swellings along the same great vertical cable, connected by valves that can be left savaged open in calm weather or pinched shut when a pressure front rolls down from the surface. Children are born small and already knowing how to hold their breath in long, pulsing intervals; the first skill taught is not speech but the reading of the air-flowers—the pale, slow balloons that the freeforest terminals release. A child learns to watch which way those balloons lean, how densely they rise, whether they ring bright or muffled when they break the skin of the lake far above. That knowledge decides whether one crosses the open gallery today or waits in the chamber salvaging mussel-shell edges and listening to the grazers rasp somewhere in the dark below.

Their hands are the most changed part of them. The fingers are longer than a lowland human’s, slightly webbed at the base, the pads thickened and faintly abrasive from a lifetime of pruning living wood without tearing it. They do not so much cut as persuade. A blade of fire-hardened reed is laid along a swelling; residual warmth and the plant’s own slow will do the rest, lignifying a floor or sealing a doorway over weeks. When gravity or current pulls a chamber out of true, they drive a single wedge of older, darker root—volcanic stone still clenched inside it like a grudge—and then redirect a graft so that new growth will lean the structure back over decades. They speak of the rhizome the way other peoples speak of weather or ancestors: it is not owned, only inhabited, and it remembers every knife that has ever rested against it.

Voices travel strangely here. In the close chambers speech is soft, vowels rounded so they will not slap against the plant-walls and return as accusation. In the open galleries they resort to a kind of throat-song pitched beneath the organ-note, a rising and falling that the water itself seems to carry farther than any shout. Names are short and are rarely used aloud; one is more often known by the chamber one tends or by the particular phosphorescent film one has cultured to a distinctive shade of cold green or marrow-blue. Light is a communal craft. Spores are gathered from the undersides of certain hanging fronds, seeded onto thin sheets of living epidermis, and fed a paste of crushed filter-feeders until they bloom. A well-kept film can last a season, giving off a steady, shadowless glow that makes every surface look as if it is remembered rather than seen.

They eat sparingly. The silver fish are taken with nets of knotted rhizome fiber only when the schools clatter through in their metallic panics; the meat is translucent, almost gelatinous, and is eaten raw with a paste of scraped algal fleece. Larger grazers are left alone unless one dies of age and drifts; then the whole settlement may gather to butcher it with shell blades, hanging strips of the dense flesh to cure in the cold current. Nothing is smoked. Nothing is hurried. Hunger itself has been trained to the multi-day rhythms of the filter-feeders that stud the outer walls like slow eyes.

At the thin edges where the settled rhizome frays into freer forest, some of the older ones go to sit for hours with their backs against trunks that have never been shaped by hand. They listen to the way the plant-mass leans a finger’s width over a lifetime. They watch the air-flowers rise and break and listen for the soft-bell sound that means the surface is still there, still cold, still holding the sky at bay. No stories are told of a time before the water. The black inclusions of old lava inside the wood are simply called “the hard memory,” and when a knife restarts against one it is considered good manners to pause, touch the spot with two fingers, and wait a breath before continuing. The rhizome refuses to forget the fire that once filled this caldera; the people have long since stopped trying to remember anything else.

They are still becoming. Every generation the webbing between the fingers creeps a fraction higher, the lungs hold a fraction longer, the eyes adjust a fraction better to the green-gold half-light. They do not speak of this as progress. They speak of it the way they speak of a redirected graft: the wood is already going that way; one only helps it arrive without tearing. In the deepest inhabited vault, where even the cultured films grow dim and the organ-note is felt in the bones more than heard, there are a few who have begun to graft living tissue from the rhizome itself into small, careful wounds along their forearms. The pale cables take slowly. In time, those people say quietly, the distinction between dweller and dwelling may thin to something even the currents can no longer tell apart. Until then they prune, they listen, they watch the air-flowers lean, and they keep the valves ready for whatever pressure comes down from the world of wind and slate-blue skin far above.
