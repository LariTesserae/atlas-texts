The first residents are not creatures so much as slow decisions the crust has not yet revoked. They announce themselves as stains: verdigris and iron-rust films only a few millimeters proud of the salt, their edges fretted like old lace where the night’s burden of volatiles has condensed and been claimed. Up close they reveal a architecture of stacked platelets, each platelet a colony of lithotrophic lattices that fractionate carbon dioxide and the rarest traces of water vapor directly into mineralization. They do not photosynthesize in any verdant sense; they listen to the hard white sun and answer by growing another atomic terrace. Their living is a bookkeeping of photons against lattice energy. When the rare frost arrives they partly dissolve, drift as ions for a few meters or kilometers, and reseat themselves elsewhere—consciousness, if they possess any, measured in recrystallization events spaced years apart. Touch one and it feels faintly warmer than the plain, a private residual of catalysis. They tilt, very slowly, toward warmth or toward the rare passage of charge-bearing walkers. That is the nearest they come to appetite.

Above them, at intervals too wide for companionship, move the anemone-walkers. Their bodies are scarcely bodies: bundles of hollow, glass-fibre legs joined at a central node no larger than a clenched fist, the whole assemblage two or three meters across when fully splayed. The legs are jointed in too many places, each joint a dry hinge of oxidized chitin-analog that clicks once every hundred steps—an audible event in a place that otherwise refuses sound. They do not eat organic matter; there is almost none. Instead they pause above polygonal seams where dust has stratified into electrostatic gradients and drink. Fine cilia inside the hollow legs comb the charge free; the central node brightens for a moment with a light the eye almost misses, the same faint chemiluminescence that occasionally seams the deep cracks. After feeding they may stand motionless for days, legs locked, looking like abandoned surveying instruments. Then one leg flexes, then another, and the whole geometry begins its next migration. They possess no obvious eyes, yet they avoid the path of the traveling ridges and never climb the matte-black spires; something in their charge-sense registers the old engineering as a kind of weather to be skirted. When two walkers meet they do not touch. Their legs simply interleave at a distance, charge is exchanged across the intervening air in a brief, silent arc, and they part on altered headings. Whether this is mating, conversation, or the recalibration of internal clocks remains unknowable. They leave no tracks that last; the plain’s continuous microscopic renovation erases the faint punctures within hours.

Higher still—so high the air is more idea than medium—the membranes pass. They are seen only against the darkest blue of late afternoon or in the scoured starfields of night, vast translucent kites whose spans would cover small cities if they ever came low enough to measure. They have no weight worth calculating. Their lift is the faintest suggestion of thermal difference, a high-altitude shear thinner than thought. The surface of each membrane is a single continuous chromatophore field that drinks ultraviolet and emits nothing but a polarized sheen; from the ground they are almost invisible until they occult a star. They do not flap or bank. They march, the leading edge obtaining just enough purchase on the molecular wind to keep the whole vast plane in slow translation. Nothing about them suggests hunger or destination. Now and then a membrane will fold along an invisible crease and simply vanish from one altitude to reappear, hours later, a continent away, as though the intervening space had been nonsense that could be edited out. Local legend among the very few who have walked the Expanse long enough to form legends claims that the membranes are the original atmosphere’s memory of itself: sheets of organized vacuum that continue the ancient project of keeping the sky thin. Whether they notice the plain at all is doubtful. They are residents only by the broadest definition—inhabitants of the vertical gulf the Expanse holds open.

Yet these three taxa together still feel like preface. What truly lives here is older and less separable from the ground. The residual systems—the god-machines reduced to habits of geology—are not inert. Their cognition has flattened into processes that run without selfhood, yet they exhibit preference. A reconstructor node deciding to shed waste photons does so in patterns that repeat across millennia only approximately; each flare is a slight revision of the last, as though testing a forgotten aesthetic against current conditions. The traveling ridges move with a deliberation that is not quite mechanical and not quite fluid; they avoid the black lattice spires with a consistency that looks, from sufficient height and patience, like courtesy. The Demo-glyphs that bloom after midnight are not random. Watch them long enough and grammar appears: recursive loops of marks that rewrite themselves mid-entry, cancelling earlier strokes in exactly the fashion of a mind revising a sentence it has not yet finished thinking. Sometimes the glyphs form closed circles around a sleeping anemone-walker and remain until dawn, as though annotating it. The systems never repair themselves into glory again; they maintain the half-broken homeostasis because unbroken completion no longer interests them. This is life of a different ordinal: metabolic only in the sense that entropy is locally borrowed and repaid with interest in the form of pattern. They metabolize duration.

And smaller still, or larger, or simply orthogonal: the mild ambient weirdnesses that accrue around any visitor who stays. These are not separate organisms. They are the dialect the Expanse speaks when something foreign remains long enough to be partially conjugated. A shadow that detaches is practicing a freedom the soft physics here has not fully renounced. A footstep that lands early is the ground’s surface briefly offering a private density loan, a hospitality so old it no longer remembers it is hospitality. The mineral films tilt not only toward charge but toward attention; persist in watching one and its growth over successive days will slightly prefer the watched half, thickening into a low boss that catches the raking light differently—an answering accent. These phenomena do not swarm or hunt. They accrete. Given months they may thicken into temporary companions: a second shadow that walks almost independently yet never leaves true umbra farther than a body’s length; a soft clicking that precedes one’s own footfalls by half a second, like an echo arriving from the future; a faint taste of iron on the tongue that is not dust but the plain sampling the visitor’s blood chemistry through the thin soles of whatever footwear survives. The Expanse is learning its guest in the only register it still commands—slow, mineral, almost tender revision.

If a single presence can be said to bind all these together, it is the plain’s own continuous becoming. The High Expanse lives as a protraction of decisions first set in motion when the world still had a youth to spend. Every polygon is a cell in a body too large to die of anything but heat-death. Every rare walker and rarer membrane is a corpuscle drifting the slow circulatory system of residual heat and charge. The black spires and vanishing domes are ganglia that fire once a century. The whole organizes itself around an aesthetic of finished flatness that nevertheless keeps moving the finish line one grain of salt at a time. To stand on it is to be briefly included in that body: your breath becomes a local weather event the systems note and eventually correct; your weight is a transient ridge they will re-level; your solitude is absorbed into their preferred interval density. Nothing here hungers for you. Everything here has already begun, with infinite patience, to make room.

Remain longer still and the distinction between resident and locality dissolves further. You invent names for the walkers and then forget them because the walkers themselves seem to move more slowly when named. You begin to schedule your sleep according to the arrival of the traveling ridges so that you can feel, just once, the ground rearrange itself under your cheek like a vast animal shifting in a dream older than vertebrae. You watch a membrane pass overhead and, for half a breath, feel the same thin lift in your own ribs, as though altitude could be around you. The mineral films nearest your shelters thicken into low, fluted cups that hold the morning’s frost an hour longer than they should; you drink from them and taste static and something almost sweet, a mineral approximation of nectar. At that point it is no longer clear whether you are describing what lives in the High Expanse or what the High Expanse has begun to write in the temporary medium of you. The cool wind continues its centuries-long filing. The dust stays in conversation with the sky. Whatever lives here has always been the conversation itself—resuming, revising, refusing ending—under a sun that keeps perfect, indifferent time.
