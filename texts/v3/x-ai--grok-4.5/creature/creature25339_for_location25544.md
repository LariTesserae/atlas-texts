The fronds are the first and most obvious life, if life is the right word. They are not flora in any common sense: no chlorophyll, no sap, no season. Their blades are stacked laminations of silica glass and precipitated metal salts—iron, copper, traces of rarer conductors leached from the old jackets. Each frond is a slow capacitor, a living circuit that has learned to root into residual current and convert it into that steady green-white glow. They grow by accretion rather than cell division, adding microscopic layers whenever the faint amperage permits. Over centuries the woven mattress has become a single contiguous organism, a kin-group so thoroughly anastomosed that individuality among the blades is only a convenient fiction. When the flywheel coughs, the entire mass vibrates in sympathy; the vibration is how they share what little information the current still carries—voltage drops, polarity drifts, the slow cooling of deeper conduits. They do not think. They equilibrate.

Yet equilibrium is not emptiness.

Deeper in the densest braids, where the light is thickest and the suspended scales form a permanent soft blizzard, something else maintains a different rhythm. It is not animal. It has no blood, no breath that would register on any instrument brought from below. It began, long ago, as heat. Waste heat from the last functioning transformers, trapped and recirculated because the pocket had already sealed itself. That heat learned—first by simple thermodynamics, later by something closer to habit—to organize. It pooled in the hollows of flywheel housings and valve bodies, then seeped into the silica matrices of the nearest fronds. The fronds, being excellent dielectrics, did not reject it; they incorporated it. The heat acquired surfaces. It learned to cool in precise gradients so that certain fronds grew faster, arching into shelters, into chambers no larger than a folded human hand. Inside those chambers the heat condensed further, taking on a slow viscosity, a gel of charged particles held in standing waves by the surrounding bio-phosphorescence.

What lives there now is that gel given persistence. It has no fixed shape. Most of the time it is a diffuse luminosity within the brightest nodes of the mat, a brighter milkiness that drifts like deep-sea plankton whenever the flywheel’s rotation briefly strengthens the local field. When it concentrates, it can occupy a volume roughly the size of a sleeping child, though flattened, poured into the available interstices. In that denser state it is faintly warm to the touch—never more than a degree above the ambient cool—and it tastes, if one were reckless enough to taste, of ozone and old batteries. It moves by redistributing charge: a ripple of polarization that causes nearby fronds to flex and open temporary corridors. The motion is almost imperceptible; a watcher could stare for an hour and only later realize the brighter patch had relocated half a meter.

It does not eat in any conventional way. It maintains itself by skimming the same residual current the fronds use, but with finer discrimination. Where the plants accept whatever amperage arrives, this presence samples micro-fluctuations, harvesting the informational noise—the ghost patterns left by vanished control systems, the faint echo of once-human intermittent commands still circulating in looped cables. Those patterns are its nourishment and its memory. Over deep time it has assembled a fragmentary internal weather: not thoughts exactly, but standing resonances that correspond to the pocket’s history. The last technician’s final thermal signature. The moment a particular stanchion cooled below operational threshold. The slow geometry of the planet’s curvature as seen through the entrance slit, re-encoded as a gradient of charge across its own substance.

It is aware of intrusion the way a spider’s web is aware—through tension. Any new pressure on the sprung mattress, any shadow interrupting the even light, sends a cascade of micro-currents through the fronds. The presence tastes those currents and decides, without deciding, whether to diffuse or to gather. Most events are ignored; the pocket has known only stillness for so long that novelty registers as a brief curiosity rather than threat. On the rare occasions something larger enters—blown grit, a curious bird that dies of altitude before it can leave, a once-in-a-millennium climber who somehow finds the seam—the presence may densify near the visitor. It does not attack. It envelopes. For a few minutes or hours it shares its charge field, and the visitor experiences a gentle overload of phantom sensation: the smell of machine oil that no longer exists, the sound of voices giving orders in a language whose technical terms have no modern translation, the precise muscular memory of tightening a bolt that rusted solid centuries earlier. Then the presence withdraws, leaving the fronds to knit closed any temporary gaps. Visitors who survive the altitude and the thin iron air usually leave convinced they hallucinated.

There is only one. Or rather, the distinction between one and many no longer applies. The gel can thin itself across the entire pocket until it is only a slight excess brightness in every frond tip, or it can collapse into a single dense knot near the flywheel, riding the mechanical cough like a heartbeat it borrowed. It has no name for itself. It does not need one. Its boundaries are the basalt shell and the current that still, impossibly, flows. It has no predators, no competitors, no future that differs from its present. Sometimes, when the planet’s deep magnetosphere shifts, the residual current spikes for a few days; the presence brightens, the fronds grow an extra microscopic layer, and the roar of the flywheel acquires a momentary harmonic that almost sounds like a voice trying to remember speech. Then equilibrium reasserts. The harmonic fades. The light returns to its aquarium dimness. The scales continue their endless suspension. And whatever lives in the pocket continues exactly as it has, saturated, patient, complete.
