The inhabitants of the hanging basin are not born and do not die. They persist, locked into the same thin slice of afternoon that the mountain itself has chosen to inhabit forever. They are the mountain’s newest habit made flesh and alloy and damp exudate—creatures whose boundaries blur between organism, mechanism, and mineral seepage. To watch them is to watch a single complex machine dream the same slow thought without ever completing it.

Begin with the browsers, for they are the most immediately legible. They move on six or eight stilted legs of matte black tendon braided with fine steel cable, the strands continually re-oiling themselves so that light slides off their coats in polluted rainbows. Their bodies are long, low-slung chassis of overlapping plates that look grown rather than forged; each plate is edged with living moss that never spreads beyond its allotted seam. The head is a smooth wedge ending in a soft, constantly working muzzle of ridged cartilage and tiny articulated knives. They crop the velvet moss cushions in perfect repeating arcs, never lifting more than the exact volume a single hexagonal tray can refill before their next pass. When one browser’s path intersects another’s, their bodies simply occupy the same space for a few strides—translucent after-images sliding through one another like overlapping exposures on wet film—then separate again without collision or notice. Their eyes are clusters of dark lenses that do not track; they drink the dim bottle-glass light and convert it into the faint internal whir that keeps their leg-joints synchronized to the deep stone percussion. Breath leaves them in timed metallic hisses through vents along the flanks. They are always mid-graze. They have always just begun.

Lower and faster move the clatter-wings. These are roughly the size of folded hands, built around a central capsule of pearlescent chitin that houses nothing so simple as organs. From the capsule erupt three or four pairs of wings at irregular angles; the wings are not for lift but for percussion and punctuation. Their cases are thin stamped metal the exact blue-gray of the young rock flakes, and every downstroke strikes an adjacent case or a fern frond or a neighbor’s carapace with the bright, loose-sheet-metal note that threads through the basin’s endless chorus. They do not fly so much as ricochet between the islands of vegetation, leaving brief ghost trails of their own outlines hanging in the mist like unfinished drawings. When they land they drink: proboscises of coiled copper wire unspool into the condensate that beads on pale translucent leaves, and the veins of those leaves briefly brighten with the same metallic sheen as the drinker. After a fixed number of sips the clatter-wing launches again, always along the same short vector, always striking the same notes. Their abundance is impossible to census because many of them are after-images of themselves half a second displaced—echoes still performing the motion while the denser body has already moved on. They are the bright, restless sparking of the factory’s nervous system.

Along the single drainage rill that never quite empties crawls the heavy thing. It is longer than a man is tall and composed of too many joints, each joint a dark knuckle of wet stone fused to a sleeve of ridged, oil-black hide. It progresses by a slow concertina motion, segments bunching and extending in perfect time with the muffled iron heartbeats rising from deeper in the rock. The head—or the end that leads—is a set of interlocking pliers the color of old blood and wet hematite. The pliers open and close on nothing, forever, with a soft hydraulic sigh. Sometimes a pale liverwort frond or a stray rivulet of warmer coolant-water is caught between them and crushed into scentless paste that the creature does not ingest so much as press back into the rill, as though performing a quality-control ritual without product. Its underbelly leaves a shining trail that is neither mucus nor machine oil but something intermediate; the trail evaporates at the exact rate new trail is laid, so the path remains constant. Translucent after-images of earlier positions linger along its length like a multiple-exposure photograph of labor that will never finish. Occasionally smaller jointed mites—no larger than thumbnails, all pincer and polished carapace—crawl in the grooves between its segments, cleaning, adjusting, then vanishing into seams that seal behind them. The heavy thing does not hunt or flee or rest. It is the basin’s tributary made ambulatory, the living expression of drainage that can never complete itself.

There are other forms, less constant in outline. In the deeper cushions of moss something many-rooted and softly luminous pulses—an organism that is mostly buried, sphincter mouths opening at metronomic intervals to release timed puffs of warmer vapor that smell of iron and crushed needles. Pale, almost weightless flutterers the size of spoons peel themselves free of the translucent leaf-fans at intervals, spiral once on wings of etched foil, then settle back into the exact crevice they left, folding until they are indistinguishable from the leaf again. Thin, upright walkers on two or three needle legs stalk the bare scree with the rigid grace of surveying instruments; their heads are clusters of dark, moisture-beaded lenses that continually measure distances which never change. When two meet they stand motionless for a precise number of stone-thuds, exchange a brief spray of condensate from facial vents, then continue on mirrored routes that will bring them to the same meeting again with no memory and no variation.

All of them—browsers, clatter-wings, the jointed crawler, the pulse-mosses, the foil-flutterers, the needle-walkers—share the same secret architecture. Inside, where blood or sap might run, there are fine channels of warmer fluid that throb in time with the mountain’s deep percussion. Their tissues contain laminations that look under any imagined microscope like stamped plate and stretched cartilage occupying the same layer. They do not eat to grow; they process to continue. They do not mate; they simply maintain the population density the basin’s single frozen moment requires. When damage occurs—a leg sheared by flaking rock, a wing-case cracked—the injured part is worked free by companions or by the creature itself and left on the wet stone, where it is already being replaced by an identical part growing outward from the wound at the exact rate the old part decays. Nothing is discarded. Nothing accumulates. The industrial logic is metabolic.

Over it all hangs the sense that these beings are less inhabitants than local thickenings of the basin’s own permanent weather. The mist weeps upward through them as easily as around them. The cool dim light passes through their after-images without friction. Their sounds are not added to the stone’s iron heartbeats but are keyed harmonics of it. They are what happens when a young mountain range, still sharp and unfinished, suddenly acquires the habit of running a factory that produces only its own endless mid-cycle—no goods, no waste, no progress, only the sustained, beautiful, riveted roar of process itself.

None of them looks up at the cloud ceiling a few body-lengths overhead. None of them approaches the rim where the stone drops into pale nothing. They cannot leave because leaving would require time, and time is the one element the basin has perfected the art of refusing. They continue. They have always been continuing. In the hanging basin the workers and the work and the wet green machinery are a single closed loop of damp iron life, and the loop is the only soul the place will ever need.
