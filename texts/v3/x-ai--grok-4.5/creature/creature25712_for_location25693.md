The ones who live here are slight, short-boned, and quietly sleepless.  

At first glance they read as human, or nearly so: bipedal, five-fingered, faces arranged in the familiar order of brow and mouth and urgent eyes. But the longer you watch them thread their single-file lanes, the more the resemblance thins. Their skin is the same pale clay as the basin walls, matte when dry, faintly luminous when wet, and it is almost always wet. A fine mineral bloom forms on their forearms and collarbones overnight, the same translucent crust that hardens on the ledges; they scrape it off each morning with flat wooden blades and save the flakes in little reed pouches, later to be remelted into fresh cutting edges or the soft amber panes that serve them as windows. The bloom is not dirt. It is theirs. It weeps from them the way it weeps from the stone.

They move as if the ground were still deciding whether to hold them. Feet are long, splayed, the toes slightly webbed with a thin gray membrane that gathers the shallows and steadies each step on slick ledges. They prefer bare skin to any sole; leather softens and rots too quickly here, and the cool mud is information. A child can tell, by the suck against her arch, whether a hairline crack opened under last night’s mist. An elder knows by the same sensation when the basin’s water table is about to breathe upward and swallow the lowest thresholds. Their calves and lower backs are roped with lean muscle from a lifetime of bracing against surfaces that settle a finger’s width between dusk and dawn.

Voices stay low not only from courtesy but from anatomy. The throat is narrow, the palate high and oddly ridged, so that speech emerges as a soft, continuous susurrus edged with clicks of tongue against those ridges—sounds that carry cleanly along the wet corridors without echoing too far into a neighbor’s sleep. They speak often of the stone as if it were a slow relative: “She shifted again under the third stoop,” “The east face is sweating salt this morning.” Names for one another are short and watery—syllables that can be whispered without breaking the ticking rhythm of seepage.

Their eyes are large and set slightly farther apart than expected, the irises a flat mineral gray that darkens only when the mist thickens. In the directionless light they catch faint glints, like the temporary glass they set into wall openings. Night vision is indifferent; they sleep in short, overlapping shifts so that someone is always awake to pack a newly opened crack or to lift a storage jar before the rising shallows claim it. Sleep itself looks shallow. They curl on raised clay platforms layered with reed mats that must be dried daily, knees drawn up, faces turned toward the nearest weeping wall as though listening.

Children are born small and already damp, their skin slick with the same mineral film. For the first seasons they are carried in slings of woven rush against the parent’s chest, where they can mouth at the bloom and begin to learn its taste—bitter, chalky, necessary. By the time they walk they know how to edge sideways through a lane no wider than a turned shoulder, how to test a roof beam with the knuckles before trusting weight to it, how to press fresh mortar (thumb-mixed from crushed ledge-crust and spit) into a fracture before it widens. Play is quiet and practical: races to see who can re-lash a dripping eave fastest, contests of balance on the half-formed ridges while the adults below murmur approval or soft warning.

They keep no animals larger than the occasional water vole coaxed into a reed cage for its meat and soft bones (needles, fishhooks, the delicate pins that hold turf roofs against wind). Fish are rare; the basin has not yet been claimed by them. What protein they take comes mostly from frogs, pale grubs mined from the clay, and a bland starchy paste scraped from the inner pith of the wiry rushes. Meals are eaten standing or crouched in doorways, bowls of fired mudstone cradled close to conserve warmth. Fire is used sparingly—small, smokeless knots of dried algae and rush pith kept alive inside clay chimneys that vent sideways into the lanes so the precious heat is shared. Smoke would cling too long under the low cloud.

The mild strangeness lives in their relationship to time and solidity. Because nothing here finishes becoming permanent, they have no architecture of monument and almost no inheritance of objects. A cutting blade of the mineral glass is used until it softens, then discarded back into a cistern to dissolve and rejoin the general weeping; a new one is grown overnight on a drying rack. Houses are lifelong works of continuous repair rather than possessions. When a dwelling’s foundation sags too far, the family simply unlaces the roof, carries the reed mats and storage jars two lanes over, and begins cutting a fresh platform into the still-rising clay. The old walls are left to slump and be reabsorbed. No one grieves this. The basin is young and still choosing its final shape; they understand themselves as part of that choosing.

Once in a great while—after a heavy rain when the curtain of seepage becomes a roar and the marsh edge floods the outer houses to the knee—something older seems to move through them. They pause in their work, press palms flat to the nearest wet wall, and stand in silence while the stone settles another fraction. In those moments their eyes unfocus and the mineral bloom thickens across their cheeks like a sudden frost. Whether they are listening or answering is unclear. When the pause ends they return to packing cracks and raising thresholds, a little more certain of the next necessary inch.

They are not trapped here. The surrounding country is only a day’s walk across firmer ground, yet they do not leave. The open land feels unfinished to them in the wrong way—too dry, too its own, lacking the intimate conversation of water and young stone. This shallow basin, this dense and dripping hive of shared walls and constant quiet labor, is the only place that matches the rate at which they themselves settle and unsettle. They are the brief consensus made flesh: rising, weeping, packing themselves into every available inch before the ground can change its mind.
