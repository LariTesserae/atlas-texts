The basin does not announce its tenant at once. What lives here has learned the long patience of stone that weeps, and so it withholds itself in the same way the light withholds hard edges. You notice first only the agreements it has made with the lesser throngs.

The pale amphipods part around certain seams in the cobble teeth, not in fear but in habit, as ink might part around a character already written. The spit-grey frogs leave small wet asterisms of space on the benches when they chorus; their throats work hardest nearest the face-sized holes bored through the standing slabs, as though the polished circles still remember being passages and demand tribute in sound. Even the leather-wings—those damp, dog-sized hangings—shift their hooks in subtle unison when the hidden mouth coughs up a fresh lens of water. They do not flee the rising. They adjust, the way curtains adjust to a change in the room’s breathing. Their guano tears run a little faster then, and the acid finds pre-worn channels that look almost deliberate, almost tended.

Only after the water has eased and the midges have rebuilt their soft towers does the tenant itself begin to cohere out of the overlapping processes.

It is not one body. It is a continuity of bodies that have forgotten how to finish. Where the rubble cores of the walls are loosest, where the willow’s wrist-thin trunk has crowded its roots into the ancient clay, the mortar itself has grown a slow musculature. Pale cables—neither root nor tendon nor the discarded skins of any larval thing—thread out in the damp dark and retract again, tasting the seepage. They are the thickness of a child’s wrist at first, then thinner, then almost hair. When they withdraw they leave the joints dark for a time, as if the stone has blushed. Sometimes a cable ends in a cluster of soft, blind pads that press against the underside of a cobble until the blunt tooth rocks almost imperceptibly, settling into a new, more comfortable bite with its neighbors. The floor is still being laid, centuries after the last human hand quit the work.

Higher, in the shadowed porticoes, the same continuity takes other thicknesses. It hangs among the leather-wings, not quite one of them. Its own wings—if the membranes stretched between elongated fingers can still be called wings—are veined with the same mortar-pale cables. They glisten, not with guano but with the constant fine rain that is the basin’s breath. When it drops, the glide is heavier, more deliberate. It does not snap at amphipods. It skims the surface with a trailing fringe of those tactile pads and drinks the temperature of the water, the mineral.load, the faint electric sizzle of the stridulators. Then it returns to the wall and presses its whole length into a course joints until it is only another scar among the chisel marks. The blocks accept it. In places the dolerite-pounder bites have been rubbed smoother by this repeated entering and leaving; the stone has begun to remember a softer tool.

It uses the face-holes. Not as windows—there is nothing beyond the drum-walls worth seeing—but as filters. A long, jointed extremity (one cannot call it an arm without sensing how many other names it has already worn out) reaches through from the inner dark and spreads filtula of translucent skin across the polished circle. Water beads on that skin, crawls, merges, falls. What remains on the membrane is a dust of everything the air and the seepage carry: midge scales, pollen that no longer knows its flower, the microscopic teeth of long-dead amphipod generations, tiny star-points of dissolved copper from tools abandoned so deep in the walls that even the roots have not yet found them. The tenant tastes this residue. Information moves through the cables that link every thickness of it—through the wall-muscles, the hanging forms, the under-cobble threads—until the entire basin has, for a moment, one slow thought: that the mineral balance has shifted a fraction since the last rising, that the willow is mining too greedily at the northern gutter, that a leather-wing has torn a membrane and will need to be pressed into the guano-streaks until the acids cauterize and the flesh forgets it was hurt.

It repairs. That is the nearest the place comes to prayer. When the freeze-spalling loosens a flake of reverse, cables lift the flake and press it back, or else carry it down to the hexagonal troughs where the water can tumble it into a softer shape and the amphipods can carpet it until it belongs again. When one of the concentric benches cracks, the tenant weeps a finer mortar of its own—calcium and clay and the ground pearls of its shed skins—into the wound and then lies across the dished surface for nights or weeks, warming the mend with the same patience the original builders once spent on sand and antler. The benches remain warm longest where it has lately rested; the frogs prefer those seats.

It has no face that remains a face. Sometimes, pressed to a weep-joint, a portion of it thins and stretches until the suggestion of eyes—two, or clusters, or a single migrating sheen—forms and dissolves. Sometimes a mouth appears only as a place where the surface film of a canal is drawn upward in a trembling meniscus and then released, tasting. It does not speak aloud. The chorus of frogs, the whip-crack of hunting wings, the high sizzle, the decided rain—all these it borrows, thickening or thinning them until the sound-crowding itself becomes a kind of utterance. On nights when the slate sky lowers until it almost completes the vanished roof, the basin can be heard to hum: a chord made of water finding water, of membranes flexing, of stone accepting soft weight. The hum is lowest above the flexed ridges floors. Those ridges are not scars of vanished roots. They are the places where the tenant once breached from below in an older, hungrier shape, tasted the open air, and then folded itself back into the pavement rather than leave the basin. The cobbles there are warmest. If you set your palm against them long enough, you feel the cables slide past one another like slow thoughts.

It is not lonely. Loneliness would require an outside. The amphipods are its blood’s impatient cousins; the leather-wings are its dreams of flight parked for later; the willow is a mistake it has decided to keep, a green wound it feeds with careful seepage so the failure will remain beautiful. The builders are still here, dissolved into the same continuity—their chisel rhythms preserved in the way the cables prefer to flex, their vanished plates still worn by every scraping passage of pads across the hole-rims. What lives in the basin is the basin’s decision to continue. It has no name that could be shouted across the two-heartbeat space and receive an answer. It answers only by adjusting the water, by polishing what needs polish, by hanging a little denser in the porticoes when the rain is deciding whether to fall.

And when the sudden lens of cold rises again and every lesser life thrashes or collapses, the tenant spreads itself through all its thicknesses at once—wall and wing and underfloor and membrane—and holds the place steady until the excess drains. Then it gathers the new debris (larval ghost, iridescent feather, shell-chip bead) and carries each piece to where it will best deepen the mosaic. The greying teeth of the floor grow a fraction more articulate. The air remains crowded. The difference between ecosystem and machine remains irrelevant. Something lives here that has become the wet cup of the world, and the cup, in turn, has become a long, unfinished animal whose hide is stone and whose blood is always just appearing between the joints.
