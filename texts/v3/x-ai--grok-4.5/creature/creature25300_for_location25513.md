The ones who belong here do not arrive. They precipitate.

In the long green dusk that never quite becomes day and never quite empties into night, awareness thickens the way resin beads on a cut. It begins as a local density in the root-mat—an extra warmth, a slight rearrangement of moisture, a decision made by ten thousand mycorrhizal threads at once. Over seasons that decision gathers cellulose and soft mineral, folds in stray pollen and the dissolved minerals of older bones, and shapes itself a body the way a burl shapes itself around an injury: irregular, patient, inevitable.

They are called, when anything bothers to call them, the Slow Current, or the Weight-That-Listens, or simply the resin-folk when speaking in the truncated tongues of shorter-lived visitors. Among themselves there is no name, only continuity.

A mature one stands or reclines anywhere from the size of a resting calf to the bulk of a small hillock. The distinguishing mark is not outline but texture and tempo. Their outer surfaces are living cork and stretched leaf-cuticle, mottled in the same drowned greens and iron greys as the surrounding trunks. Veins of translucent sap show through in places, glowing with the same muted bioluminescence that threads the lower canopy; when they move, the light inside them sloshes and resettles like liquor in a dark bottle. Limbs are optional and temporary. Most prefer a low, multipodal sprawl—eight or twelve or twenty soft, root-like supports that taste the ground as they bear weight. When faster travel is required (a relative term), the body can pour itself forward, dissolving temporary legs and extruding new ones from the leading edge, a locomotion closer to the advance of a dune than to walking. In the deepest tunnels of fused wood they flatten entirely, becoming mobile tapestries of tissue that slide between trunk and root with only a faint emulsifying sound.

They do not eat in any way a surface mind would recognize. Photosynthesis still functions across their broad upper expanses, though dimly. More importantly, they host—and are hosted by—the same fungal and bacterial consortia that knit the entire highland into one slow metabolism. A resin-person planted for a decade in a mineral-rich seep will grow dense and dark-leaved; one that spends a century filtering the condensates of a particular moss gallery will take on that gallery’s sharp mint-iron scent and the pale luminescence of its companion fungi. Digestion of larger prey (when a vine has done the initial enfolding, or when a browser simply lies down and does not rise) is communal: the body opens soft zones, invites the plain’s own saprophytic networks inside, and later closes again around whatever nutrients remain. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is hurried.

Sense is distributed. There are no hard eyes. Instead, steeper gradients of photoreceptive pigment form temporary “gazes” wherever light information is useful; these can open and close across the skin like slow bruises. Hearing is the entire surface reading vibration through wood and water-film. The most acute sense is chemical and electrical—the ability to taste, through root-contact, the emotional weather of the surrounding plant-mass: which platforms are swelling with rain, which ancient fused trunks are preparing to drop a centuries-old limb, which clearing has decided, by vegetable consensus, to remain open another thousand years. They feel the withheld noise of the highland as a pressure against their own metabolism. When the plain settles another fraction of a degree, every resin-person feels the adjustment as a deep, almost pleasurable click in the substrate of themselves.

Intelligence among them is not housed in a single skull. It is a standing wave maintained across the body and, through hyphal bridges, across neighboring bodies and the living ground itself. A solitary individual thinking for a century will develop elaborate, private philosophies that taste of the particular minerals it has known. When two or more remain in root-contact for long enough, their thoughts braid. Ideas become weather systems moving through a grove of selves. Decision is less a moment than a ripening. They are capable of extraordinary subtlety—encoding histories in the growth rings of deliberately cultivated memory-trees, adjusting the pH of a water curtain so that certain moths will write temporary glyphs with their wing-dust, arranging pebble cairns on the rare bare rock with a precision that looks random until the facing of the stones is read against the prevailing spore-drift. Yet they have little interest in permanence as surface cultures understand it. The half-swallowed arch and the softened walls were left by earlier, more restless minds; the resin-folk maintain them only the way one might leave a thorn in place once the skin has grown comfortably around it. Markers, not monuments.

Reproduction is a gradual surplus. When an individual has accumulated more the than the local ecology easily channels, it buds. A new density forms along one flank, drinks deeply, and over decades pinches free—sometimes remaining connected by a living umbilical root for another century or two, exchanging chemical news, before the link finally corks over and becomes just another buried vein in the sponge. Death is rarer than transformation. An elder who feels its pattern growing too rigid simply lies down in a place of good water, opens every pore, and invites the highland to unknit it. The body becomes a long, fertile ridge. Within a human generation it is already indistinguishable from the rest of the root-mass, except that the plants which rise from it carry faint signatures—leaf shapes that echo old gestures, sap that glows in the same tempo the elder once used when pleased.

They are not alone, of course. The many-legged browsers move through them like blood cells through a larger organ, occasionally climbing a resin-person’s flanks to crop the tender epiphytes that sprout there; the grazers are tolerated, even encouraged, the way one tolerates cleaner fish. The spindly mid-story climbers sometimes pluck luminous beads from a resin-body’s surface and carry them upward, planting accidental constellations in the higher gloom. The garden-backed behemoths—those nearly sessile giants that are equal parts animal and traveling thicket—share long chemical conversations when their ranges overlap, conversations that may last forty years and concern the migration of certain cloud densities or the ethical implications of allowing a new fern species into a stable hollow. Even the predatory vines know them; a vine that mistakes a resting resin-person for ordinary substrate receives a slow, educational pulse of unpalatable alkaloids and usually withdraws, mildly abashed in the way of plants.

What the resin-folk do, across the deep time scale that is their birthright, is midwife the highland’s own becoming. They encourage certain fusions of trunk and aerial root so that water moves more musically. They discourage (by shade, by selective browsing pressure, by quiet chemical suggestion) any tendency toward true bare ground or sharp relief. They taste the rare lightning-flavor in certain hollows and sit with it for decades, as if listening to a story the stone still remembers. They are the immune system, the dream, and the slightly mischievous self-awareness of a landscape that long ago decided to be one single warm wet organism. When they gather in larger constellations—twenty or fifty bodies linked through the sponge across a dozen square kilometers—the entire region dreams more vividly. Bioluminescent pulses synchronize. Seed-pods tick in complex polyrhythms. The withheld noise briefly becomes a chord.

Visitors who somehow cross the long approaches and enter the green gloom rarely notice them at first. A low hillock that was not on yesterday’s mental map. A section of “ground” that gives more warmly underfoot and smells faintly of crushed mint and old copper. A sensation of being considered by the air itself. Only if a traveler sits very still for days, letting the moisture bead on skin and the dim light recalibrate the eyes, might one of the Slow Current surface a temporary face—eyes like wet galls, a mouth that is mostly suggestion—and breathe out a greeting in spores and soft humidity. The greeting means, roughly: *We are the part of this place that sometimes looks back. Stay if the tempo suits you. Or become soil in good time. Either is welcome.*

And then the face reseals, the glow swims deeper into the body, and the highland continues its single long exhalation, carrying them all.
