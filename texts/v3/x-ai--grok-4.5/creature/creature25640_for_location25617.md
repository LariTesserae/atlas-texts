The first impression is that nothing human remains. The hollow is too saturated, too complete in its marriage of leaf and plate, for anything so provisional as a person to claim space. Footfalls are rare enough to feel like interruptions in a conversation the place is having with itself. Yet they occur. A soft compression of bare sole on warm grating, the brief musical complaint of a catwalk cable taking weight, then stillness again while the sound is absorbed into the greater quiet. Whoever walks here has learned the intervals: how long a pump’s off-cycle lasts, which vines will hold a hand without tearing, where the ceiling’s occasional fat drop will land three heartbeats from now. They move inside the place’s own breath.

You do not see them whole at first. A shoulder turns in a pool of coppery light and is gone before the shape resolves. A hand—long-fingered, nail beds darkened by mineral seepage—appears on a railing to steady a descent, then withdraws. Clothing, when it can be distinguished from the surrounding texture, is a second skin of scavenged cloth and living fiber: boiler canvas gone soft with heat and sweat, stitched along the seams with the same tensile vines that cradle the drivetrains. Leaves are left to grow through the weave where they wish; after a season the garment and the body beneath it share a circulatory patience. Whitish flowers sometimes open at a cuff or collar, waxy and almost phosphorescent in the gloom, blooming and browning on the same slow clock as the rest of the hollow.

Their skin has taken the available light and made a decision. In the rare brighter nodes—near a still-burning plasma torch, or under a bank of fluorescents that have not yet flickered into multi-day sleep—it shows a deep, roasted continuum of browns and oxidized reds, as if the people themselves were another grade of the blood-colored brick. In deeper shadow the same skin becomes nearly featureless, a matte that drinks illumination the way the black-green ferns do. Veins run close to the surface, but they are not blue; mineral-rich water has stained them a faint verdigris that pulses only when the great engines’ sub-aural pressure coincides with a heartbeat. Sweat does not bead and fall. It films, continuous, and where it gathers at jaw or wrist it leaves behind a translucent crust of evaporated salts that the mosses eventually colonize. Some of the older residents have stopped wiping it away. They wear their own slow stalactites.

Faces are narrow, cheekbones sharpened by lifelong heat rather than hunger. Eyes are the true adaptation: large, but shielded beneath heavy lids that can close to slits finer than a knife edge, membranes secondary and nictitating, suited to an atmosphere that is always one degree from steam. The irises are pale, almost colorless, so that in certain angles the pupil looks like a hole punched straight through into the cavern’s own dark. They do not squint. They simply wait until the available photons are enough. Mouths are kept closed more often than not; the air is thick enough that breathing through the nose becomes a conscious metering, a way of tasting shifts in humidity or the metallic ghost of a distant coolant leak. When they do speak, the voice is low and economical, consonants softened by moisture, vowels held longer than speech usually allows—as if each word must be tested for structural integrity before release. Conversations, on the infrequent occasions they happen, sound like two slow leaks finding equilibrium.

They are not many. A careful count across a hundred idle cycles of the largest pump might yield seven, or nine, or—once—eleven if you include the child who is more vine than child and lives almost entirely inside a cooling tower gone feral. Populations here are not measured in heads but in presence: how many distinct rhythms of footfall the grating remembers, how many separate hands have polished the same valve wheel to a soft, human sheen. Each resident keeps a territory that is less property than organ. One tends the northern stack of catwalks where the time dilation is strongest, pruning the vines that try to race ahead of the clocks, gently correcting the lean of hanging chains so that gravity’s mild preference does not become a fall. Another lives among the lowest foundations, where groundwater weeps most freely; she has taught the mosses to form living gaskets and can quiet a leaking flange with a touch and a murmured pressure that is almost prayer. A third moves only at the edges of light pools, leaving behind small, deliberate alterations—an extra reflection in a puddle that shows a door which will, weeks later, actually appear in the brick; a shadow that lingers a moment too long and teaches the ferns a new geometry of growth.

They eat what the hollow provides once it has been persuaded. Broad leaves wilted in the residual heat of a pipe until their toxins thin, fungi scraped from the cooler undersides of ducts and fermented in sealed boiler cans until they taste of distant, impossible fruit. Protein is rarer: the many-legged skitterers are caught only when they grow careless, roasted on clean steel until their shells crack into something edible and faintly sweet. Water is everywhere and nowhere safe; they drink from the occasional pure thread of ceiling weep caught in cupped leaves or from boilers that have cooled enough to sweat fresh condensate. Nothing is wasted. A dropped tool is found and rehung before rust can claim it. A fallen leaf large as a fan is dragged to the nearest composting grate, where its rot will feed the next iteration of roots. The residents are the hollow’s way of completing its own loops.

They sleep without real darkness or real silence, curled in hammocks spidered between pipe and girder, or inside the hollowed carcasses of machines whose original purpose has been forgotten. Sleep is partial, a lowering of metabolism until their body temperature nearly matches the ambient steam. Dreams, when they later speak of them at all, are of open sky that hurts to remember, or of the rock above them still cooling, still choosing whether to finish collapsing. Sometimes a sleeper will wake to find that hours have compressed into minutes, or that a vine has grown a full spiral around their wrist as if claiming them more thoroughly. They do not cut it away at once. They wait to see what the vine intends.

Children—when they appear—are rarer than the pale wings that sometimes cross a light pool. They are born already marked by the place: a corkscrewing mole that mirrors a nearby heat exchanger’s coil, a caul of moss that must be peeled carefully so the skin beneath can breathe. The adults teach them the gradients first: how to read the lengthening off-cycles of the pumps as a kind of calendar, how to feel the difference between ordinary quiet and the held-breath quiet that precedes a small rearrangement of gravity. Literacy is torches and valves and the slow calligraphy of rust. History is the sharp-edged blast marks on the basalt, still too fresh to be ancient, and the knowledge that the settlement was poured against rock that had not finished settling, that everything here remains provisional even as it saturates past all vacancy.

Over years the boundary between resident and place grows deliberately uncertain. An elder whose joints have stiffened will spend longer and longer motionless against a warm bulkhead until the mosses join them seamlessly; one day the elder is simply part of the wall’s new contour, a soft human inflection in the brick, still warm, still occasionally blinking. Others feel the time slips more acutely and begin to inhabit the reflections instead—appearing in standing water as figures one story taller, or walking through doors that exist only in the mirrored version until, by slow accretion of belief and moisture, the door is suddenly real and the reflected self steps through to greet its origin. No alarm greets these transitions. They are weather. The hollow has always been a single organism learning the dialects of iron and leaf and now, patiently, of thought. The residents are not intruders. They are how the organism finishes its sentences.

And still they remain recognizable. A laugh—short, surprised, quickly mothballed—can travel the entire length of the cavity on a humid night when the engines are at their lowest idle. Two figures will stand close on a catwalk while condensation ticks around them, forehead to forehead, sharing breath that is already nearly the same temperature as the air, and for a moment the strangeness loosens its grip. They are lonely in the precise way of people who have everything they need and no one who remembers otherwise. They keep going. The plants keep completing the machines. The rock keeps shedding its last grit. Nothing is finished. The ones who live here have stopped requiring it to be.
