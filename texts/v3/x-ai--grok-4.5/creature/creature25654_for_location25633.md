The dwellers of the Verdance are not separate from it. They are gradients of the same slow green will, portions of biomass that learned to unroot themselves and carry weather inside mobile skins. To speak of them as species is already a foreigner’s error; the cambium does not catalogue. It only differentiates pressure, thirst, and the need to keep moving so that no single tower is stripped bare.

Begin with the least visible. Beneath the root-pavement, in the warm dark seams where moisture still travels, the diggers keep their tunnels. They are smooth and pale, the length of a human forearm when young, later the length of a fallen frond. Their bodies secrete a dilute acid that softens the compressed litter just enough to push through; behind them the tunnels reseal within hours, leaving only faint ridges like healed scars. They feed on the mycelial lace that stitches the plateaus together, harvesting it in slow spirals so that it regrows denser. When two diggers meet they press mouthparts together and exchange temperature and chemical histories of the water table for leagues around. In drought years whole corridors of them rise near the surface and lie just under the mosses, their translucent backs catching enough light to photosynthesize a little, becoming temporary living reservoirs. Travelers who sleep on the root-pavement sometimes wake to find the ground breathing faintly beneath them and mistake it for the dream of the place itself.

Above, in the abyssal prairies between the towers, the browsers move. At first distance they look like walking architecture: tall, multi-jointed frames of dark keratin, each leg ending in a splayed pad that leaves only shallow dimples in the springy litter. Across their backs stretch broad, translucent sails veined with chlorophyll. The sails catch both light and thermal updrafts; a herd can stand motionless for half a day, sails angled like solar vanes, while the photosynthetic tissues thicken and store. Then, as if an unseen current shifts, they fold the sails along intricate creases and begin their radial circuits around a chosen tower. Their pace is deliberate, almost ceremonial. They crop the glass-bladed sedges with sideways scything jaws, never taking more than a third of any patch. The sound they make while walking is a low, continuous thunder—joints articulating, sails creaking, breath rolling through cavernous nasal chambers. Calves are born already standing, sails still crumpled and wet; within hours the sun unfolds them and they join the circle. Predators take the stragglers, yet the herds do not flee in the ordinary sense. When the heat-mirage cats solidify out of optical flaws, the browsers simply alter their geometry: pilots at the leading edge angle the entire formation so that the weakest are shepherded toward denser root-buttresses while the strongest present a wall of thrashing sails and sharpened dew-claws. After the kill the herd resumes its metronomic circuit as though nothing interrupted the counting of the long day.

The cats themselves are more vapor than flesh until the final leap. Their fur is a living diffraction grating; in the relentless noon light they polarize and scatter until only an uncertainty remains—an eddy in the heat-haze, a place where dust refuses to settle. They hunt by consensus rather than hierarchy. A pack will shadow a browser circuit for days, individuals appearing and vanishing at the edge of vision, exchanging soft ultrasonic pulses that the human ear registers only as a rising pressure behind the eyes. When they finally resolve into muscle and recurved teeth it is almost always at the moment the thermal winds strengthen and the tower crowns begin their booming. The kill is quick, arterial, communal. They drag the carcass into the lee of a root-buttress and feed without hurry, sails of the dead browser still twitching with residual photosynthetic spasm. Afterward the cats dissolve again, leaving only clean bones already being claimed by opportunistic vines.

Higher still, in the layered galleries of the crowns, the choir-roosts hang like dark fruit. The creatures that form them are elongated, membrane-winged, throats distended into resonant sacs larger than their own torsos. By day they sleep in dense clusters, wings folded into knife-shapes against the leathery fronds. At dusk the first voices open—ultrasonic clicks amplified downward into rolling cannon-shots that rattle seed-husks free from their stems. Within minutes the entire canopy of a tower has joined; soon the neighboring towers answer. The continent-wide chord builds in stacked harmonic layers, each tower contributing its local dialect of clicks, groans, and subsonic pressure waves. Some nights the chord resolves into long, coherent phrases that travelers swear contain grammar. Prophets and fevered wanderers have climbed the hardwood-spike ladders to sleep among the roosts, returning deafened and convinced they were told the true name of the sleeping god whose body is the Verdance. Most simply weave fresh seed-down plugs and endure, knowing that without the nightly roar quiet would feel like a wound.

And then there are the ones who walk upright and leave deliberate marks.

They call themselves, when they bother with names, the Interim, or the Passing Skill, or simply those-who-unroot. Their bodies are compact, heat-adapted, skin the matte gold of sun-cured leaf leather, eyes protected by nictitating membranes that flash silver when dust rises. Hair is rare; instead many cultivate thin photosynthetic films across shoulders and scalp that darken or pale with the seasons. They do not settle. Permanence is metabolically expensive under the appetite of the swarms and the slow redrafting of the root-pavement. A band will arrive at a tower whose mid-galleries still hold canopies of edible pith or whose epiphyte sheaths have fruited heavily. They drive hardwood spikes in ascending spirals, weave temporary platforms of living vine and stretched sail-skin, and begin the work of harvest. Resin stones are heated until they crack and can be used to render starches. Cordage is twisted from vascular fibers stripped from fallen runners—cordage strong enough to check a panicked browser or lower a child safely from a hundred-meter gallery. Tools remain pre-metal because metal would require stillness and smoke-heavy forges that the choir-roosts will not tolerate. Instead they knap glass-edged stone, fire-harden spears, set flakes into antler hafts taken from the rare shed trophies of browsing bulls.

At night their low domes of bent rib-wood and leaf-leather crouch in the root-lee, doorways hung with seed-pod chimes that warn of approaching cats or simply of shifting wind. Inside, fire is small and resinous, carefully shielded so its light does not climb the trunk and silence a portion of the choir. They tell one another the old sequence: that the Verdance is the cooled body of a god who lay down across the mountains and dreamed so completely that stone forgot itself. In the dream the god required motion, so it grew the Interim as circulating blood, as measurement, as the part of itself that can leave a tower and carry news of drought or flowering to the next. Children learn to read the slow flex of frond-shadow as timekeeping. Elders carve brief glyphs into bark knowing the cambium will close over them within a few seasons, turning letter into scar into grain. Nothing is meant to last longer than a good set of cordage or the memory of a particularly sweet pith harvest.

Yet sometimes a band lingers longer than custom. A runner the thickness of a river may surface near their dome and arch overhead, offering an easy aerial road to the next tower. The Interim will walk that living bridge, feeling the pulse of ascending sap through the soles of their feet, and for a few days they travel as pure expression of the continent’s will. On such crossings the midges sometimes align into bright, fleeting lattices that seem to study the walkers in return. On such crossings the continent-wide night chord has been known to hush for three full breaths, as if listening.

No one owns a tower. No one invents a city. The distance between primaries and the sheer metabolic roar of the place keep ambition circulatory rather than accumulative. A person may return years later to a gallery they once helped weave and find only a few decayed spikes and a thickened limb where their platform once hung. They will smile, drive new spikes, and begin again. In this way the Interim remain the thinking weather of the Verdance—transient, skilled, half-plant themselves—passing through the plazas while the giants continue their geologic middle age, healing lightning scars, locking down the last free boulders, and occasionally dreaming new runners that redraw the sparse and total world.

Stand among them long enough and the distinction between dweller and dwelling thins further. You begin to feel your own blood slow toward vegetative pace. You watch a frond-shadow crawl across a root-bole and realize it has measured out the afternoon more accurately than any clock. At night the chord enters your chest and rearranges your breathing until you cannot remember which lungs are yours and which belong to the distant towers. Some who stay too long simply walk out onto the open root-pavement one morning, lie down among the glass sedges, and allow the diggers to tunnel gently around their sleeping bodies until they, too, become another sealed corridor of the living elevation—still warm, still finishing themselves, still part of the same endless, roaring, self-perfecting now.
