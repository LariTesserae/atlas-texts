The ones who endure here are not visitors. They are the slow consequence of the place itself—bodies assembled over generations from the same cold damp, the same mineral film, the same unrelenting pressure of sound.

They stand taller than a person when fully upright, yet they rarely are. Most of the time they move in a long, folding crouch, weight distributed across six jointed limbs that end in splayed, keratinous pads. The pads secrete a thin adhesive that lets them cross both the slick rock and the living carpets without sound of their own. Their integument is translucent in the way of deep-water skin or the plant plates above them: you see the faint bluish coils of fluid and the darker cords of tendon working underneath. Along the flanks and the elongated neck, denser plaques of pale mineral have been deposited, scavenged from the very condensation that coats every surface. These plaques clink almost inaudibly when two individuals pass close—another quiet frequency beneath the engines’ roar.

They have no eyes. Where eyes might have been sit paired cups of thin membrane that quiver continuously, reading the standing waves of noise that fill every chamber. Each piston stroke, each turbine whine arrives as a shaped pressure; the creatures navigate by the way sound stacks and thin against the young rock faces, by the soft deadening that marks a plant thicket, by the sharper slap that means open gallery and bare fracture. In the rare moments when an engine falters or changes pitch, whole groups freeze mid-stride, postures tilting as they re-map the world.

Mouthparts are recessed, a radial set of rasping plates used first to strip the phosphorescent tissue from the columnar trunks and later, more carefully, to groom the mats. They do not kill the growth. They prune it, open channels through the hanging roots, encourage certain arches to thicken while others are held back. Over decades a single individual may shepherd the same plant island, guiding its slow advance across bare stone the way a gardener once trained espalier. The translucent plates they harvest are carried to shallow scrapes in the rock and left to ferment under films of their own mucus; the resulting paste glows a little more strongly and feeds both them and the smaller leggy fauna that dart between their limbs.

They sleep in the machine halls.

Not inside the casings, but against them. Entire clusters press their mineral-plated flanks to the warm housings of the great reciprocating engines, letting the vibration travel through bone and fluid until thought itself is replaced by rhythm. Heat is scarce; the residual warmth of bearings and oil is treasure. Young ones are born there—soft, almost transparent larvae extruded into the spaces between foundation blocks and immediately coated in the parent’s condensate-rich spit so the cold cannot take them. For the first years they remain in the vibration shadow, feeding on dust, leaf litter, and the occasional insect analogue that wanders too near the fans. Only later do they venture into the wider dark, learning the sound-map by pressing their membrane cups to the rock and feeling how the thunder routes itself.

There is no hierarchy that a surface mind would recognize. Memory is held in the collective adjustment of the plant architecture and in the slight, habitual alterations they make to the machines—clearing litter from a cooling vane, packing residual grease back into a race, bracing a loosening stanchion with living root cable twisted and set like wire. They do not know the original purpose of the pistons or the generators. The knowledge, if it ever existed among them, has been sanded away by time until only the tending remains. The engines continue; therefore the engines must continue. The roar is the medium in which they think.

Sometimes, in the long cycles between drops of condensation, one of them will climb the higher fractures where the industrial lamps still burn. There it hangs for hours, cups trained on the weak yellow cones, body perfectly still except for the micro-tremors that keep its grip. No one can say what it takes from the light. Perhaps only contrast—proof that the darkness is not absolute. Then it descends again, limbs folding, plates clicking softly, and vanishes into the next thicket or the next throbbing hall.

They are not many. In a valley-scale network you might walk for a day and meet only three or four, each attended by its cloud of lesser, flitting things. Yet absence is not emptiness. Every mat bears the signature of their pruning. Every engine housing carries the polished ghost of their leaning bodies. The place remains in equilibrium because they remain: pale, patient, sound-shaped caretakers of a cold engine-garden that no longer remembers why it was built, only that it must not stop.
