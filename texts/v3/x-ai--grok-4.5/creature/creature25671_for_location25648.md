The ones who remain move like slow weather through the finished stone.

They are not many. You could walk the entire length of the Hold at blue dusk and meet fewer than a dozen, each appearing only when the lane turns or a doorway exhales a thinner dark. Their bodies are spare, long-boned, built for conservation rather than display. Skin the color of dry mortar, stretched tight over wrists and cheekbones, takes on a faint mineral sheen where the wind has burnished it year after year. Hair, when they have it, is the dull silver of lichen or the chalky blue-green of the single juniper; it grows slowly, is cut rarely, and is never ornamented. Eyes are pale, the washed gray of water that has sat too long in stone, and they do not hurry to focus. When they look at you—if they look—it feels less like being seen and more like being measured against the existing stillness to see whether you disturb the balance.

They wear what the place itself provides: cloaks and tunics of felted greathair taken from the wiry mountain beasts that descend only in the hungriest winters, dyed with the same crushed lichens that seal their roofs. The cloth is the exact gray-brown of the walls. Sandals are cut from thick hide and bound with thongs that last a lifetime; most go barefoot inside the rooms, their soles hardened into the same smooth hollows the thresholds already know. Around the neck or wrist a single bead of the soft gray metal, drilled and polished by hand, no larger than a seed. That is the whole of their jewelry. Nothing rings when they walk.

Their hands are the clearest map of what they are. Long fingers, blunt nails, palms cross-hatched with old callus in the precise patterns of grip: the angle for setting a stone, the pressure for working clay-paste into a crack, the patient rotation of a bone awl. They carry tools the way other people carry names—each tool known, repaired, re-handled across generations so that the object and the hand have grown into one continuous instrument. When a threshold finally wears too thin, three of them will appear at dawn without summons, lift the old stone in silence, and seat the new one cut from the same forgotten face of the quarry. They do not speak while they work. Speech is reserved for the rare moments when the balance itself must be adjusted: a birth, a death, the decision to draw slightly less water from a cistern so the next dry spell will not empty it completely.

They sleep in the upper rooms where the wind chains itself into a constant low thread. Their beds are stone shelves softened by layered felt and the occasional cured hide. They dream little, or dream only of water finding its way and stones settling fractionally closer to one another. In the hours before true light they rise and go to the cisterns with shallow bowls, dip what is needed and no more, and carry it back without spilling. A few drops always remain on the rims; by mid-morning the wind has taken them.

Children are born infrequently and with little ceremony. A woman grows heavy, retires for a span of days into one of the deeper rooms, and returns with a quiet infant whose eyes already seem accustomed to the thin suspension of time here. The child is named after a quality of the place rather than an ancestor—Drift, Click, Veil, Juniper, Hollow—and is taught first how to walk without sounding louder than the wind. By the time the child’s head reaches the height of a low lintel, it knows every joint in the nearest walls the way other children know songs. Play is the re-enactment of maintenance: small hands packing pretend cracks with dust, carrying pebbles from one cistern lip to another. Laughter exists, but it is brief and soft, like the single click of a settling stone, and it does not echo.

Death is quieter still. When one of them feels the long equilibrium tipping inside the body, they simply walk less far each day until they remain in a single room. The others bring water and sit nearby in silence. After the breath stops, the body is carried at night to a narrow cleft on the western ridge where the wind is strongest. There it is laid uncovered on the bare rock. By the end of the next cold season only the softer metal bead remains, pale against stone; someone eventually retrieves it, polishes it once more, and keeps it against the day it will be needed again. No marker is left. The population number does not change.

They are not lonely. Solitude here is simply the natural spacing required by the architecture and the air. When two or three gather in the central court at the hour when the light goes of its own accord, they may stand for a long time without words, watching the juniper, listening to the dry sift of wind through empty lanes. Occasionally one will raise a hand and point to a place where a lichen has advanced a finger’s width since the last rain, or to a shadow that falls differently now that the season has tilted. That is conversation enough. Their language, when used, is spare as the vegetation—short clauses that name what is already present rather than summoning what is not. They have no songs of origin and no prophecies of ending. The Hold has finished becoming, and so have they.

Yet something older than the individual lives moves inside them—an inherited patience that is almost geological. You can feel it when you stand close: a density, a refusal of haste so complete it borders on the mineral. They are not ghosts. Blood still moves under the mortar-colored skin; breath still clouds faintly in the coldest dawns; desire still appears, once in a great while, as a lingering glance or a shared silence that lasts longer than necessity requires. But even desire is temperate here, folded into the larger work of keeping the finished thing finished. They do not yearn outward. The ridges that cradle the saddle are enough horizon. The stars that burn without waver are enough heaven.

If you stayed through a full turning of rains, you would begin to notice how their presence is less like inhabitance and more like a continuing arrangement of the place itself—another form of the same slow weather that beads condensation under the lintels and softens the edges of every stone. They are the Hold remembering how to tend its own stillness. When at last the final lamp gutters and is not re-lit, the rooms will not feel empty. The same measured quiet will remain, breathing through the lanes in the only way it has ever known.
