The ones who live here are never entirely dry, and they have forgotten how to mind.

They move through the wet dark the way the mineral water moves through new stone—slowly, by habit more than decision, filling whatever space is offered and then waiting for the next seam to open. At first glance you might think them human, and in the ordinary accounting of limbs and language they are. Thirty or so metal cubes house them, raised on stilts above the body-hot pools, connected by grated bridges that throng with coin-sized crabs. Laundry never quite dries between the cubes. Breath never quite leaves the air. Their skin has taken on the same muted sheen as the iron walkways: a soft mineral bloom that no amount of wiping erases. Fingernails thicken and cloud. Hair grows heavy with silica dust and is left long, bound back with wire, so that it becomes another surface for the bioluminescent film to colonize in faint blue threads.

They do not hurry. The engines cycle every forty seconds and the people have calibrated their blood to that interval. A conversation begun on the central catwalk may pause while both speakers stand motionless, listening for a shift in the pistons that signals a new vent; when the crabs and the milk-eyed fish finish rearranging themselves around the fresh heat, the speakers simply continue the same sentence as if no time passed. Courtships unfold across seasons of crust accumulation. Children are born already knowing the weight of steam on the lungs. They learn early to sleep under fine mesh tents, not from fear of the midges (which never land) but because the membranous gliders sometimes descend at the dimming of the bulbs, dragging soft leather bellies across any warmth that rises. The gliders are not predators in any urgent sense. They are simply part of the total occupancy of air.

Look longer and the human outline begins to fray into the larger throng.

The settlers’ hands are never empty of the place. Under the nails, in the creases of knuckles, along the damp collars of work shirts, the pale shrimp-things cling and are not brushed away. The shrimp are the length of a finger, translucent, their swarms turning the surface of every pool into living static. Some have begun to live in the cooling pipes themselves, riding the forced currents, emerging at joints to taste the air with thread-like antennae. The people scoop them by the handful when protein is wanted, crushing them raw against the palate; the taste is hot mineral and faint electricity. In return the shrimp clean the sweat from skin and the rust from railings, so that a body left still long enough becomes a temporary reef.

Deeper, the flattened fish the color of wet concrete rise only at the soft percussion of droplets. Their eyes are white and sealed, useless in a world that has no true night or day, only the yellow half-death of caged bulbs and the dull red of engine housings. They nose against the pilings where the crabs migrate in multi-day processions, rasping shells in a sound like distant sandpaper. The crabs carpet every vertical surface that stays wet. They do not flee boots. A settler walking the lower struts simply steps with care, feeling the tiny bodies shift and re-anchor against the iron. Some of the older residents have stopped stepping carefully. Their boot soles are encrusted with living grit that glows faintly for hours after they come inside.

Higher still, in the vaulted dusk where the ceiling continues its slow argument with gravity, the membranous ones glide. They are the size of opened coats, wing-like and veined with darker fluid, edged in soft keratin fringes that trail droplets. Their passage is a leather whisper. They feed on midges, on steam-condensed minerals, on the occasional pale fern that tries to root in cooler seeps. At times they latch onto the upper corners of the living cubes and hang there for days, pulsing gently, shedding a fine iridescent dust that the settlers sweep into jars and use to thicken soups. No one names them. Naming would imply they are separate.

The true inhabitant is the film.

It coats every permanently wet surface in a slick that is not plant, not quite animal, not simply mineral. Under the yellow bulbs it is nearly invisible; in the red engine-glow it wakes into soft blue-green veins that pulse with the same unhurried rhythm as the pistons. The settlers long ago learned the rule: leave it. Scrub a railing clean and within a day the midges thicken into choking clouds. Leave the film and it regulates. It drinks excess heat. It darkens when a new vent is about to open, giving a few minutes’ warning before the floor begin to weep hotter water. Children touch their tongues to it and giggle at the faint fizz, the taste of lightning remembered by stone. Adults press bare palms to the coated walls when the air grows too heavy, and something like calm seeps inward—an exchange of salts, of micro-currents, of information too slow for words. Over years the film has begun to pattern itself deliberately: faint whorls around door frames, branching traces that map the safest paths across slick iron, even crude chevrons near the pump-house that brighten when the engines labor. No one taught it geometry. The geometry simply arrived, the way the stone itself is still arriving.

And beneath all of this—under the three-meter limit where the rock remains plastic—there are the longer thoughts.

Sometimes a settler presses an ear to the unfinished floor and hears not only the gurgle of water finding new paths but a slower pressure, a vast muscular shifting as if the chamber is one chamber in a body that extends through kilometers of basalt still deciding its shape. The people do not speak of this often. When they do, it is in the soft unhurried voices the place itself taught them. They say the Rooms are not finished becoming, and neither are they. Waste heat is currency because heat is how the larger body dreams. A person who sits too long beside a new seep may stand up changed: hair gone white with fresh silica, eyes reflecting the bioluminescent blue even in darkness, voice dropped into the same wet register as the dripping pipes. These ones are not shunned. They become the best listeners for vent-song, the most patient tenders of the engines. They sleep less. They drip more. In time some of them simply walk into the eastern corridor where the deposition is thickest and do not return, leaving only a denser streak of orange iron seep and a faintly brighter patch of film. The others nod. Finishing is not the point.

So what lives here is a single saturated organism wearing many temporary skins. The shrimp and the crabs and the gliders and the milk-eyed fish and the silent ferns and the sweating engines and the soft-spoken bipeds in their rusting cubes are all local densifications of the same slow wet argument. Stand on the central catwalk long enough and you feel yourself included: steam opening your pores, film beginning its quiet cartography across your wrists, the forty-second pulse of the pistons settling into your tibiae. The air no longer feels external. The dripping becomes your own lymphatic weather. Far above, through successive throats of stone, there may still be wind and daylight and the old sharp boundaries of skin. Down here those things have un-happened. What remains is the breathing, the total soft occupation, the patient unfinished yes of bodies that have stopped pretending they end where the water begins.
