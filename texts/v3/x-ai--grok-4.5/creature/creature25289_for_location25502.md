They are not first a people you would name. They arrive in the noticing the way pressure changes arrive—first as a slight adjustment in the hum that every Grove sings into the thin air, then as a pattern of openings and closings along the coral bark, then as warmth that is not quite body-heat moving through the galleries. Only later do shapes distinguish themselves from the living wood that houses them.

The oldest among them have skins the color of the plateau’s xylem plates, ochre and ash-lavender, though the color is not pigment so much as a slow incorporation of the substrate’s own dust. Their flesh has learned the local chemistries. Where a lowlander would carry blood, they carry a cooler emulsion that tastes, if you were ever to taste it, of mineral and faint resin; it does not freeze at the altitudes where they walk the open crust between cities, and it thickens purposefully when the plateau exhales one of its long bass notes so that the vibration passes through them without rupture. Their lungs—if the word still applies—are multilayered membranes that can seal against the copper-dry air or open into broad, leaf-like surfaces when they rest against a municipal phloem tap. Drinking is not a separate act; it is a docking. They press mouth or wrist or the soft hinge behind a knee to the living spout and receive the metered emulsion that tastes of sweetness only because the Grove remembers rain.

They grow into the wood the way the wood once grew into stone. Infants are born soft and nearly translucent inside the unheartwood chambers where gravity has long since been persuaded to behave like a courtesy. The Grove’s immune memory tastes them at once—sap-pressure signatures, the faint electrical bloom of new nerves—and begins the long conversation of recognition. By the time a child can walk the spiral stairs that prune themselves open and reseal, their footsteps leave temporary pheromone-maps in the cambium; doors open not because hinges turn but because the tree has decided this particular pattern of tread and scent belongs. Some children never fully differentiate from the interface. You will see them years later as half-laminae along the inner walls: faces that surface only when the circumferential logic engines need a living interpreter, eyes that track ionic differentials the way others track conversation. They are not trapped. They are simply denser where the city is densest.

Others remain mobile, and their mobility is a true ecology. Walk the mid-level terraces at the hour when the high haze thins enough for the sun to become a coin you could almost spend, and you meet the foragers who still cross the open plateau. They move in loose knots of three or seven, never alone, because the mineral prairie has its own hungers. Their boots (grown, not made, from cork-stone and tenacious mycelial thread) leave almost no mark; the crust reseals behind them with a sound like a held breath released. They carry no water. At the rare ice-fog they stop and let every surface of their clothing and skin drink the sublimating vapor while the crystal-mites boil up in frantic silver herds and the kite-foxes descend in silent, membrane-winged crescents. The foragers trade galls and polished spores with the foxes—an old contract neither side fully remembers making. In return the foxes warning-scream when a square kilometer of surface prepares to lift or yaw, though usually the Briarholds themselves announce it first: that tectonic chord felt in the teeth, in the fillings that some still carry from lower-world ancestries, in the calcium lattice of every bone.

Higher in the crowns, where the last true leaves—black, waxy, almost metallic—turn on millennial hinges, live those who have surrendered locomotion almost entirely. They are the chorus-keepers, the ones who fretted the resonant cavities into their final tunings. Their bodies have elongated and braced; many braid themselves into the leaf-scales so that when the wind rises and plays the Grove like an instrument they become part of the answering chord. Speech among them is partially sung, partially pressure-modulated through the shared vascular webbing. A single conversation can last weeks, its meaning distributed across frequencies too low for ordinary ears and too slow for ordinary patience. Visitors from other Groves sometimes spend years learning to hear one sentence. What they hear, when they finally do, is rarely news. It is calibration: confirmation that the ambient power still hums in its unresolved stack, that the logic engines still compute with sap that remembers older suns, that the long equilibrium holds.

Yet equilibrium is not stillness. Inside every trunk the true crowds never quiet—soft-bodied browsers the size of a forearm that graze the luminous corks of the galleries, thread-snakes lit with their own slow chemiluminescence that map the vascular failures before they clot, diggers whose shovel-skulled heads keep the heartwood from sealing itself into pure mineral death. The posthumans who dwell deepest have partnered with these. Some wear living coats of thread-snake light, using the glow to read the pressure histograms that scroll across the inner bark like script. Others host colonies of the soft browsers along their spines; the browsers feed on shed keratin and excess heat, and in exchange they whisper—chemically, continuously—the health of the surrounding wood. Predation happens, yes: a sudden absence where a browser was, a kite-fox’s needle teeth flashing once in a cavity. But the noise that remains is oceanic, a living murmur of stridulation and wingbeat and sap-pop that rises at times into a roar capable of drowning every deliberate word. Those who have lived here long enough no longer experience the roar as chaos. It organizes. It tells them which galleries are open, which logic districts are dreaming in slow ion, which direction the next slow tectonic courtesy will tilt the walkways.

At the edge of any Grove, where the last root-buttresses sink into the ancient plant-stone, you can stand with them and look outward. They do not call the open space empty. They call it the long hearing. Kilometers of cool reticulated crust under the dim vault, and somewhere beyond the haze another Grove singing its location into the wind. The song is not metaphor. It is the stacked chord of its power cavities, fretted and hummed and wind-played, carrying recognition markers the way other cities once carried radio. The people at the edge listen with the whole surface of their bodies. Sometimes they answer—by striking the buttress roots in complex polyrhythms with tools of mineralized tendon, or simply by standing very still and letting the Grove they belong to modulate its own fretting in response. Information moves. Gossip. Spore reports. The slow news of which younger shoot on the plateau has finally broken the height that matters, which filter-ray flock has changed its tattooed symbionts to a new bruised-fruit palette, which logic engine has begun, after centuries of circumferential thought, to dream in a new constant.

They have never seen a lake. The concept reaches them only as a paradox from the deep ancestral stories: a place where the emulsion runs free and useless across the ground. They do not miss it. Moisture is handled by better chemistries now—methyl ethers traded through stomatal windows the size of old-world arches, crystal hydrates locked and unlocked by the smallest shifts in pressure. When they dream, they dream of roots that once searched downward and of rains that once fell, but the dreams arrive already mineralized, already stiffened into the same stiff beauty as the plateau itself. Grief, when it comes, is quiet and architectural. A friend is lost to an unexpected yaw of heartwood; the city absorbs the body into the immune memory so that for years afterward certain doors will still open for a tread that is no longer there. The loss is real. It is also another fretting of the chord.

Walk one of the higher helices at the hour when the residual light goes copper-green and violet, and you may meet one who has chosen temporary solitude—a rare thing. She sits where gravity has been locally negotiated into a shallow bowl, her back against bark that sweats its clear gel-pane around her like a forming window. Across her lap lies a young kite-fox with a torn membrane; she is stitching it with thread drawn from her own adapted tendon, the needle a sliver of crystallized sap. She does not speak when you approach. Speech is optional here, almost decorative. Instead she lets the Grove taste your signature through the wood, and when recognition passes she shifts sideways so the bowl accommodates you too. For a long time the only sounds are the distant oceanic murmur of the interior crowds, the soft wet sound of stitching, the almost-subsonic tick of the plateau thinking far below. Then, without prelude, she begins to hum a fragment of the city’s unresolved power chord, and the bark behind you both adjusts its resonance until the three of you—woman, fox, guest—are briefly inside one tone. In that tone you understand something that has no lowland translation: that they are not inhabitants of the Briarholds so much as one more pathway the ancient vegetal mass has grown in order to continue hearing itself across the high, dry, endless afternoon.

They remain. They calibrate. They load-bear the weirdness so that certain constants can stay optional and life can stay exact. When a new shoot finally matters, they will prune the first staircases for whoever comes after. When the next ice-fog comes—and it will, in its historical time—they will stop whatever calculation or hunting or slow swaying they are doing and drink with every available surface while the world briefly steams. Then the equilibrium will settle again, thick as law, sparse as the air, loud as the singing cities that are also trees. And you, standing among them long enough, will feel your own edges begin to sand into proverb, your own thirst renegotiated, your own footsteps recognized by wood that has already begun, quietly and cordially, to consider you home.
