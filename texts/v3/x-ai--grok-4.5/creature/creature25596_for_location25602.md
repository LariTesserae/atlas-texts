The first things that move are the ones that never needed names.

They come in low packs across the roof-roads at the hour when the light is least decisive, hard little hooves ticking against the matted bronze foliage like a single long instrument being tuned. Each animal is roughly the size of a mastiff ground down by thrift: deep chest, abbreviated neck, eyes set wide and dark under a shelf of bone that keeps the perpetual grit out. Their coats are the color of the rock after centuries of shoulders have polished it—dull animal sheen, neither gray nor brown. They do not so much graze as harvest with scissors. The sound of a hundred mouths working the metallic leaves is constant, companionable, almost conversational. When a gap opens in a thicket they pour through it without haste, and when the wind veers they simply lean into the new note and keep cropping. Their young are born already knowing the pitch of the wind that means safety and the slightly higher one that means the edge is near. No one herds them. They are simply the town’s moving understorey, the part that walks.

Above them, in every eave and hollow, the short-winged fliers dense themselves. They are not birds exactly. Their bodies are furred rather than feathered, the wings more like stiffened mats of the same silver lichen that carpets the ground, and they launch in sudden clattering veils that sound like dry seeds poured from a height. They catch the thin air in pulses, never gliding far, preferring to erupt, settle, erupt again. At night their colonies thicken the darkness with a low communal drone that threads through the deeper organ note of the wind. Children learn early which colonies will tolerate a slow hand and which will lift as one and leave the ear ringing. The fliers eat the smaller insects that ride the temperature gradients; the insects, in turn, mine the resin blisters of the trees and the soft grit at the roots. Everything is already spoken for.

Lower still, almost mineral themselves, the tough-uddered beasts stand in their stone stalls socketted into living roots. They are larger, slower, built like outcrops that learned to breathe. Their milk is the color of old ivory and tastes of whatever cuticle and salt the creature has processed that season. People speak to them in the same register they use for walls—low, unhurried, testing for hollowness. When an animal finally lies down for the last time its teeth are set into tool handles and its hide is stretched into the roof-roads as another walking surface. Nothing is discarded that can still bear weight or edge.

And then the ones who build.

They are not tall. Height is a memory the mountain no longer keeps, and the people of the Last Shoulder have answered in kind. Their bodies are compact, dense, jointed for climbing ladder-streets and for bracing against the wind’s steady lean. Skin takes the grit the way the rock does: permanently dusted, polished in the places where garments rub or where one shoulder has passed another in a narrow lane a thousand times. The color is the same oxblood-to-bone range as the light itself. Eyes sit deep, adapted to the coppery indoor dusk that passes for day under the canopy and the overhanging upper storeys; tears are almost unknown, moisture too expensive. Lungs are efficient bellows; a person can walk the entire shelf without the thin air becoming a thought. Hair is worn short or bound with vine-cord, because long hair becomes another surface the grit must be negotiated with. Many braid small flutes of hollow stem into the bound hair so that every movement adds a quiet harmonic to the wind’s continuo.

They move the way the lichen advances: by the grain. A journey across town is a sequence of handholds, ladder rungs polished by grandmothers, gaps narrow enough that bread can be passed without fully extending an arm. Their doors are single slabs hung on wooden pintles that have been greased with the same animal fat that feeds the lamps. Inside, rooms are low, walls thick with the slow accretion of generations—dry stone rising straight out of living root, beams lashed with vine hardened in ash, niches already carved for the tools that will outlast the hands that made them. A family may open one new room when a child comes of age and then stop for decades, letting the thicket knit the addition into the whole. Nothing is unfinished. Everything has been leaned into its final practicable shape.

Their voices have learned the wind’s language. They do not shout against it; they speak through it, placing consonants in the brief shadings where the organ note dips, letting vowels ride the longer tones. Conversations carried along a roof-road sound half like song, half like the town itself thinking aloud. Children are taught the bone xylophones early, instruments whose scales match the wind’s favorite pitches, so that even play becomes another strand in the continuo rather than a challenge to it. Names are often short and fretted with fricatives that cut cleanly: a sound that can be recognized when the grit is flying and the fliers are erupting. Lineages are counted in centuries the way the herd beasts are. A person will speak of a great-grandparent’s great-grandparent the way lowlanders once spoke of last year’s weather—something that simply continues.

They drink the milk and the thick sap. They mash the resinous seeds into a sour beer that warms without flooding. Plates are leaf sheaths polished to waterproof; knives are flaked stone classical edges or the larger herd animals’ teeth set in wood. Metal is rare enough to be ceremonial—small copper beads at a throat, a single hammered edge kept for the most exacting work and passed down until it becomes more memory than tool. A wall is raised by eye and mallet over a generation and expected to stand for twenty more. When someone tests a new stretch for voids the hollow boom travels the lanes like a low bell, and neighbors pause to listen with the same attention they give a change in the wind.

At the lip of the Shoulder, where the last soft curve of stone begins its resignation toward the plains, they keep their dead. Niches are pecked into the drop-off, open to the teeth of the wind. The bodies are wrapped in the same waxy leaves they ate while living, the same leaves that form the town’s outer clothing and roofing and the tallies of years. No one pretends the dead are gone. The wind takes their moisture the way it takes everything else, slowly, and the shimmer of salts that glitters in the deep joints of the rock is understood to be part of the same remembering. From that edge a living person can look out across the sanded world—warm under its eternal dust, almost smooth—and feel no fragile claim. The green knot behind them is loud and dim and complete. They do not speak of surviving. Surviving is a word for places that still believe in arrival or departure. Here there is only the continuing.

Some evenings, when the temperature pulse slows and the insect drones thin to a single thread, a few of the older ones sit on the outermost roofs and let the wind play the small flutes in their hair. They are not waiting for anything. The mountain made its last gesture, and they have answered by becoming the gesture’s voice, its density, its thrift, its refusal to be finished. Below them the browsers murmur. Above them the fliers settle. Around them the lichen advances one more thumb-width, unnoticed, exact. The place does not dream of elsewhere. Neither do they.
