The Potting does not keep a single tenant. What lives there is a braided insistence, a slow compaction of appetites that have forgotten how to leave. Begin with the obvious pulse and the picture will not stay still long enough to be wrong.

At first the living things present as the small, practical fauna that use every joint as furniture. The tile-foxes are the most numerous: angular, dust-coated, their bodies faceted like the mosaic they sleep against. They do not hunt so much as audit. A tile-fox will spend an afternoon testing the tension of a lashing with its teeth, then curl into the exact hollow left by a root’s swelling, becoming another packed course in the wall. Their kits are born already colored for the current repair layer; when the binding above them is later replaced, the young emerge a slightly different grey-ochre, as if the place has edited their coats overnight. They lick the mineral rime and, in doing so, move the memory of older firings from one surface to another. Nothing is wasted; even their silence is load-bearing.

Higher, the hinge-birds keep the vertical weather. They are scarcely larger than a closed fist, beaks worn smooth as tools that have outlived their handles. They nest inside the hollow knuckles of the cribwork where previous generations of binders left deliberate voids “for the air to think.” When light shifts a true degree—something the Potting measures more carefully than any clock—the birds spill out in dry clicks and reseat themselves one shelf higher or lower. Their droppings are fine grit that the browsers immediately polish into the pottery bloom. The birds do not sing. They keep time by the small percussive arguments of their feet on lath.

The many-legged browsers are softer, almost reluctant to be animals. They move like living spittle across the oldest vessels, grazing the pale efflorescence that weeps from overloaded seams. Where too many successive repairs have stacked meaning on meaning, the bindings exude a slow, sweetish crust—dried decoctions of intention, of names spoken while the gut was still wet and twistable. The browsers harvest it. In return they leave a faint sheen that makes the next hand that touches the joint hesitate, just long enough for the place to notice it has been noticed. They are not alike; some are flat as hands, others cylindrical and segmented like the clay coils curing overhead. Their eggs are indistinguishable from the unfired spirals until the spirals begin, very quietly, to walk.

Then there are the long ones. Mostly throat, mostly patience. They drape through the upper espalier screens like rope that has decided to remember circulation. For days they may not move. When they do, it is to drop a single loop into a clear space and hang there, tasting the vertical air currents that rise along the cribwork and fall through the central braids. What they listen for is not prey but slack—any joint that has begun to forget its tightness. They swallow the slack. Afterward the structure sits a fraction denser, and the long one is a fraction longer. No one has seen them eat anything else. No one has seen them end.

These are the named creatures, the ones whose outlines still hold if you do not stare past them. But the Potting has been remade in the same shallow bowl so many times that outline itself has become a kind of joint, and joints here are where life prefers to thicken.

Move closer to the seams and the inventory complicates. The vegetation is not flora in any simple register. It photosynthesizes, yes, but what it converts is less photon than remainder—the leftover heat of hands that insisted, the faint charge of words spoken while packing ash and tile. Leaves are thick and matte and exactly as warm as skin that has been turned toward daylight and then cupped. Their veins run in the same lapped, pegged logic as the walls. Cut one (no one cuts one lightly) and the sap is slow, fibrous, already half thread. Roots do not drink; they lock. They swell inside mortar lines and drill holes until they are no longer roots but living trenails holding the previous attempt to the next. In the deepest inner pocket, where the mosaic rises into its stepped nest and the green braids into one fat column, the plants have begun to carry secondary surfaces. A leaf shows its ordinary stomata; a breath later it shows a tiny, perfect mortise. Neither is decoration. Both are usable. The hinge-birds nest in both.

People, when they are present, are difficult to separate from the rest of the fauna. They come—have always come—as repair. Their hands learn the automatic inventory: press every lashing, taste the rime, tuck a new cutting into any crack that has started to show that pale, sourceless light. They speak little because voices become local weather almost immediately, thickening the air into something that must then be bound or browsed. Over seasons their gait shortens to the same low, close movement as the tile-foxes. Their clothing frays into the same twisted fiber that lashes the crib. Some begin to sleep in the undercut ridges. A few, after enough cycles of patching and being patched, notice that the outer walls no longer end where they used to. The made ground continues under their feet a few steps beyond the theoretical boundary, thinning in confidence but still warm, still jointed. At that point the distinction between tending the Potting and being one more binding inside it sinks to the status of a rumor, like the original earth beneath the work.

Yet even this is not the full count. Density is the governing law, and density here has learned to precipitate inhabitants the way saturated air precipitates dew. In the places where reality thins—where a tile offers a spiral to one glance and an open hand to the next, where looking up reveals both sky and a low ceiling of the same cribwork you stand inside—something uses the inconsistency as a body. These are not visitors. They are the extra geometry given appetite. You may catch them only in the wrong-length shadows: a presence that is mostly grip, folding itself through a joint that exists solely when you are not measuring. They do not drink. They do not eat the rime. They take up the slack in the idea of the place. Each time the Potting is rebuilt in its own imprint, each time another layer of ash and tile and naming is pressed into the same warm bowl, the pressure finds nowhere to go but further into actuality. What emerges is quiet, patient, and already competent with lash and wedge. It climbs by the same holds the roots love. It sleeps in the double surfaces. It is not separate from the tile-foxes or the long ones or the people whose hands have become additional fauna; it is what they are becoming when the seams can no longer hold every kind of insistence apart.

Stay long enough and you feel the claim settle on your own weight. Your tongue stops expecting water. Your fingers develop an involuntary census of every peg and fiber you pass. The held warmth becomes the temperature of your thoughts. At that stage it is no longer accurate to say that something lives in the Potting. The Potting lives in the complicated plural—crafts itself into fox and bird and browser and throat and hand and leaf and the thin extra rooms that open when light loses its nerve. It keeps its heat. It tightens. It continues, slow degree by slow degree, while the daylight outside goes on behaving as if the low green bowl were still only a kiln-yard or a forgotten corral, and not a mouth that has learned to close around everything that ever tried to finish it.

The original depression remains, far under the youngest tiles: a shape that cannot be unmade and cannot be left. What lives there is that shape, finally given enough layers of careful, waterless, totally spoken-for life to stand up and check its own bindings.
