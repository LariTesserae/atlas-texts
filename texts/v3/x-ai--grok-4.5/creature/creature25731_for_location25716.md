They are compact.

That is the first thing the place itself seems to insist upon, before any name or lineage. Not small in the way of delicate things, but compact the way a tightly packed seed is compact, or a resting frog, or a fist that has forgotten it might ever need to open all the way. Their bodies carry little spare length. Limbs are short, dense with slow muscle, jointed for climbing wet root and for bracing against the soft give of peat. Shoulders are rounded, almost continuous with the neck; heads sit low and forward, so that the gaze is naturally cast slightly downward and outward at once—toward the next foothold, the next drip, the next pale tuber swelling in the dark water. When they stand still (and they stand still often, for long unhurried intervals) they appear almost like another fold in the moss architecture, another rounded hummock that happens to breathe.

Skin or hide—it is difficult to separate the two—holds a perpetual cool sheen. In the light-shafts it shows as a deep slate or olive-brown, finely pebbled like the skin of certain toads, yet overlaid with a sparse, water-shedding down the color of wet limestone. The down thickens along the outer arms, the ridge of the back, the crowns of the heads, becoming almost a pelt in those who spend more time in the upper galleries where the drip is thinner and the cold bites sharper. In the deeper, wetter sockets of the settlement the down thins again to a sleek plush that water simply ignores. Between the fingers and toes the flesh continues in thin, strong webs, not broad enough for true swimming but perfect for spreading weight across sphagnum, for cupping a wriggling crayfish-thing, for pressing moss into a tight water-shedding seam. Nails are dark, thick, more claw than nail, kept short by constant use against stone and root.

They do not surface often in memory as separate from the green. Faces are broad, cheekbones low, eyes large and darkly liquid with a nictitating membrane that flicks sideways whenever a heavier drip wanders too close. The eyes catch the softened vertical light and hold it a moment longer than expected, giving them a wet-stone gleam. Noses are flat, nostrils able to pinch nearly shut. Mouths are wide but reserved; when they speak the sound emerges low in the chest, soft-edged, with a moisture to the consonants as if every word is shaped around a held breath of mist. Teeth are small, even, slightly translucent, suited to the pale tubers, the smoked river-flesh, the mineral-rich liverworts they fold into every meal. There is little sexual dimorphism that an outsider would easily read; bulk and the particular weave of a hanging garden or the pattern of scarification (thin, pale chevrons cut just deep enough to bead and then seal with resin) speak more of role and kinship than of sex.

They move the way the water moves here: rarely in straight lines, never in haste that would tear the living floor. A crossing from one platform to the next is a sequence of testing weights, of hands and feet finding the same root-ribs that grandfather hands packed into place. Children (there are children—rounder, darker-eyed, their down still more like fine seal fur) learn by clinging and riding the hip or the back until their own webs strengthen. The old become even more compact, spines curving into permanent gentle questions, skin roughening into maps of healed seepage burns and the spiral scars of long-ago fungal stitches. They sleep in the peat sockets the way otters sleep in holts, bodies fitted together for shared warmth, breath slow enough that the mist of it simply joins the general atmosphere.

Clothing is scarcely separate from body and dwelling. Strips of beaten bark fiber, softened willow bast, and the long glossy leaves of the light-well plants are twisted and felted into kilts, pouches, shoulder drapes that double as gathering cloths. These are never truly dry. They are simply less wet than the air, and they carry the same green-black scent as everything else. Ornament is practical first: a net of hollow stem beads that rattle softly only when the wearer wants them to; a comb carved from pale crustacean shell and worn in the denser crown-fur; thin rings of pliable root grown and trained around upper arms while still living, so that the body and the ring age together. Fire is known but rationed into small, smoldering peat hearts kept alive inside clay-lined hollows—enough to smoke flesh and warm a birth or a deep wound, never enough to open a dry pocket larger than a curled body. Light is borrowed from the shafts or coaxed from clusters of pale, cold-glowing fungi cultivated along the busier paths.

They tend rather than build. To watch them work is to watch the settlement continue its own metabolism. A woman (or one who carries herself at that season of obligation) stands hip-deep in a black basin, parting the duckweed with slow hands, lifting gauze-like root mats to check the pale tubers below, pinching off runners, replanting. Nearer a light-well an elder and two younger ones are weaving a new gallery: living willow wands bent and bound with bast, interlaced with fungus-cord that will harden as it dries into something tougher than either material alone. Elsewhere a compact figure moves along a moss-browsing trail, not hunting so much as negotiating—nudging the dense-furred grazers with a soft staff of alder, clipping certain fronds for the drying racks, collecting droppings for the warmer compost pockets that heat the sleeping folds. Speech among them is economical and close-range, full of references that point as much as they name: that seepage, the third tilt of the old breakdown, the place where the pale flitters dust the leaves at the turn of the aquifer’s breath. When larger decisions gather—whether to open a new mezzanine after a fresh spall, how to re-weave a walkway the rising water has softened—they simply accumulate in one of the broader shafts, bodies quiet, and the talk remains almost as low as the dripping until consensus has the same texture as the peat itself: layered, dark, holding together without any single visible stem.

They are not separate from the soft-footed fauna. The long-legged birds will step aside but do not flee. The large dark salamanders share the permanent drip-zones and will climb a seated thigh if the mineral slick is richer there. Even the unseen predators of the upper dark seem to understand the settlement as simply a denser thicket in the same forest of wet stone; deaths occur, quiet and local, and are folded back into the composting under-layers with the same practical grief given to a collapsed gallery or a light-shaft that has begun to close. Kinship extends outward along dry spines of debris and inward into the peat. An individual will speak of “my galleries” and “our water” in the same breath, and of the young rock’s newest crack as something that happened to the larger body they all inhabit.

There are stories, of course—low, mist-softened stories told while hands keep working. That the first of them rose from the same aggressive dissolution that opened the caverns, shaped by the same cold upward pressure of water. That they are what happens when moss and marrow refuse to remain distinct. That far above, on the ordinary surface, there were once kin who chose sun and hard distance, and that those kin grew long and dry and lonely. The stories do not insist. They simply hang in the air like the curtains of filmy fern, dripping occasionally into the black mirrors of the pools, and then the listeners return to the perpetual gentle work of thickening the settlement one living layer at a time.

Stand long enough in a light-shaft and one of them will eventually pass near you—not startled, not challenging, merely adjusting their path by the width of a shoulder so that both of you keep your footing on the narrow dryer spine. They may glance up, membrane flickingonce across the dark eye, and give the smallest inclination of the low head. The gesture carries no invitation and no dismissal. It is simply acknowledgment that you, too, for this moment, are inside the same saturated body: the cold green district that is marsh and wound and city at once, quietly, completely occupied.**They are the deep-quilted ones, the peat-kin, the low-shouldered keepers of the wet green wound.**

They emerge first not as a census or a portrait but as a density already in motion: compact silhouettes shifting among the hanging curtains of liverwort and the thicker moss-pelts, their outlines repeatedly mistaken for another rounded hummock or a fold in the living architecture until a hand extends, webbed and dark-nailed, to test a root-bridge or pinch a runner back into the black water. No single image holds them for long because they are not separate from the place’s slow respiration; they thicken it. Line by line they become clearer only as the cold drip, the mineral breath of young stone, and the overlapping ranges of grazers and salamanders allow.

Bodies stay close to the center of gravity. They are shorter than the surface-remembered human measure, built dense and rounded like seeds that refused to elongate toward any absent sun. Torsos are barrel-thick yet pliable, sheathed in skin that is pebble-textured and cool-slick, ranging slate-olive to near-black, with a sparse water-shedding down that thickens into true soft pelt along the shoulders, outer arms, spine-ridge, and crown. That down darkens or pales with the micro-climates they favor: sleeker, almost seal-plush on those who work the permanent drip-zones and deeper basins; denser and more limestone-dusted on the upper mezzanine tenders where seepage thins and the stone’s fresh edges still glitter. Between fingers and toes the flesh persists as strong, elastic webbing—narrow enough that they remain walkers and climbers first, yet perfect for spreading weight across trembling moss, cupping a slippery crustacean, or pressing wet fiber into a watertight seam. Joints are economical; knees and elbows carry extra soft padding of fat and callus that lets them kneel or brace for hours against peat and fluted stone without bruise. When they rest they curl into themselves or into one another, fitting sockets of moss the way the large dark salamanders fit the vertical grooves.

Heads sit low and slightly forward on short, powerful necks so the default gaze angles down and out—toward footing, toward the freckled surfaces of the shallow basins, toward the next place a hand must steady. Faces are broad, features low-relief: large liquid eyes of deep amber-black that hold the shaft-light a heartbeat longer than expected, each protected by a sideways-flicking nictitating membrane that answers every errant heavy drop. Nostrils are flat slits able to seal against mist and sudden sheet-flow; mouths wide but restrained, lips thin, the soft sounds they make shaped lower in the chest so that speech travels only a few bodies’ distance before the moss drinks it. Teeth are small, even, faintly translucent, edged for scraping mineral film from stone, shearing pale elongated leaves, and portioning the firm flesh of tubers and the smoked, fish-like creatures of the braided shallows. Ears are modest, close-set, often half-hidden in crown down. Across seasons and roles the differences remain subtle—bulk, the particular spiral scarring of healed resin-stitched cuts, the weave-pattern of a personal hanging garden—more than any stark dimorphism.

They exhaust no bright fire and own nothing metallic that survives the perpetual wet. What they wear is grown, beaten, felted, and never fully dry: kilts and shoulder drapes of willow bast and long glossy light-well leaves, pouches of resin-sealed bark, netted beads of hollow stem that can be stilled or set softly rattling by a shift of muscle. Thin living rootlets are trained around upper arms or wrists while supple, so rings and cuffs grow with the wearer and darken to the same wet hues. A comb of pale crustacean shell might sit in denser head-fur; a cloak-layer of especially thick moss can be pinned across the back for longer upper-gallery work. Infants ride in close hip or back slings of the same living fiber until their own webs strengthen and their down seals; elders curve into even tighter compact postures, spines question-mark gentle, skin mapped with old seepage lines and the pale chevrons of kinship marks cut shallow and sealed.

Their work and their being are the same continuous maintenance that the cavern already performs. Hands (webbed, claw-tipped, patient) spiral fresh moss into sleeping folds, bind living willow wands into new hanging galleries with fungus-cord that hardens as it takes, part duckweed to lift and check tuber mats, nudge the dense-furred hare-sized grazers along preferred browse lines, collect droppings for the warmer compost pockets that heat the peat-beds, and reseal clay-and-silt pit linings after every aquifer breath that leaves fresh lace of silt and seed. Children learn by clinging, then by mirroring: the careful foot placement on a root spine, the exact pressure that persuades a fern curtain to yield its water without tearing. Teams accumulate without summons when a fresh spall drops or a light-shaft begins to constrict; bodies simply gather in the broader beams of lowered daylight, low voices inventorying small wet events until the next thickening—new platform, reopened path, reinforced mezzanine—has the layered inevitability of peat itself rising. They do not expand outward so much as intensify: older floors sink into deep compost that warms the newest sockets; boundaries between households remain suggestions of denser planting, orientation of seepage catchments, and who habitually sings (soft, rhythmic, almost breath) while tending which willow gallery.

Among the abundant soft-footed fauna they move as another overlapping territory rather than as lords. The long-legged stone-colored wading birds step aside with three unhurried paces and resume stillness; the large glossy salamanders climb a seated thigh if the mineral slick is richer there; midges thicken and thin around their compost heaps without true swarming sound; even the pale flitting things of the black upper pockets register mostly as the soft punctuation of droppings into leaves, and as distant cousins who share the same rejection of dry distance. Predators appear as sudden small absences, a thrash quickly muffled, then the wet quiet resealing; the peat-kin answer with the same register—local, practical, folding loss back into the under-layers that feed the next green advance. Kinship language stretches easily: “my fold,” “our braid of water,” “the rock’s newest white hairline” all spoken as conditions of one shared metabolism.

Voices stay low by material conspiracy and by choice. Conversation is close-range, moist-edged, full of deictic precision rather than abstract flourish—that third drip-face, the tilt where the old breakdown meets the alder thicket, the hour when the aquifer lifts the lower spines an inch and then retreats. Stories exist, told while hands keep working the tubers or the bindings: that the first compact forms condensed from the same aggressive dissolution and upward-pressing groundwater that opened the young karst, marrow and moss declining to stay distinct; that far above, in the ordinary sun-struck world, elongated kin once chose hard distance and bright fire and grew lonely inside their dryness; that every fresh crack in the still-sharp stone is both peril and invitation to thicker living. The stories drip and return like everything else; they do not demand belief, only another layer.

Stand in a light-shaft when the surface day presses brightest and the cold mist turns the beam solid. One of them will pass—low-shouldered, down beaded, webs flexing against the narrow dryer spine of old debris made street. They adjust course by the width of a body so footing remains mutual. A dark eye flicks its membrane, the low head inclines a fraction, and the gesture contains neither welcome nor warning, only the acknowledgment that you are briefly inside the same saturated, quietly active body: a city of free-standing yet continuous peat-and-willow organs, a cold marsh with streets, a young wound whose every surface has already been claimed by film and frond and the slow, dense, completely occupied breath of the peat-kin. They continue past the next bend, the next shaft, deeper into side chambers and upper breakdown terraces, thickening as they go. The place remains full.
