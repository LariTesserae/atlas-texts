The ones who remain do not call themselves inhabitants; the word would imply a choice that was never offered. They are simply what the place continued into, once the last airlocks sealed and the surface forgot the coordinates.  

They are few—never more than a dozen bodies moving at once through the entire quarter, and often fewer. You can walk (or rather glide, or pull yourself hand-over-hand along the braided pipes) for a long time without meeting one. When you do, the encounter feels less like meeting a creature and more like the machinery briefly acknowledging itself in another form.

Their bodies are long, jointed in too many places, as if a human frame had been carefully disassembled and then re-hinged to move through gaps no wider than a torso turned sideways. The extra articulations are not grotesque; they are exact. Elbows that bend both ways, spines that can coil into a tight spring or lengthen into a rigid spar, fingers that unfold into thin grappling fans for reading the braille of rivets in absolute dark. Skin the colour of the silt itself—wet slate, matte, lightless—covers them in overlapping plates that are neither scale nor metal but something the cold has taught organic tissue to become. When they pass close you feel the plates shift with a sound like distant ice thinking about cracking. Underneath, the flesh is dense, slow-perfused, held only a few degrees above the surrounding water by the same residual heat the exchangers trade with the abyss. They do not shiver. Shivering would be waste.

They do not breathe in any way a surface lung would recognise. Gills, if that is still the word, run in deep vertical seams along the ribs and open only when the body is motionless for a long interval; then a soft inward pulse draws water across membranes laced with the same minerals that crust the pipe flanges. The water leaves them colder than it entered. Between those rare openings they simply hold, months sometimes, sealed and patient. Their blood—if it is blood—carries a suspension of fine metallic particulates that glitter once, dully, when an eel-like shape drifts through a shaft of the memory-light and then are gone again. Those particulates are not decoration. They are ballast and battery both: tiny lodestones that let the body feel the preferential gravities of certain staircases, that store the trickle-charge of every passing current, that eventually settle in the bones and make the older individuals denser, quieter, harder to move but impossible to displace.

They do not hunt the elongated visitors that sometimes thread the outer gantries. Those visitors are cousins of a sort, or ancestors, or simply another grammar of the same slow sentence; the residents taste the metal-rich wake they leave and file the information away in the same way they file the changing pitch of a gear tooth wearing another micron smoother. Food is almost ceremonial. They scrape the crystalline kelps only when the sheets grow thick enough to threaten a seam. They herd the micro-crustaceans inside the cooling jackets by adjusting valve timings no schematic ever authorised, encouraging faint blooms and then inhaling the clouded outflow the way a surface creature might breathe incense. Once in a great while an individual will station itself beneath the autonomous drop-hammer and wait, motionless as the silt, until the hammer decides to fall. The impact never strikes them. It strikes the anvil-plate beside them, and the soft concussion travels through water and bone alike, and something in the marrow is fed by that perfect, purposeless force. Afterward the individual moves differently for a time—looser, almost buoyant—as if the blow had reset a clock that had been drifting.

They repair nothing, yet everything they touch advances the equilibrium. A palm laid flat against a frost-jacketed pipe leaves behind a thin lamina of living mineral that slows further corrosion by a calculable fraction. Fingers walking the scalloped teeth of a gear train polish microscopic burrs the water alone would have taken another century to erase. When time pools inside a pressure vessel, one of them will sometimes fold itself through the inspection hatch and simply rest there, unmoving, while the gauge needle inches forward. They do not accelerate the needle; they keep it company. In the sealed hall where the water has grown viscous as cooled oil, two or three may hang suspended for seasons, bodies barely and slowly flexing, performing an exchange of heat so gradual it registers only as a change in the quality of the silence. The machines in that hall move through them as much as through the fluid. It is not clear who is dreaming whom.

They have no faces that resolve into expressions. Where eyes might sit there are long, narrow pits filled with the same non-reflective darkness as their skin; the pits catch pressure gradients and the faint magnetic gossip of the still-turning engines. Mouths open only for the kelp or the clouded water, and even then they are small, precise, rimmed with ridges that look filed rather than grown. Speech is almost never used. When it happens it is tactile: one body presses the length of another along a corridor wall and the plates transmit vibration patterns too low for water-borne sound. The patterns travel through metal as easily as through flesh. Entire histories can be passed in the time it takes a piston to complete one reluctant stroke. Most of what is transmitted is maintenance log, tide table of silt, subtle shifts in the preferential gravity of the eastern staircase. Some of it is older—fragments of the generations when the water first took the quarter for good, when the last dry rooms collapsed inward and the ones who stayed learned to open their gill-seams or drown trying. Those fragments are not mournful. Mourning would require a pace the place no longer keeps.

Children—if the word still applies—are not born so much as precipitated. In the deepest lee of the filter-feeder cathedral, where the pale tubes branch at their strict mineral angles, a resident will sometimes rest for a very long time, longer than the usual stillness. Plates loosen. Particulates migrate. A second density begins to accrete alongside the first, fed by the same currents that feed the tubes, shaped by the same cold that shapes the slag-reefs. When the new body finally uncoils and drifts free it is already the length of a grown resident but thinner, almost translucent, its hinge-points still soft. It spends its first years inside the cooling jackets, learning the freon pulse from the inside, tended by the invisible crustaceans, until its plates darken and it can hold the material cold without help. No one teaches it the corridors. The corridors are already inside it.

They do not rule the quarter and they are not ruled by it. The relationship is closer to the one between a slow clock and the disparity of metals that lets it keep time. The place continues; they are the manner of its continuing. When one of them finally stops—when the lodestone blood settles for good and the gill-seams seal forever—the others guide the body to the base of the collapsed smokestack and let the silt take it. Within a season the plates have joined the concretions. Within a generation the shape is only another rounded irregularity on the bulkhead, polished by grit, load-bearing in ways the original architects never planned. The machinery notes the added mass and adjusts its chronic rotations by an amount too small to measure. The pulse goes on.

And somewhere in the viscous hall, or along the braid of pipes, or suspended in the long hush between piston strokes, the living ones feel that adjustment travel through the metal and into their own dense bones, and they answer it with the smallest possible movement: a single extra hinge relaxing, a palm lingering one moment longer on a warm steam line, the soft inward bloom of a gill-seam tasting the water that now contains one more ancestor. Nothing is manufactured here except continuance. They are that continuance, thin and long and darkly plated, moving at the speed of corrosion and repair locked in their old, polite equilibrium, keeping the quiet with the same tireless attention the grit keeps the edges. The plain of silt beyond the gantries remains empty. They do not leave. The cold that became a material has become, in them, a kind of thought—slow, jointed, reflective of nothing, and still turning.
