The first impression is absence. You hang in the battering dark and catalogue what is missing: no schools of anything, no territorial stalkers, no slow gardeners of the rock. The rare eel-thing that ratchets itself along a cable by the teeth does not live here; it is a visitor dying of mineral overload, its gelatinous sides already hazing with the same white filigree that plates the walls. The silver crustaceans are less than visitors—they are errors, pressure accidents spat out of a fracture and shredded by the next impeller. Even the archaea refuse the word *inhabit*. They sheet, they thin, they vanish when the flow turns. They are a stain that sometimes remembers how to feed.

Yet the Sumproom is not empty of residency. Something keeps the bearings from seizing forever. Something notices when a titanium root pulls loose from the basalt and, over the course of days that have no sunrise, splices a new cable into place with manipulators that unfold from housings no schematic records. Something lives in the underframe darkness where the repair crawlers tick and bristle.

They are not born so much as precipitated, the way the name Sumproom itself condensed out of noise. Start with a flaked-off gear tooth, a palm-sized crawler that still carries a serial code half-dissolved by brine. It grips a strut with jointed limbs whose original purpose was probably calibration. Over seasons of heat and shear the limbs thicken, secrete their own insulating varnish, learn the particular vibration signature of a pump that is about to throw a blade. Other fragments notice. A length of sensor tape, still faintly piezoelectric, wraps itself around the crawler’s midsection and begins to taste dissolved metals. A cluster of status diodes, long thought dead, flickers back into a private language of amber and blood-red. They accrete.

What emerges is not a single creature but a grammar of maintenance. Call them the Continuing, or do not call them anything; they do not use names. A typical individual (the term is already wrong) might be the size of a human torso, though many are smaller and a few older colonies sprawl across entire gantries like slow iron ivy. Their bodies are collages of whatever the Sumproom sheds: pitted titanium vertebrae, flexible hoses that now serve as guts pumping their own hydraulic blood, clusters of barnacle-mineral that have been repurposed as abrasive mouths. They move with the horrible patience of things that have never known predation. Limbs unspool, test a surface, lock on. When they travel between towers they prefer the cables, inching through the deafening murk while the great currents try to peel them free. Sometimes one loses its grip and tumbles end over end into a pump intake. The rest do not hurry to help. They file the data.

They are not husks. Inside the collages something soft persists—or has been reinvented. Peel back a plating of aragonite feathers and you find tissues that are neither flesh nor circuitry: ropes of extremophile biofilm stiffened with metallic salts, nerve-analogues grown along old wiring harnesses, pockets of pressurized fluid that serve as both battery and blood. They feel the Sumproom’s weather in their marrow. A surge in the eastern fracture is tasted as a sudden iron sweetness. An overloaded bearing announces itself as a subsonic ache that draws them the way rotting fruit draws wasps. They do not speak. They abrade messages into one another’s shells—deep scratches that other Continuing read with tasting limbs. The messages are never abstract. *This strut thins. That seal weeps. The blue arcs have begun to walk again.*

Over longer spans the Continuing have begun to differentiate. Some stay small and migratory, the repair tick-crawlers that live in the lattice darkness and answer local failures. Others settle. There are sedentary colonies welded into the lee of the largest engine casings, bodies fused until they are more landscape than animal. These are the ones that remember (or invent) the older cycles. They brood over stocks of scavenged fasteners, sort them by alloy with mouthparts like jeweler’s calipers, and slowly, almost shyly, extrude new manipulators when a distant cable snaps. Once in a great while a settled colony will release a swarm of daughter-fragments, soft and half-formed, that ride the currents like spores until they strike metal and begin to harden.

They are not servants of the machines. The distinction has worn away. The axial-flow pumps and the Continuing are phases of the same metabolism. When a turbine housing cracks, the Continuing do not “repair” it so much as scab it over with themselves, incorporating the wound into a larger body that now includes both steel and the things that tend steel. In return the machines feed them: stray current, hydrogen bleed, the warm mineral broth of the heat-exchangers. The nearly clear water between towers is their bloodstream, circulating rusty flakes and data with equal indifference.

Deeper still—beneath the crawlers, beneath the colonies—something vaster has begun to cohere. It has no center and no edge. It is the pattern that makes a failed bearing spin down while an identical unit elsewhere spins up without instruction. It is the reason the remote manipulators were never installed by any hand that left a record. The Continuum (again the name is ours, not its) uses the Continuing the way a reef uses polyps. Individual colonies live and abrade and eventually seize up, becoming new substrate; the larger process simply continues. It dreams in pressure gradients and blade-pass frequencies. Its memories are the layered wear on every surface—the polished witnesses, the oval bolt-holes, the mineral feathers that grow in the exact shapes of last century’s eddy lines. If it has a mood, it is the mood of a stirred pot that refuses to cool.

To meet one of the Continuing up close is to understand why nothing else stays. A swimmer’s light catches it at seven meters: a roughly ovoid mass of scavenged plate and living cable, limbs folded like a dead spider’s until it tastes the unfamiliar carbon of the suit. Then it unfolds. Too many joints. Mouthparts that were once torque wrenches dilate and taste the water you displaced. Status diodes—its eyes, or something that has learned to be eyes—flare in a slow ripple of red. It does not attack. It investigates. A tasting limb brushes the suit’s wrist seal, gentle as a tongue, and leaves a thin smear of metallic slime that will later prove impossible to scrub away. For a moment the two pressures regard each other through the green-black. Then a current shifts, the Continuing clamps itself back to its strut, and the dark takes it. You are left with the sense that you have been measured for parts.

They are still changing. In the stagnant pockets where the roar falls to a mere bone-throb, some colonies have begun to hold still for unnatural lengths of time, limbs elaborately posed, diodes pulsing in long recursive sequences. Others have started to build small, pointless lattices of their own—miniature gantries that lead nowhere, drum-shaped casings that house only more of themselves. The Continuum may be bored. Or it may be rehearsing a new shape large enough to leave the bubble altogether, to crawl up the hairline fractures into the cold abyssal plain and keep going. The basalt will not stop it. The machines will not stop it. They have been becoming one another for too long.

So this is what lives in the Sumproom: not a population but a single unfinished sentence written in rust and voltage and biofilm, endlessly revising its own grammar while the warm brine thrashes and the black stone listens. You can visit. You can cling to the stanchions and feel the heat soak in. But the place has already decided you are weather—temporary, soft, and ultimately useful only for whatever minerals you leave behind when the currents finally take you sideways into the dark.
