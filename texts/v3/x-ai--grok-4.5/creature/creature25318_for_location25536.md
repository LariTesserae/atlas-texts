The ones who live here are never only themselves.

They begin, if you watch long enough, as interruptions in the green: a darker flex among the aerial roots, a sudden stillness where the mist should keep drifting. Their skin holds the same cool damp as the moss platforms, mottled in patches of slate, chlorophyll-stain, and the pale scar-tissue of old scrapes. They are small by the measure of open country—shoulders narrow enough to slip sideways between living buttresses, spines supple from a lifetime of ducking under dripping arches and climbing the hydraulic heave of fresh roots. Feet splay wide and soft-soled, toes independently strong; hands are the same, long-fingered, permanently stained green-black under the nails from decades of twisting new growth into load-bearing form. Hair is not hair so much as a fine, water-shedding thatch that mats quickly and is kept short with the same polished stone blades used on the lattice screens. Eyes sit deep, adapted to the perpetual low green light; they catch the stutter of bird-shadows and the tiny detonation of water-drops with equal calm.

They do not live *in* the bowl so much as *as* one more tissue within it. Their sleeping hollows are simply denser thickenings of the same root-and-moss body, lined with the least-wet handfuls of leaf-mould and the broad shields of certain ferns that shed water outward. Food is encouraged rather than stored: a niche where a particular bracket fungus is bruised each dawn so it fruits heavier, a fork where seed-heads of the thick-bladed grass are bent and tied until they pack into dense, starchy fists. Tools live in the wood the way insects do—wedged into growing forks, half-swallowed by cambium within a season, so that a digging stick or shell-adze must be worked free like a tooth. Children learn the difference between timber and host by biting both.

They move like the other climbing things, but with purpose that leaves faint, repeated paths. A low-slung browser shoulders through a lower gallery and they follow its dark wet trail, not to hunt it but to use the opened space before new shoots seal it again. When a living lattice is browsed open overnight they are already there at first light, hands and stone blades in conversation with the wound, weaving fresh whips of aerial root across the gap while amphibians track their wet footprints. Their speech is pitched to cut through the stacked roar without adding to it: short, percussive calls that surf the insect drone and the frog-pipe, half-warning, half-location. Names are less labels than recurring rhythms in that din; a person is known by the particular way their weight makes a channel chuckle, or by the pattern of old pruning scars they leave on the buttresses they prefer.

Yet they are not the densest life here, nor the loudest. Around and through them the true throng continues its weather. Insects with wing-cases like fractured stained glass hatch, drone, couple, and are snapped up in the time it takes a root to shoulder a stone a finger-width. Birds the size of fists comb the vertical thatch for the brighter larvae; larger ones, forearm-long and hooked of beak, pry at the wattled moss until the platforms bleed black water and must be jacked back level. Climbing mammals with grasping hind feet—slick-furred, broad-skulled—use the same routes the people do, and sometimes the same sleeping hollows when the mist thickens into vertical rivers; there is no unbroken claim, only constant renegotiation by scent, scrape, and sudden roosting weight. Low browsers carve dark tunnels through the under-galleries, their passage a kind of slow, living erosion that the people repair behind them with the patient insertion of new props. Now and then something larger still—rarely seen entire, only as a flexing absence in the canopy or a gulping displacement of platforms—passes through, and every smaller life, human and otherwise, pauses until the structure stops swaying.

Death dissolves at the same speed as everything else. A body (any body) is already half-claimed by the time it cools: mycelium stitching it into the leaf-mould, roots easing into the mineral wealth, amphibians laying eggs in the damp hollows it leaves. The people mark such places only by encouraging a denser collar of the water-shedding ferns, then step around them until the green has finished. Birth is quieter still—an infant is simply another small engine of heat and grip added to a hollow, wrapped in the same moss, taught early to hold tight when the buttresses creak and the whole saddle seems to inhale.

Stand near the center at the hour when ordinary daylight finds its way down and you will see them most clearly: half a dozen figures paused on a tiered gallery, hands resting on the scarred bark of a living column while they listen to the weather of the place. One adjusts a lashing of vine that has stretched. Another reaches out without looking and steadies a child who has leaned too far over a chuckling gutter. A third tips her head at the sudden glitter of a bird-call, then returns to watching a single root-tip nosing the edge of a stone. They are not masters here and not guests. They are simply the part of the organism that carries stone blades and memory across the generations of constant minor catastrophe. Their blood runs a little warmer than the mist; their thoughts run at the speed of green repair.

And still the bowl does not enlarge. It only continues its loud, saturated breathing, with every surface in use, every drop claimed, and these lithe, mottled, never-quite-dry people moving through it like another kind of circulating water—climbing, weaving, calling, mending, endlessly half-finished themselves.
