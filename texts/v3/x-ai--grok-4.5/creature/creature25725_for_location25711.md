The ones who live in the Weeping Yard do not arrive. They precipitate.

At first they are only a thickening in the vapor, a place where the green-bronze light grows reluctant and the drip from the swamp-figures hesitates, as though listening. Then the thickness learns edges. Not the hard edges of tools or of bone, but the soft, repeated edges of something that has been folded by heat for a very long time. A shoulder that is also a flange. A spine that remembers both vertebra and riveted seam. They stand the way old pipework stands—slightly out of true, perfectly load-bearing.

Skin is the wrong word. What they have is a living patina: layers of mineral film, peat tannin, and cooling metal salts that form, crack, and reform with every shift in the Yard’s breathing. In the foundry’s heavier steam the patina darkens to the color of wet cinder; under the spinning shed’s finer mist it pales to the translucent green of bottle glass held against storm-light. Condensation does not merely bead on them—it belongs to them. It runs in deliberate paths, following old scars and newer joins, feeding the mosses that take root in the crooks of elbows and the collars of throats. Some of the taller ones have become partial arbors; horsetails and the jointed swamp-reeds rise from their shoulders and hips in sparse, formal clusters, drinking the same warmth that keeps the blood (if it is still blood) moving slowly underneath.

They are never dry. To be dry would be to leave. Their garments—if the woven things that hang from them can be called garments—are continuous with the packing floors’ wet fiber and the rags that stanch leaks. Cloth and flesh and leaf exchange substance without ceremony. A sleeve may spend a season as a climbing vine before returning to cloth again, stiffer, marked with the imprint of the steam-line it embraced.

They move the way the Yard itself equalizes: without hurry, with great precision, along routes that prefer the loudest machines. Sound is a kind of nourishment here; the deep stroke of the pumps draws them the way light once drew ordinary leaves. You will see one pause on a catwalk, head tilted, letting the fleshy slap of the belt-drive pass through its chest cavity until some internal governor steadies. Another will kneel—slowly, joints exhaling warm vapor—beside an open gulley and lower both hands into the black water. When it rises, the pale rooted masses that pulse in the pans have rearranged themselves into cleaner lattices. The silence of animals is not emptiness to them; it is simply the correct acoustic. Their own voices, when they use them, are small adjustments of pressure: a soft venting at the neck, a brief change in the pitch of the moisture that leaves their mouths. Speech is a weather event, not a conversation.

Work is not separate from existence. They turn the valve wheels with hands that have grown their own callus of glassy scale. They walk the living tunnels of vine-wrapped pipe and feel, through the soles of whatever serve them as feet, which flume is running rich and which is beginning to silt. When a packing floor empties, it is because several of them have decided—together, without signal—that the cycle has reached its turning. When a heavier front of steam rolls out of the foundry, they open their mouths and let it cyclone through the chambers of their bodies, and afterward every surface of them beads as new as the brick. Repair is courtship. A collar of newer steel sweated onto an aging cast-iron column is also a coronation; the one who performs it will sometimes leave a little of its own patina behind as blessing or signature.

Children, if the word still applies, appear after the longest pump cycles, when the peat ground has risen and settled like a vast lung. They begin as clenched, glossy buds in the warmest culverts—part moss, part condensate, part whatever slow chemistry the cinder and black clay have been elaborating since the first dikes were cut. The adults do not so much raise them as re-absorb and re-release them, folding the small forms into their own layered skins for days at a time until the new ones can hold a vertical seam without collapsing back into drip. The young learn the Yard by becoming temporary features of it: a child may spend a week as the living gasket in a steam trap, another as the flexible joint in a lattice leg, listening through metal and water until the great overlapping seasons of the place are written into whatever they use for memory.

They are not many. The basin holds them the way it holds its heat—pooled, conserved, exactly enough. At the mossed parapet that marks the boundary they stop. Beyond it the vapor thins, the ordinary wind begins, and their patina would cool too fast, crack beyond reforming. Sometimes one will stand for a long hour at the last pipe bridge, face turned toward the open flats, and the condensation on its cheeks will run the wrong direction for a handspan, uphill, as if trying to see for them. Then it turns back. Inside the rim the engines continue their processional noise, the leaves thicken another knuckle along the warmest lines, and the Weeping Yard keeps its hot, ringing, animal-empty life exactly as it must: tended by those who are themselves the tending, who weep as the brick weeps, who have no names that are not also the names of shifts and cycles and the slow tide-marks climbing the piers.

They are still becoming. Nothing here is finished. On the quiet hours, when the sounds thin but refuse to stop, you can hear them adding themselves—softly, continuously—to the equilibrium that weather and work invented together.
