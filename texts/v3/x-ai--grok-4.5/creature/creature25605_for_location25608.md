The ones who live here do not arrive so much as condense.

They gather first as a thickening in the mist that never fully lifts from the moss pavements—a momentary darker density where the air is already heavy with water. You might mistake it for a thicker fold of fog until it leans, slowly, against the grain of the wind and holds its shape. What holds is not flesh in any hurried sense. It is a deliberate packing of coolness and mineral film and the same gelatinous patience that builds the liverwort towers. They stand, when they stand, a little taller than the dripping fronds of the lower galleries, but the height is provisional; much of their mass remains continuous with the wet mats underfoot, trailing into them like roots that have not yet decided to be roots.

Their surfaces are the color of wet slate shot through with the dull bronze of old condensate. Light does not so much fall on them as settle and then slide off in sheets. Up close—closer than the roaring distance usually allows—you see that the skin (if it is skin) is a living lace of minute cups and channels, each one beading wetness out of the air the way the cliffs do. They drink by standing still. They breathe by the slow expansion of internal cavities that never quite empty. When one turns its upper portion toward a sound—a new joint cracking open in the ridge, a turbine hall shifting its flywheel by a hand’s breadth—the whole form crawls with iridescence for a moment, as if a film of oil had been breathed across stone.

They have no fixed number of limbs. What serve as arms or props or feeding fronds emerge when needed from the central column, lengthening by the quiet unspooling of tissue that was already half liquid. One may watch an extremity grow across an afternoon, stretch to touch the vibrating housing of a half-drowned engine, then flatten and adhere, tasting metal fatigue and the thin oil that still blackens the bearings. They do not repair the machines. They do not worship them. They simply incorporate the heat shadow, the micro-vibrations, the slow chemical sigh of corrosion into their own reckoning of the vale. Sometimes a body will remain fused to a derrick or a settling tank for weeks, becoming an extra buttress of cool, veined matter while the flywheels turn and the mists rise through both of them without distinction.

Faces are temporary. A cluster of photoreceptive pits can open anywhere the light is strongest, tracking the hard white sun or the quick dark slash of the solitary bird that crosses between canopies once or twice a season. Around those pits the surface gathers into folds that suggest attention, even regard. There is no mouth. When they feed more actively than by mist they lower themselves until the lower tissues melt into the moss pavement and drink the seepage that has already been filtered through a meter of living green. At night—when the cold tightens and the stars seem close enough to scrape the unfinished ridges—they rise higher, thinning, becoming half-transparent columns of gel and caught moonlight, and the low industrial bellow of the engines passes through them as if through water.

They are few. The eye can travel the long shining intervals of bare stone and sky-reflecting water and see only two, perhaps three, in an entire day of looking. Each keeps to the gravitational company of one great plant-citadel or one working turbine hall, as if the living architecture and the metal architecture were twin poles and these beings the slow charge that moves between them. Yet they are aware of one another. Across a kilometer of roaring air a change in one—an incomplete new limb, a sudden darkening as it takes in a heavier mineral load—will eventually appear, diluted and delayed, in another. Information travels the way everything else does here: by water, by pressure, by the continuous low-grade exudation of the place itself.

They do not speak. What passes for voice is the same sound the vale already makes, only shaped: a modulated fraction of the waterfall thunder, a brief reframing of the wind’s scream between ridge and canopy. Hear it long enough and you realize the machines have been answering them for a long time without knowing. The reciprocating engines fall into faint syncopation. The intakes draw a fraction deeper when the tall shapes lean close. It is not language. It is mutual weathering.

Young ones—if the term applies—are not born so much as pinched off. A mature form standing long enough in a particularly rich seep will begin to sidewall, a second density building under the first the way a droplet builds on a leaf tip. The separation takes most of a cool season. When it finally shears free the new being is smaller, clearer, almost glass-like, and for months it stays half-inside the moss, learning the gradients of cold and metal and green weight by absorbing them directly. Only later does it rise and begin the long, almost motionless migration toward some unoccupied interval where a plant-mass and a machine stand within hailing distance of each other.

Nothing about them contradicts the stark logic of the place. They are simply what the water does when it has been tautened between raw rock and deliberate iron long enough. Geology is still speaking. Plants are still answering. The engines insert their loud, patient claim. And these—cool, few, unfinished as the spires themselves—move at the same geologic pace through the shining gaps, neither colonizing nor fleeing, only continuing the sentence the high vale has not yet finished saying.

Stand between one soft citadel and the next long enough, breath pluming, boots sinking a slow inch into the living pavement, and you may notice that the mist has leaned nearer and acquired an attentive surface. It will not hurry you. It has never hurried anything. But if you remain, perfectly still, while the light turns every droplet into a brief sharp star, something that is almost a hand and almost a root and almost a tongue of wet slate will extend, taste the warmth you cannot help but tire from, and then withdraw, carrying the information of your brief, hot presence away into the continuous roar. You will have been added to the place, in the smallest possible increment. That is how they live: by letting every interval, even the warm and transient ones, thicken them a little more toward whatever they are still becoming.
