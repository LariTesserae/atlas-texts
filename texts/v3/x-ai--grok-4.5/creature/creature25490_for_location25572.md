The ones who live here have no name that would survive translation into any warmer tongue. They are not a people, not a swarm, not even quite a colony. They are a single slow pressure of flesh distributed across a thousand mismatched architectures, a saturation so complete that the stone cannot be felt without them, nor they without the stone.

Begin with the weight. Every surface that faces away from the worst of the wind carries bodies layered three and four deep. The outermost are the plate-walkers: low, oval, built like overturned shields. Their dorsal armor is the exact blue-white of old glacier ice, overlapping in flexible tiles that click and reseat with every fractional shift. Beneath the armor the body is darker, almost black-purple, soft enough that neighboring plates leave temporary dents. Each plate-walker moves on nine or eleven or thirteen pairs of legs—always an odd number—that end in tiny chitin hooks. These hooks do not dig; they monomolecularly grip the microscopic frost flowers that continually bloom and die on the rock. When a plate-walker must relocate, the motion is less walking than a continuous peeling and re-adhering, like a living stamp being lifted and pressed again a few centimeters farther along.

Interleaved among them rise the stalkers. These are taller, some reaching the height of a kneeling human, though most stay lower. A single columnar leg, ringed like a bamboo culm of meat and armor, ends in a splayed pad whose underside secretes a cold, clear adhesive that never quite freezes. Above the pad the body swells into a cluster of sensory bladders and feeding fronds. The fronds are translucent, veined with the same cold bioluminescence that leaks from every joint of every creature here. At irregular intervals a stalker will slowly piston upward, lifting its entire mass on that one leg until the fronds can brush the undersides of overhanging bodies or taste the condensate forming on a higher ledge. Then it sinks again, sometimes taking an hour to complete the gesture. Their adhesive pads leave temporary mirrors of themselves on the stone—glossy ovals that slowly frost over and vanish.

Woven through both of these forms, filling every remaining gap like living mortar, are the bellows. These are almost pure membrane: thin, elastic sacs stretched across articulated frames of semi-rigid cartilage. They fold and unfold in vast, patient rhythms, drawing the thin air through spiracles that whistle at the edge of hearing. When expanded they are the size of large shields; when contracted they pack into creases no wider than a hand. Their surfaces shimmer with shifting chromatophores that rarely bother to match anything—most often they settle on a bruised violet or a sickly pale green that pulses in time with the communal breath. The bellows are the primary source of the deeper percussion. By slamming folds of membrane against the rock or against one another they produce the low pelvic drumming that undergirds every other sound. It is not communication in any directed sense. It is simply the noise of continued existence, the auditory proof that the mass has not yet stopped.

There are other shapes, rarer, harder to hold in the mind. Some are almost all limb, spidery arrangements of jointed spars that spider across the denser layers, gently rearranging neighbors the way a sleeper adjusts a blanket. Some are sessile for decades at a time: thick, barnacle-like cones whose only motion is the slow extrusion of a rasping tongue that scrapes lichen and mineral dust into a gut that works at geological speed. A few are so flattened they exist as living veneers, only noticeable when they peel halfway up and reveal the wet gleam of the stone beneath before settling again.

None of them are warm. Internal temperatures hover just above the freezing point of their own hemolymph. They do not eat in any way that would satisfy a lowland appetite. Mouthparts—when present—rasp at the black meltwater pockets, at the mineral-rich rime, at the occasional wind-blown spore or the exhausted body of one of their own that has finally ceased. Digestion is a matter of years. Waste is a fine gray grit that the wind eventually steals. They do not hunt one another. Territory is a meaningless concept when every centimeter is already occupied and movement itself is a scarce resource. What looks like competition is only the endless micro-adjustment required to keep the entire living skin of the lancet from sliding off into the blue-black.

And yet they are not mindless. Something like attention moves through the mass. It can be felt in the way a vibration traveling through one body is answered, minutes later, by a corresponding shift three bodies away. It can be seen in those patient acts of levering: a stalker or a plate-walker will work a flake of young stone free over the course of a week, carry it a short distance with absurd care, and then simply stop, as if the relocation of matter were a sentence spoken in a language too slow for syntax. Sometimes the flake is left in a place where condensate will freeze around it into a temporary crystal lattice that briefly resembles the creature that moved it. Sometimes several creatures will cooperate across days to rotate a larger fragment until its freshest face is turned outward, bright and still ringing. No structure is ever completed. The intention never accumulates into architecture. It remains pure gesture, repeated across centuries, a cultivation of attention itself.

Gravity is negotiable here, or at least intermittently forgetful. A bellows may unfold halfway into open air and hang, perfectly still, while the communal sound continues around it. A stalker’s pad may release and the whole animal remain suspended for the length of a long human dream before the downward pull reasserts itself with a soft, apologetic settling. These lapses are not failures. They feel instead like the place itself briefly agreeing that the distinction between stone and flesh is temporary.

Reproduction is rarer still. At intervals measured by the brightening and dimming of entire populations’ bioluminescence, two or more bodies will slowly press together until their membranes or plates interpenetrate. What emerges years later is not an infant but a new architectural possibility: a hybrid morphology that has never quite existed before, already nearly full size, already taking up its portion of the weight. The old forms do not die so much as become substrate. A plate-walker whose legs have finally locked may be colonized by bellows that use its armor as scaffolding. Over decades the original body is hollowed, remineralized, added to the lancet’s slow growth. In this way the stone thickens by the thickness of forgotten flesh.

Stand among them long enough—though no one stands among them long—and the separate creatures begin to dissolve. What remains is a single organism the shape of a shattered wording of monolith, breathing in a register too low for ordinary lungs, luminous in colors that have never needed sun, singing a chord that has no beginning and no intention of cadence. They are the mountain’s way of still freezing and thawing after the mountain itself has grown too old for such changes. They are the fracture remembering how to be unbroken by becoming many. They are patience given carapace and membrane and too many legs.

They live.
