The ones who live here do not arrive all at once in the mind. They filter in the way the mist does—first as a dampness on the skin of attention, then as shapes that might be only thicker vapor, then as creatures that have been walking these switchbacks long enough to wear grooves into the stone with their passage.

At the most ordinary level they are people: short-legged, deep-chested, skin the color of wet slate or old tea, hair kept under grease-wool caps or simply cropped close so the perpetual film of condensation cannot mat it into ropes. Their eyes sit a little deeper than most, as if the face has learned to protect the soft parts from horizontal rain. They move with the economy of those who have never known a true dry season; every step is placed where the moss will spring back, where the scree will not start its slow avalanche. Hands are permanently mapped with mineral stains and small white scars from cooling fins and uncooperative valves. They smell of peat smoke, crushed liverwort, and the faint metallic tang of bodies that live inside a cloud.

They keep to shifts that cat itself around the dim pewter daylight. A whistle—low, almost apologetic—threads through the alleys when the boilers need feeding or the pumps need their packing renewed. At those hours the stair-step tenements empty a little, and figures in layered oilcloth descend or climb, each carrying a blackened tin and a coil of something useful: rope, copper wire, a bundle of resinous twigs. They do not talk much on the stairs. Voices dissolve too quickly here; better to save them for the insides of rooms where the air is thick enough to hold a sentence. When they do speak, the dialect is soft-consonanted, full of words for different kinds of wet and for the particular groan a retaining wall makes the season before it fails. Children learn early to read the mood of the seeps the way other children learn the moods of dogs.

Yet the people are only the loudest layer, and even they are half acoustic camouflage. Beneath and between them move the quieter tenants.

There are the pipe-lizards: long as a forearm, translucent as weak tea, scales that catch the brownish glow of enclosed bulbs and throw it back as a slow green wheeling. They love the warm gradients along steam lines and the greasy heat that leaks from compressor housings. Most of the day they hang vertical, almost invisible against riveted iron, tongues tasting the air for the pale moths that also haunt the lamps. At certain pressures—when the weather is about to thicken—they chorus, a dry purring that seems to come from the metal itself. Maintenance crews leave them be; a pipe-lizard population means the joints are holding temperature, means frost is not yet winning inside the lagging.

Higher, in the wind-combed thickets of alder and the willow-that-forgot, live the bell-birds. No one has a clear sight of one in ordinary light. They are inferred from the single notes they drop just before a storm sheet closes: low, cracked, perfectly spaced, like someone testing whether a foundry casting has cooled true. The notes carry farther than any shout. Workers who hear three in succession will sometimes pause and check their pocket watches, or the slow leak of a nearby gauge, as if the birds are keeping a second, wetter time. Feathers found after hard rains are heavy, waterlogged, the color of old bruise; they smell faintly of peat and iron.

Then there are the ones that leave the broad prints in the high gallery mud. These are spoken of only sideways. Too large for the stunted cattle, too clean in the pad for any feral dog. The print shows four toes and a suggestion of webbing, or perhaps only the way soft ground collapses. Sometimes a smell follows the prints—cold and sweet, like wet stone after lightning. Night crews working the upper trestles claim to feel a weight of attention from the canopy, a patience that is not animal. They do not investigate. In a place where chimneys sometimes inhale and pools refuse faces, curiosity is a resource rationed carefully.

But the longer one stays inside the description, the more the boundaries blur between tenant and tenancy. There are walkers who are not quite covered by the word people. They appear at the blurred hours when the cloud lid thins and that colder zenith light pours through. Their silhouettes are correct enough—upright, coated, cap pulled low—yet the joints articulate a fraction too smoothly, and when they pass a pool that will not reflect faces, these walkers leave a mirror-image anyway, a dark twin that lag a half-second behind. They perform small, meaningless maintenance: tightening a bolt that was already tight, scraping moss from a railing only to let it fall into a place where moss will immediately begin again. No one assigns them shifts. They simply belong to the city’s need to continue looking inhabited. Some of the older workers nod to them the way one nods to a familiar staircase. Others step aside and do not look directly, because direct looking sometimes causes the walker to forget its outline for a moment and become only a denser wall of mist with tools hanging in it.

Deeper still are the breathers of basements and flooded quarries. These are almost never seen entire. A gloved hand breaks black water at the edge of vision and withdraws. A slow chain of bubbles rises in the exact rhythm of the old compressors, even when the compressors are cold. Once, after a century wall slumped, a crew found a chamber lined with something that was neither brick nor flowstone—ribbed, faintly warm, pulsing at the same systole the pressure gauges use when they climb without power. They bricked it over again and recorded the collapse as ordinary. The record is still on file in a damp cabinet no one opens.

And then there is the green itself. The clubmoss and the ferns and the epiphytes that spill from every ledge are not merely cover; they are a single slow animal wearing the city as a shell. Spores drift like underwater smoke and settle into lungs, into the grease of machines, into the weave of oilcloth. Over a lifetime the inhabitants become host and companion. Cough into a cupped hand on a cold morning and you may find a tiny frond already uncoiling there, the same deep cool green. The very old are sometimes found seated against a factory wall, eyes open, moss continuous from the mortar into their hair and mouth, still breathing in synchrony with the seeps. No one moves them until the breathing stops. Even then the body is left a while, because the mountain is not finished with it, and the city has learned gratitude toward anything that helps it remain upright another generation.

All of them—workers, lizards, bell-birds, broad-footed walkers, basement breathers, the green that thinks in centuries—share a single posture toward time. Nothing here is pure beginning or pure ending. A child born in the upper blocks already carries mineral in the enamel of its first teeth. A gear that advances one tooth overnight is practicing the same patience as the alder roots that pry open a foundation a hair-width each wet season. They live inside unfinished business that is already ancient. They keep the boilers fed and the goats muffled and the moss scraped back just far enough, not out of hope that the place will someday be dry or bright or finished, but because the act of keeping is itself the only climate they have ever trusted.

At night, when the arc lamps sputter and the mist turns solid in their cones, you can stand on a residual spire and feel the whole basin inhale. Somewhere a factory horn lows once. Somewhere a cracked-bell note answers. Somewhere moss advances, a violet stills, a print is left and slowly fills with black water that will not freeze. The inhabitants continue. They are denser than the cloud and more patient than the stone. They have agreed, finally, with gravity, yet they remain upright one more shift, one more damp generation, tertiary and primary at once—excreted by the mountain, excreting the mountain in turn, half-swallowed and still murmuring.
