The tenders are not the first thing the place admits to having.

What lives here begins as pressure and appetite distributed through the endless damp. In the cork continents that form the true thickness of every wall, entire nations of soft architects click and rearrange themselves by generations measurable in the slow advance of a single growth ring. They are pale, segmented, barely more decisive than the sap around them; their mouths are continuous rasps, their legs a practical rumor of bristles. They do not think of themselves as separate from the plant. When a vascular channel needs widening they simply eat in that direction for a century or two, leaving behind polished tunnels that later serve as pilgrimage routes for things with eyes. Their castes are distinguished only by the particular resins they secrete—some that harden into temporary scaffolding, some that glow with a patient foxfire visible only after the eyes have finished adjusting to the permanent dusk. To walk the great galleries is to feel their multitudes as a subliminal grinding in the soles of the feet, a reminder that the world is continuously being digested and rebuilt from within.

Above them, and through them, move the larger grazers.

These are the ones the tenders call clouds when they are feeling poetic and “slow hunger” when they are not. Vast, translucent bladders of tissue veined with chlorophyll they did not invent, they drift the atriums on thermal ghosts, their undersides a meadow of patient cilia that comb the newest shoots into paste. A single grazer may take three days to cross a gallery you could shout across. They are not stupid. Something in the arrangement of their distributed ganglia understands the difference between a stewarded graft and a wild growth, and they prefer the latter the way certain rivers prefer the older stone. When one dies—usually by simply forgetting how to remain buoyant—it settles like a collapsing cathedral onto the marsh floors, and the mosses race to claim it before the smaller lives can finish the conversion. The tenders do not mourn them. They harvest the durable interior membranes for rain-catchers and sailcloth against the rare outer winds, cutting with the same obsidian sickles their ancestors used when the high places were still short enough to scare a child.

Then there are the things that wait.

Predators here have all learned the same long arithmetic. Some are living geometry: coils of armored vine-muscle that spend decades pretending to be load-bearing architecture until a silhouette crosses a weeping membrane at the wrong angle. Others are more honest about their patience. Filter arrays span entire vertical kilometers—gossamer nets of living silk and sensory hairs that taste the air for the specific damp signature of something worth inhaling. When they contract it is almost polite, a soft origami collapse that leaves only a brighter patch of gloom and a few drifting motes that used to have names. Still others hunt by becoming landscape features no one remembers commissioning: meltwater caches that develop appetite, or hanging gardens whose flowers face inward and exhale a perfume that makes distance itself feel negotiable. Nothing chases. Everything arrives.

But the ones who would recognize themselves in a story are the wardens.

They are upright, mostly, though “upright” is a local consensus rather than a biological absolute. Generations of climbing and grafting and sleeping in the crooks of living rafters have left them elongated, jointed in places other peoples keep solid, their fingers capable of wrenching a cutting free with that single practiced twist that does not bruise the cambium. Skin the color of wet bark, or of the pale underside of a leaf, or of the silver waterlines that never become streams. Eyes large and dark to drink the exhausted green-gold light; many of them carry a secondary pair of nictitating membranes that let them stare too long into the reflective pools without losing themselves to the angles that do not exist. They dress in the plant’s own discards—bark cloth beaten soft, fiber cordage, the occasional iridescent sheath shed by a dying grazer. Their tools are grown as often as made: living wedges of a half-domesticated parasite vine that can be encouraged to swell and split a trunk along desired planes, knives of glass-sharp silica plates secreted by certain wall-beetles, needles of thorn that still remember being alive and sometimes try to root in the wound they close.

They do not rule the world-plant. They edit it, the way the description said. Their inheritance is a grammar of interventions so old it feels like instinct: which nodes to bandage when the structure begins to torsion too quickly, which forgotten rooms to seal again with braided red fiber before someone walks into a century that no longer opens outward, how to read the faint pressure differentials that tell when an internal cloud is about to become weather with opinions. Children learn to walk first on the trembling moss floors, then on the vertical faces, then—if they show the talent—across the unspace that sometimes opens when two galleries decide they were always the same corridor. The old ones move slowest of all, not from frailty but because they have learned to match the plant’s own tempo. Some of them have names that are simply the locations they have kept stable for a human lifetime. Others have stopped using names entirely and answer only to the particular pattern of sap-flow they can induce by pressing a palm to a membrane.

They tell stories, but the stories are mostly maps that refuse to stay flat.

And sometimes, in the warm hollows where the air grows heavy enough to remember shapes, something else condenses.

These are not quite spirits and not quite weather. They form the way dew forms—suddenly inevitable—taking the outlines of animals that never evolved here, or of people who might have tended the grafts in configurations the plant has since pruned away, or of nothing any living warden has language for. They have weight. They leave footprints that steam. They tilt their not-quite-heads with a curiosity that feels like being recognized by your own reflection in water that is also looking somewhere else. Approach and they dissolve back into mist and residual intention, but the footprints remain a little longer than physics suggests they should, and the tenders step carefully around them for days afterward. Some wardens claim the shapes are the plant dreaming of all the other ways it might have grown. Others think they are simply what happens when too much memory is stored in too much water under too little sky. A few of the older editors have learned to stand very still inside the densest of these condensations and listen. They come away with new grafting techniques no one taught them, or with the sudden certain knowledge of which distant atrium is about to forget its own distances. They never speak of what else they hear.

At the outermost membranes, where frost still sometimes writes temporary letters on the leaves, a different population comes and goes: the ones who look outward.

They are fewer. Restless by local standards. They wear heavier layers against the true wind and carry water that has been boiled free of the plant’s basements. Some are born with a hunger the endless galleries cannot feed—an ache for horizons that do not curve back into more of the same baffled twilight. They make short, careful expeditions along the thinning edge, mapping the places where the megastructure finally admits it has a boundary, even if that boundary is only more curve vanishing into more dim. A few never return. The ones who do bring back samples of outside air (thinner, colder, tasting of distances the plant has never digested) and stories of seeing, once or twice, something that might have been another continent-fold of living architecture far enough away to look almost blue. The stay-at-home wardens listen politely and then return to their grafts. The world is already total. What lies beyond the membrane is at best a rumor the plant itself has not yet decided to incorporate.

And underneath every category, older than the tenders’ oldest songs, something like a mood moves through the entire structure—an attention without a center. It is not a god and not a mind. It is the cumulative weight of millennia of slow decisions made by cambium and cork and root and the countless small wills that tunnel and graze and wait. On certain days the light sweats a little greener. Distances grow generous or miserly according to no schedule the wardens have charted. Rooms that were pruned return for a night and then close again as if embarrassed. The plant holds together because holding together is the only habit it has never broken. Everything that lives inside it—clicking multitudes, drifting hungers, coiled blanknesses, elongated editors with red fiber and patience, curious weather that almost has faces—exists downstream of that single continuous refusal to become anything simpler.

They are all, in the end, expressions of the same long experiment: what happens when growth is given forever and almost no instructions.

The wardens keep editing. The grazers keep combing. The coils keep pretending to be walls. The footprints keep steaming a little longer than they should. And far below, somewhere that has become legendary even to them, the true ground continues not to matter.
