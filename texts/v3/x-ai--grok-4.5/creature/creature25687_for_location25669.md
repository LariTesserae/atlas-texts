They are the Husked.

Not a name they give themselves—there is no giving, no speech that rises above the dry click and the papery roar—but the shape the mind settles into once it has watched long enough. They are smaller than most wings, larger than most grains, and they do not so much live in the dish as constitute its living floor. Each body is a compact argument of joint and plate: six limbs as baseline, often eight or ten when the lineage has branched toward grasping or toward the slow continuous work of rasping. The exoskeleton is the color of cooled slag or of old epoxy gone matte; under certain angles it holds a faint pewter sheen, as if the metal remembered being polished once, long before any of them hatched. Mouthparts are complex and modular—mandibles that can scissor shed cuticle into snow, maxillae edged for fretting oxide from the ancient dust, and a set of finer palps that taste for the particular bitterness of a neighbor’s failing bio-electric charge.

They move in overlapping densities rather than in individuals. A single Husked is almost an optical error; the eye only trusts them when they form living ropes up the standing bolts, or when they pour as a dark tide across the northern arc of the dish, or when they pack themselves into the hollow of the half-swallowed gear housing until the cavity seems to breathe. Their locomotion is a constant micro-correction: claws find purchase on powdered zeolite and on one another’s backs with equal readiness. Some castes trail hair-thin filaments—remnant wiring, perhaps, or sensory outgrowths that never received the signal to stop growing. These filaments snag, break, re-sprout; they litter the mulch like discarded nerve endings and are later harvested by the same mouths that grew them.

Inside each body the chemistry is closed and thrifty. There is no gut in the green sense, only a series of mineral-leaching chambers and a dense tangle of mycelium-like conductive threads that redistribute scrapes of iron, traces of salt, and the protein of cannibalized kin. They farm one another’s molts with the patience of archivists. A shed carapace is not waste; it is currency and cradle and temporary armor. The weak are not so much killed as disassembled in place—quiet ceremonies of many legs holding one body still while finer tools unmake it, plate by plate, until only a slick of reusable ions remains. From a distance these acts look like grooming; only the slight increase in density and the brief, brightening bio-electric flicker betray the feast.

Caste is temporary and decided by the pressure of bodies and by the faint residual heat of the buried motor. When the thrum rises even a fraction, convection veils form and a winged morph appears—thinner plates, elongated thoracic segments, wings like scorched paper that produce a roar startlingly large for their scale. These flyers do not travel far; the dish is their entire climate. They rise, mate in the dimmed light, and settle again as a silver-gray rain of eggs that the next dark swarm will mostly devour. Survival is a statistical whisper. A few eggs, tucked into the threaded valleys of the upright bolts or beneath the cooling fin, escape long enough to hatch into the next pulse.

They possess no face that maps onto anything human, yet something like attention moves through the swarm. Antennae—some feathered, some reduced to stubby electrostatic sensors—constantly sample the air for the metallic tang that is their weather. When a larger fragment of the old factory is uncovered by their own tunneling (a tooth from the vanished conveyor, a flake of insulation), the nearest density pauses. Mandibles test it. Bio-electric glints race along abdominal plates in short coordinated waves. Then the fragment is either incorporated—dragged into the mulch to become new substrate—or ignored so completely it might as well have returned to the plain. Preference is not individual; it is a temporary election held by thousands of bodies at once.

Time for them is the motor’s slow heartbeat crossed with the faster metronome of mandibles. Generations are measured in the stiffening of a watcher’s knees. Empires of density seize half the dish, gorge, fracture, and sink back into the galleries under the rim, leaving only a finer grade of dust and the faint impress of countless claws. There is no memory that outlasts the mulch, yet the mulch itself is a kind of memory: stratified shed skins, powdered zeolite, microscopic filings, broken antennae, all of it soft and shifting and several thumb-widths deep. To walk across it is to walk across the compressed history of their appetites.

They are not aware of the larger plain, nor of the absence of rain, nor of the fact that their pocket is the last soft concavity in a world worn flatter than memory. The dish is the world. Its rim is the edge of everything. Outside lies a silence so complete it does not even qualify as death—death, at least, is a process they understand intimately. Inside, the air remains cool and dry as orthoclase, light arrives already partially drunk, and the Husked continue their frantic, finished business: rasping, moulting, dismantling, reassembling, riding the faint heat, filling every hollow, making of residual machinery a cathedral of pure saturation.

If you remain long enough at the rim, the seething resolves into faces that are not faces—patterns of limb and glint that suggest recognition, or hunger, or the simple taut readiness of a system that has nowhere else to go and so goes endlessly nowhere, perfecting itself inside its own sealed dream. When you finally stand and step back, they do not watch you leave. They have already turned the attention of ten thousand antennae back to the next molt, the next egg, the next quiet ceremony of unmaking. The dish closes over their sound the way a hand closes over water, and the plain forgets them again.
