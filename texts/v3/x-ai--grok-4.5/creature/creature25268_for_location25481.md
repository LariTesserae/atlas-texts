The ones who live here are never in a hurry, and after a while you understand why. Speed would be a kind of blasphemy against the pace the place has chosen for itself.

They are tall, but not with the brittle elongation of low-gravity folk; their height is a slow, deliberate reaching, vertebra by vertebra, as if each of them has decided independently to meet the verticality halfway. Skin the color of wet clay or bruised orchid, always sheened, always faintly cool to the touch even though the air is bathwater. You could mistake them at first for another layer of the vegetation—until one turns its head and the eyes catch soft dome-light, pale green or amber, unblinking for long stretches, lashes beaded like the moth wings that sometimes settle on their shoulders and are not brushed away.

They do not wear clothes so much as cultivate them. A living kirtle of moss and fine vine starts at the collarbone and descends in controlled cascades, rooted in shallow dermal ports that weep a clear nutrient lymph. The growth is pruned daily with small obsidian blades kept tucked behind one ear; the trimmings are tucked into the same ports so nothing is lost. Over this green layer they sometimes shrug a lattice of thin titanium mesh, scavenged from earlier construction phases, hammered soft and draped like chain-mail that has learned manners. The metal ticks faintly as it warms to their skin. When they move along the catwalks the undernodes of the mesh leave momentary darker prints in the water film, spoors that fade within seconds.

Their hands are the clearest signature. Long-fingered, yes, but the last joint of each finger splits into three finer tendrils that can braid around a conduit, tease open a stubborn hydraulic valve, or slip into the soft binder-stone to adjust a root anchor while the stone is still curing. At rest the tendrils coil back into the appearance of ordinary fingertips. Children (there are children, though they are quiet and infrequent) practice this coiling and uncoiling for hours on the lower aprons of the catch-basin, sitting cross-legged so the warm black water laps their knees, trying to catch the pale moths without harming the dusted wings. Success is rare and celebrated only by a shared exhalation that briefly clears the mist around them.

They sleep where the heat exchangers sigh, clustered in hammocks of living cable that the vines have been encouraged to weave. Sleep is light and rotational; at least two adults remain awake on any given level, walking the slow inspection routes, palm to bulkhead, listening with bones for micro-fractures or for the particular thrum that means a new deck ram is about to wake. When a section does advance—when the polite clank sounds and another meter of empty air is claimed—the whole community feels it as a ripple through the soles of the feet. Work stops. Faces turn toward the expanding edge. Someone begins a low harmonic in the back of the throat, a sound almost below hearing, and others join until the note densifies the wet air itself. It lasts under a minute. Then the vines are guided forward, the atomizers recalibrated, the capillary felts extended, and the place continues become.

They speak little aloud. When they do, the words ride the same frequency as the turbine murmur, easily mistaken for part of the machinery’s digestion. Most exchange happens by touch: a tendril-tip pressed to another’s wrist floods both nervous systems with shared sensory memory—where the moss is thinning, which riser is carrying a faint metallic taste that means filterwork tomorrow, the exact sweet-sour of a fern crozier ready to uncurl. Jealousy, ambition, the sharper social hungers have not vanished, but they are expressed as slight redistributions of care: one gardener denser attention here, another there. Scarcity is almost impossible to maintain when the walls themselves sweat nutrient and the light never fails.

Once in a great while a new one is made rather than born. It happens in the sealed engine rooms during the brief windows when the doors irreversible-cycle open. Two or more adults enter with a lattice armature and a slurry of cultured cells and binding gels. What emerges months later is already half-grown, dripping, eyes open, the moss cloak already sparging across its shoulders. These constructed kin are no less loved; they simply carry a different silence, a faint internal tick that never completely matches natural heartbeat. They tend to choose the deepest undercroft for their sleep coils, nearer the turbines, as if homesick for the first sounds they knew.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing flees them. The small jointed service-creatures click past their ankles and are sometimes lifted, examined, set back on course with a corrective twist of a leg joint. The moths land and drink from the corners of smiling mouths. The place and its people have reached a seamless metabolic agreement: the inhabitants keep the unfinished geology knitting itself true, and in return the gorge-that-is-not-a-gorge keeps them endlessly warm, endlessly wet, endlessly in the middle of becoming. They have no word for outside. When the rare thought of horizontal distance arises, it is experienced as a mild nausea, quickly soothed by setting one’s back against a bulkhead and feeling water sheet across both spine and steel until the boundary dissolves again.

They are still inventing their own fossils. In unused niches you can already find deliberate caches—shed mesh, a child’s first tendril-sheath, a single moth wing pressed between layers of drying moss—placed where future drips will mineralize them into something that might one day be mistaken for memory in stone. The makers of these caches do not call it hope. They call it maintenance.
