The dwellers do not arrive so much as condense.

At first they are only the heat itself taking slower shapes: densities in the pewter gloom where the clay’s own breath thickens and folds. A softness gathers against the inner curve of a half-raised gallery, longest on the side that still remembers fluid. It does not yet have edges. Warmth pools inside it the way moisture pools in the hand-span mirrors; it holds the dim light rather than reflects it. Only after many slow cycles of drip and dry does the softness remember it can press back. Filament by filament it parts from the wall that cradled it, leaving a smoother scar that the termite-builders will later mistake for finished work and build upon.

What stands free is small—never taller than the highest fluted spire, often far less—and perpetually unfinished in the same register as the ground. The body is a low, ambulatory hollow. Outer surfaces remain clay-colored and faintly glossy, plated in irregular lozenges that fit together like cooling mudflat. Between the plates, thinner membrane shows the dim internal glow of the same warmth that rises from the earth; when the creature turns, the light shifts under the skin like banked embers under ash. Limbs are optional and temporary. Most often it moves by peristalsis of the whole form, a rolling pour that leaves behind a brief shine of moisture later drunk by moss. When it needs height—to nose the wet undersides of the broad leaves, to listen at the lip of a tower—it extrudes three or four soft struts, each ending in a splayed, tactile pad that tastes mineral and memory. The struts can stiffen with crystalline fibers borrowed from the young rock, then dissolve again when the need passes. Nothing is kept past its usefulness. Everything is renegotiated with the surrounding unfinishedness.

They are many, yet the eye never quite manages a census. In the densest heart of the mound-city they press gently together, sharing walls, exchanging titers of heat and quiet information through the plates. A cluster may look for a time like one larger organism—an irregular, breathing tumulus perforated by the same arched galleries the smaller builders construct. Then it calving: individuals loosen, pour down separate ramps, go out among the spaced thickets to feed. Feeding is not predation in any abrupt sense. They settle over a patch of the wine-dark undersides or a cushion of moss and simply become continuous with it for a while. Chlorophyll and slow sugars migrate across the membrane. When they rise again the plant is threadbare but already greening at the edges, as if grazed by something that also knows how to apologize in the language of growth. The small amphibians sometimes rest against them, throat-sacs pulsing in shared rhythm; the iridescent beetles rake algae from their lower plates and are not disturbed. Even the sightless tower-raisers will incorporate a dweller’s discarded lozenge into a new buttress, sealing it over with fresh spit-clay until it becomes architecture again.

Their senses are continuous with the courtyard’s own weather. They hear the condensation forming before it falls—each future droplet a tiny bright pressure against the skin. They taste the difference between a mist that will pass and a mist that will stay long enough to wake the pocket wetlands. When a heavier rain begins to melt some outer facade of the settlement, dozens of them flow toward the damage without alarm. They lie down along the softening ridges and lend their own firmer interiors as temporary rebar, holding shape until the tireless mandibles can pack and rebuild. By morning the tower stands altered, carrying inside it a faint new curve that once belonged to a living flank. The city dreams through them; they house the city in turn.

They do not speak, yet the air between their bodies is never empty of meaning. Micro-currents of warmer and cooler breath braid into temporary glyphs that another can lean into and answer. A grammar of proximity: the angle at which one plate faces another, the duration of shared heat, the choice to harden a limb or keep it fluid. In this language they recite the only history that matters here—the ongoing minutes of becoming. They remember, in the crystalline fibers, every previous version of the hollow: when the clay was wetter, when the first broad leaves were only coiled fists, when the mounds were lower and farther apart. That memory is not kept in any skull. It is sifted through the whole ambulatory hollow, so that an individual who pours itself thin across a warm ledge is also a living archive of seams that no longer exist.

At the center of the densest courts something older sometimes gathers. Several dwellers braid together more tightly and for longer, plates unlocking and interlocking until the boundaries go uncertain. The resulting presence is no taller than before, but denser, a concentrated dusk that makes the pewter light seem brighter by contrast. Heat deepens. The smaller fauna orbit it at respectful distance. Moisture beads on every nearby frond and does not fall. In that gathered state they appear to listen downward, past the embryonic rock, past the last residues of the fluid that pressed the place into shape, toward whatever vast patience first decided to cup this hollow in the palm of the world and then look away. The listening lasts a night, or a season of nights. When it ends the braid loosens. Individuals pour outward again carrying some new instruction that is really only a new patience: raise the western galleries half a thumb higher; leave the southeastern wetland undrained one day longer; let the thin climbers lace that particular gap until they form a living door. The builders sense the shift and adjust without knowing why. The plants lean infinitesimally closer. The courtyard continues.

They die the way everything here ends—by unfinishedness becoming too complete. An elder’s plates lose their inner light first, cooling to matte. The form settles against a ledge or into a shallow seam and simply stops renegotiating. Moss takes the lozenges one by one. Soft-furred mammals may widen the resting place into a den, sleeping inside the gentle architecture of what used to move. The builders plaster over it; roots thread it. In time the shape is only a slightly warmer swell in the clay, a place the condensation prefers to bead. No monument. The memory of that particular hollow of warmth is already distributed through every other body that once shared heat with it. Nothing is lost that the courtyard still needs.

Stand among them long enough and the distinction between inhabitant and place thins to a membrane you can almost see through. Their slow pouring locomotion is only the clay remembering forward. Their feeding is the vegetation’s own dream of circulation. Their gathered listening is the dim light trying, for a moment, to look back at itself. What lives here is the courtyard’s unfinished thought given temporary skins of heat and patience. It has no name that would survive translation onto harder lands. Inside the hollow it does not need one. It simply continues—lush, quiet, spaced, and always still arriving.
