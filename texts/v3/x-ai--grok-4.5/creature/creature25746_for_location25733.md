They are the ones who never dry.

You notice the feet first, because the ground demands them. Broad, splayed, the toes webbed by soft membranes that have thickened into permanent paddles, the soles ridged with callus that never hardens all the way into horn. Each step plants and sucks free with a wet kiss that has become the city’s second heartbeat. They do not lift those feet high; they shuffle, glide, lean into the silt so the body stays low and stable on surfaces that still remember being liquid. Ankles are thick, almost continuous with the calf. When they stand still for long moments—quern work, gut-scraping, the endless ramming of slumped walls—their weight sinks them half an inch into the paste, and they rise again without comment, trailing gray ribbons.

The skin is next. It is not amphibian skin, though it shares the same glisten. It is mammal skin that has surrendered every follicle, every hope of fur or scale that might catch the endless condensate. It shines constantly, a living oil that mixes with the mineral film settling out of the air. Color runs from dull pewter to the warm brown of steeped tea, mottled where older scars have refused to tan the same way. Across the shoulders and the heavy upper arms the skin thickens into soft plates, almost like the beginnings of the mineral crusts that armor the scavengers, but these never fully mineralize; they stay flexible, slightly puffy, good for bracing against wet stone. In the folds—armpit, groin, the deep crease where neck meets collarbone—the sheen becomes a thin mucus that smells of iron and old broth. They touch one another constantly in the press of the warrens, and that mucus transfers, a second language of recognition.

Bodies are short, dense, built for torque rather than reach. Centers of gravity sit low. Ribcages are barrelled, made to hold air that is half water vapor; you hear the wet bellows of their breathing even when they are not speaking. Arms are long relative to the legs, ending in hands whose fingers carry the same slight webbing as the toes, the nails reduced to thick, translucent shields good for prying and scraping. They climb well—bone rafters, cantilevered clay ledges, the vertical shafts that serve as lungs for the stacked houses—but they do not leap. Everything is three-point contact, deliberate, bodies kept close to whatever surface they use. When they carry loads (wicker-and-hide sperm-sacs of filtered slurry, bundles of jointed eel-things still twitching, plaques of freshly cut sinew) they lean so far forward they seem about to fold, yet never do.


The face is where the wet has done its most intimate work. Eyes are large, dark, set wide, the lids heavy and permanently beaded with droplets that they never wipe away. The eyes themselves have a nictitating veil that flicks sideways in the thicker mists; you only notice it when the light catches the sudden inner membrane. Noses are flat, nostrils able to pinch almost shut when they submerge to clear a clogged canal grate or to harvest the pale, phosphorescent amphibia that pack the deeper channels. Mouths are wide. Lips thick. Teeth are not for display; the fronts are spatulate for scraping algae-films that never get to be true plants, the backs heavier for cracking the silica cases of the insects they roast on hot stones above the vent-seeps. Their tongues are startlingly precise tools—long enough to clean the eyes, sensitive enough to taste mineral content in the water they cup in their palms a hundred times a day.

They speak in a register trained to slice the roar. Consonants are percussive, made with tongue against palate that clicks wetly; vowels are open and carried on diaphragms that have learned to push through the pressure of sound. Names are short, often one or two syllables that can be barked across a canal without dissolving. Conversation is constant and layered—instruction, warning, endearment, Trade, the recitation of which walls are slumping today, which packs of the warm-blooded dog-things have turned scrap-hungry, which vents are running hotter. They do not fall silent. Silence would be a kind of drowning. Even in the half-sunk storerooms where the water table glints a handspan below the sleeping shelves, they murmur in their sleep, a low communal thrum that answers the geothermal gut-rumble of the basin itself.

Children are born already sheened, eyes open, lungs already sealed against the wet. They learn to swim before they walk, or rather the two are the same motion. A mother will slip her infant into the nearest canal channel where the soft late-stage amphibia throng, and the child dog-paddles among them, tasting the same water, learning the same currents. By the time the adult dentition comes in, a child can filter a mouthful of silt for the tiny jointed eels that live inside it, crack them, and spit the empty tubes without breaking stroke. They grow fast and dense. There is no lean season to slow them. Adolescence is marked only by the darkening of the shoulder plates and the first deep string-scars where sinew harnesses have rubbed for years.


They keep no pets. The hairless warm-blooded packs that run the upper walkways are not companions; they are co-tenants and occasional prey and occasional threat, depending on the crash-cycle of numbers. Sometimes a family will toss scraps to a particular group until the animals bed near their platform, a living alarm system whose communal yammering changes pitch when larger plated scavengers nose too close. The relationship ends the day hunger reverses. Bones of those dog-things appear in the same middens as everything else, scraped clean, cracked for marrow that tastes of the same mineral broth that cooks in every belly.

Tools never leave their hands for long. A mature adult’s palms are a topography of specific calluses: here the groove from the bone-hafted pounder used to re-ram earth walls, here the polished oval from twisting sinew into cord, here the knife-edge ridge from cutting gut. They make fire the way they make everything—persistently, with damp fuel that has to be coaxed, the resulting smolder more smoke than flame, yet enough to bake the clay blocks and sear the frogs and drive off the worst of the night-dim insects. They remember, in the muscles rather than in any lasting record, exactly how many generations particular ledges have held. When a fresh joint opens in the weeping stone, they are there within a day with slabs and sinew, not to stop the stone’s arrested birth but to incorporate the bright new wound into the warren, another vertical surface to be built against.


They do not dream of elsewhere. The mist is the sky. The endless unfinished floor is the world. To walk for hours and find only the same dripping density is not monotony; it is proof of rightness. A face glimpsed across three canals may be kin or stranger or future mate or last week’s quarrel; the closeness makes every category provisional. They quarrel loudly and reconcile by shared labor the same hour. They grieve by packing the body into a silt niche with the same gestures used for wall repair, then eating what the scavengers do not take first. Nothing is wasted because waste itself is only another denseness waiting to be reabsorbed.

In the perpetual half-light their eyes catch one another and hold. Something passes that is not quite thought, not quite heat: an acknowledgment that the basin continues, that the noise continues, that the wet sheen on every body is the same wet, forever. Then they look away and resume the work—grinding, lasing, poling the hide boats, shouting the prices of fresh-caught phosphorescence, ramming earth, birthing, dying, thronging. They are the city and the city is them: a single warm-blooded crust breathing in the pewter gloom, unfinished as the stone, eternal as the drip, crowded beyond any outside measure of comfort, and utterly, precisely at home.
