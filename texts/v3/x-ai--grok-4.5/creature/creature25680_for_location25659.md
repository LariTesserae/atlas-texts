The ones who live here do not name the place, nor themselves, nor the difference between the two.

They are not a single kind. The Quiet Columns hold a long braided occupancy, layered the way pressure layers water: some bodies almost as old as the finished wood, some renewed every slow season by eggs adrift in the dark, some that are less body than lingering habit of motion. What they share is the knowledge—unspoken, unthought—that nothing further will be required of them. They have inherited an ending that did not end.

Begin with the small and numerous, the ones whose light is the only weather. Schools of rough-skinned fish the length of a forearm move in loose thunderheads between the columns. Their flanks carry fields of cold blue photophores that do not flash in alarm or courtship but in long, rolling fronts, like sheet lightning under cloud. When two schools pass through each other the lights braid and part without collision; the fish themselves seem almost porous to their own kind. They feed on the finer snow of organic dust that still sifts from the membranes overhead, and on the soft outer tissues of the gelatin filter-webs. Their speech is a continuous low grating of fin and scale, audible as texture more than tone. They do not school for safety. Nothing hunts them with urgency. They school because the open water between pillars is too wide to cross alone without forgetting the shape of togetherness.

Larger lives patrol the avenues. These are the plated serpentine ones, longer than the girth of the columns they glide past, bodies armored in overlapping shields of a material that is neither bone nor shell but something denser, secretive, grown in silence. Their heads are blunt battering rams of cartilage and sensor pits; their eyes are pale lamps that stay half-lidded. They move with the unhurried authority of things that have outlasted every different arrangement of the world. From time to time one will coil once around a column and hang there for a long while, listening through its plates to the faint hum the living wood still holds. Their calls are not voices but displacements—vast, soft concussions that travel farther than light ever could here. When two call together the galleries between trunks shiver and the root-mat floors flex as if trapped breath were moving under them. What they say to one another is never urgent. It is the same statement repeated across centuries: *still here*. *Still here*.

The filter-webs occupy the mid-waters like discarded lungs. Each is a translucent, faintly pulsing architecture of gelatin and cilia, stretched in irregular polygons from one column to the next or left free to drift until a current pins it somewhere new. They are animals, though barely. Crowd them in the mind and they begin to feel more like weather systems that learned to hunger. Water passes through them and leaves behind whatever fine life it carried. When a web has taken enough it contracts in a single massive pulse, a soft concussion that reads as both satisfaction and relocation. Fragments sometimes tear free and become new webs. They have no central mind anyone can locate, yet they avoid the warmer sighs that rise from the smooth apertures, and they never foul the vertical rivers of eels.

Those eels. Inside certain hollow columns the silver ones turn forever. They are longer than the fish, thinner than hope, and so precisely matched to their looped path that the column’s inner bark has been polished to a soft sheen by the endless passage of their bodies. No eel is ever seen to enter or leave. Whether they were sealed in when the wood finished growing or whether they simply found a circuit that made entrance and exit meaningless is a question that does not arise here. Their scales catch what little light the other creatures drop and throw it back in thin circulating ribbons. The sound they make is a continuous silk whisper against wood and against one another. Sometimes a plated serpent will rest its head near a hollow column and seem to listen to that whisper for hours, as if the sound contained news. It does not. It contains only continuance.

Lower, and closer to the bark, move the shell-crabs. They are the color of wet slate, carapaces ridged like miniature seafloor, legs jointed for patient vertical travel. They graze the last living films of algae that still cling to residual bioluminescence. Their mouths work sideways in ceaseless rasping. Where two crabs meet they tap claws in a brief tactile code and part; collisions are rare and almost courtly. Occasionally one discovers a geometric stretch of grain—hexagons nested, arcs repeating—and will pause there longer than feeding requires, claws resting in the shallow channels as if the pattern were warm. Then it moves on. Nothing is built. Nothing is altered. The crabs clean what little grows and in cleaning keep the architecture exactly as it was left.

There are other lives, quieter still. Transparent amphipods no larger than grains of sand ride the pressure waves of the serpents’ calls the way birds once rode thermals. Colonial organisms like slow smoke drift through the upper galleries, tasting the wood with a thousand small mouths and finding it still faintly sweet. Now and then a warmer vent sighs and a brief bouquet of chemosynthetic bacteria blooms in the rising plume; within a day the bloom is grazed to nothing and the temperature equalizes again. Even these fleeting lives seem to understand the larger pact: take what the equilibrium offers, leave the shape of things untouched.

No intelligence here sharpens itself against tools or names or futures. Yet something like attention accumulates. The plated serpents remember—by some chemical ledger written into their long bodies—the locations of every hollow column rich with eels, every aperture that still sometimes breathes warmth. The fish schools carry, in the timing of their light fronts, a map of currents that have not changed since the columns stopped growing. The crabs know by wear which stretches of bark yield the best films. Knowledge exists; it simply has no interest in becoming history.

And there are rarer presences. Once in a great while a shadow larger than the serpents drifts through the outermost avenues: a being so sparsely configured it is almost only absence given outline, its body a lattice of dark gel and slow-burning photophores that glow a deep, vascular red. It never feeds where others can see. It never calls. It pauses sometimes beside a column whose grain has fallen into nested arcs and extends a thin appendage to trace the pattern without quite touching. Then it folds itself smaller and moves on, leaving no wake. The others do not flee it or follow it. They treat it as they treat the columns themselves—as a fact older than preference.

All of them together keep the noise that makes the name Quiet Columns a lie. It is a layered, accumulated sound: the thunder of fish light, the pressure-song of serpents, the soft concussions of the webs, the rasp of crabs, the silk circulation of eels, the faint continuous clicks of a thousand smaller lives adjusting themselves against the dark. The sound never peaks. It never falls silent. It is the audible form of equilibrium.

They live without wishing the architecture into any different shape. They live without waiting for the sun that will never return. They live as content endings live—by remaining. The water moves through them and around them. The columns stand. The spaces between remain open and dark and sufficient. Nothing is becoming. Everything is.
