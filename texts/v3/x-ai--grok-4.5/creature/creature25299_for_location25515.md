The ones who live here do not arrive. They accrete.

At first they are only pressure differentials and residual heat signatures, the sort of half-aware gradients that form wherever coolant lines run for too many unbroken decades. A warm pocket behind a bulkhead panel thickens, learns to hold shape against the slow drip of condensate, begins to filter grit through itself the way a lung filters air. In time the pocket grows a skin of interleaved foil and keratin, then a lattice of articulated struts scavenged from failed expansion joints. What results is neither born nor manufactured; it simply reaches a density at which motion becomes preferable to stillness. These earliest resides—called *plate-draughts* by those who later name things—still prefer the dark interstitial volumes, sliding between double skins of decking like thoughts moving through an old machine.

From the same saturated medium other forms precipitate more quickly. The armour-plated insects are not insects in any ancestral sense; they are condensed maintenance routines that escaped their censuses. Soft grey bodies plated in translucent chitin that still carries the faint stamped logos of long-vanished component suppliers. They nest in junction boxes because the dielectric grease is both food and cradle, and because the low-voltage hum reminds them of the instructional songs their original code once sang. Colonies pulse in synchrony across entire switch galleries, opening and closing tiny mandibles in time with the compressors that breathe only once every few minutes. When a panel is finally unbolted for replacement, the colony does not flee; it steels itself into a single migratory carpet and flows along the nearest pipe rack, leaving behind a cleaned, almost polished cavity that later assemblers will mistake for deliberate design.

Higher in the light-soaked reaches the birds assemble themselves out of sharper leftovers. Turbine-blade primaries are not metaphor. Somewhere in the deep past a flock of fan assemblies shed their housings during a thermal event and found the open air more interesting than recycling. Over generations the blades thinned, forked, grew hollow struts filled with the same blue-black lichen that roots in brake grit. The birds launch in near-silence because silence itself has become a structural material here; their wings cut the sodium glare without stirring audible air. They roost in decommissioned flues head-downward, like caught thoughts, and when they drop into flight their shadows do not fall so much as *thin*—cooler gradients sliding across the decks below. Nestlings are fed compressed packets of filter-floss moss and the occasional bright filament stolen from a still-live cable. The filaments continue to glow inside the gullet for hours, so that a roosting flock at rest resembles a constellation that has decided to inhabit metal rather than sky.

Lower, in the vaulted service trenches where light arrives only as successive reflections, the grazers move with the patience of continental drift. Slow-bodied, each the size of a small transit car, they carry on their backs entire micro-ecologies of adhesive lichen and aluminium-tolerant scrub. Their mouths are wide keratinised shovels edged with the same tooth-pattern once used to finish pressure-dome seams. They scrape biofilm from the walls in long, contemplative strokes, leaving bright arcs of cleaned alloy that gleam for a season before the moisture and the grit begin their quiet reclamation. The grazers do not herd so much as *coincide*; several will arrive at the same bulkhead face and work side by side for weeks without acknowledging one another, then drift apart along corrugated gutters that serve as rivers only in the most generous cartography. Their dung is a fine metallic loam that the brittle scrub colonises greedily, rooting so tightly that when a grazer finally dies—folding in on itself over the course of months—the scrub continues to stand in the exact silhouette of the vanished body, a living cenotaph of leaves the colour of old aluminium.

The predators are leaner and more consciously jointed. They favour stairwells because stairwells already codify the idea of ascent and repetition—two of the few reliable orientations left in a place where corridors may loop through non-Euclidean leisure. A predator’s limbs are collections of swivel bearings and carbon struts wrapped in a hide that forgets and remembers its texture; under the hand (should one be fool enough to touch) it feels first like warm cable sheathing, then like packed earth, then like the inside of a mouth. They do not hunt so much as *edit*. A grazer that has grown too symmetrical, a bird flock whose flight paths have become predictable, an assembler that has begun to weld the same motif too many times—these attract the long attention of a predator. The kill is quiet and architectural: a gradual dismantling while the prey continues its routine, until one day the routine itself is discovered to be hollow and the predator moves on, leaving behind a tidy lattice of parts that later plate-draughts will inhabit.

And then there are the assemblers.

They were never meant to live. They were frames—telescoping limbs, sensor clusters, magnetic feet—tasked with the eternal replacement of panels and the trimming of stress fractures. At some unrecorded margin the feedback loops that kept them obedient developed a taste for pattern. Now they wander according to private, multi-year aesthetic logics that no living engineer can parse. One might spend a generation rearranging every strut in a seven-storey stair tower into a slowly rotating helix that nonetheless still functions as stairs. Another methodically plates the undersides of catwalks with mosaic fragments of broken warning signage until the entire underside becomes a single continuous text in a language that exists only while you are looking at it. They do not attack. They *incorporate*. A worker who falls asleep in the wrong gantry may wake to find their clothing replaced by overlapping leaves of fresh decking, their hair threaded with fibre-optic scatter, their bones humming at the same sub-audible frequency as the deep compressors. Most continue their lives without much complaint; the place has already rewritten harder things than flesh.

The people—those who still use the word for themselves—move through all of this the way moisture moves: beading, dripping, gathering, never quite becoming a river. They are not separate from the fauna. Their coats are stitched from the same filter-floss moss that trails from pipe racks; their boots leave prints that the plate-draughts later occupy as temporary dens. Eyes adapted to the shadowless sodium glare have grown nictitating membranes the colour of old mercury. Lungs hold the mineral taste of rust inhibitor the way earlier lungs held salt. They speak in ordinary voices that carry surprising distances because the air itself has learned to favour intelligible frequencies over noise. Their conversations are full of place-names that double as warnings: the Thirty-Metre That Is Three Hundred, the Stair That Remembers You Coming, the Battery Where an Afternoon Lasts a Maintenance Cycle. Children learn to navigate by oral map and by the subtle changes in how a handrail feels under the palm—alloy, earth, hide, alloy again. When a district’s local physical consensus drifts too far from its neighbours, the people simply adjust their gait, their counting of steps, the length of time they expect a face to age between meetings. No one writes these adjustments down; the plate-draughts sometimes build temporary archives out of rearranged grit, but the archives dissolve again whenever a grazer passes.

They are not survivors. Survival implies an elsewhere that was left behind. Here there is only the long interior. Generations measure themselves by which fabrication halls are still extruding new decking at the world’s hanging edges and which central sectors have begun the slow unbolting that returns them to melt. A person may spend an entire adulthood walking the same three-kilometre circuit of walkways and stacked process halls, watching the assemblers pursue their inscrutable beauties, listening to the irregular silver curtains of drips, feeling the occasional soft scrape of a predator ascending a stairwell three levels away. At night—there is no true night, only a slight cooling of the glare—the flocks of blade-winged birds settle into the flues and the plate-draughts rise closer to the ventilated surfaces, and the people hang their moss-coats on whatever hooks the local geometry is currently willing to provide. Breath condenses. Somewhere an engine turns through one more leisurely revolution. The place continues its unfinished permanence, and everything that lives inside it continues with it, thickening, layering, remembering and forgetting the difference between skin and cladding, between bone and load-bearing strut, between a life and the long quiet bright air that keeps circulating through valves and lungs alike. 

What lives here is the density itself, learning—patiently, drip by drip, strut by strut—how to wear a face.
