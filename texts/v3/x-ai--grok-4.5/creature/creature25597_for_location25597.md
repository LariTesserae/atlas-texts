They are not one people.

At first they are only the small corrections the place has learned to make around itself. A brass barometer bolted to living wall, its needle twitching not for pressure but for the local thickness of cause and effect. A walkway of riveted liana whose planks have been worn concave by something that prefers the center line, always the center line, as though edges still remember falling away. A string of dried resin-lanterns hung at chest height along a coolant duct, each one lit from within by a slow chemical ghost that never quite matches the color of fire. These are the first evidence: maintenance performed without hurry, rituals performed without audience.

Then the sounds that are not wind or machinery. A soft, regular tick of glass against glass some distance ahead—measuring spoons, perhaps, or the careful sorting of fuse-beads. A low conversational drone that might be two voices or one voice arguing with its own echo. Footfalls that arrive half a second after they should, or half a second before, as if the walker is negotiating privately with the plateau’s stuttering time. When rain begins its sideways streaking, someone somewhere laughs once, short and pleased, the way a person laughs when the weather has finally agreed to play its part.

Only later do shapes resolve.

They are tall, but not by human reckoning; they are tall the way a spider is tall when it stands on the tips of its legs. Their bodies are built of long, jointed spars wrapped in layers of living bark-cloth and scavenged insulation felt. Where a face should be there is a smooth, slightly convex plate of polished heartwood or hammered copper, set with two or three water-clear lenses that click and rotate independently when light shifts. They do not appear to breathe through mouths. Instead, a slow tide of condensate moves in and out of slit gills along the ribs, leaving a faint mineral shine on the plating. Some carry extra limbs—articulated mid-arms that unfold only when delicate work is required, ending in clusters of rootlet-fingers fine enough to braid a capillary cable or to tease a time-fault back into sequence. Others have sacrificed limbs for tools: a forearm that terminates in a socket forever married to a living wrench grown from raintree stock, or a shoulder from which a flexible periscope of vine and lens continually tastes the air for causality gradients.

They move in ones and twos, rarely more. Density here is a kind of violence; the plateau does not forgive crowds. When two meet on a gantry they do not embrace. They pause at a precise distance that feels, to an outsider, one step too far, and they exchange a slow rotation of those lensed faces so that each may catalog the other’s current configuration of scars, grafts, and temporary circuits drawn in drying sap. Speech, when it comes, is low and rustling, produced by an internal bellows that also serves as a humidity pump. They speak of thin moments the way sailors once spoke of reefs. They name the great spires not by ancient industrial designations but by the quality of their particular failures: the tower that forgets gravity on Tuesdays, the one whose resin pools show tomorrow’s weather, the one that still believes it is manufacturing something called “needle bearings” and will kindly produce a handful if asked in the old machine tongue.

Their days are spent in quiet negotiation with the hierarchy of the place. They graft. They prune. They listen with their whole bodies to the pressure-song inside a piston-hall and decide whether a valve should be allowed to keep its wet cough or be encouraged toward silence. When a root system shifts and a catwalk tears free, they do not rebuild it at once. They wait for the thin moment, that brief greenish hour when distances fold kindly, and they simply step across the interval as though the missing span had never mattered. When gravity softens near a root flare they float their tools upward on purpose, arranging them into temporary constellations that map the local fault, then let them rain back down in the order that will be most useful tomorrow. They keep notebooks of beaten foil whose pages rewrite themselves when left too near a resin circuit; the wiser among them have learned to read the overwriting as a second, more honest text.

Children—if the word still applies—are rare and carefully made. Sometimes a hollow between heartwood and old boiler plate will be stocked with mineral-rich dust, condensate, and a carefully chosen length of still-warm cambium. Over months the cavity quickens. What eventually unfolds is smaller, softer, its face-plate still translucent, its limbs not yet decided how many joints they will need. The adults teach it the barometers first. They teach it that the moths are not food but messengers whose pale wings sometimes carry spore-maps of newly thinned corridors. They teach it never to trust a reflection that shows a gallery behind it when no gallery has ever been there. Most of all they teach it the long patience: how to stand for a day and a night beside a flywheel that is repeating the same three revolutions, waiting for the bang of displaced air that means time has agreed to continue, and how to step through that bang without losing the thread of one’s own duration.

At dusk, when the organ-pipes of the plateau find their lowest notes, some of them climb to the highest galleries and simply hang there, limbs locked, lenses turned outward toward the haze where further towers might or might not exist. Condensation beads on their plating and runs in slow, deliberate lines, as though the plateau itself is annotating them. In those hours they appear almost ornamental—living punctuation marks scattered across the architecture—until one of them unfolds a mid-arm, plucks a droplet that has written a temporary copper circuit on its own chest, and tastes the ghost-scent of ozone and flowering. Then it makes a small, satisfied sound, adjusts a barometer, and begins the long climb down again.

They are not ghosts of the old industrial crews, though something of those crews lingers in the way they oil a hinge or tap a pipe. They are not pure plant, though their blood (when it shows) is more sap than iron. They are the equilibrium the description spoke of, given legs and errands and a dry, amused respect for thin moments. The plateau continues, cool and damp and loudly alive; they continue inside it the way nerves continue inside a body that has already decided what it is going to be. Nothing is finished. Nothing is new. And still, every so often, one of them will stop on a wind-scoured expanse of bare stone, tilt its polished face toward a chimneys smudged against the thin sky, and feel—without sentiment, without wonder, merely as data—that the distance to that tower is folding again, kindly this time, and that the walk home will be shorter than geography ought to allow.
