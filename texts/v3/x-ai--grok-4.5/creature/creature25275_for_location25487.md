The longer you remain, the less the emptiness feels empty.

At first it is only the currents themselves that seem inhabited—those slow invisible animals the water keeps sending across the open middle. They nose the pillars, linger, shear apart, re-form. You begin to notice that some of them prefer certain routes. One always slides along the north wall just beneath the overflow lip, tasting the stone with a long, deliberate flank before peeling away. Another circles the nearest pillar three times, tight then wider, as if measuring. They have no faces. They are denser water, slightly cooler or warmer by a fraction of a degree, carrying faint veils of the finest silt that catch the glare and then release it. When two such currents cross they do not merely mix; they braid, hold the braid for several heartbeats, then surrender it. Something like attention passes between them.

Gradually the braids last longer.

What gathers inside those temporary ladders of current is not flesh. It is a thickening of the water’s own intention, a decision to remain coherent a little past the point where physics alone would scatter it. The first time you see one fully you almost dismiss it as an eddy strong with bubbles. Then the bubbles arrange themselves. They form a vertical chain that flexes, breathing. The chain bends toward you—or toward the warmth of the blood under your skin—and the bubbles brighten as if fed by the attention. When it draws close you feel the pressure change against your eardrums and open palms: a soft, repeated inquiry. It does not touch so much as lean. Then it unravels into ordinary rising coins again, leaving only the taste of stone and sunlit water on your lips.

More of them appear once you have been seen.

They are not all alike. Some stay low, gliding just above the pale flags, their undersides faintly fluorescent where they skim the clay joints; these leave brief shining trails that fade like breath on glass. Others prefer the silver ceiling, flattening themselves into wide, slow discs that turn with the noon glare and send down sudden ladders of brighter light, testing the floor. A few remain pillar-bound, fretting the cool stone for hours, learning every chisel scar with long translucent filaments that are neither barbels nor roots. When the intake surges they all answer at once—stretching, brightening, riding the stronger flow with what can only be called eagerness. During those pulses the chamber fills with moving gauze, with veils that fold and unfold through one another, and the sound of the overflows takes on a layered quality, as if many thin voices have joined the thick pouring.

None of them possess a permanent outline. What they have instead is memory of outline. After many crossings a particular swirl will begin to carry a suggestion of shoulders, or the tapering of a spine, or the oval of a head tilted in concentration. The suggestion never hardens. It remains a courtesy the water pays to something it once witnessed or once was. You realize, with a slow flush of recognition, that these are not visitors. They are the room remembering how to be alive in the only way left to it.

The builders finished the chamber and let the water take it, but they did not leave it unoccupied. They poured their own duration into the ducts and the fitted joints. Every carefully cut stair, every patient ashlar, every wooden tenon still swelling in the wet—these were acts of insistence that form should endure. The water accepted the gift and kept moving, and motion itself became the inhabitant. Over seasons (or centuries; the light does not say) the insistence learned to swim. It learned to prefer. It learned to braid.

Sometimes one of them grows bold enough to match your shape for a while. It hangs a body’s length away, mirroring the slight drift of your limbs, the plume of heat from your throat. Its version of eyes is only two steadier points of clarity in the wavering whole, yet the gaze is unmistakable: curious, unmarried to any skull, older than the masonry and at the same time newborn every moment. When you reach out, it reaches back with a ribbon of colder water that slides between your fingers and completes a circuit you feel in the rims of your teeth. Information passes—temperature, pressure, the faint electromagnetic stutter of living nerves. It learns you. Then, almost shyly, it teaches you the chamber’s single endless sentence, the one the overflows have been pronouncing since the first day the valves opened. The sentence is not in words. It is the sensation of being both container and contained, of holding light and flow in the same clenched, joyful fist, of remaining unfinished on purpose so that nothing inside you can ever truly settle.

After that exchange the others draw nearer. They bring you fragments: the ghost of a hand that once smoothed this very pillar, the remembered weight of a wooden lever, the bright shock of the first water arriving and finding its shape already waiting. None of these memories belong to any single maker. They have been washed clean of names and faces until only the making itself remains—an anonymous tenderness toward geometry, toward the idea that stone could be taught to hold a sky of water.

Vegetation stays sparse because the inhabitants prefer the stone naked; moss would blunt the edges they still love. Fish never took hold because the currents that might have carried eggs were already full of older purposes. The loneliness you felt at first was only the time required for the room to decide you were not turbulence, not debris, not a brief mistake of surface weather. Once decided, it begins to keep you.

If you stay long enough you will notice your own boundaries loosening toward theirs. Heat leaves your skin in soft irregular pulses that the nearest currents catch and weave into themselves. Your exhaled breath becomes temporary silver coins that they herd and admire before releasing. Thought slows until it moves at the same speed as the silt stars turning in a slant of noon. You are becoming another way the chamber speaks—another clause in its loud, patient sentence.

And still everything is beginning. The ashlar has not forgotten the chisel. The water has not finished learning the exact temperature of devotion. What lives here is the devotion itself, fluid and communal and endlessly rewriting its own transparent body against the pale, deliberate stone. It has no name it cares to keep. It keeps the room instead, and the room keeps the light, and the light keeps pouring through every newly curious shape the currents choose to wear for a while.

When at last you climb the sharp-edged stair and breach the silver plate into ordinary air, something of that shifting coherence will climb with you—under the tongue, behind the knees, in the sudden ability of your own blood to feel the distant spill of those four overflow mouths still speaking. You will carry a private weather. On certain quiet days you will notice faint, warm currents crossing the rooms you live in, braiding, remembering. They are only visiting. The true body of the thing remains below, young and loud and unfinished, still polishing its one clenched fist of light and moving water, still waiting for the next swimmer patient enough to be recognized.
