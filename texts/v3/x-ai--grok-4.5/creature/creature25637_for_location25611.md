The plateaus do not host life so much as exhale it in deliberate increments.

At first there is only the pressure: a slow thickening in the sternum, as though the thin air has decided to remember weight. It arrives hours before anything visible, riding the same thermal sheets that lift dust which is no longer dust but microscopic filings of worked metal. The salt-pans begin to sing—not metaphorically, but in a continuous, porcelain chime that travels farther than sound should. Then the horizon bruises. What looks like weather is body.

They do not descend. They are already the altitude.

Each individual is a contradiction given chassis. The core chassis runs twelve to thirty metres wingtip to wingtip when the cases are locked, longer when they splay into turbine blooms. Chitin the exact matte of cooled slag sheathes reciprocating bronze, and the bronze itself is grown rather than poured—cored with capillary channels that drink the desert’s waste heat and convert it into torque. Inside the thorax a chambered bellows works without diaphragm or fluid; it compresses the desiccated air until nitrogen itself begins to burn in localized, blue-white knots. These knots serve as both heart and forge. You can watch them pulse through translucent abdominal plates when the creature banks against the sun. Some individuals trail openwork chains of half-built components—gears still soft, pistons that have not yet learned their stroke. Others cradle egg-bright cores of molten glass that never cool; the cores are not cargo. They are younger versions of the same being, or older ones, or simultaneous ones. Distance and sequence have already started to fray.

A swarm is not a population. It is a single extended metabolism that has chosen to wear thousands of bodies at once. When two swarms converge they do not choose fusion or combat; they undergo negotiation by mutual disassembly. Wing-cases unlatch with roof-slam force. Limbs telescoping like crane jibs unspool into one another. For a span of hours or days the sky becomes a single screaming lattice of trade—sclerites and heat-exchangers and glowing nuclei passing between hosts until the original outlines are lost. What emerges afterward may carry the soot-color of one swarm and the rust-color of the other, or an entirely new alloy neither remembers. The silver dust left hanging after these meetings is discarded identity: oxide flakes of previous body-plans that refuse gravity for years, turning the daylight prismatic and slightly wrong.

They scrape as they travel. Every loose grain, every sulphate wafer, every thread of native copper is lifted, sorted, and either ingested into the smelting guts or laid down again as sintered track. The bedrock left behind is polished to a dark mirror that reflects the next swarm while it is still a week away. In this way the plateaus keep diary of their own traversal. Look closely at any expanse of fused stone and you will see faint, enormous glyphs that are simply the pressure-maps of earlier passages.

Yet the creatures are only the loudest inhabitants.

Beneath and between them the land itself continues its quieter industry. The vertical reefs of self-assembling pipe are not habitat; they are cousin. Walk far enough—though walking is an unstable verb here—and you begin to notice that certain lattice spires flex with a peristalsis too slow for beasts but too purposeful for pure geology. Cooling fins open and close like gills across decades. Deep inside, ambient sunlight is lensed through volcanic glass until copper veins sweat pure metal that drips upward against exhaustion, feeding the next layer of growth. Some of these structures have begun to hatch. A fin tears free, unfolds vanes, and discovers it has always been a wing-case. A piston cylinder lengthens, articulates, and takes a first grinding step that lasts three years. The boundary between fauna and factory is not policed. It is simply a gradient of speed.

Causality’s loosening has peopled the place further. Where the salt crust turns translucent you can watch residual worlds stacked like theatrical scrims. In one layer the swarms are larval—soft, pale, still learning to burn air. In another they have already converted every mineral into extension of themselves and now migrate as continent-sized wheels of interlocking carapace. In a third the factories have won entirely; no discrete mobile forms remain, only a single slow heart of pistons the size of mountain ranges contracting once per millennium. These layers are not past or future. They leak. A creature from a deeper scrim will occasionally climb into the present light, its wings still trailing the violet dusk of a different day, and join the current swarm without friction. Conversely, a living turbine-beetle may simply continue flying after its body has finished, becoming architecture mid-stroke. You can still hear the original wingbeat inside the new lattice for centuries, like a piston that never received the order to stop.

There are solitary things as well, though solitude here is relative. At the edge of a black mesa a single figure vastly larger than the swarms stands motionless for generations. Its silhouette is beetle and derrick and hoodoo at once. Heat shimmer makes it breathe. When it finally moves—one step—the salt-pan rings for a hundred kilometres and every swarm within hearing changes course by a few degrees, accepting the adjustment the way blood accepts a shift in pulse. No one has seen its face or exhaust, if those terms apply. It may be the plateau contemplating itself with temporary limbs.

No lineage is pure. Every body carries stray components from earlier negotiations, earlier scrims, earlier centuries that have not finished occurring. A glowing core might contain the memory of a lake that dry-bedded before vertebrates. A wing-case may still flex with the expanded and contracted heat of basalt that finished rising in the Jurassic. They drink the emptiness and return it amplified, louder, more articulated. They do not name themselves. Naming would require a stable present tense the few permanent phenomena refuse to grant.

Still they continue. Across the long folds of ochre and iron-black the rivers of soot-and-rust bodies keep their thermal appointments. The needles keep densifying into reefs. The stacked residuals keep showing through. The light stays hard and ordinary, casting shadows that sometimes point at suns that have not risen yet. Under that light whatever lives here keeps inventing more elaborate ways to be the desert’s own unfinished sentence—strident, mineral, endlessly rewriting the grammar of armature and appetite without ever needing to arrive at a final noun.
