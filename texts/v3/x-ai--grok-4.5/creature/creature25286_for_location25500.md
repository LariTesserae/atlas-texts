The cold has tenants.

They are not the first thing you meet. The first things are the pressures and the drips and the long unresolved chords of metal under strain. Life here arrives late, the way a stain arrives late on a bulkhead: inevitable once the conditions lock in, but never the point of the design. Still, it is here. It has learned the shifts.

Begin with the smallest that stay.

In the permanent black film that sheets the lower plating, where condensate and runoff pool into a continuous shallow tide, there live the filtrates. They are not quite algae, not quite colony-gel. Translucent veils the color of diluted machine oil, edges frayed into microscopic cilia that taste dissolved iron and the faint sweetness of leaking dielectric. They bloom in the hours after a big pump cycle drops, when the water lies almost still and the sodium-vapor light goes syrupy. Touch one and it shrinks without haste, collapsing into a denser bead that later re-spreads. They do not fear the boots that sometimes pass through them; they simply reform in the footprints. Over seasons measured in scale buildup they have begun to lay down a thin porcelain crust at the edges of the deepest sumps—mineral parchment, brittle, inscribed with the ghosts of their own feeding. No one scrapes it. The crust is softer than rust and less trouble.

Larger, and rarer, are the jointed pale things that use the warmer runoff channels the way other worlds use rivers. Picture a length of living cable unspooled and then given too many knees. Segmented, each section sheathed in a matte, almost chalky carapace that never fully dries. They prefer the outfalls where heat exchangers dump their softened water; there the temperature stays a few degrees above the ambient metal chill, and the flow carries enough organics—sheared gasket particles, old lubricant broken down to fatty acids, the occasional lost glove slowly becoming soup—to keep them moving. Most of the time only the twin feathery antennae show, tasting the current. When the big pumps cycle down and the channels shallow, the whole animal may surface in a slow, deliberate heave, dorsal vents sighing a vapor that smells of wet copper and crushed millipedes. They are not aggressive. They are not curious. They are simply dense with the patience of things that have never seen a predator larger than a backflow. Tracks in the silt basins—the ones that appear overnight and terminate against blank bulkheads—are usually theirs: a staggered double row of dimpled presses that look almost like the memory of a tool rather than a foot. By morning the rising film has already begun to erase them.

Above, in the dead volumes between cable trays and the undersides of duct trunks, the sheet-bats roost. Calling them bats is laziness; it is the closest verb. Their bodies are stitched from scavenged composites—foil insulation, frayed reflective tape, shards of flexible printed circuit that still hold a ghost charge. Membrane wings stretch between elongated phalanges of braided wire and something like keratin grown around a polymer core. They do not fly so much as they fall with correction, riding the thermal blooms that rise off compressors and the sudden cold sinks that open when a cryogenic purge vents. You hear them more than see them: a dry, papery flutter underneath the industrial roar, like pages turning in a book no one is reading. At shift-change, when certain lighting strings brown out, they launch in loose constellations to skim the fog banks, mouths (or the apertures that serve as mouths) combing the air for the midges that sometimes hatch from the warmer scums. They nest in the highest, driest interstices, gluing scraps of themselves into hanging purses that look like discarded manufacturing offcuts. If you shine a hand-lamp into one, eyes like dulled solder beads regard you without heat, then the whole colony rustles and reseals.

There are larger previousnesses.

Somewhere past the third freight basin, where an entire district was swung into place at a five-degree disagreement with the local vertical and then simply left that way, something keeps a territory. The geometry itself is herding. Walk the catwalks there long enough and you begin to notice that loose tools migrate. A torque wrench left on a railing will, over successive watches, be found one landing lower, then another, always progressing toward a particular confluence of drain and shadow. The thing that does this is seldom seen whole. What is seen: a long, low silhouette when the bioluminescent film flares after a blackout; the wet gleam of too many load-bearing joints flexing in sequence; exhalations that fog the air with a smell like overheated bearings and deep loam that should not exist here. It does not hunt the jointed channel-dwellers. It does not molest the sheet-bats. Its interest is in the unfinished. Fresh cut-marks on beam ends attract it. Newly torqued flanges are tasted and then left stamped with a secretion that slows corrosion in one pattern and accelerates it in another, as if it is editing the plant’s own revision process. Some maintenance crews leave offerings—broken end-effectors, spent filter cartridges, the occasional sandwich in foil—at the edge of its favored bay. The offerings disappear. The geometry grows slightly more navigable for a while. No one has decided whether this is thrall or treaty.

And then there are the ones who still punch clocks.

They are fewer than the plans assumed. The living quarters were framed in early and then half-submerged when a retention wall was deprioritized; now the dry modules hang like barnacles on the higher gantries, reachable only by long climbs through weather that is entirely homemade. The people who remain have the same unfinished look as the complex. Skin gone pale and slightly glaucous from years under amber light that never completes its spectrum. Coveralls permanently darkened to the knees by the black film, the fabric itself hosting thin colonies of the same gray-green filament that clings to the pipes. They move with the economy of those who have internalized the soundscape; they can hear a drop-forge’s rhythm change half a kilometer away and know whether it means a hull plate is true or about to start a crack that will take three shifts to nurse. Conversation is short, pitched to cut through the continuous pour of cooling races. They name the fog banks the way surface people name weather fronts. They keep small gardens of the pale, leafless switches that force up through expansion joints, not for food (the growths are mineral-bitter and faintly electric on the tongue) but because the soft verticals are the only calendars that do not belong to pump-cycles. When a crane swings a new bay into place and the sparks fall like orange snow, some of them stop to watch, faces turned up into the brief warmth, expressionless the way people become expressionless when the place they live is still being born and might yet decide against them.

There is one further presence, harder to locate.

It is not housed in any single body. It is the slow consensus of scum crust, channel-dweller secretion, sheet-bat nest glue, territorial edit, and human grease all feeding back into the condensation cycle. On certain nights—nights measured by the grid’s deepest hum rather than by any sun—the bioluminescent film on the black water brightens past its usual weak green and begins to hold afterimages. Walk the edge of a cooling plaza then and you may see your own outline kept for several seconds after you have moved, or the silhouette of something that walked there a shift ago, or the faint schematic of a staircase that has not yet been swung in. The complex is dreaming in phosphorescence. The dream is mostly about more complex: longer sightlines, newer unfinished edges, deeper wet. Everything that lives here, filament to claw to gloved hand, participates a little. The cold does not care, but the cold is no longer the only author.

So that is who lives in the wet metal night: the film that writes itself, the pale cable-things that breathe copper, the foil ghosts that correct their falls, the patient editor of unfinished flanges, the thinning shift-crews who have forgotten what dry boots feel like, and the larger slow thought that is the place thinking about its own expansion with borrowed neurons of slime and spark and stubborn human insistence. None of them rule. None of them are tourists. They are the secondary products of a machine whose primary product is itself, and they have made of that waste stream a country. The far end of every vista is still only more weeping industrial dark. They go there anyway.
