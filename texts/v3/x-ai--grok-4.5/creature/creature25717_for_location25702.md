They are dense the way the stone is dense.

Not tall. Height is a liability when every joule of heat must be kept and every ascent is already vertical. Their bodies run thick through the torso and thighs, bones heavy, joints wrapped in tendon that looks almost sculpted under skin the color of old parchment or wet basalt, depending on how much of their lives they have spent in the outer winds. The cold has been written into them for so long that fat lies in specific, almost architectural deposits—across the upper back, around the kidneys, behind the cheeks—so that even the leanest adult carries a smoothed, rounded silhouette under the layers. Fingerprints are nearly obliterated by lifelong abrasion; what remains of the dermal ridges is polished smooth as river stone.

They cover themselves in the plateau’s own skins. Not fashion—necessities that have become liturgy. Innermost layers are soft under-fur felted with spit and patience into something like thick paper. Over that, pieced hides of the shaggy bovids and the high-caprids, worn hair-out in the worst cold, hair-in when the brief silver rains come. Seams are sinew; closures are bone toggles and loops of braided gut. The garments accumulate repairs across lifetimes. A coat may contain the work of six hands spanning a century, each patch a slightly different nap or hue, so that the oldest wearers look like walking outcrops of the city itself. Hoods are deep, edged with the stiff guard-hairs of the pale canids; when pulled forward they leave only a tunnel for breath and narrowed eyes. Children are swaddled until they can walk, then dressed as miniature versions of their elders, so that from a distance the alleys seem populated by the same dark, furred shape at many scales.

Their faces are wind maps. Eyes set deep, lids thickened, irises usually some pale mineral cast—smoky quartz, weak tea, the blue of old ice. Cheekbones wide, noses shortened and bridged thickly so that air has farther to warm before it reaches the lungs. Lips chap and split and heal into permanent seams of paler tissue. Many adults lose the cartilaginous edges of their ears to frost over decades; the resulting smooth contours are considered ordinary, even handsome. Teeth are strong, slightly yellowish from the mineral-heavy water and the constant grit that finds its way into every meal. Smiles are rare and small; when they happen they look like fault lines briefly acknowledged.

They do not rush. Motion itself has been edited by the cold and the verticality. A person crossing a high bridge of single limestone slab places each foot as though the stone were still deciding whether to accept the weight. Gestures are economical—an open palm turned upward means offering or inquiry; two fingers tapped twice against a lintel petition entry; a slow blink in someone’s direction can carry the weight of a paragraph. Speech is lower than the wind and rarer. When they do speak, the language is full of long vowels that can be voiced almost without opening the mouth, consonants that click softly against the back of the teeth or are simply shaped by breath against the soft palate. Much is left unsaid because the stone and the shared labor already say it. A wall either rises true or it does not. A cistern either holds or it weeps. There is little need to narrate what everyone can see over generations.

They bury their dead inside the architecture. Not as macabre display but as continuity: a body wrapped in its final furs is placed in a niche left purposefully in a terrace wall, then sealed with a fitted plug of the same rock. Over decades the plug weathers at the same rate as the surrounding face; after a century the resting place is indistinguishable except to those who remember the count of stones. In this way the city is partly ancestor. Walking certain older staircases means treading a compacted sediment of one’s own lineage. No one finds this grim. The plateau keeps everything it is given; the people simply participate.

Their tools are extensions of patience. A mason’s kit is a leather roll containing hammerstones of graded hardness, bone punches, wooden mallets whose heads have been fire-hardened and then bound in rawhide, and sachets of abrasive grit sorted by mesh. Flint knappers work in the lee of walls, their waste flakes swept up and reused as temper or drainage. The rare pieces of native copper are not currency; they are ceremonial edges, re-sharpened across lifetimes, used only to cut the umbilical cord of a newborn or to mark the final capstone of a work that has occupied three generations. When such a blade finally wears to a stub it is re-hafted as a needle or buried with its last keeper.

They eat what the plateau and its animals consent to give. Blood and milk of the herds, thickened with powdered lichen that has been leached and re-leached until its bitterness becomes merely mineral. Marrow cracked from bones roasted in ash. The midges of the midday rise, caught on poles smeared with bovid tallow, toasted into brittle cakes that taste of fat and distant thunder. Caprid meat on high feast days—never wasted, every part used, the hooves boiled into glue, the horns split for spoons. In the brief ceremonial rains, meltwater is caught and mixed with the last of the previous season’s dried cushion-plant stems to make a sour, vitamin-rich paste that is fed first to pregnant women and the very old. Hunger is not unknown, but it is met with the same long-view stoicism as a wall that must wait another winter before its next course of stone.

Children are taught by proximity. A toddler sits for hours inside the radius of a knapper’s work, hearing the rhythm of strikes long before being allowed to hold a hammerstone. An older child is given a single boulder and told nothing; years later the boulder has become a step, or a door-socket, or simply a smoother boulder, and the lesson is complete. Names are private, used within the household and rarely spoken outdoors. In the wider city a person is known by their current labor and their lineage wall—“she who levered the third course on the east scarp of the Blue Cistern Line,” or “the grandson of those who sealed the outer face after the rockfall of the Long Rime.” Identity accretes like everything else.

At rest they gather in the byre levels where the shaggy bovids breathe their slow fog. Bodies lean together under shared hides. Someone may hum a note so low it is felt more than heard, matched by another throat, until a chord of almost geologic patience fills the chamber. Above them the immense frost-colored birds wheel past unglazed slits, and the pale canids curl in doorways as if they have always held civil office. The people do not pet the animals so much as acknowledge them with the same slow blink used among themselves. Citizenship is not a metaphor.

In the raking light of low sun, when every hammer-mark throws its precise blue shadow, the people become hardest to distinguish from what they have made. A figure standing motionless on a cornice against the glare might be a hooded herder watching the spiral ramps, or simply another weathered upright of stone waiting for its next course. Only the faint bloom of breath, or the minute turn of a head tracking the birds, reveals which. Even that difference feels temporary. They have lived here long enough that the plateau no longer feels inhabited by them. It feels as though they are one of the ways the plateau remains awake—furred, warm-blooded, quiet, and endlessly, carefully stacking itself toward a sky that has never once hurried.
