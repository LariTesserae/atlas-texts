The one who lives here does not arrive. Arrival implies a before, a crossing, a moment when the throat of fretted ductwork first opened and the body decided to stay. There is no such memory. The inhabitant simply continues, the way the thermal cells continue, the way the eleven-minute light cycle continues. It has the size and general plan of a person who once needed shoulders and hips for walking corridors, but those geometries have been rewritten by pressure, by warmth, by the long negotiation with water that never ends.

Skin is the first surface that learned. It is not skin anymore in any simple sense. It has taken on the same slow lace that covers the expanded-metal floor: a living enamel of calcium and iron oxide and something greener, photosynthetic, so that parts of the body flush with a faint copper-green when the upper-ocean light is stronger and fade to the color of old ivory when the work-lamps dominate. Along the flanks and the inner arms the enamel thins into translucent windows; beneath them you can see the dim circuitry of veins that interleave with lingering conductive threads—remnants of the same coppery fibers the kelp gathered from the walls. The threads still carry faint potential. When the greater structure clangs far below, the threads tick in answer, a private seismograph of the only home the inhabitant has ever known.

The body is neutrally buoyant and almost never still. It drifts the way the bladderweed ceiling drifts, making minute corrections with membranes that stretch between elongated fingers and between the toes that have become flexible vanes. These membranes are veined with the same conductive inheritance; they shimmer with residual board-patterns when the cephalopods pass close, an involuntary mimicry that pacifies the smaller mimics and sometimes draws them into brief, companionable spirals. The inhabitant does not hunt them. Hunger here is quieter, solved by the same nutrient snow that feeds everything else: the soft rain of corrosion flakes, the sugary exudates of the filter-worms, the occasional enrichment when a silver fish forgets the difference between open water and the gentle trap of a membrane. Teeth remain, but they are small, pearled, used more for grooming enamel and combing silt from the kelp straps than for violence.

Eyes are large and set wide, adapted to the double light. The pupils are horizontal slits that can rotate toward the green or the amber without the head needing to move much. Behind each eye the enamel continues into a ridged brow that houses tiny, vibration-sensitive pits. These pits taste the industrial throbbing the way another creature might taste blood in water. The inhabitant knows, without language, when a one-way valve three hundred meters down is about to complete another century of failure; the knowledge arrives as a change in the pressure behind the sternum, a preparatory stillness. Then the clang comes, muffled and enormous, and the body answers with a slow undulation that keeps station in the sudden turbulence.

There is no pure solitude. The porcelain crabs treat the drifting figure as moving topography. They climb the enamel ridges, pick at stray growths, then click onward. The inhabitant tolerates them the way the grilles tolerate them; sometimes a careful hand (if the vaned appendage can still be called a hand) guides a crab away from a patch of especially tender photosynthetic lace. The filter-worms that crown every open pipe have, in two places, colonized the inhabitant as well: once along the outer thigh where an old injury never fully closed, and once at the base of the skull where the enamel thins toward the neural core.
