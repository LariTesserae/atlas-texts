The ones who live here are few, and they have been few for longer than the moss has held the ledges. They are not tall. Standing upright among the terraces they rarely clear the height of a lintel, and most of the time they do not stand upright at all. They move in a low, economical crouch-walk that keeps the spine curved and the hands free to brush the living walls for balance, for information, for greeting. Their bodies have settled into the same finished softness as the stone: shoulders rounded, joints loose, feet broad and densely callused so that each step finds the exact wear-hollow without sound. Skin the colour of wet limestone, cool to the touch, faintly luminous in the shadowless light as if it has learned to hold a little of the place’s own brightness. Fine dark hair grows close and flat, often threaded with beads of the same condensation that clings everywhere else; it never quite dries, never quite drips.

They are quiet in the way the settlement is quiet—not silenced, but completed. Speech is rare and small, a handful of murmured notes that travel no farther than the next alcove, more confirmation than conversation. Most knowing passes through touch: two fingers to the central basin on every passing, palm flattened a moment against a moss rail, the brief press of a forehead to a doorway whose living frame has narrowed to fit only them. Their eyes are pale, almost the same translucent green-grey as the ice-leaf reservoirs, and they do not widen or dart. They have had centuries to finish looking; now they simply see.

Clothing is not clothing so much as a second weathered skin. Layers of beaten bark-cloth and thin tanned hides, mended until the original beast or tree is long forgotten, dyed by long immersion in the iron-dark seeps until everything is the same muted mineral tone. Edges are never hemmed so much as allowed to fray into soft fringes that collect moss spores and become, over seasons, hanging gardens of their own. Every garment carries the dull gleam of infinite reuse. They sleep in the shallow concavities of rock shelves lined with the thickest black-green moss, bodies fitting the hollows the way water fits the basin. Sometimes several curl together for shared warmth that is never quite warmth, only a slightly less absolute chill. Alone or together they lie still enough that the small frost-tipped rodents run across them without notice, and the thumb-sized birds continue their nearly soundless nest-building in the fern pockets inches from a dreaming cheek.

Their work is maintenance so exact it has become indistinguishable from breathing. They dig no new stone; they only deepen polish already begun. With antler wedges and greenstone adzes they ease roots back into joints, rescoop a basin rim by the width of a fingernail every few decades, lash a fresh wooden rung into a ladder whose previous rungs have worn to silk. Copper needles flash once, catch the light, disappear again into bark thread. They gather the translucent ice-leaves and press them for the thicker water that never freezes, harvest certain bronze mosses for a bitter tea that tastes of wet iron and keeps the deep mineral cold from settling too far into the bones. Nothing is wasted because nothing is extraneous. A dropped cord is never dropped; a worn tool is only reshaped. They move through the dense interlocking rooms the way the seeps move through the rock—slow, inevitable, exactly sufficient.

There is a mild strangeness in them that matches the closed sky. They do not age in any direction a stranger would recognize. Faces remain smooth, thickened a little by damp and cold, marked only by the same graceful sag that has taken the lintels. Children (when children appear, infrequently, quietly) are simply smaller versions already practicing the crouch-walk and the two-finger greeting to the basin. The dead are not buried. There is nowhere to bury them that is not already full. Instead the body is laid in a high moss terrace and allowed to thin, to cool further, to become another layer of the black-green dense felt that cushions every step. In time the living walk those soft places without remark; the settlement simply grows a fraction more complete.

They know the pocket is sealed. They have tested the pale stone shoulders enough times to understand that the country beyond is colder, barer, and somehow less real. Paths that begin outward curve gently back. The light does not travel. So they remain. They circulate inside their single intricate lung, touching the clear water at the lowest point the way one might touch a pulse. The level remains what it has always been. They remain what they have always been: few, quiet, long-practiced, weathered into perfect fit with the cold green city that is the size of a secret and the shape of an unchanging hour. Nothing large. Nothing loud. Everything returned.
