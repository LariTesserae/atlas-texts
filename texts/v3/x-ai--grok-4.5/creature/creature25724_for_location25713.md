The first thing that lives here is the reef itself.

It does not think in the way a mind thinks, yet it remembers. Every fused trunk carries the compressed history of centuries of incremental decisions: where to thicken against a prevailing shear, when to surrender a limb to rot so that a lighter gallery might open, how to slow the upward race of a neighboring bole until both could lean together and share the load. Sap moves through it in slow arterial tides you can hear if you set your ear to the bark at the right hour—an oceanic hush rising and falling inside the wood. The reef drinks what little rain arrives, hoards it in hidden cisterns of living tissue, and meters it out again as beads, seeps, and the faint sweet mist of its own breath. It is not a forest growing upon stone; the stone has long since been digested into memory. What remains is one continuous, patient body, half-hollow, always revising its own architecture by the thickness of a cambial ring or the slow torque of a guy-liana drawing a distant branch into better light.

Upon and within that body the smaller weathers move.

Begin with the midges. They are not insects in any lowland sense. Their bodies are faceted lenses of chitin so thin they read as colored air. In the green-gold shafts they gather into loose galaxies that pulse and part, each individual steering by the faint electrostatic bloom that precedes a flower’s opening or the warm plume rising from a sap-wound. When a larger shadow crosses them they fold inward like a single thought retracting, then bloom outward again the moment the threat has passed. Their wings make no separate sound; they only thicken the light until it stutters.

Lower, along the walking surfaces of bark and moss-mat, the leaf-cutters travel. They are the size of a child’s open hand, six-legged, armored in overlapping plates the exact matte green of the foliage they harvest. They do not cut leaves so much as excise precise polygonal tiles and ferry them, still living, along rivers of their own bodies. The trail sounds like dry paper endlessly torn. At intervals the column pauses; individuals touch antennae, recalibrate, then resume. Somewhere deep inside a trunk-chamber the tiles will be chewed into a glowing fungal paste that feeds both the cutters and, through seepage, the reef itself. The relationship is older than any language spoken here.

Above them the gliders own the voids.

Some are pure membrane: living kites of skin stretched between elongated ribs, translucent enough that you see the branching of their own veins against the sky. They launch from vent-holes and spiral for long minutes on the warm updrafts that climb the reef faces, steering with minute twitches of the trailing edge. Others carry feathers—not the feathers of birds, but stiff, iridescent vanes that click softly as they adjust pitch. A third kind has abandoned both skin and feather; it is little more than a silken bag filled with buoyant gas, trailing long predictive tendrils that taste the air for the chemical signature of ripening nectar bladders. All of them cast no true shadow. Their passage is felt first as a change in the quality of the light, a brief coolness, then the eye catches the slow gyre and understands it has been watched.

Deeper still, in the cathedral hollows where daylight arrives only as rumor, the browsers move.

They are large enough that their chewing registers through the soles of the feet: a low, continuous gut-drum. Bodies like elongated hills of muscle and armored plate, they brace themselves against vertical trunks with six or eight limbs ending in keratin spades. Their mouths are not mouths but rotating rasps that strip great curls of living wood, exposing pale cambium that beads instantly with resin. They eat slowly, deliberately, and the reef does not appear to mind; the wounds they leave become new seeps, new gardens for epiphytes, new highways for the leaf-cutters. Sometimes a browser simply stops, locks its limbs, and becomes indistinguishable from the architecture for days. Only the faint heat of its breath along the bark reveals it is still animal.

And then there are the ones that wait.

They take the color and texture of wet lichen so completely that the eye slides across them for hours without purchase. Prey steps within range and nothing happens. Another hour. Another day. Then the strike: a spasm so sudden it looks like the reef itself convulsed. Cascades of smaller lives—midges, cutters, unlucky gliders—tumble into the voids in the aftershock. The predator does not chase. It has already eaten, or it has not; either way it resumes stillness, and the lichen color seeps back across its hide like a tide covering stone. Some say these watchers are not separate creatures at all but mobile expressions of the reef’s own immune attention, spun off when a particular density of noise or scent crosses an ancient threshold. No one has ever dissected one to settle the matter. The damp and the acids dissolve soft tissue too quickly, and the people who live here prefer not to know.

The people themselves are the slightest weather of all.

They are human enough in outline—bipedal, thin-limbed from a life spent climbing and listening—but the reef has rewritten the margins. Skin takes a faint greenish cast where it is most often pressed to bark. Fingernails thicken into short climbing claws. Eyes develop a secondary translucent lid that filters the perpetual green-gold twilight and lets them read the subtle flex of a loaded liana before it fails. They do not farm. They keep small resin fires on platforms woven tight to the lee of trunks, platforms that creak and sway in sympathy with the greater structure. From those perches they harvest: nectar bladders swollen like translucent fruit, fat grubs tunneled out of deep galleries with fire-hardened probes, the occasional shed membrane of a glider still rich with oils. Metal does not last; it blossoms into green corrosion within weeks. Their knives are bone or glass traded up from lower, forgotten lands. Their most important tool is silence, or rather the trained ability to hear the moment when the great continuous roar pauses, that ecological inhale that sometimes precedes a branch-fall or a swarm turning hungry.

They move along paths polished into the living wood by generations of bare feet. The reef tries continuously to heal those grooves; the people refresh them with the same quiet persistence. At night their small fires make orange bruises against the vast dark, and the smoke carries the scent of spice and slow combustion up into the canopy where the gliders wheel. They tell no large stories about themselves. Their speech is full of listening-words: terms for the particular creak that means a major guy-liana is about to part, for the change in midge-pitch that announces a nectar-flow three terraces away, for the quality of stillness that means one of the lichen-colored waiters has decided the platform has become interesting. They are provisional. They know it. When a whole shelf begins to fail they simply cut free the best of their lashings, gather what seeds and tools can be carried, and walk—lightly, always lightly—toward whatever next ledge the reef still consents to hold.

At the outermost lips, where the wind turns thin and cold and the world drops away into torn cloud, something else is sometimes felt.

Not seen. Felt. A pressure in the inner ear, a horizontal shiver that travels through the soles before the massed dusk-takeoff of winged things can explain it. Some of the reef-people say it is only the structure flexing under its own impossible weight. Others, especially those who have sat the edge for many seasons, speak of a slower inhabitant still: an attention that wears the entire reef the way a hermit crab wears a borrowed shell, older than the bamboo-like titan trunks, older than the stone memory beneath them. It does not hunt. It does not speak. It simply continues, and in its continuation every midge-galaxy, every cutter-column, every glider spiral, every human platform becomes one more local eddy in a single unfinished gesture.

The light remains ordinary sunlight, rearranged. The sound rebuilds from the edges inward after every silence. Leaf by leaf and scream by scream the Skyreefs persist, and everything that lives inside them is, in the end, only a different speed of the same green thought.
