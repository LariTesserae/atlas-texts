The ones who live here do not know they live in a city, or that the city is made of their own discarded skins and the thin breath of their passing. They know only the next slight warmth, the next brush of current that carries particles soft enough to seize, the next wall that must be thickened before it sags. They are many, and they are one continuous appetite folded into thousands of nearly transparent bodies.

Begin with the amphipods, because they are the clearest geometers. Each is a compressed comma of chitin the color of diluted milk, antennae longer than the rest of it, forever tasting the corridors. Their eyes are sealed white discs that register nothing; vision would be useless in a dusk that never changes. Instead the antennae map every plane by the faint drag of water sliding across minute setae. When two amphipods meet head-on in a tube barely wider than either of them, they do not reverse. They freeze, interlace the outer rami of their antennae, and remain locked for several heartbeats while mineral crystallites begin to nucleate along the mucus both have already extruded. A new strut is born between them, still soft, still drinkable. Then they continue past each other, each already forgetting the encounter, each already laying down the next invisible filament that will harden into architecture. Their lives are a single long sentence of touch-and-cement written across the rising lattice.

Beside them, and often inside the same minute chambers, move the armored worms. They are shorter than an eyelash and thicker only at the mid-body where the bristles flare like tiny black combs. The armor is not true shell but overlapping plates of secreted protein stiffened by the same minerals that build the towers; when a worm dies those plates become ready-made bricks, quickly raked into place by the amphipods’ busy limbs. Alive, the worms are patient snipers. A worm will lodge itself in a half-collapsed doorway, bristles locked against the rim, mouthparts folded like a closed trap. It waits while the marine snow drifts past, while smaller mites and nauplii are herded by the micro-currents into the cul-de-sacs the city itself has grown. When prey drifts within a body-length the worm unfolds in a single liquid spasm, envelopes the mite, and returns to stillness. Digestion is external and brief; the emptied husk is released to drift a few millimeters before another amphipod snatches it and mortars it into the nearest unfinished wall. Nothing is wasted. The worm does not hunt for sport or territory. It hunts so that the wall can keep rising, and the wall rises so that the worm will have new doorways in which to wait.

Higher, on the unfinished terraces where the seep water still fans out warmest and richest, the filter-feeders have claimed dominion. They are colonial, each individual a soft, pulsing vase no larger than a grain of pollen, fused at the base into shared mats that drape the mineral plates like living lace. Their only motion is the slow dilation and contraction of the crown of tentacles, forcing water through internal sieves that catch whatever the amphipods and worms have missed. The sound they make (if it can be called sound) is a continuous silk-soft hiss, the collective breath of the upper districts. When one vase fills with too much sediment it detaches, drifts a short distance, and resettles, leaving behind a calcified scar that later inhabitants will treat as floor. The colony expands by simple division; there is no leader, no queen, only the local decision of each vase whether the current is still generous enough to stay. In places the mats have already roofed entire avenues, turning open galleries into dim tunnels where the amphipods must crawl belly-up against the ceiling of living tissue.

Scattered among all of them, almost too fine to count as inhabitants yet indispensable, are the thread-hydroids. They look like nothing so much as strands of clear saliva stretched between towers and left to wave. Each polyp is a microscopic flower of stinging cells that fires only when touched by something smaller than itself. They do not build. They ride. Their mucus, however, is stickier than any other, and it traps the newest, finest crystallites before the larger animals can claim them. In this way the hydroids lace the entire rising structure with an invisible scaffolding that prevents total collapse when a gas bubble escapes or a tower sags. They are the city’s quiet engineers, paid only in the chance collisions that bring them food.

And then there are the ones too small to name securely, the translucent mites and copepodites and bacterial veils that coat every surface in a living gloss. They are the first skin over fresh mineral, the film that softens stone into something an amphipod can grip. When predation thins them, the walls grow brittle and chalky; when they bloom too thickly the filter-feeders choke and the upper terraces slump. Their density is the city’s secret thermostat.

None of these beings remembers yesterday’s layout. Memory here is written only in the shape of the corridors themselves: spiral ramps that once followed a rising plume of warmer water, now cooled and solid, still used because the gradient of least resistance has been fossilized into stone and mucus. A cul-de-sac that collected detritus last week continues to collect it this week, even though the original animals who shaped it are long since incorporated into its walls. The city remembers for them.

Sometimes a larger shadow passes the mouth of the pocket—an eelpout, a scavenging isopod the size of a thumbnail, something that still possesses the concept of elsewhere. The shadow never stays. Inside, the density is too complete, the passages too fine. The true deep is a rumor of featureless cold that the pocket’s citizens never test. Their world ends at the fracture’s lip, and everything necessary happens within the clenched-fist volume where chemistry and hunger continue their slow, meticulous argument.

What lives here is not a population of species so much as a single metabolic weather system wearing many brief skins. Each skin knows how to seize, how to secrete, how to die productively. Collectively they translate the blind seep of mineral-laden water into avenue and atrium and quiet residential gallery. They are the unfinished sentence the dark keeps writing to itself: cold, hungry, precise, and perfectly content never to be read.
