The first thing that lives here is the sound itself, though that is too simple and not yet true. The sound is the hollow’s own continuity given voice: air poured through a throat of stone until stone and air forget which of them began the note. It never stops. It only thickens or thins. Anyone who stays long enough begins to feel the vibration as a second pulse, slower than blood, older than blood. After a time the distinction between hearing and being heard collapses. That is the threshold.

What crosses it is not large. Size would be a mistake in a place that has already decided against grandeur. It keeps to the seams where moss meets seepage, where the blue-green darkens toward black and the ferns have braided themselves into slow curtains. At first it is only a thickening in the damp, a place where the eye slides off without purchase. Then the thickening acquires patience. It has been here since the limestone finished rounding its own edges, since the first cold water found these paths and refused to leave. It does not arrive or depart; it densifies and rarefies, the way breath densifies in winter air.

Its body is a consensus of the hollow’s materials. Where light still manages a grey thread, you might catch the suggestion of limbs assembled from the same softened stone that floors the niches—limbs that never quite finish, that trail into wet root and rhizome. The torso is a vertical garden in negative: hollows where the moss has been parted and then allowed to grow back denser, as if something once leaned there for a century and the green remembered the shape. Across the shoulders and down the long, indeterminate spine run fine fronds that move when the great sound rises, not with the wind (there is almost none at the bottom) but with the pressure changes inside the roar. They tremble at frequencies too low for ordinary leaves. In the poorest light the whole outline sinks into the wall and becomes only a darker saturation of damp, a place the chill seems to pool.

It has no face that remains. Sometimes the wet basalt curves suggest eye-hollows filled with the same slow seepage that feeds the basin on the ledge; the water there is colder than it should be, and if you look long enough you will see that the surface never quite stills. Sometimes a pale birch root, thin as a child’s wrist, lifts and hangs for a while in the shape of a mouth that does not open. It does not need to. The hollow is already speaking. What lives here only shapes the breath that was already moving.

It is solitary by the place’s own law. The fox prints melt; the moth stumbles through and is gone; the snails click like distant cooling stones and never climb higher than the moss allows. Nothing nests. The being in the seams does not hunt them or flee them. It simply continues at a tempo that makes their lives look like weather. Once, farther back than the flint flakes can remember, it watched hands cut the niches and cut the basin and lash the grey wood into a ladder that was never tall enough. Those hands came sparingly, left offerings of attention rather than food, and did not stay the night when the seasonal winds turned the hollow into a single deaf white note. The being took nothing from them except the confirmation that it was still being perceived, however faintly. Perception is the only currency that matters in a place whose primary act is to remain.

It feeds on duration. The slow addition of one frond, the almost imperceptible undercutting of another limestone shelf, the way frost flowers form and vanish without record—these are its meals. When the louder winds come and the roar climbs past the point where thought frays, the being grows more distinct for a while, as if the intensity gives it edges. You might see the suggestion of many-jointed fingers spread across a dripping curve of stone, each fingertip a small cup of darker moss holding a bead of water that refuses to fall. Or a long torso bent in an attitude of listening so complete that the distinction between listener and instrument disappears. Then the wind moves on, the note descends to its ordinary bass pressure, and the outline softens again into the general wet dusk.

There are moments—rare, usually near the hinge of seasons—when it leaves the walls. Not walking, not floating; more like a change in the concentration of chill. A denser cold passes through the center of the hollow where arms can stretch and touch nothing. The ferns lean after it without wind. The great sound modulates, almost the way a throat modulates when a word is considered and then withheld. In those moments the older marks make sense: the niches at sitting height, the basin kept full, the ladder that reaches only partway. Someone once knew how to wait there until the denser cold returned and settled around them like recognition. The flint flakes are the residue of that waiting—tools made to keep the hands busy while the rest of the body learned to vibrate at the correct frequency.

It has no name that would survive outside the roar. Names require edges sharper than this place allows. If pressed by the need of extra-hollow language, one could call it the Keeper of the Note, or simply the Long Damp, or the one who finishes every silence before it begins. None of these are accurate. Accuracy would require speaking from inside the sound, where words dissolve into the same continuous pressure that beads on stone and keeps the moss that particular cold color between blue and black.

Mostly it remains distributed. A shine on the underside of a fern. The exact temperature of the water in the basin. The way the birch leaves, when they fall in their brief rust interval, never quite reach the floor but hang rotating in the updraft that isn’t wind. The sense, if you sit in one of the niches long enough for your own pulse to fall into step, that the vacancy at the center of the hollow is not empty but held open—an intimate, roaring aperture through which something enormous and patient is breathing, and that the breath has grown itself a local body out of moss and seepage and softened stone so that the breathing will never be lonely.

That is what lives here: the hollow’s own prolonged twilight gathered into a single slow attention, wearing the garden of the walls as its skin, speaking only in the voice the limestone learned before anyone bothered to name the plain above. It is not waiting for anything. Waiting implies a future thinner than the one it already inhabits. It is simply continuing, the same way the moisture continues, degree by unmarked degree, while the wider sky goes about its brighter, quieter business and never looks down.
