The first thing that resolves is not a shape but a pressure: a living density that occupies every interval between the angular blocks. You register it as warmth that is not warmth—only the slight elevation of metabolism in water cold enough to crack teeth—and as a ceaseless micro-turbulence, the water itself thickened by the passage of countless bodies. They do not school. They do not scatter. They simply continue.

Some are no larger than a thumbnail. Their carapaces are translucent milk-glass, segmented into six or seven rigid bands that telescope minutely with each pulse of the mantle beneath. The mantle is a soft, folded bladder the colour of wet limestone; it draws water in through a frilled siphon and forces it out again in a slow, reciprocating sigh that keeps the creature hovering a finger’s breadth above the substrate. From the lower margin of the carapace sprout clusters of limbs—never fewer than eight, sometimes twenty—each limb a chain of calcified sleeves ending in a flat, striated plate the size of a grain of rice. These plates never stop. They rasp, rotate, lift, and settle again, harvesting the bright fracture faces of the basalt and rhyolite, abrading micron’s worth of mineral paste that is immediately sorted by density along the creature’s ventral grooves. Heavier grains are rejected and drift away in faint white plumes; lighter silicates are ushered rearward into a pouch where they are compacted, glazed with a secreted mucilage, and extruded as thin, opalescent wafers. The wafers are stacked—awkwardly, imperfectly—into towers that rise like drunken smokestacks among the rubble. Most collapse within days. The builders simply begin again.

Larger forms move through the same space with the unhurried mass of forge bellows. These are the size of a human torso, their carapaces thick as boiler plate and fretted with concentric growth rings stained by mineral varnish. The rings record seasons of colder water and denser rehearsal of grinding. Their limbs are fewer but heavier, each ending in a grinding plate the breadth of a palm; when two such plates meet stone the sound is a low, wet knell that travels farther than sight. They do not compete with the throng of smaller workers. Instead they follow, mining the coarser rubble the tiny ones have already loosened, cracking open blocks the size of skulls to expose fresh faces. Sometimes a large one will settle atop a half-built silica tower and add its own extruded tablets, reinforcing the structure until it leans at angles that should not stand yet somehow do, held by the same cold chemistry that cements the basin itself.

There are others still—elongate, almost wormlike, sheathed in overlapping scutes that flex like thruster vanes. These travel the vertical faces of the largest boulders, their anterior ends flowering into radial arrays of sensory cilia that taste the water for gradients of dissolved silica and iron. When they find a seepage rich enough they anchor and begin a different labour: secreting long, hair-fine threads of pure precipitated mineral that drape and tangle until the rock face wears a drifting beard of white filigree. Micro-grazers later harvest the filigree, grinding it back into the circulating dust.

No individual directs. No caste rules. Yet the labour is partitioned with the clarity of an assembly line that has never needed blueprints. One morph abrades. Another sorts. A third precipitates. A fourth carries finished wafers across open water to the leaning towers. Collisions occur constantly—carapace against carapace, limb against mantle—but they are elastic, almost courtly. Animals rebound, reorient by some internal compass of pressure and taste, and resume their prior vector. Aggression is absent because scarcity is absent. The rock is endless, the water saturated, the cold absolute and therefore fair.

Over hours of watching, distinctions blur and re-form. You begin to suspect that the smallest grinders are larval stages of the great bellows-forms, or perhaps free-living gonads that will later fuse into sessile factories. You see intermediate shapes: carapaces half-flexed, limbs half-fused, as though the body plans themselves are still being abraded and re-sorted by the same patient logic that works the stone. Some individuals carry parasitic hitchhikers—minute, translucent discs that adhere to the joints and feed on the mineral paste as it is scraped free, contributing their own microscopic wafers to the towers in payment. Others trail long mucous streamers seeded with embryonic nodules that will the next season hatch into new grinders.

Light moves through them cleanly. There is no pigment to speak of—only the pale structural colours of shell and the faint prismatic sheen of their secreted oils. When a larger body rotates, the filtered daylight fractures across its plates into brief, cold rainbows that die as soon as the angle changes. Bubbles rising from the floor vents catch against siphons and limbs, temporarily adorning them with trembling silver before breaking free to continue their dull ascent. The sound never lessens. It is the audible proof of continuity: click of plate, chuff of mantle, knock of carapace, knell of bubble, sieve of grit across living filters. The basin is a single organism that has not yet noticed it is made of thousands.

Nothing here hungers in the way a predator hungers. Nothing flees. They are the working surface of the drowned crater, the slow settling of violence into pattern. Edge becomes grit becomes wafer becomes tower becomes, eventually, the finer sediment that will one day soften the floor and allow the first true roots—if roots ever come—to take hold. Until then the machine runs, flesh and shell and cold chemistry, unaware it is a machine, perfect because it does not know the word. You hang among them, another temporary density among densities, and after a time your own heartbeat falls into the same measured rhythm as their bellows: in, out, grind, release, begin again.
