The longer one lingers, the less the city feels empty and the more it feels withheld. What lives here does not announce itself. It has weathered into the same patience as the stone, the same refusal of hurry as the water. At first there are only the obvious remnants of presence: a gray bird tilting between updrafts, a dark sliver vanishing into a weep-hole, the soft cartography of lichen that might be growth or might be memory. These are not the inhabitants. They are the small change left in the pockets of a place that long ago spent everything else.

Deeper, in the vertical streets where the mist never fully lifts and the walls stand close enough to feel like slow company, other motions occur. They are easy to miss because they share the temperature and tempo of the runoff. A film of moisture will thicken along a cornice, then decide—without any visible decision—to detach itself and travel as a slender, upright shape down the face of a stair. The shape does not walk so much as continuous. Where it passes, the beading water briefly aligns, inquiring, then resumes its older habits. Sometimes several such alignments coincide in a gallery and stand near one another without touching, exchanging nothing that registers as sound; the air simply grows a few degrees softer, as if condensation itself were conversing.

These are not spirits. They leave damp prints that take hours to evaporate, and the prints are shaped almost like elongated hands or the soft pads of something that long ago traded toes for better purchase on wet vertical stone. Their bodies—when the mist thins just enough—are the same pale gray and iron-ochre as the blocks they move among. Surfaces fretted with the same shallow honeycomb. Edges blunted. It is difficult to say where a shoulder ends and a buttress begins when one of them rests. They appear to wear the mineral deposits of centuries the way other creatures wear skin. In some the deposits have thickened into ridges that look intentional, almost architectural: low crests along the skull, lattice-like aprons where water is encouraged to channel down the torso and drip from the fingertips, feeding the city even as they borrow from it.

They do not build. The city finished its arguments with form long before they became what they are. Instead they maintain by occupation. A cistern that has begun to silt will find one or two of them floating motionless inside it for weeks, their presence altering the micro-currents until the sediment settles into patterns that keep the overflow channels clear. A terrace whose outer course has loosened under freeze-thaw will receive the slow pressure of their weight night after night until the stone remembers how to sit. They eat almost nothing that would be recognized as food. Lichens abraded between slow fingers. The thin copper-grass chewed for its mineral bite. Mostly they drink the city—its constant gift of condensed sky—and in drinking they seem to listen. On rare clear evenings when the higher cloud deck parts, clusters of them climb to the broadest open squares and stand facing the same direction, not watching any horizon so much as allowing the scaled vastness to pass through them. In those hours their surfaces darken with retained water and then pale again as it leaves, a breathing visible only as changes in sheen.

There are smaller lives that depend on them and that they, in turn, shepherd without possession. In the deepest weep-holes live translucent things the size of a thumb-joint—neither fully salamander nor wholly something else—whose feathery gills comb the runoff for microbes. The tall ones will sometimes pause and let these creatures migrate across their damp limbs from one channel system to another, reseeding neighborhoods of stone that have gone quiet. In return the small ones clean the honeycomb pits where parasites of pure mineral might otherwise crystallize and crack the living surface. Higher, in the niches that catch the least light, live mats of a slower lichen that grows in raised script, though the script means nothing linguistic; it is simply the shape that colony takes when it has elongated itself along the same routes the tall ones favor. When a tall one dies—slowly, over decades, freezing into a final posture against some favored wall—the script-lichen eventually overgrows the body until body and wall are no longer distinct. The city gains another course of slightly warmer stone. Nothing is buried. Nothing is wasted. The continuum simply thickens.

They are few. Across distances that turn districts into climates there may be only hundreds, perhaps fewer. Enough to keep the water preferred in certain paths, enough to blunt the loneliness of the acoustics without ever filling them. Their generations are not counted in births so much as in gradual distinctions: a ridge of deposit that appears on none of the older individuals, a new patience in the way a particular courtyard’s mist is allowed to pool. They remember the builders only as the city itself remembers—through the earlier, less-softened angles still buried inside the walls, through the rare chamber whose geometry remains too precise to be accidental. The builders are not gone. They have simply finished becoming weather. What lives here now is the same process continued into softer tissues, a continuum of damp endurance that no longer requires the idea of a separate people in order to persist.

Sometimes, very rarely, one of them will stand long enough in a narrow light-well for the ordinary daylight to find it fully. What surfaces then is neither tragic nor triumphant—only exact. A tall column of wet mineral patience, faintly reticulated with ochre, regarding the stacked world that made it possible. Then the mist closes, the shape resumes its seepage upward or down, and the city continues, fully itself, under the cool wet light that never quite becomes rain.
