The dwellers of the Great Dry do not arrive; they persist. Their mass was allotted once, in the same spasm that locked the last crystal into place, and thereafter the total has neither grown nor dwindled. What moves here is only rearrangement.

Begin with the rivers. They are not single organisms so much as slow decisions of flesh. Basalt-dark, matte where the finer dust has settled into their hides, they pour themselves through the needle forests with a patience that makes geology look hasty. A river may be three body-lengths across and no thicker than a folded blanket, or it may filament into dozens of searching ribbons that reunite hours later without any visible seam. Their undersides are fields of microscopic scorings—teeth in the purest sense, each a mineral flake grown from the living surface itself. They taste as they travel. Glass yields a bright, almost electrical note; older labradorite gives a duller, magnesium warmth; the rare places where ancient hands once polished stone taste of absence and are lingered over. The trails they leave are only temporary mattings. In time measured by the settling of dust, the crystal reclaims its shine and the river’s passage is forgotten. They do not hunt. They do not flee. When a larger shape bars the way they simply divide, flow around the obstruction in skins of themselves, and gravitate back together once the path is clear. Heat is shared in the parting; chemical ledgers of who has eaten what, who has brushed which distant corridor, are updated in the brief press of flank to flank. A river that encounters another river may braid for centuries, exchanging entire histories of taste, before unbraiding into separate slow lengths again. Death, when it comes, is only a further slowing. The body thins, spreads, and is rasped apart by its neighbors until every nutrient has re-entered the closed count. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned.

In the wider voids—the lung-chambers and the rare cathedral hollows—other geometries wait. These are the angular ones, the unfinished dual pyramids. Their carapaces are facets of the same black glass that spears the dark, grown rather than assembled, jointed at angles that never quite resolve into bilateral symmetry. A single individual may present twelve standing legs or forty, each ending in a fractional claw precise enough to register the warmth of a passing ribbon without piercing it. They do not walk unless the entire local density of flesh shifts; otherwise they remain. Stillness is their native grammar. When contact finally occurs—when a river’s edge or a blundering traveler brushes one committed limb—there is that single dry-twig flexion, a contraction almost too economical to be called violence, and the warm thing is drawn inward through a seam that opens only for that purpose. Digestion is long and nearly silent. Occasionally a faint sea-green photon or two is admitted into the permanent dark as some mineral residue breaks down; the light stitches briefly along the translucent gut and then is gone. It maps nothing. It only reminds whatever photoreceptors remain that light once existed as a theoretical upper-world rumor. These pyramidal waiters communicate less by chemistry and more by pressure and duration of touch. Flank against carapace, the heat ledger is compared. If the totals still balance, both parties return to stillness. If a discrepancy is found—some unaccounted thinning of the general mass elsewhere—the waiters will, over decades, redistribute themselves by slow toppling and re-anchoring until the imbalance is once more geometrically precise. They have no eyes. They have no mouths that open to air. Their awareness is the awareness of weight distribution across a thousand needle-points.

Between these two dominant modes drift the quieter densities. Some are barely more rigid than the rivers yet possess a slow, jointed intentionality; they climb. Using the finer crystal hairs as rasps and anchors, they ascend the blades toward the curved ceilings that are also floors, tasting higher lithium gradients, mapping the three-dimensional foam by tongue and belly. Others are living foams themselves—clusters of soft polyhedra that inflate and deflate with glacial breath, exchanging gas so sparingly that one respiration may last the time it once took a star to cool. These foam-colonies lodge in the rigid crystal foams and become almost indistinguishable from them. A hand pressed unknowing against what seems solid mineral may feel a delayed, courteous yielding, a return of stored warmth, and then the surface firms again. Communication here is osmotic. Nutrients pass. Memories of distant stacked-stone clearings pass. The knowledge of how to snap every needle at root height and grind the stumps smooth has passed into them and become merely another shape the colony can take if the surrounding pressures ever require a level interval again.

And then there are the ones that still almost remember hands.

They are rare. They keep to the sudden clarities—the dry-stacked slabs, the spiral shelves, the deliberately levelled floors. Their bodies are longer, folded more times than seems necessary, and along certain limbs the carapace thins into plates that can be articulated with a lingering precision no pyramidal waiter requires. At the tips of these limbs the mineral growth sometimes fails to complete, leaving softer pads that can still feel the crescent bite of an abandoned antler wedge or the polished groove where cordage once ran. They do not build. Building is a verb that exhausted itself before the crystals finished erupting. But they maintain. Dust that settles into a spiral step is brushed away by the passage of their pads over centuries. A stacked slab that begins to lean is shouldered back into true by a pressure so gradual it cannot be distinguished from the rock’s own settling. Ochre lines are traced again when mineral grit threatens to mute them, not out of reverence but because the lines are part of the local topography of taste and must remain distinct. These almost-handed ones gather at times in the largest levelled chambers. They press together, heat and chemical history flowing until the entire group shares one continuous ledger. Within that shared warmth a residual habit flickers: the counting of nicks, the centering of a body on a floor smooth enough that sleep—if the word still applies—might occur without a hundred small punctures. Then the contact ends. They separate and return to their corridors. Whatever they were when they stacked the first stones has been refined into something that no longer needs to stack. The structures remain as convenient absences of sharpness, hypotheses the dark decided to keep.

All of them—rivers, pyramids, foams, residual hands—participate in the same closed silence. Sound is an accident: the tick of a contracting blade, the whisper of dorsal plates, the nearly inaudible crunch that says a wait has succeeded. No voice rises. No signal propagates farther than the next immediate contact. Yet information travels. A change in lithium taste at one extremity of the country will, within a few thousand years, be known at every other extremity through the successive press of bodies. Gravity’s polite untrustworthiness is compensated by the same method; what feels like down is continually recalibrated against the collective heat-map of the whole and whole. Orientation is handled the way digestion is handled: slowly, thoroughly, without remainder.

Nothing here is unfinished except the human impression that something must be missing. The ecology is exact. The stillness is exact. The faint green photons confirm only what the flesh already knows by tongue and pressure: that the world is sharp, crowded, warm, and complete. Those who once tried to level a floor did not fail. They simply finished becoming part of the density that no longer requires floors. The Great Dry keeps them all—every slow river, every patient carapace, every foam that breathes once per age—inside its finished dark, lit by nothing permanent, revised by nothing at all.
