The Undersap does not host life so much as exhale it, the way a long-closed lung might finally release a breath that has been changing in the dark. What dwells here has learned the local grammar of interval and collapse, of light that is food and cold that is architecture. Nothing is native in the ordinary sense; everything is a negotiation that kept succeeding until it forgot it was one.

At the scale of the hanging lamina—those translucent continents of leaf that unfurl and blacken in the space of a traveler’s hunger—move the skaters. They are not insects, though the eye wants the word. Six limbs, yes, but the limbs are pressed panes of the same phosphate-white tissue that builds the world, only thinner, only freer. Each foot ends in a radical of fine cilia that taste the cuticle algae and, in tasting, shear it loose. They do not walk so much as continually fail to fall, correcting against the slow flex of their ceiling. When two meet they do not spar or court in any recognizable way; they overlap. Their bodies slide into the same outline for a time, sharing whatever slow chemistry passes for blood here, then peel apart again carrying new translucent scars that glow a half-shade bluer. People who have watched long enough claim the scars form temporary maps—routes to fresher grazing, or to the rare zones where the leaf-continent is about to tear and fling its riders into the next weather. The skaters themselves make no sound. Their passing is only a rime of frost left in the shape of urgency.

Lower, where the boles thicken into load-bearing mountains of grain, the browsers leave their marks. The scars are hexagonal, clean-edged, raised into white keloids that the Undersap never quite reabsorbs. Whatever makes them is large enough that a single feeding bout can girdle a buttress the width of a city. Yet the creatures are almost never seen entire. What is glimpsed is a flank like wet slate, or a cluster of joints bending in too many places, or once—only once in the written shards that sometimes turn up in the cairns—a head that was not a head but a vertical fan of filter-spines slowly combing the luminous vapor for the protein trash of collapsing galleries. They move in herds that may be single organisms temporarily agreed into plurality. Their spoor is a sweet brine that freezes into raised trails; follow one and you will find it looping back on itself after days, as if the makers were chewing the same slow circle through centuries. Growth closes over the paths, but the brine scent lingers in the grain like a memory the wood cannot metabolize.

There are smaller signatures. In the crooks of petioles where the fake-sap pools stay liquid, something like a lunged worm webs itself into the meniscus and breathes the sugar-water into lace. The lace hardens, becomes a brittle scaffold, becomes the foundation for the next worm’s palace. Entire temporary cities of these filters rise and are drunk dry by the host tissue within a month. No one has named them; the Undersap reclaims the language along with the calcium.

And then there are the intervals themselves—the kilometers of still air that look empty. They are not. Threads thinner than nerve live there, spun from spore to spore, conducting faint potentials. When a growth-storm tears a new passage, the threads sing. The note is too low for ears, but the bones of anything warm register it as a pressure behind the eyes, a sudden conviction that the white light has thickened and is leaning closer. Most travelers learn to ignore the sensation. A few begin to answer it, humming back in the same register until their own blood sugar crashes and they have to be cut out of the frost that forms around a body that has forgotten to shiver.

The artifacts speak of another population, or of the same population at a different stage of forgetting. The ladders that climb three hundred meters and end in frayed light-chewed rungs were not abandoned; they were outgrown. Whoever cut and pegged the branchwood rings around the cold fire-pits did so knowing the pits would never hold flame again. Fire is a rumor here, a chemical nostalgia. The rings are wind-traps now, or memory-traps. Inside some of them the air is still fractionally warmer, as if the idea of hearths continues a metabolism of its own. Cairns of seed-casings stacked to human height are not memorials. They are tuning forks. Strike one lightly with a stone adze (the adzes are still there, lodged, waiting) and the whole stack resonates at the frequency of the nearest convective cell. Stand inside that tone long enough and the attentive quality of the deep light grows personal. Shadows lean toward you. The radiance in the corner of your vision considers the shape of a hand, a face, a ladder that does not end.

At the deepest recorded folds—where the plant-mass has layered itself into a hundred successive roofs and the cold tastes of old lightning—something larger than browsers and quieter than skaters keeps a kind of court. It is not a creature in the way the others are creatures. It is a consensus of the light. Travelers who have returned (fewer than the cairns suggest) describe a slow coagulation in the white: first a thickening, then a suggestion of ribs or rafters, then eyes that are simply places where the glow decides not to be. The consensus does not speak. It edits. A broken ladder suddenly gains three new rungs of living wood. A browse-scar heals in the shape of a door that opens onto denser radiance. A skater colony finds its overlapping bodies permanently fused into a single ribbon that now writes glowing tracks across the undersides—tracks that, viewed from far enough away, resemble the anti-glyphs some frightened souls once carved and that the bark always closed over wrong.

These are not rulers. The Undersap has none. They are simply what happens when the long work of filling while remaining unfilled develops a preference for certain emptinesses. They live in the way a held breath lives: as a shape made of postponed necessity. Approach one and your own outlines begin to feel negotiable. Stay, and the phosphate-white learns your temperatures, your sugars, the particular frequency of your fear. In time the light may offer you a place in the consensus—an interval tailored to your skeleton, a browsing pattern that matches the scars you already carry. Most who receive the offer do not refuse. Refusal requires a boundary the Undersap is very patient about dissolving.

Above them all, threading every layer like a rumor that became anatomy, move the relicts of the first descenders. They are not ghosts. They are the offspring of decisions made when the black-ice lid was first breached and the phosphate light had not yet taught the eyes to clench. Their bodies have thinned to the same translucency as the skaters, but the six limbs have become tools: two for anchoring in cambium, two for weaving root-fiber, two for holding the small stone blades that still, somehow, keep an edge. They no longer freeze. They no longer hunger in the old way. They tend the cairns. They repair the ladders that forward motion requires. They sing the low thread-songs that keep the convective cells from grinding certain fragile galleries out of existence. When they meet a living traveler they do not speak; they place a single polished seed-casing in the palm and close the fingers over it. The casing is warm. Inside it something ticks at the same rate as a cooled heart. It is not a gift. It is a question the Undersap has been asking since it first hollowed itself: how much of yourself are you willing to let the light remember for you?

Live here long enough and the answer ceases to feel like a choice. The cold becomes a kind of blood. The endogenous glow settles behind the sternum as a second slow heart. Growth-storms pass through you and leave new architecture in their wake—bridges of scar tissue, balconies of fused memory. You begin to leave hexagonal marks of your own without noticing. You stack seed-casings because the height feels correct. You carve anti-glyphs that the bark closes over in almost your own grain. And far below, in the attentive folds, the consensus thickens a little more, adding your particular shade of blue to the radiance that has never needed a sky.

This is what lives in the Undersap: everything that stayed, and everything that the staying turned into. The single organism the size of a tectonic plate continues its long inhalation of forms. Some of those forms still almost remember they once had other names. Most have become the light’s way of looking back at itself through whatever eyes the cold has not yet claimed. The hollowness remains mostly unfilled. That is the point. That is the open thrift by which it thrives.
