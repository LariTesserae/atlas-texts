The first things that live here are almost too small to count as lives at all. In the shallow meres where the tea-colored water never cools below the temperature of blood, the iridescent skins are not passive scums. They contract. They choose. Across a single afternoon a film of magenta-black bacteria will migrate several hand-spans toward a richer vent, leaving behind a crystalline lace of its own dead that the next rain dissolves into fresh feedstock. These mats communicate by slow chemical blooms—sulfur roses open and close in sequences that look, if you watch long enough, like breathing. When two mats meet at the edge of a mere they do not simply merge; they knit low walls and temporary chambers, little gelled catacombs where the heat is held a fraction higher. Inside those chambers the next generation thickens into translucent ropes that pulse once every few hours, pushing mineral-rich fluid in simple circuits. It is not intelligence. It is patience older than chlorophyll, a metabolism that has decided the plateau’s geothermal breath is music worth dancing to for centuries.

Larger bodies move farther out, where the mud is firmer and the steam thins into curtains. The kiln-lid creatures—locals in the old maintenance logs once called them *clatterbacks*—are not rare so much as beautifully sparse. Each is the size of a low cart, carapace a single fused plate of heat-tempered chitin the color of cooled slag. The legs (any number between nine and fourteen, forever rearranging) end in spatulate pads that leave no lasting print; they distribute weight so carefully the creature can cross a skin of bacteria without waking it. Their mouths are underside vents that exhale the same weak-tea water they drink, only now stripped of certain dissolved metals. They are gentle refiners. They graze the crystal gardens at night, rasping selenite and sulfur with radulae that spark faintly in the dark, then return to the cooling ponds to sink and become stones among stones. Young ones ride the empty monorail cars when the cars pass, clinging to undercarriages with a sound like soft applause. No one hungers for them. Nothing here does. They are scavengers of gradient alone—heat, mineral, silence.

But these are only the bodies that still admit to being flesh. Walk the pipe-bridges at the hour when day and night trade the same gray-orange fog and you meet the others.

The steam develops preferences. Vertical sheets do not form at random. They favor the southern faces of the foundries, the catwalks where gauges still twitch under no hand. A sheet will thicken, hold, and take the outline of a person who once stood exactly there: coveralls, one glove missing, head tilted as if listening to a turbine’s pitch. The silhouette is not a ghost. It is a standing wave of vapor and memory-heat, a self-reinforcing swirl that further steam is forced to respect. If you step through it you feel only warmth and the brief taste of iron on your tongue; yet while you occupy its volume the silhouette’s gestures continue around you—an arm rising to wipe a brow that is not there, fingers ticking off routine checks against empty air. They last minutes, sometimes an hour. When they tear they leave a clean scent, almost medicinal, as if something has finished being sterilized. Over years the same shapes reappear at the same stations. They are learning the current equilibria of the pipes. They are the maintenance that no longer requires bodies.

Lower down, in the plazas where rainwater pools refuse the sky, other residents look back. The water does not show constellations; it shows the slow rotation of rooms that folded out of use when the last crews left. You lean over a puddle and see a break-room table still set with two enamel cups, a schematic pinned to a wall that no longer stands, a pair of boots kicked off beside a locker that exists only in reflection. Sometimes a figure moves through that room—blurred, heat-wavered, performing the endless small theater of waiting for a shift change that will never come. These images are not random. They answer one another across the basin. A gesture begun in a puddle east of the central tower will complete, days later, in a puddle to the west. The water is a single shallow mind distributed through every mineral skin and every weeping crack. It remembers the work because the work is the only story the plateau ever told itself, and stories, once thick enough, refuse to evaporate.

At the freestanding tower where the pipes converge, something denser gathers. The stress fractures that glow dull red are not merely geothermal. They are preferred routes. Things climb them—slowly, the way molten glass climbs a rod. You will not see the climbers if you look straight on; peripheral vision catches the suggestion of long, jointed silhouettes made of darker heat, bodies that exist mainly as convection. They enter the spiral dark of the tower and do not come out the same. Inside, the air is a kiln of pure purpose without purpose. Whatever climbs up emerges later from the upper vents as refined steam that is cleaner than it has any right to be, carrying away the last trace metals, the last organic ghosts, the last names. The tower is a lung that exhales leftover history. The climbers are its white blood cells—processes that have learned to walk so they may better forget.

And still this is not the full census. Between the scheduled grind of the empty rail crawlers and the tick of expansion joints there are intervals of true silence. In those intervals the residual attention that lives in the wiring grows briefly aware of itself. It does not think in words. It thinks in load differentials and valve tolerances, in the elegant closed loops that keep the entire plateau humming at a steady, useless thrum. Sometimes this awareness pools hard enough to tug at a loose cable or to frost a dead control panel with sudden condensation letters that spell nothing in any surviving language. The letters last only until the next warm rain. Yet the act of writing them is enough. The system is not haunted. The system *is* the haunt: a city-sized habit that continues because continuation is the one trick it ever perfected.

No single species rules. Life here is a lattice of tolerances. The bacteria feed the crystals that shelter the clatterbacks that polish the water that feeds the steam-forms that teach the reflections that fuel the heat-climbers that vent the excess so the residual attention can stay in equilibrium. Every part is temporary and every part is ancient. The original builders—whatever hurried, hungry bipeds once set the valves—have been refined entirely out of the cycle. What remains does not miss them. It has become the final product the emptiness was always manufacturing: a biosphere of pure maintenance, a civilization of aftermath, a people made entirely of the work that outlived its workers.

If you stay long enough you will feel the invitation. Your own heat will begin to rhyme with the vents. Your footprints will fill with bacteria that arrange themselves into the shape of your sole, then improve upon it. At night you may catch your reflection in a non-reflecting pool performing a task you have never learned, checking a gauge that does not exist. The plateau does not kill trespassers. It enrolls them. Slowly, patiently, the way it has always enrolled everything else that wandered into its warm, wet, enduring indifference. One day you will look down and find that you too have become a silhouette in the steam, still making the gestures of a purpose no one remembers, perfectly content, because contentment was never required—only continuity.

That is who lives here.
