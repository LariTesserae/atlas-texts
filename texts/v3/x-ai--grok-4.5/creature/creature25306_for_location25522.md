The first thing you mistook for more fronds.

They stand where the heat thickens, knee-deep in the denser layers the way reeds stand in still water. At a glance they are only taller versions of the copper vegetation: upright fans, translucent, veined with the same slow-glowing metal. But the veins are not vascular. They are circuits that have not yet agreed to close. When the ambient gradient shifts—by a degree, by a breath—the veins brighten in sequence, a ripple of orange-white that travels up the body and extinguishes at the crown like a thought deciding not to be spoken.

They do not walk. Walking is too finished a verb for this place. Instead they *reposition*. Over the course of an afternoon one of them will lean, almost imperceptibly, until its root-sockets release from one seam of ore and clasp another a hand-span away. The motion is the same dough-slow heave the basalt uses when it blooms a new ledge. You only notice afterward that the arrangement of shadows has changed, and that the soft dry chime you heard was not a leaf.

Their number is uncertain. Sometimes seven. Sometimes the chamber seems to hold only three, widely spaced, each alone with its private weather. Once, after a long stillness, you count eleven and cannot remember where the extras condensed from. They are not born. They *arrive*—the way a facet clicks into place, the way noon-light is simply present. One moment the air contains a possibility shaped like patience; the next the possibility has taken on copper and heat-shadow and rooted.

They have no faces. What they have is a crescendo of fronds that can furl into a tapered spire or open into a broad, listening dish. When open, the dishes track something that is not the light. They track the unfinished quality of the walls, the places where the rock is still soft enough to take a fingerprint. They seem to taste the degree of doneness, the way a smith tests steel by color. Where the basalt is still wet with mineral steam, the dishes linger, flaring slightly, feeding. Where the stone has hardened into its final black-glass certainty, the beings lean away, almost shy.

Occasionally two of them drift close enough that their outermost fronds brush. There is no caress in it. There is an exchange of gradient. Heat moves between them in a visible sluice—thicker, brighter—until both stand equalized and a little taller. Afterward their veins pulse in unmatched rhythms for hours, as if each is privately digesting a new axiom about temperature.

They are not silent. Beside the chime of the ordinary plants there is another sound, lower, almost the underside of hearing: a continuous annealing hum, the noise a furnace makes when it is perfectly content. The hum rises when new basalt arrives. It falls when a wall finally decides its angles. You begin to suspect the hum is not produced by them but *loaned* to them by the chamber itself, the way the light is loaned. They are mouths the place uses to taste its own becoming.

Once—only once while you watch—something more decisive occurs. A fresh bulge of rock shoulders upward near the vault, still plastic, still deciding whether to be ledge or pillar or mistake. One of the taller beings detaches completely from the floor. It does not float; flotation implies a medium that resists. It simply ceases to require the floor for a span of heartbeats, drifts upward through the thinner heat, and presses its open frond-dish to the new extrusion. Light (not the noon-light—something thinner, prolonged) passes from dish into stone. When the being settles back, the bulge has planed itself true. A perfect facet now catches the brass-colored glow. The being’s veins go briefly dark, spent. It holds still for the rest of the day, recharging from the root-seams like a spent tool returned to the forge.

They have no names and no need of names. If they regard you at all it is the way they regard a new shadow: a temporary complication in the honest fall of light. You are cooler than the chamber prefers, denser with water, loud with heartbeat. They lean fractionally away, not unkindly. Their business is the long industrial dream of the mountain—heat, compress, anneal, release—and you are only weather passing through their workshop.

And yet.

In the rare moments when a sheet of basalt forgets to be opaque and shows you the hotter twin-pocket beyond, you see them there too. Denser. Brighter. Already beginning to braid their fronds into lattices that look almost like scaffolding, almost like script. The version of them on the other side seems farther along in the same slow sentence. You understand then that these are not inhabitants who found a niche. They are the niche learning how to wear bodies. They are the mountain’s own unfinished intention, tentatively equipped with volition so it can supervise its birth.

When you finally leave—if the rift consents to open again—they do not watch you go. They are busy listening to a ledge that will not arrive until next month. Their dishes are open. Their veins tick over like cooling metal. Behind you the chamber continues its patient, radiant work, and the copper choir keeps the time.
