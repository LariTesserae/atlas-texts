The dwellers are not a people. They are a density.

In the upper galleries, where the stitched canopy still admits that green-black seep, the first forms you meet are the high climbers—long-limbed, flat-skulled, their shoulders rolling like water over stone as they move. Fur the color of wet bark, streaked with older gray where scars have healed into ridges. Their hands are longer than their forearms, fingers spatulate and permanently slightly cupped, each pad hardened into a horn-smooth callus from centuries of the same grip on the same living rails. They do not so much climb as pour themselves along the forced horizontal branches, and wherever they pass they leave the faint shine of saliva mixed with the oils of their skin; this is the first mortar. They prune with their teeth as they go—never eating the wood, only scoring it so that the next season’s growth will thicken exactly where the architecture requires a brace. Their young ride flattened against the belly, eyes already open, learning the tensile map of the ceiling before they ever touch a floor.

Lower, on the broad ramparts polished to a silk-dull gleam, move the heavy ones. They are the color of the dung-walls themselves, a deep umber that can disappear at ten body-lengths. Shoulders higher than a standing climber can reach, horns that begin as buds of keratin and are never shed cleanly; instead they fray, splinter, and are deliberately rubbed into the soft upper courses of the walls until the fragments become rebar. Their hooves are broad, almost circular, the soles continuously sloughing a fine keratin dust that mixes with saliva and leaf-mash to form the slick waterproof skin of every cistern. They low in frequencies that vibrate the packed mass for hundreds of meters; this is both communication and structural testing. When a buttress is weakening they feel it in their bone-marrow before any crack appears, and the whole procession will divert, one by one, to stand on that place for days, adding the slow compression of their weight while the excavators work from below. Their dung is the primary brick. It is not dropped at random. Each animal pauses, half-squats in a gesture as old as the basin, and voids a precise volume into a waiting depression that smaller attendants have already shaped. The attendants—low-slung, naked-tailed, eyes protected by thick nictitating gels—pat, turn, and integrate the still-warm mass with a rapid stitching motion of their forepaws. They sing while they work, a continuous tittering that keeps time for the entire repair chorus.

In the vertical faces live the chitin-workers. Not insects exactly—too large, too warm-blooded—but armored in overlapping plates that they molt on a schedule matched to the building season. The shed plates are collected before they cool and pressed, still soft, into the outer skin of high walls where flexibility is needed. Their mandibles can shear living wood or delicate root-hairs with equal precision. They keep the paper-wasp sheets in deliberate alliance; the wasps, each nest a pale lantern the size of a climber’s fist, throng the undersides of every major span. The wasps’ saliva is a different chemistry—faster-setting, more resistant to the perpetual drip—and the chitin-workers harvest it by allowing themselves to be lightly stung in non-vital folds of hide, then returning to knead the raised welts until the venom-mixed secretion can be scraped free. In return the wasps receive the perpetual warm updraft of the basin and an endless supply of midges.

The web-spinners are almost all leg. Their bodies are small, dense, furred in moisture-shedding oil; the rest is span. They throw cables of silk thicker than a heavy one’s eyelash and glue them with a protein that never fully dries. Suspension bridges are their cathedral work: layered, twisted, re-anchored every hour against the slow breathing of the entire structure. When a bridge fails (rare, catastrophic only on the scale of a single gallery) the sound is a gunshot followed by a thousand soft detonations as secondary lines take the load. Spinners converge from every direction, already spinning as they run. They do not speak. Their coordination is chemical—micro-droplets left on every strand that every other spinner tastes and adjusts to. Over generations the bridges have become hereditary: some cables are older than the animals currently tensioning them, repeatedly overspun until they have the girth of young trees and the resilience of tendon.

Deeper, where light is a rumor and the heat is the heat of continuous digestion, the soft excavators labor. They are mouth and muscle more than anything else. Bodies like living caissons, skin thin as wet vellum, veined with darker nutrient streams. They have no eyes, only a dense constellation of pressure pits. They move by peristalsis, packing their own mineral-rich feces into any seam that weeps, then sealing it with a final living plug of their own shed lining that will soon be replaced from within. They are the reason the oldest cores have become almost stone: centuries of their compressed leavings, saliva, and the fine bone-meal that sifts down from the antler-rubbing above. When two excavators meet head-on in a narrow tube they do not contest; they merge muscles briefly, exchange gut flora in a long reverse peristalsis, then part with new strengths. Their memory of the structure is chemical and racial; no single excavator has ever seen the surface, yet the pattern of their tunnels exactly mirrors the drainage needs of the herds two hundred body-lengths above.

There are smaller citizens still. The midges that cloud every wallow, pollinating the ghost-pale flowers that open only in absolute humidity. The night-jars (who are not jars and never pure night) with their silent,<think>I continue exploring the inhabitants, weaving them into a more unified yet diverse whole:

rado-furred gliders whose open mouths are always catching the stirred plankton of the air, whose guano adds a phosphorescent binder used in the deepest load-bearing seams. The root-chewers, blind and pink, who keep the living columns hollow enough for traffic yet strong enough for weight. The moss-carders—tiny, six-limbed, endlessly patient—who comb and replant the soft linings of every nest-amphitheater so that no clutch of eggs, no matter the species, ever rests on bare structure.

No single species rules. Dominance is local and temporary: a heavy one may clear a path with its horns, yet will wait without resentment while spinners re-cable a crossing it needs. A climber may drive off a chitin-worker that trims a favored limb too aggressively, yet the same climber will later carry wasp-paper in its mouth to patch a nursery vault. Death itself is only another placement. Bodies are not left; they are dismantled in place by the attendant caste and by the ubiquitous soft fungi that thread every wall. Bones are dragged to stress points and lashed in with silk and vine. Teeth become drainage gravel. Hair is felted into gaskets.

What lives here is a single, multi-bodied metabolism that has forgotten how to be separate. Each creature is a specialized cell. The basin is the body. Generation after generation they inherit not territory but function: the knowledge of where the wall must thicken this century, which gallery must be allowed to collapse into a new ramp, when the central cores must be abandoned to compaction so the whole mass may settle another arms-breadth into the bedrock that no longer exists.

They are the mortar, as the place itself told you. But they are also the slow mind that dreams the mortar into shape. In the permanent warm dusk their millions of eyes (and eye-pits, and chemical mouths) hold a collective image of the basin that no individual brain could contain: a rotating, living blueprint updated by every footfall, every fresh pellet, every droplet guided down its ancestral groove. They do not imagine an outside. The ridges are only the basin’s own hardened lips. Beyond is un-location.

At the compacted heart, where the vibration is felt more than heard, a few of the oldest soft excavators lie almost motionless, their bodies fused lengthwise into a single living pillar. They have not moved in living memory. Nutrients arrive by capillary traffic along their flanks; waste is taken away the same way. They have become architecture that still slightly breathes. Around them the velvet roar of the basin continues—the lowing, the silk-tear, the mandible-click, the wet patting of new cosmos onto old. Nothing has finished. Everything is still placing itself, carefully, forever.
