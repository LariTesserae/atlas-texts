The dwellers here are not arrivals. They are precipitations that learned, over spans too slow for ordinary counting, how to keep precipitating in the same loose shape.

At first you notice only the smaller ones, the ones that still look like the quiet mineral life the plateau itself admits. Dark iron-bacterial mats thicken along certain seepage lines until they acquire a slow, purposeful creep, advancing uphill against the water’s polite descent, leaving behind a cleaner stone that gleams as if freshly broken. The silica blooms, those brittle glass sheets, sometimes flex without wind. They lift at the edges, taste the striped air with their fracture planes, then settle again into new radii. The white crystalline fans do more: they ring. When a drop strikes their uppermost lattice they answer with a note just below hearing, a pressure in the teeth and the hinges of the jaw. These are the larvae, or the spores, or the thoughts the larger dwellers no longer need. They are not separate. They are the shed visible edges of something that prefers to remain mostly deranged from visibility.

Stay longer—let the white light pin your shadowless body in place—and the greater ones begin to cohere out of the recurring accidents of the place.

They favor the freestanding curtains of undercut stone. There the constant weeping has already half-dissolved the difference between solid and fall. A dweller will stand in that multinational thinness until its outline is only a temporarily thicker curtain, a place where the water decides to pause and remember weight. You see them first as vertical interruptions in the sideways ribbons of light: taller than a human, narrower, composed of layered translucencies that never quite settle on a single opacity. Basalt-dark at the core, then schist-glimmer, then the clear glass-ghost mineral, all of it veined with moving water that is not blood and not sap but something that performs the same office—carrying memory from one extremity to another. Their upper portions—call them heads only for convenience—flower into wide, shallow bowls or funnel shapes that catch the high light and pour it back out in disciplined sheets, exactly as the etched slab does. In that poured light you sometimes glimpse the parallel grooves of their own making, recut daily by the act of existing.

They move the way the plateau builds: by controlled failure. A foot (a denser laminate of stone and sealed water) sets itself down; silica precipitates around the contact; the previous foot fractures free with the soft glass-sound you already know; the whole form advances one patient increment. They can wait years between steps. They can also, in the breakdown galleries, arrive all at once, as if the porous membrane has simply torn and spilled them forward from some adjacent version of the same ledge. When they travel in company—rarely more than three or four—their sounds interlock. Each droplet that leaves them is timed to the droplet of the next, constructing those delayed and anticipatory thunders into phrases. The phrases are not language in any mouth-borne sense. They are continuous low arguments about balance, about how long a column may lean, about whether the water that falls upward is arriving or returning. The wind that threads the corridors sometimes carries a whole conversation past you like a cool silk banner, already dissolving.

They leave the marks because the marks are how they remain locatable to themselves. The ring of wedged stones is not a camp; it is a mnemonic weights-and-measures, a place where a dweller can stand and feel the exact drag of this particular latitude on its internal hydrology. The shallow steps pecked into a weeping face are not for climbing. They are calibrated catchments; each concave tread holds a volume that corresponds to one of their elongated heartbeats (a slow flood and empty of the interior water). The upright slab with its water-clock grooves is older than any individual. It is a communal lung. Dwellers come singly, press their funnel faces to it, and let the measured sheets run through them, aligning their private time with the plateau’s older, illegible sentence. When they finish they are more sharply visible for a while, edges harder against the haze, as if the clock has temporarily convinced reality to hold their shape with greater conviction.

In the ordinary stretches of the plateau they are almost courteous, almost shy. They keep to the denser pillars, loose their mineral larvae carefully, and step aside if your single-file path coincides with theirs. Their regard—if the way the light bends around them can be called regard—feels like mild curiosity without appetite. You are weather to them: a brief, warm anomaly that will pass and leave no precipitate.

But in the breakdown zones they become more themselves, which is to say less decided. There a dweller may occupy three overlapping leanings at once, each trailing its own curtain of upward water. Your silhouette walking ahead of you on the cliff may suddenly turn and look back with a face that is nothing but poured brightness and parallel grooves; when you blink, the silhouette is only yours again, yet the coolness behind your eyes has deepened. Sound-circuits complete themselves through their bodies: the splash you have not yet caused is already dripping from their lower laminations, so you drop the stone you did not know you held, and the circuit closes with a sensation like quiet satisfaction. Sometimes mist-planes thicken into temporary walls that are also their outstretched interiors; breathing those walls is breathing their memory of salt and lightning and the long work of wearing the membrane thin. You leave such a gallery lighter, as if some dense knot of ordinary causality has been dissolved out of you and taken into their slow, circulating water.

They are not immortal. Periodically one allows itself a more complete fracture. The body peels into curtains, the curtains into sheets, the sheets into the ordinary seepage and the ordinary brittle blooms. What remains is only a slightly richer rust-black staining along a new set of lines, or a crystalline fan that rings for a few extra decades with an almost personal note. The others do not mourn. They simply step through the residue, pick up any useful cadence the departing one left in the air, and continue the old weather’s work. New ones precipitate where the light is hardest and the columns already lean past reason—born from the plateau’s refusal to remain identical to itself.

What lives here, then, is the plateau’s own hesitation given temporary, walking form. They are the bright cool country trying to decide, drip by deliberate drip, whether it is stone remembering water or water inventing stone, and whether the sky pressed so close is a ceiling or a mirror. While they deliberate, everything continues to weep, and the illegible sentence lengthens down every face, and the membrane stays beautifully, usefully porous. You may walk among them for as long as the light lasts. They will not hinder you. They have been failing to finish their sentence since before maps forgot how to hold ink, and they are in no hurry to reach a period.
