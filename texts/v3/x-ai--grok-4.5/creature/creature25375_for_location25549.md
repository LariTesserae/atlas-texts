The dwellers here are not visitors and not conquerors. They are gradients of the same patience that built the place.

Begin with the ones that never leave the holdfast. In the thickest galleries, where arborescent algae lock branches until the corridor is a single calcified lung, there live the sessile choirs. They are not animals in any hurried sense. Each begins as a soft, iridescent polyp no larger than a thumbnail, settling on a living stipe while the stipe is still lengthening. Over decades it secretes thin, concentric chambers of translucent aragonite, chamber inside chamber, until it has become a spiral tower the height of a forearm. Inside the spiral a slow fire of bioluminescent bacteria is tended like a hearth. The light is not for hunting; there is almost nothing to hunt. It is for speaking. When one tower brightens, the neighboring towers answer a few hours later, a spreading hush of rose and pale green that travels the ridge like a thought shared among stone. They feed on the dissolved glitter that the veils trap, drawing it in through microscopic cilia that move once or twice a day. If you press your ear to the calcium (though nothing here has ears in the old way), you hear a sound like blood moving through a distant wrist—faint, continuous, content.

Higher, in the floating canopies where giant kelp trunks braid into vaults, move the ribbon-bodies. They are longer than a person is tall, flat as kelp themselves, and nearly the same color, so that at rest they vanish into the very fronds they browse. Their locomotion is a single, endless undulation so slow it can take a morning to cross from one trunk to the next. They do not swim so much as persuade the water to forget them forward. Along each flank runs a row of feathery sensors that taste mineral freshness and the faint electric lament of a frond about to senesce. When they feed they do not bite; they dissolve a precise window in the kelp’s outer skin and sip the sugar-rich sap for days, leaving a clean scar that the plant seals without haste or alarm. Sometimes two ribbon-bodies meet on the same stipe. They align, edge to edge, and exchange a slow river of glowing cytoplasm through temporary mouths. Afterward each carries a few new cells of the other, and their patterns of luminescence drift closer together for years. They have no eyes. Light arrives to them as warmth gradients and as the subtle polarization shifts caused by the bubbles of trapped air that sometimes linger in the upper canopy. Those bubbles fascinate them. A ribbon-body will coil beneath a shimmering lens for a week, rising and falling with it, tasting the alien dryness that leaks through. On rare days when a bubble collapses, the creature that was attending it carries a changed sheen for months afterward, as if it had dreamed of another medium and kept a souvenir.

In the hanging basins where water clarities become unnatural and distance turns optional, the banner-makers assemble. They are not single organisms. They are temporary nations of the golden and indigo micro-life that already curtains the magnetic lines. When the slow fever-currents align just so, millions of these motes lock flagella and form vertical sheets that can stretch thirty meters tall and paper-thin. The sheets possess a collective resting metabolism barely above zero, yet they are able, once or twice a season, to contract in sequenced waves. The result is a soundless billowing that looks like slow applause or like a lung remembering air. Organisms that brush through them—rare fish, straying jellies, the occasional ribbon-body—emerge coated in a brief electric memory: for an hour afterward they can sense one another’s positions across an entire cirque without scent or sight. Then the banner loses interest, disperses into ordinary glitter, and the borrowed awareness fades. No banner has ever formed in the same place twice. Each is an unrepeatable sentence written in living punctuation.

Deeper, among the talus slopes still shedding glassy basalt chips, live the gravel-minds. They are colonies of microscopic foraminifera that have learned to mortar themselves into the exact shape of the fragments they inhabit. A single chip may contain thousands of them arranged as a three-dimensional circuit. They feed on the white sludge of gentle decomposition, precipitating it into denser latticework, so that over centuries a loose scree slope becomes subtly intelligent. The intelligence is geological. It remembers every current that has ever crossed it by the pattern of mineral deposition left behind. When a new pillow lava cracks upward through the slope, the gravel-minds nearest the wound begin to pulse mild acids that etch guiding channels, inviting the warm mineral freshets to flow where the colony prefers. They do not resist the mountain’s rise; they edit it. Walk (swim) across such a slope and you feel a faint, tickling cognition under your palms, as though the ground were mulling whether to accept your weight as data.

And then there are the ones that almost never appear, the ones the place itself seems to dream only when the light is strongest and the trapped surface bubbles are most numerous. They are called, when anything bothers to call them anything, the brief lungs. A brief lung begins as an ordinary adventitious leaf that has unfurled into one of those floating lenses of air. Instead of being reclaimed when the bubble migrates, the leaf thickens, hollows, knits a delicate internal bellows full of symbiotic algae that can photosynthesize in either medium. For a few days or weeks it drifts free—part plant, part translucent animal, part temporary atmosphere. It respires openly, tasting true air for the first time in the mountain’s young memory. Its movements are imperfect and wondering; it tumbles, rights itself, extends frond-fingers toward the wrinkled skylight far above as if considering the unthinkable ascent. Most are recaptured by rising canopy or dissolving bubble. A very few are not. Those few rise through the last meters of water, breach into the wind for a single afternoon, photosynthesize with manic intensity under unfiltered sun, and then—still translucent, still half-liquid—let themselves rain back down as soft, spore-laden droplets. Wherever they strike the upper forests, new growth later appears that carries a faint, inexplicable preference for the memory of dryness. The brief lungs do not survive the fall. They are the mountain’s way of asking a question it is not yet ready to answer.

None of these beings name themselves. None keep tools or cairns or straight lines. Their generations are counted in the same centimeters-per-century as the rising cordillera. They inherit nothing but orientation: toward light that arrives almost unbroken, toward calcium that is still plentiful, toward silence spacious enough to hear a frond collide with another frond three valleys away. They are not waiting for the peaks to break surface. If anything, they are the reason the peaks remain content to stop just shy of air, thickening, complicating, finishing themselves in the brilliant shallows where everything that lives here has already learned to breathe without haste, and to be many things at once without contradiction.

The place does not need more inhabitants. It is already densely kept. What lives here is the unfinished thought of a young mountain breathing green, and every creature is only one more clause in that long, warm, underwater sentence.
