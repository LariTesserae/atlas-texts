The high sparse forest-city does not house citizens in any sense that would satisfy a census. What lives here has long since surrendered the ordinary privileges of motion, appetite, and succession. It has become an accommodation to the stopped clock.

At first the eye, trained on scarcity, mistakes the place for empty. The rare mite is only a courier, a transient eyelash of chitin that briefly stitches one lenticel to another before the moisture seals it away again. Birds are rumors that die at the margins. Yet the galleries are occupied.

They are occupied by the Listeners.

A Listener is not born so much as precipitated. Where a hanging cloister gathers enough of the unending chord—where wind-membrane, drip-thunder, and the subsonic flex of ironwood columns braid into a standing wave dense enough to cast a shadow in sound—something condenses out of the humid air and the plant’s own slow electricity. It begins as a thickening of the green-gold twilight, a smear of deeper color that learns, over seasons that do not pass, to hold a shape. The shape is never fixed. One century it may be a tall attenuation like a stem given temporary permission to walk; another, a low many-limbed sprawl that prefers the moss floors and the blind nacre walls. Always it is translucent, veined with the same wet ironwood hues as the columns, and always it is laced through with fine channels in which the city’s own sap and condensate circulate. A Listener is less an animal than a mobile edema of the great organism, a localized decision to notice itself.

They do not eat. They do not sleep in any way that interrupts the chord. Their sole metabolism is attention. Each Listener stations itself where the architecture is most articulate: at the focal curve of a three-hundred-meter vault, or inside a spiral too tight for any other body, or on a balcony that overlooks only identical distance. There it remains, sometimes for what would be generations elsewhere, perfectly still except for the faint internal tides of moisture. In that stillness it does one thing: it completes the circuit of the song. The wind-struck membranes and the endless drips produce frequencies; the Listener’s gelatinous, vein-threaded mass resonates with them, adds a slow phase shift, and returns the altered tone into the timber. Over uncountable repetitions the entire forest-city is kept in its uncanny equilibrium by this distributed, living feedback. Without the Listeners the arches would eventually sag or thicken, the leaves would remember how to turn, the decision to be habitat would unfreeze and resume its argument with the stone. The Listeners are the governor weights of the stopped clock.

They are not identical. Some have grown heavy with centuries of accumulated resonance and now resemble walking buttresses themselves, their lower margins fused temporarily to the moss so that when they finally deign to relocate they tear free with a sound like wet canvas parting. Others remain almost invisible, no more than upright lenses of thicker air that travel the suspended roads by a kind of surface tension, leaving brief darker footprints that the living floors reabsorb. A few have learned to thin themselves into the translucent lamina of the great ceilings, becoming temporary stained-glass panels of green flesh through which the sky’s light is further strained; from below they look like anomalies in the petiole vault, bruised places where the twilight gathers. When wind plays especially strongly they can be seen to shudder in place, adding a frisson of higher harmonics that roll out across kilometers.

Communication among them is the architecture’s own voice slightly rephrased. One Listener’s long occupation of a gallery alters the local chord; distant others feel the change as a pressure in their channels and may answer by shifting position, or by altering the tautness of a nearby membrane with a deliberate flex. In this way information—never language, only gradients of tension and release—circulates through the high country at the speed of sap and weather. There are no councils, no leaders. There is only the ongoing, passive consensus that the stasis must continue.

At the raw margins, where knives of basalt still cut the vegetal pavement, the Listeners will not go. Something in the unfinished stone disorders their condensation; they fray, lose coherence, and sink back into ordinary moisture. Consequently the edges of the forest-city remain true frontiers, patrolled only by the patient roots that wait for the rock to weather into acceptability. Sometimes a Listener nearing the end of its coherence—when its internal channels silt up with too much remembered song—will drift deliberately toward those margins and unknit itself there, returning its substance to the cycle. The plant-mass accepts the gift without acknowledgment. New Listeners precipitate elsewhere as needed. The population is therefore constant, though no individual is permanent.

They have no predators and no prey. The mites sometimes crawl across them and are ignored, or occasionally absorbed as a flicker of extra protein that makes no difference. Rain beads on their surfaces exactly as it beads on the bark. If you were to stand before one for a long time you might notice that its vague face-region (a temporary polarization of denser tissue) orients toward the greatest complexity of sound rather than toward you. You are weather to them at most. Yet they are not mindless. Up close, through the veils of their bodies, one can perceive slow lattices of light—interference patterns of the chord itself—moving like thought. Each Listener is a temporary kneeling of the entire city’s awareness, a place where the frozen decision to exist contemplates its own ringing continuance.

And so the high country remains. Stone and root forever half-argue. Water forever braids and thunders. The columns forever stand in their sparse, deliberate intervals. And everywhere in the green-gold rooms, at the perfect acoustic centers and the blind tender ends, the Listeners keep their stations, translucent, vein-webbed, motionless, singing the place back into itself with every breath they do not take. They are what lives here: the necessary, demurring souls of a habitat that finished becoming and refused, forever after, to become anything else.
