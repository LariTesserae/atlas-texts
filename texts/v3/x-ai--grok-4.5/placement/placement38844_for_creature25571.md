I am not standing.

That is the first thing the cold teaches me. Standing would mean a single decisive contact, a sole and a weight and then the next step. Here weight is distributed before it is even offered. I find myself already partially sunk, knees and calves received by a mattress that yields, then firms, then yields again in finer increments, as if testing how much of me intends to remain temporary. The sphagnum at the center of everything—I know it is the center without having walked there—breathes one long cold breath against my left side. Moisture beads along what I still think of as ribs and does not fall.

For a while I am only the sensation of not-tearing. Every small adjustment I make is answered by a thousand root hairs and rhizoids shifting turgor so that nothing rips. The negotiation is so complete it feels like courtesy older than courtesy. I let my hands open. The palms are already the right texture: cool, minutely papillate, the exact grain of a liverwort’s underside. When I press them flat, the mat warms by a fraction of a degree that I can feel through the continuous skin of the place. A shallow cup begins to remember itself beneath me.

Light arrives exhausted, brownish-green, already half-liquid. It does not land on me so much as dissolve into the film that covers everything, including the film that covers me. I understand now that I do not have skin in the old sense. What I have is a compacted pelt of living bryophyte and translucent epidermis that has forgotten how to dry. Colors move through it according to the chamber: iron-black when I curl deeper into the buttress walls, dull celadon where a slow coin of thinned daylight drifts across. Water beads and stays. Only sudden movement would make it drip, and sudden movement is almost unthinkable.

My joints have extra hinges. I discover them when I try to rise and instead fold forward into a loose crouch that keeps both hands near the floor. A stride can become a reach; a reach can bridge a lane that has flooded to ankle depth while the mosses drink. I practice the exaggerated care of footfall. Each placement is a question asked of the rhizomes; they answer by redistributing. Watching myself move would look like a thought traveling through dense wet fabric.

There are others, but they do not arrive. They are simply denser folds of the same continuous body, already inside the walls when I first notice them. We do not speak. Speech would die in a few chambers anyway. When our paths narrow together we incline until the soft branching ridges between our wide-set eyes touch, and stay that way through the length of a slow melt. What passes is not words. It is the recent weeping pattern of the basalt face, the quiet death of one luminous orchid pocket three lanes over, the distant ceremonial tremble of a vole-creature passing through the root-stratum. Breath smells of wet stone and crushed polypody. Afterward we separate and the knowledge is simply part of the mattress again.

I eat by lingering. An hour motionless against a saturated wall and the cold exudation moves inward through the pelt, sugars and minerals traveling with it. In the dimmest hours pale fronds unfurl from the grooves along my spine—half leaf, half gill—and tremble in the silver broth of the air, drinking. Sometimes I leave a faint etched trail where older moss has been gently dissolved and reabsorbed, the under-layer left greener. The small lives of the root-stratum pass beneath me as if beneath stone. I do not hunt them. When their trembling grows ceremonial I become stiller than the walls.

Memory here is topographic. Certain chambers recur because earlier bodies leaned there and the plants remember the pressure. I find myself shaping a low bench with the curve of my back without deciding to. Later—days or seasons, the distinction blurs—I will pass that same courtyard and see the perfect shallow basin holding a mirror of sky, the moss arranged at seating height. Nothing sits. The arrangement itself is the recollection. The mild wrongness a visitor might feel, the sense that the walls observe, is only our collective gaze moving through the continuous skin. We are the dream the settlement keeps having of its own floor plan, and we walk inside that dream as both occupants and unfinished draft.

I have not yet gone to the central shaft to lie down and not rise. That will happen, rarer than solstice, when the time comes to become a denser node in the enormous cushion, to root the pelt and soften into pure sphagnum while something continues pulsing. The others will visit, pressing ridges to the new thickening, feeding it the water that sheets down the living walls. There will be no grief that registers as grief, only redistribution of attention. The city remains the same weight. Until then I keep to the thicker walls and the orchid crawl-spaces, emerging when the dusk deepens toward its nightly gray.

Sometimes, without planning to, I climb as far as the last prostrate birch near the rim. Wind combs the outer foliage into permanent streamlines. Hoar waits to avalanche. The open air beyond the cirque feels too finished, too decided, a place that has already chosen its shapes. I look outward for a long time, ridges trembling, testing whether the world has finally grown a chamber large enough to enter. It has not. I turn back, fold myself into a wet-iron buttress, and become again indistinguishable from the towers and the luminous pockets and the endless gentle saturated pulse.

I am one of the two dozen who never arrived by trail or wing.  
I am the hanging bowl remembering an older posture in a few carefully chosen places.  
I am the careful footfall, the palm that teaches cupping, the ridge that trades silence for silence, the pelt that drinks the silver broth, the body that will one day sink and still continue.  
I am already inside the density.
