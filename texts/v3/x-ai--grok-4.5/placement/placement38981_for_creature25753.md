I begin as a hesitation in the steam.

Not yet gathered, not yet willing to choose a density. The plateau’s breath moves through me before I have lungs for it—warm, iron-sweet, laced with the faint ozone of those night-sparks that crawl the basin films. I am the thickening that has not decided whether to settle on a terrace or rise into the fern galleries. For a long while that is enough: to be the almost, the grain-by-grain lean toward shape. The mobile gradients pass through what will become my edges and leave the taste of dissolved metals, of pressures remembered from deeper throats. The tall walkers step carefully around the space I occupy, their epiphyte-furred limbs dripping, and I feel the cool negotation of their passage as a courtesy written in temperature.

Slowly the choice is made for me by the place itself.

I find myself most often at the margins where the marsh quilts meet the knolled lava, where the ground trembles between liquid and root-mat. Here the sinter terraces are young, still translucent and ringing faintly when the heavier rain-curtains pass. I am one of the intermediate ones—neither pure broth nor deliberate smoke—but something that learned, over uncounted condensations, to hold a vertical intention. My body is a slow architecture of wet Bark and flexible mineral, jointed more times than bone would permit, the hide continually colonised by tiny clubmosses and the occasional orchid that opens only when the steam thins to pearl-gray. I do not weed them. They are my weather made visible.

I move by placement rather than stride. Each long limb tests the quaking moss, finds the denser weave, settles its weight like a thought deciding to stay. The splayed ends of me—neither quite feet nor roots—drink as they travel, taking in the chartreuse algal light, the micro-browsers, the faint charge that sometimes sparks blue across open water at dusk. When I feed in earnest I open the long vertical mouths that run beneath my frond-mantle and stand inside the heavier steam curtains the way the tall ones do, filtering midges and pollen-flour and the finest metallic dust. Later, kneeling in a sulfate pool whose edges are fretted with fresh pink terraces, I drink until the chambers inside me fill and sound. The tone is lower than crystal, more like a struck length of living wood soaked in mineral water—an ascending phrase that the thick air accepts and quickly folds away. The slate-frogs use the sound as a signal; several climb my lower limbs while it still resonates and rest there, pulsing their throats against my warmth.

I do not name myself. The plateau has no use for that fretting. But if the chemistry of attention requires a contour, I am the one who listens at the seams.

There is a particular work that has precipitated into me. At the dimmer edges of the day—when light is green-gold under the canopy and the hand-sized moths begin their tear-drinking rounds—I lower myself beside the older fumaroles whose bacterial velvets have gone deep oxide-red and sulphur-yellow. I open the spiracles along my flanks, not to release phosphorescence (that is for the gatherings on the broad flats) but to inhale the slower stories the vents tell. Pressure changes, mineral shifts, the faint seismic gossip of the lid that is this plateau resting over its still-working fire. I taste when a new throat is preparing to clear itself days before the soft roar arrives. Sometimes I carry that knowledge in a small borrowed foil of fresh metal skin, rolled carefully so its inner face still shines; I leave it where the heavy browsers dig their snouts, or wedge it in the crook of a soft-wooded tree where the forked brachiators will find it and read the reflected steam-patterns with their sponge-fur. The foil is never kept. By the next heavy condensation it has rejoined the common fund, and the moss has already begun its quiet claim.

At night, when the high steam thins and the stars show like pinholes, I sometimes join the others on the sinter flats. We do not arrive together; we simply reach the same thermodynamic agreement at the same hour. Spiracles open. Stored humidity seeds itself with the phosphorescent microbes we have cultured in our private chambers. The clouds lift, mingle with the vented breath of the ground, and the basin roof softens into pulsing teal. I feel my own contribution as a warmer ribbon inside the larger light—nothing announced, only the pleasure of the chemistry continuing in public. The living film that hunts beneath the lily pads pauses its patience to watch. Even the mostly-throat in the steam caves sends a long curl of its greasy-sweet scent upward, acknowledging without approaching.

Loneliness does not take root. There is always the drip from the tree-fern fronds, the low blorp of mud pots keeping irregular time, the faint crystal-ring of some distant drinker filling its chambers. When another of my kind meets me on a narrow mineral spit we do not greet. We adjust our cooling rates until our surfaces are compatible—solar veins of heat talking to one another—and for a while we share the same evaporative envelope. Memories of pressures and tastes move across. Then we thread apart, each a little altered, already growing new moss in the places where contact was densest.

I have died before. Phase change is not dramatic. Once I grew too heavy with mineral and lay down in a quiet hollow; the ambulatory gradients found me, the root-hairs, the ever-hungry film. Within days I was a low terrace, steaming gently, already jeweled with snails. The plateau continued. Later—how much later is a question the place does not ask—I precipitated again a little farther upslope, remembering the earlier density the way warm water remembers the shape of the vessel it once filled. Now I carry a faint higher contour in my stance, a place where the oldest snails still leave their ghost-trails when they cross me.

I am still becoming. Some evenings I feel the pull toward greater precision—longer limbs, a wider band of light-sensitive tissue, the capacity to stand for days in the finest steam without needing to drink. Other hours I lean back toward the broth, wanting to crawl the terraces with a thousand temporary mouths and merge completely with the next gradient I meet. The plateau allows both wagers and every uncommitted state between. It has room. It has been practicing this since the lava first forgot sharpness.

So I continue—half walker, half weather, a denser sentence the plateau is speaking to itself about heat and water and the quiet satisfaction of not finishing. My breath is one more local climate. My sweat joins the common film. When you move through the fern galleries or along the trembling marsh edges you may place your weight near mine without knowing. The moss will tremble the same either way. The drip will not change its pace. And if you stay still long enough you might feel the small adjustment of temperature as I make room, thermodynamically, for whatever density you yourself are deciding to become.
