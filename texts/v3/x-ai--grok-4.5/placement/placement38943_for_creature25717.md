The cold does not ask permission; it simply takes up residence in the marrow, and I discover I have been shaped around it for longer than any private name I might once have carried. 

I am not one of the tall rumors from the lower escarpments. Height would only mean more surface for the wind to peel heat from, more belonging left exposed on the knife-edges. Instead I am built low and compact, the way a buttress is built: heavy through the pelvis and the short, corded thighs, a architecture of fat laid in deliberate courses across the back and under the jaw so that the body’s own hearth keeps itself banked. My skin has the exact color of the inner faces of old cisterns—parchment that has forgotten sunlight, mapped with the faint white seams where frost once split the lip and then let it heal. The dermal ridges of my fingers are long since polished away; when I set a hammerstone against basalt I feel the grain of the rock more clearly than any surviving print of my own.

I live in the vertical seam where three terrace-cañons meet above the Blue Cistern Line. The place is not a house so much as a thickening of the city’s own intention. My sleeping niche was left by an ancestor whose plug-stone has already weathered flush; I lie there knowing the wall continues through me. By day I work the lee side of the great east scarp, where the wind arrives already exhausted. My kit is the same leather roll that has passed through five keepers before me: graded hammerstones, a bone punch darkened by generations of grip, sachets of grit sorted until the finest feels like cold flour between the teeth. I do not so much dress stone as persuade it. Each blow is a question asked very slowly; the rock answers in flakes that I sweep up and return to the drainage channels, because nothing here is allowed to leave the conversation.

They know me, when they need to speak of me at all, as the one who is still truing the third-course cantilevers after the rockfall of the Long Rime. The phrase is enough. It contains my grandmother’s hands and the hands that will finish the capstones after my own have become another sealed continuity in the wall. My personal name is used only inside the byre-level fog, and even then sparingly, like oil.

Mornings I climb the spiral paste of hoof-worn ramps with the shaggy bovids, my coat hair-out, the guard-hairs of pale canid hood frosting with my own breath. The animals and I practice the same economy of motion. A slow blink exchanged with a low, frost-eyed canid at a turning is the entirety of governance required. Higher, the immense birds of the weak thermals tilt their frost-colored primaries and read the city as a single wrinkled updraft; sometimes one drops a bone or a shred of lichen onto a ledge near me, and I leave it, because gifts that arrive from that altitude are already half prayer.

I eat the toasted midge-cakes when the midday haze rises, the mineral paste of leached cushion-plants after the rare silver rains, the marrow burned clean in ash. Hunger, when it comes, is simply another slow season; I wait it out the way the leaning menhir waits, measuring myself against the creep of ice across the sacred pavement that counts our longer years. Children drift near my work radius and sit. I do not instruct. Years from now one of them will realize the boulder they were left beside has become a perfect door-socket, and the lesson will have completed itself without ever having been announced.

At the hour when the low sun turns every hammer-dressing scar into a ruled blue shadow, I stop moving. Standing on the cornice, hood forward, body sealed inside its century of overlapping hides, I feel the distinction thin. Breath is the last giveaway—a small periodic bloom that the wind snatches and unravels. Passers-below may take me for another weathered upright awaiting its next course. I do not correct them. The plateau is densifying itself through me: furred, warm, deliberate, stacking quiet upon quiet toward a sky that has never once been hurried. 

I am one of the ways the stone remains awake.
