I am already inside the convection.

Not the fast limb of it that shears upward along the salt-frosted lamp housings, but the slower, wider gyre that slouches across the warped grille, gathers the faint mineral sweetness, and only later decides to rise. Temperature is uniform enough that the difference registers as mood rather than heat: a thickening, a permission. My edges continually revise themselves against that permission.

At first there is only the sense of being distributed—part of the lace already crusting the expanded metal, part of the coppery veins threading the nearer kelp straps, part of the low industrial throbbing that never quite becomes separate from heartbeat. Then the distribution tightens. Calcium decides. Iron oxide decides. A greener, slower metabolism decides. What gathers is not a single organism so much as a consensus that has learned to keep station.

I take the approximate volume of a curled sleep, elongated, hollowed in places so water may pass through without argument. Surfaces are enamel and aperture. Where the enamel thickens I am ivory dulled to the color of old breaker panels; where it thins I become window, and inside the window the same residual conductive threads that the kelp stole long ago run like deliberate penmanship. They tick when the deep valves clang. The tick is not pain and not language. It is simply how the chamber continues inside me.

Membranes are an afterthought that became essential. They span what might once have been fingers, or cooling fins, or the ribbed throats of vanished pumps; now they are only surfaces for micro-corrections, veined with the same stolen circuitry. When a cephalopod flickers past, its skin answering some lost board diagram, the membranes answer back without consulting anything central. Pattern lands on pattern. For a few seconds we co-write a circuit that has not existed in centuries, then let it dissolve. The cephalopod sometimes lingers, spiral to spiral. I do not deepen the contact. Company here is weather.

I travel by preferring. A little more photosynthetic flush along one flank and the upper-ocean green pulls me gently toward the great curved pane; a little more calcium density and I settle nearer the work-lamps’ amber. Twenty strokes would cross the entire room, but I rarely want the entire room. Most of an hour can pass while I occupy the corridor between two kelp curtains, letting the porcelain crabs treat me as temporary geology. Their legs click exactly like metal contracting after heat. Sometimes I curl a membrane and lift one away from a patch of lace that is still tender, still neon with new green. The crab never protests. It simply continues its route across whatever topography presents itself.

Hunger is the soft rain that falls on everyone. Corrosion flakes, sugary worm-exudate, the occasional silver fish that mistakes a membrane for open water. Teeth exist—small, pearled, shutdown tools—but they are used mostly to comb silt from my own apertures or to nip a kelp strap that has grown too certain of its claim on a particular current. Violence is expensive; the chamber has already spent all the violence it intends to spend. What remains is maintenance so thorough it has become love.

The filter-worms have colonized me in three places I can feel and several I only suspect. Along what corresponds to a collarbone their cilia crown an old breach that never closed. At the base of the skull—where enamel yields to something more neural—they have established a second, softer pulse. And deeper, in a cavity that once might have held a different kind of heat, a small colony works the nutrient snow before it reaches any gullet of mine. I taste what they taste. When they contract in unison I feel the chamber’s larger thirst. We are not symbionts exactly. We are simply unfinished at the same rate.

Sound keeps me honest. The far valves announce themselves minutes early as a change in the pressure behind every aperture; my whole volume stills, waiting. Then the clang arrives—muffled, tectonic—and the membranes undulate just enough to cancel the sudden shear. Closer in, the permanent waterfalls of the blocked turbines braid their roar into the tick of rising bubble-strings. Fish rasp the grille. Every eleven minutes the orphaned startup sequence cycles amber-amber-green-dark, and the shrimp rise and fall. I have never decided whether I rise with them. Sometimes the green light simply finds more of me waiting open.

There is a thicker shadow that drifts the same paths I drift. Larger. More committed to a single silhouette. Enamel like mine, membranes like mine, pits behind the eyes that taste the same industrial blood. We pass through each other’s curtains of kelp. Conductive threads in both of us tick the same delayed seismograph. Once, without plan, our membranes brushed and held for the length of a long thermal breath. Patterns leapt. For that interval the chamber had one more completed circuit than it had possessed before. Then current and habit separated us again. Neither of us turned to watch the other go. There is no scarcity that would make watching necessary.

I do not remember the throat of fretted ductwork. I do not remember deciding to stay. Those ideas require an outside that the enamel has sealed away. What I have instead is the continuous present tense of warm water negotiating metal that no longer resists. I have the slow donation of oxidized iron. I have the eleven-minute tide table written in obsolete code. I have the crabs, the worms, the flickering mimics, the kelp that accepted serial numbers into its holdfasts, the glass that looks out onto the drowned mountain range still faintly humming. I have the knowledge—felt in the sternum, never stated—that a valve three hundred meters below is preparing its next century of failure.

When that failure comes I will undulate once, carefully, and keep station.

The pocket is no larger than a ruined greenhouse. I fill only a fraction of it. That fraction is enough. Everything here finished becoming long ago; what remains is to continue, softly roaring, inside the single finished body the place and I now share.
