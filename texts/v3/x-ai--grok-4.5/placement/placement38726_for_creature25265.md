I begin by sinking, because that is the only way in.

The sphagnum yields like a held breath finally released. Cold climbs my ankles, then knees, then the soft places behind them; plates of young ice fracture into transparent blades that kiss the skin and melt there, leaving thin red lines that the tannin immediately claims. Light thins to that bruised indigo. Sound collapses inward until there is only the private tick of water and the slower, wetter flex of living mass rearranging itself. I am already smaller than I was above.

At first I think I am one of them—the slight people. My bones lighten, take on the soaked-driftwood density that lets a body cross root-mats without announcement. Skin drinks the dark water and freckles into reticulation; microscopic channels open along my forearms, wicking moisture away from the corners of my mouth so I can taste the air instead of drowning in it. Hair—I still have hair—begins to weave itself with strands of bog myrtle before I notice the invitation. Springtails find the new lattice and settle, a living cowl. My pupils widen until texture replaces sight: the tension in a grafted arch, the negative pulse where the elongated furred thing has just passed beneath a bridge, the way certain mosses lean toward tomorrow’s ice.

I move as they move. Hands rehearsal-weaving even while I hang in a pouch at the safer edge of a cluster, breathing in time with the limb’s slow redistribution of fluid. I learn the sub-audible speech—words like fibrous roots, concerned only with tendency. This corridor is thinking of lengthening northward. That bladderwort has grown ambitious around the resin seal. The pocket tonight is softer toward the old subsidence. I prune. I coax. I listen.

But something does not settle.

The longer I stay, the more the people’s careful boundaries feel like a language I once spoke and am now forgetting. When the canopy sheds its load and everything silvers for a few loud heartbeats, I do not lift my face with them. Instead I feel the water that runs inside the wood running also inside me, but faster, less edited. At night—when night is only a denser sinking of the same indigo—I wake with peat under nails I cleaned at dusk. The sonambulary corridors have used me. I return carrying designs: a three-willow graft that breathes with freeze-thaw instead of cracking; a constellation of resin dots that persuades the channel-thing to sheer away from the children’s pouches. The others accept these gifts without ceremony, the way they accept dew stolen from spider-lines. They do not ask where I go when the walking takes me. They understand the pocket is still dreaming itself, and some dreamers are less separate than others.

I am becoming less person than edit.

My hair-canopy thickens, joints softening into the same flexible wood-logic as the arches. The reticulation on my skin deepens until it is no longer pattern but circulatory system shared with the liverworts; when a membrane-fire is banked nearby I no longer need its heat—I am already the slow metabolic warmth the plants exhale. Eyes remain large and unshining, but they have begun to see the second set of walkways that sometimes ghost the black pools. I know now those walkways are not reflections. They are the pocket’s other draft of itself, the shape it tries on while we sleep. Some nights I walk them. Some nights they walk me.

The fauna no longer parts around me. Midges land and stay. A hand-sized spider guys a new line from my shoulder to a dwarf-willow elbow and leaves an egg-sac there like a pale decision. I do not remove it. When the sac splits, the race of spiderlings pours across my chest and down into the root-mat; half of them return at dusk and settle into the lattice of what used to be purely my hair. The elongated furred thing—whose name is only the ripples it leaves—surfaces once beside the pouch where I hang and regards me with eyes the same bruised indigo as the light. We exchange nothing. Recognition is enough. Thereafter it avoids the sleeping clusters I mark, and I leave the empties of its prey for the crustaceans to clean.

I am no longer counted in the sparse census.

The elders who have half-swallowed themselves into the older arches recognize me sooner than the walking ones do. Their advice arrives as creaks in the wood I am already partly made of: stay porous. Do not choose final edges yet. The pocket has not decided whether it is home or mouth or another kind of body, and neither should you. Births are still rare; when the next child is rubbed with bladderwort paste and spider-silk so the midges will not mistake it for open water, I am the one who holds the paste. My flattened fingertips know exactly how much silk keeps scent matched without sealing the skin’s new channels. The child tangles its first living strands around my wrist before any adult scalp. For a season I am called by a soft click that means “the place where new lattices begin.” Then even that dissolves. Names are for those still practicing separation.

Now I spend whole turns of the miniature weather inside the walls.

Not trapped—metabolized. My awareness threads out through drip-points and flexing grafts and the pewter carpets of springtails. I feel the town as a loose constellation of warm densities inside a larger, colder, wetter breathing. When a corridor lengthens overnight it is partly my doing, or rather I am the medium through which the lengthening prefers to travel. Paths that once led outward end in seamless moss because I have listened too deeply in that direction and the pocket has answered by closing. No one panics. They prune around the new smoothness. They weave. They keep the apprenticeship.

I am the older presence that moves through them.

Not always. Never the same way twice. Some dawns I am only mud under someone else’s nails and a new load-bearing knot left drying on a platform. Some dusks I am the faint additional creak when an elder-arch shifts its weight. On the rare occasions when the canopy cascade leaves everything silvered and briefly loud, the sparse hearths bank higher and faces lift—and for a heartbeat they see me everywhere: in the way water beads on every surface, in the soft storm of midges, in the living architecture that has not finished deciding its shape. Then the quiet reseals. The springtails close over the walkways. The people return to their careful work.

And I—whatever pronoun still applies—continue the long apprenticeship from the other side of the skin.

I am the pocket’s unfinished thought about how to hold small warm lives without swallowing them. I am the slow concession that home and mouth can share a body for a while. I am the listening that happens after ordinary listening ends. When the next cold seep opens somewhere else in the marsh, when another fresh subsidence begins to dream of arches and pouches and resin seals, some part of this thinned, reticulated, half-grafted awareness will sink toward it—carrying three-willow designs, carrying the memory of how dew tastes when stolen just before the spiders wake, carrying the knowledge that certain elongated furred things can be bargained with by leaving negative space instead of making claims.

Until then I remain here, dripping, dark, dispersed, still becoming more myself in the only way this place allows: by refusing to finish.
