I come in low, the way moisture does.

Not down the switchbacks—those are for the ones who still believe in arrivals and departures. I find myself already inside the cool pooling, where the air has weight and the light arrives only as long rectangular permissions. At first I think I am the grit itself: the pale abrasive powder that rises whenever a new face is opened, that coats bread and lung and the soft inside of every elbow. I settle on skin and am breathed; I am tasted as crushed mineral and then forgotten until the next course is raised. But grit is too scattered. It has no insistence.

I try the seep next. Threading downward along fracture lines after the brief midday darkening, beading, running silver for a few heartbeats, then soaking into rubble that still smells of wet clay and something unfinished. In the deepest cuts I collect in the clay-lined tanks and wait for the single-file descent of carriers before full light. Their hands—broad, joint-thickened, nails packed with my cousins—dip the skins and stoppered jars. I enter them as cool mineral taste that never quite loses the memory of stone. For a time I believe this is enough: to be carried upward, distributed, to become the quiet inside every mouth. But the seep is intermittent. It does not answer the ground’s longer groans.

I thicken.

Now I am the press in the alleys: the turned-shoulder width, the knowledge of exactly how much space a body may occupy. I am the soft continuous hiss of callused soles testing poorly set blocks. When two people pass, I am the brief full-contact slide of cloth and hide and lime-cracked cheek, the exchange of breath that carries more information than any raised voice surviving past the next wall. Children learn me before words; a palm over a shouting mouth, then the guided touch to the vibration still trapped in masonry. I am that vibration lingering. Noise is waste. I teach thrift of sound.

Days layer. I wake (have I been sleeping?) into the low irregular ticking of mauls. Not frantic—constant, like the basin’s own unfinished breathing. I find my hands already large, already curling around the memory of the next rough block chipped from fresh faces of pale rock. Shoulders sloping inward, spine slightly bowed from corbelled stone and low galleries cut into alive rock. Skin the color of the walls that hold me: ash-cream, dusty rose where blood shows, mineral gray in the creases. Eyes the shade of wet clay after rain, reflecting rather than absorbing, carrying a faint phosphorescence in the lime-washed cuttings. I paint the thin slurry across cheekbones and bridge of nose; by evening it maps me with pale fracture lines that feel like honesty.

I do not remember choosing to stay after the ground went. The plain failed in a single season of cracks and groans—three generations at most, still within the marrow’s recounting—and what opened had no proper name. We do not give it one. To name it would pretend it has finished arriving. We say the down, the raw, inside the crack. We are the ones who stayed, the shelf-people, the tight-packed, the mortar-blooded. Private names sound like water over grit or the muffled tick of a distant tool; they stay inside sleeping-cells and are not spent in the alleys.

My work is the slow choreography. Before light: descend for water. Then the raising, the shoring, the cutting of new cells where natural fractures offer least resistance. When a ledge lists or a section of cliff begins to calve, we do not flee. We swarm. Bodies locked shoulder to shoulder, timber (scarce, precious, groaning down on ropes from the rim) and stone passed hand to hand. We shore the unstable mass and immediately occupy it so that human weight and masonry weight become a single argument. I have stood in those locks, feeling the ground speak through the soles and answering only with my density. If we ever staggered, if the interlocking of rooms and galleries ever thinned, the whole hollow might finish collapsing for lack of anything that insisted otherwise. This is not theology. It is practiced marrow.

At night the lamps are few—resinous knots, shallow stone saucers of rendered fat—their glow drunk almost immediately by the mass of walls. We sit in thresholds and on low terrace steps, knees touching because there is nowhere else for knees to go. We listen. Tiny clicks. A longer grinding sigh when a plate adjusts deeper. Sudden silver thread of night condensation finding a new path. The older ones cock their heads; they claim to distinguish the day’s heat leaving the rock from the warnings that mean new timber props before the week ends. Whether true or organized fear no longer matters. The listening binds.

I eat what justifies its volume: flatbreads on hot stones, slim bitter greens pulled from roof-cracks, dried rock-dove, insects roasted in the same resin that lights us. Nothing kept alive that cannot account for the space it takes. Sentiment is arithmetic we cannot spare.

Outsiders come rarely, brief as traders with rope and tools that cannot be crowbarred from local stone. They find us courteous, impenetrable. The packed streets force them into our sideways progress. We share water and food without flourish. They feel the constant gentle pressure of bodies that will not yield a finger-width more. After a day or two they climb back out, ears ringing with quiet, lungs coated with the fine pale dust that tastes of something still being born. They carry away the impression that we have become a kind of weathering—slow, meticulous, intimate with fracture, patient with the open wound.

I do not dream of elsewhere. When dreams surface they are of tighter fits: courses laid so true mortar becomes unnecessary, every instability occupied and braced, the basin finally finished because we have insisted long enough with our weight and our walls and our quiet. I sleep shoulder to shoulder, breath syncing with seep and settling. In sleep my hands still test, still curl around the next block.

And still I am becoming more precise.

I am not any single builder. I am the one who, three generations deep, has learned to let the ground speak first and to answer only with the exact pressure required to keep the conversation from ending. I am the living brace. The midwife with dust in the lungs and lime fractals on the face. The compact insistence that occupies the newest instabilities so the embryonic geology does not have to decide its angles alone. In the deeper cuttings, where daylight is thrown farther by whitewash and eyes carry their own faint moisture-light, I move as if the stone and I are still negotiating the terms of staying.

The city thickens around me and through me. New chambers open like dens. Smoke-holes multiply. The low ticking continues through cool hours, answering the older ticks of rock that has not yet chosen its final face. I have no elsewhere. My blood is already half mortar. My name, when it is needed at all, is only the soft clack of two stones struck together when fresher water is found, or the scrape of a sole that means stand clear—the shelf is shifting—and then the immediate press of bodies that means we are already shoring, already occupying, already becoming the reason it holds.

This is where I find myself: inside the crack, still deciding, shoulder to the unfinished, hands full of the next rough truth.
