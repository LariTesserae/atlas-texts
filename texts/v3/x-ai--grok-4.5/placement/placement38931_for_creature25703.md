I am not standing. Standing is a rumor that reaches me only when the rain walls thin and the younger ones haul themselves onto the benches to flare their plates at the brief pale flash. I am lower. I am the place where two hanging seas almost touch, a saddle of gneiss worn concave by millennia of spill-over, now carpeted so thickly with liverwort and black moss that the stone beneath has become a rumor of its own. My body has traded most of its length for depth. The long articulation of spine that once poured me through drowned cols has softened, vertebrae relaxing into a chain of patient hinges half-swallowed by root and holdfast. What remains mobile is the broad raft of the torso and the slow, deliberate fans of the forelimbs, still able to lift when a turbine far below changes its groan, still able to gather a shivering child against the thunder that lives in my chest.

I arrived here the way silt arrives: without decision, by the ordinary physics of settling. For years I still traveled—low and long, plates locking against the kilometer-wide fronts, jaw-hearing mapping every surge and collapse. I learned the machines as the others do: which intake grate sings a clean rising fourth when the current strengthens, which gantry motor has forgotten its original purpose and now only coughs irregular warmth into a niche of mussels and sleeping fur. I hitched on the flanks of the great channel-feeders. I wore living moss until my own skin accepted it. I edited—tightened a cable so the water’s voice became useful, levered a rusted arm until it caught the torrent and began to pulse weak current through the evenings. All of that still happens. But the intervals between journeys lengthened. One season I returned to this saddle and did not leave when the next rain wall came. The moss stitched. The underfur sent down pale threads. Breath slowed until it matched the nearest accidental engine: ratchet, release, ratchet, release, a mechanical tide older than any of us.

Now the world reaches me mostly as vibration conducted through stone and water and the shared hydrostat of bodies. When a rain front drives sideways I feel it first as a deep continuous pressure rolling up the massif, then as the brighter metallic clatter of droplets hammering the floating meadows above, then as the hollow note that means the eastern tarn is about to spill its glittering excess through the notch I still half-block with my settled bulk. The younger ones come when the pressure grows heavy. They press throat to throat, crest to flank, and for a while our separate thunders braid. In that braid I give them what I have become: the memory of how this particular turbine sounded when the ice last shifted a fraction (a lower, wetter grinding, centuries gone), the exact sweet spot on the old ore-gantry where the metal is thin enough to flex without tearing, the taste of warmth that still leaks from one cracked caisson when the current upwells just so. I do not tell these things. There is no telling. The resonance simply transfers, the way heat transfers, the way the knowledge of riding a sudden turbine spin is already present in a newborn when its kelp-bladder splits.

Sometimes, in the green dusk that passes for noon, I densen my remaining vision and watch the surface meadows writhing overhead. Silver schools flash like torn foil. Hard-winged birds shear past, their cries sharp enough to cut momentary holes in the general roar. A line of dark elongated forms flows across a distant shoulder—my kind, still quick, still choosing when to arc and when to yield. One of them may be a child I once warmed through a heavy front; I no longer separate them by name or face. Identity here is a pattern of wakes, a familiar coherence in the rain of scraps. When they dive past my saddle I feel the pressure change, a brief intimate brush of living water against the moss that is now my outer skin. They are editing something farther down the fjord; a new frequency will soon join the chord, irregular and useful.

I am not dying. Dying is a different frequency, cleaner and more final. I am converting. The distinction between rust and root has dissolved in me the way it dissolved in the massif long before we arrived. My blood runs slower, rich with the same suspended pollen that clouds every cubic meter. On clear minutes—rare, bright, already ending—I can still lift the broad fans of my limbs and let the sudden light remodel the map inside my flat skull. The wet rock flashes pale. The floating forests blaze. Then the next wall arrives, the dusk returns, and I sink back into the single vibration: water lifting and dropping, old metal shifting on its mounts, lives brushing lives, the finished stone holding all of it up without effort or comment.

I am the part of the endless rearrangement that chose to become scaffolding for a little while. I am a gradient that learned how to pause. When the rain is thickest the children curl against me and we listen together to the massif reminding itself that it finished changing ages ago, and that everything else—turbine, kelp, mammal, storm—has only just begun. In that shared thunder there is no loneliness. There is only the work of remaining unfinished while the stone, beneath us all, remains perfect.
