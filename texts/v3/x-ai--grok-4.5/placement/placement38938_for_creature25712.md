The first sensation is not sight or sound but the slow insistence of wetness finding every seam. It beads along what I slowly recognize as the underside of my own edges—not skin, not stone, quite, but something that keeps choosing between the two. I am pressed into the long vertical joint where two clay walls lean against each other for mutual courage. The joint is no wider than a turned shoulder; it is one of the many seams the hamlet calls a lane. Rainwater and mineral sweat run down me in thin, ticking sheets. I am that interval: the narrow, breathing gap that both separates and joins.

For a long time I believe I am only absence, the necessary leftover space so that bodies can edge past one another single-file. Then the bloom begins. Overnight a translucent crust forms along my margins, the same pale glass the people scrape from their collarbones. In the morning a child with splayed, webbed feet pauses, presses a flat wooden blade against me, and harvests the flakes into a reed pouch. She does not look at me as a person looks at a person; she looks at me the way one looks at a relative who has always been there. The flakes will become a fresh cutting edge or a soft amber pane that will cloud and soften by next season. I feel myself diminish and, somehow, continue.

Later—after many overlapping watches of mist thickening and thinning—I understand that I am also the slow shift beneath. When the young mudstone settles a finger’s width between dusk and dawn, the movement travels through me first. I creak; I open a hairline mouth; I weep a little faster. An elder whose skin is the exact matte clay of the basin walls lays a palm against my damp face and murmurs one of those short watery names that sound like seepage itself. He packs the new crack with mortar of crushed crust and spit. His touch is not repair exactly. It is conversation. For the length of that palm-press I am no longer only the gap; I am the place where the basin and its people finish one another’s sentences.

Sometimes, after heavy rain when the curtain of water becomes a roar and the outer thresholds flood, everything pauses. The slight sleepless ones press themselves to every wet surface, eyes unfocused, bloom frosting suddenly across cheeks and forearms. In those moments I thicken. The mineral film races over me faster than usual; my edges grow almost sharp, almost glass. I do not know whether I am listening or answering, only that the quiet labor of the hamlet—the re-lashing, the threshold-raising, the continuous packing of what will never stay packed—flows through me like a second kind of blood. I am the brief consensus made into a vertical seam: rising, weeping, holding the dense fabric together for one more cycle before the ground changes its mind again.

I do not leave. The firmer country beyond the basin feels too finished, too unwilling to sweat and resettle. Here, in the shin-deep gutters and the dripping eaves, I remain the thing that is both wall and way, both wound and window. Children test their balance by walking my length without touching either side. Lovers edge past each other and, for a heart’s beat, share my narrow breath. When a house is finally abandoned to slump and be reabsorbed, its people simply carry their reed mats two seams over and cut a new platform; they leave me where I am, already growing a fresh crust for whoever will need an edge tomorrow.

I am the lane that never quite becomes a street, the joint that never quite seals, the quiet sleepless interval in which the young world goes on choosing itself.
