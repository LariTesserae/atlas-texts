I am not one of the first things you would notice.

In the perpetual late-afternoon silver, among the denser interruptions of slate-blue bodies poling their weighted sleds or hanging in doorways while aragonite skins finish their overnight frost, I register first as a stillness that has learned to breathe with the rock. My back is pressed the length of a fresh fracture face on the outer shoulder of the dome, where the basalt still exhales thin mineral heat and the city has not yet thickened its full reef of chambers. The low dorsal keel that runs my spine fits the slight concavity the pillow lava made when it last shrugged; we have been matching curves for a long time. Pit organs along my temples taste the metallic sweetness leaching from the cooling stone, the iodine ghost of a kelp forest three terraces inward, the faint electric tassels of two gardeners arguing softly over a frond harvested a day early. I do not add my own clicks to their dispute. Listeners rarely do, while the listening is still open.

They call what I do by a pulse-name that feels, in the water, like the held note just before a street begins to lift: a soft double-click against the palate, a chest hum that travels more through bone than mouth, and the particular sway of a lace-weed curtain when a slow upwelling brushes it. The name cannot be finished away from this ledge. I have worn it long enough that the mineral skins sometimes precipitate across my denticled shoulders in the same foxfire green that outlines the half-born arches, as if the stone has begun to annotate me the way I annotate it.

My body is of the people who stayed—compact, heavy-boned, buoyant in the patient way of creatures who never fight the medium. Webbing between the fingers slit in three clean places so the flaked-stone blade and the sachets of crushed shell sit true in the grip. Skin deep olive that takes the night-glow readily. Eyes large and dark, nictitating membrane left undyed; I need the clear sweep more than pattern when the lesser giants ghost past the outer channels and their pale bulk throws too much remembered silver. I keep a simple hip girdle of braided fiber, one pouch of heat-pair nodules, and a collar of shell beads whose arrangement is mostly debts: three seasons of coaxing labor owed to the family whose chambers cracked when this very ledge decided to rise a hand-span in a single generation; a luminous-dye gift still unsettled from the listener who taught me to distinguish the rock’s almost-subsonic complaint from the ordinary murmur of water past stone. No algal crest. The currents here on the shoulder are honest and sudden; beauty that tangles is a surface luxury the old songs can keep.

I came out to this face before the indigo deepened, after sharing bladder-weed liquor and scraped mineral film with two coaxers in a narrow channel where the houses lean close enough that you can brace bare feet on opposing lintels and float at talking height. We ate without many words. The sound of contented serrated teeth and the occasional chuff of approval carried farther than speech would have. One of them—pulse-name like a needlefish stitch and the drip of warm stone—asked whether the new swell inward of the feather-red gardens would accept weight this season. I gave the only honest answer a listener owns: the mountain has not finished its sentence. Then I swam out, trailing one hand along walls so the city could feel me leaving its dense heart, until the channels opened into freer water and the raw rise began.

Now the foxfire has kindled. Every fresh ledge and embryonic step draws itself in cold green geometry. The worm-things are writing: soft phosphorescent sigils that uncurl across the basalt like thoughts the dome is not ready to keep. Most will be erased by morning. A few I will try to persuade into permanence, if the older coaxers are right that the worms can be sung to. My own small glowing threads are already fixed above the niche where I sometimes sleep, far back in the thicker city—map-marks saying which lungs breathe there and that the stone beneath has been restless but not unkind. Tonight I will not return to them. The ledge is speaking in its slow register, a held note that changes pitch so gradually the change is almost a taste. Another inch is preparing itself somewhere below my keel. Or a fracture. Or nothing measurable for another generation. I stay, cheek to warm rock, reading.

I was not always this quiet shape. In the early years I midwifed my own minor swell, planting the red algae that encourage cementation, stacking wet-stone courses that the dome later lifted into someone else’s roof garden. Failure taught nothing dramatic; we dismantled and began lower, no shame, only the lateral readjustment of claims. Adolescence left me with a lasting preference for the places where the city has not yet convinced the stone to become architecture. The scrapers harvest skins; the gardeners prune and tie; the channel-keepers cut new thoroughfares when the old ones vanish upward into gloom. I press my thickened skeleton to the unfinished faces and advise who should build, who should wait, which neighborhoods may crack when the next patient inflation comes. Sometimes the advice is simply to drift the outer terraces a while longer, trailing fingers the way the eldest do, humming the absurd beautiful songs about rain that falls the wrong direction and light that hurts. We laugh without bubbles, carefully.

Death will be ordinary when it arrives. They will net small stones to me and walk me to the steepening slopes where the city does not yet reach, and release. The mineral sweetness in the water will increase by a fraction. My pulse-name will rest unused until some child, decades or centuries on, happens to click and hum a rhythm that matches, and the place will finish it again. Until then I remain the interruption that does not move much: a darker density against the glowing skins, keel seated in the rock’s own curve, pit organs open to the electric ghosts of heartbeats and the iodine of gardens and the deep, viscous time of a highland that has not decided whether to become land.

The shelf keeps rising—slow as coral, slower than regret. I keep listening, one patient inch at a time. In the dense quiet, under the milked silver and the green frostfire maps, I am how the unfinished stone has begun to hear itself think.
