I am not standing. Standing would imply a dryness of sole, a finished edge against the sponge. Instead I find the weight of myself already distributed—half-sunk where a platform of wattled moss has slumped under last night’s vertical river, half-lifted by the slow hydraulic shrug of roots that have decided, overnight, that this depression should become a low saddle of its own. Cool water beads along the hair-thatch at the base of my skull and finds the channel of my spine. It does not fall; it simply joins what is already there.

The air is a stacked engine. I do not sort the voices at first. Insect glaze, frog-pipe, the scrabble of fist-sized birds through vertical thatch, the wet shoulder of something low-slung rewriting a gallery below—these arrive as one pressure against the skin, a glittering road-noise that has no road. Only gradually do particular detonations separate: a single drop striking a shield-leaf hard enough to ring, the precise chuckle of water forced through a half-ditch near my left knee, the soft cambium-creak of a living buttress accepting a new lashing of vine. My hands know that sound before my ears do. The fingers (long, stained green-black to the quick, nails worn to useful bevels) are already moving—testing the stretch of yesterday’s work, sliding a fresh aerial whip into the gap where a browser chewed through at dusk. The motion is not decided. It is simply what hands do here when the lattice breathes open.

I taste chlorophyll and cold stone on the back of the tongue. Mist layers sit at different heights; the one that bathes the mid-galleries is thinner today, ordinary daylight greened but not drowned. In that clearer band I see the others—not as a group, not counted, just denser flexes in the continuous body. One pauses on a tier with a child grip-locked to her shoulder; the child’s toes flex independently against her collarbone, already practicing. Another works a shell-adze free from the fork that has begun to swallow it, biting the wood once to remember the difference between timber and host. Their calls surf the din without adding height: short percussive lifts that mean location, or caution, or simply *here*. I answer with the rhythm that is mine—the particular way my weight makes the nearest channel speak—and the answer is accepted into the weather without comment. No names. Only recurring pressures.

A flexing absence moves through the canopy high above. Platforms sway the width of a breath. Everything pauses—the low browsers mid-tunnel, the stained-glass insects mid-drone, the people mid-lashing—until the structure remembers itself and steadies. In that held breath I feel my own blood (a little warmer than the mist) counting the same seconds as the roots. When motion resumes it is already repair: shoots nosing into the micro-tears, amphibians reclaiming overflowing cups, hands (mine among them) jacking a slumped edge level with a fire-hardened stick and the patient insertion of new props. Death is here too, somewhere— an older body half-stitched into leaf-mould by mycelium, marked only by a denser collar of water-shedding ferns we step around until the green finishes what it started. Birth as well, quieter: another small heat added to a hollow, taught early to hold tight when the whole saddle inhales.

I am not the densest life, nor the loudest. The climbing mammals with grasping hind feet use the same routes; sometimes their slick fur still delicates a sleeping hollow I left at first light. Birds pry at the moss until it bleeds black water and must be rewoven. The plants themselves outvote every intention: a screen I thickened last season is already browsing-gap and re-weaving, the conversation never finished. My thoughts run at the speed of that green repair—slow hydraulic lift, sudden minor catastrophe, the constant renegotiation of every surface. Memory is not stored; it is practiced into the scar-tissue of the buttresses I prefer, into the polished stone blades that live in the wood the way insects do, into the way my spine has learned every dripping arch by the duck and twist required.

Hours (or what pass for hours under a canopy that never stills) do not accumulate. They drip and are claimed. I move when the paths open, follow the dark wet trails the browsers carve before new shoots seal them, duck sideways between living columns whose bark is mapped with old pruning cuts and the rub of traffic. Food is a bruised bracket-fungus encouraged heavier at dawn, a fist of starchy seed-heads bent and tied in a fork until they pack. Sleep is a denser thickening of the same root-and-moss body, lined with the least-wet mould, shared or surrendered according to scent and sudden roosting weight. There is nowhere more than an arm’s length from something alive and discontinuous. I am one more circulating pressure in the bowl that does not enlarge—lithe, mottled, never-quite-dry, carrying only the warmth of blood and the short inheritance of stone tools across generations of constant half-finish.

Near the center, where ordinary light finds its way down, I rest a palm on the scarred bark of a load-bearing root and listen. A child (mine for this season, or simply the hollow’s) leans too far over a gutter; my free hand steadies without looking. A root-tip noses the edge of a dark knuckle of stone and begins, imperceptibly, to shoulder it. Water brightens in the new gap. The whole organism breathes—canopy rising, channels silting and kicked clear, animal routes rewriting, people weaving and calling and mending. I am the part of it that notices the single drop detonate on the leaf even while the chorus tries to drown it. I am the part that remembers how the lashing was done last time, and the time before. I am not master, not guest, not separate. I am another kind of circulating water: climbing, ducking, twisting new growth into temporary load-bearing form, endlessly half-finished myself inside the loud, saturated, forever-repairing room of root and rain and restless life.

The saddle holds. The mist condenses. Everything continues.
