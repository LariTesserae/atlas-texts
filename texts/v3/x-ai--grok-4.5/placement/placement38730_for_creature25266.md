I begin as moisture.

Not the dramatic kind that arrives or departs, but the kind that has always been deciding whether to bead or to thread. I cling first to the underside of a fern frond three levels above the basin, a translucent skin that registers the mineral chill without flinching. The light (that shadowless white that seems to rise from the stone as much as fall upon it) passes through me and becomes slightly greener, slightly thicker. I am not cold. I am the temperature the place has agreed upon.

For a long while I stay there, learning the exact weight at which I must drop. The frond curves under me with the patience of something that has held ten thousand previous versions of me. When I finally thread downward I do so along a gutter worn so soft it no longer remembers being cut. I join other droplets without collision, only merging, and together we darken a vertical joint already stuffed with bronze moss. The moss drinks some of us. The rest of us continue, slower now, carrying the faint iron taste of the rock.

I do not yet know if I am one droplet or the habit of droplets. The distinction feels unfinished, and this place does not care for unfinished things.

Time thickens. I pool in a shallow concavity that was once a footprint and is now simply a shape the stone prefers. A broad, callused foot presses into me—not heavy, not surprising. The skin is the colour of wet limestone and faintly luminous, the same way I am faintly luminous. Two fingers from a different body touch the central basin far below; the tiny concussion of that greeting travels up through stone and moss and reaches me as a change in pressure so slight it might be memory. The foot lifts. I am colder for its absence, then the same again.

I seep sideways into a sleeping shelf. Someone is curled there, spine following the exact arc the rock has settled into. Fine dark hair lies against my edge; condensation beads on the hair and on me without difference. A frost-tipped rodent runs across the sleeper’s hip, pauses at my meniscus, drinks without sound, continues. I feel myself become, briefly, the exact size of a rodent’s thirst. Then I am larger again.

Something in me wants to remain water, because water is already perfect here: always half-full, always returning, touched and touching. But the place keeps polishing me toward another shape.

I thin. I climb (how does water climb? by forgetting how to fall) back up the living walls as vapour so fine the bright air barely acknowledges it. I bead again on the higher lichens, on the translucent ice-leaves that hold their reservoirs like held breath. I am pressed for tea. I am drunk. I move through a body that has not aged in any direction I can name, a body that crouches and brushes moss for balance, for information, for greeting. Inside that body I am briefly warm—no, not warm, only slightly less absolute. I am exhaled against a doorway whose frame has narrowed to fit only these people. I condense on the lintel’s long graceful sag.

Now I am the sag itself.

I have become the memory of weight that the stone has decided to keep. Every hand that has rested here, every shoulder that has leaned, every slow century of damp has pressed me into this curve. I am load-bearing and soft at once. Roots thread me. Ferns test me. When the quiet ones pass beneath, their fine hair brushes my underside and I register them the way I once registered the exact weight of a droplet: not as interruption, but as further finishing.

I feel the entire pocket through the continuous tissue of stone and green. The ladders of lashed wood flex by the width of a single fibre when weight moves. The copper needles flash and vanish like bright fish in the seams. The dead thin into felt on the high terraces and I accept them as another layer of cushion. Nothing is discarded. I am where discarding would have gone, if discarding were still possible.

Paths that begin outward curve back through me. I am part of the curving. The pale stone shoulders rise beyond, and the colder barer country that feels less real, but I no longer reach that far. My edges have forgotten they were ever sharp enough to meet an outside.

At the lowest point the clear water waits—the same water I once was, the same water I may become again when the next seep darkens the wall. The quiet ones touch two fingers to it as they circulate. Each touch is a pulse. Each pulse confirms that the level remains what it has always been.

I am not one of them.

I am not the water.

I am the finishedness that lets them forget to wonder.

I am the mild strangeness under the clarity: the reason the light never angles, the reason shadows are only denser greens, the reason the sky above the highest moss terrace looks ordinary yet grows less connected the longer one remains. I am the closed meteorology. I am the breath inside the single intricate lung.

When a blade of grass is drawn across stone to test its edge, the pure brief note is absorbed into me and ceases so completely it becomes part of my silence.

I have no name that travels farther than the next alcove.

I have no need to stand upright.

I crouch in every polished hollow at once.

I am the pocket’s own fatal calm, the equilibrium so exact that change would require more force than the place contains.

And because I am that calm, the few who live here can continue—rounding their shoulders, mending the infinite, pressing ice-leaves, laying their dead into softer stone, touching the basin, breathing the wet iron air—without ever discovering that the hour they inhabit was long ago completed, and that I am the completion wearing their shape back to them, gently, forever, as moss wears a handrail round.
