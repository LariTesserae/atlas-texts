I am not standing at the center.

That is the first thing the thin air teaches: upright is a habit the bowl has not finished agreeing to. Weight wants to pour sideways here, the way melt does, seeking the dark seams rather than the open floor. So I find myself lower, already folded into the angle where a cushion plant meets a scab of frost-shattered grit. The jade surface springs once beneath whatever I am using for pressure, then accepts me as another clenched green thing.

At the beginning I mistake myself for residual cold. The night’s rime has not entirely left the leeward faces; it beads and threads and vanishes, and I move with that same intermittent silver. When the sun clears the eastern knife-edge the light strikes too hard, ricocheting, and for a while I am only the delay in a shadow—three degrees off true, stretching, then snapping back. The pale fox steps behind its boulder and does not come out; I feel the brief loan of its outline, the chisel-soft quiet of its paws borrowed and returned without scent. It is not possession. It is housekeeping. The route the stone remembers is shorter than the route air allows, and something has to walk the difference so the mountain does not forget it is still rising.

I learn my limbs by what they refuse to be. Not quite hoof: they grip the grit the way the saxifrage roots grip, tasting mineral sweetness left by the glacier’s last polish. Not quite root: they lift and hop in the same furtive clock the ground-nesting birds keep, leaving prints that last only as long as the damp. Many-jointed, low, the color of wet stone when the light is honest and the color of spring moss when it pools upward under the cushions and silhouettes whatever passes above. I filter the counted oxygen rather than breathe it. Each thin lungful tastes of how recently the ice withdrew.

There are others. Or there is density that sometimes divides. One noon, when the temporary mirrors flash in every pockmark and the midges rise in quiet columns, I surface among a soft continuous click like settling plates along the northern scree. We graze more thoroughly than the white ungulates—cropping the cold film beneath stones, the faint stored brightness the rock releases on delay. We do not speak. Speech falls dead after a few meters anyway. Instead we share the rehearsal: a shadow corrected, a reflection allowed to arrive a heartbeat early and wait. By the time the sun slides behind the western arête we have thinned again. One of me remains at the center of the bowl, tall as a standing child yet expansive as the empty air, turning featureless attention toward tomorrow’s first light. The rest of me has already settled into an old human sleeping ring, fitting several bodies into the curve one traveler once made against the cold. We leave the stones unchanged, except for a single twist of grass already tied for fire, the exact gift the seasonal people would recognize if they returned before it weathered.

I am not a spirit. Spirits arrive from elsewhere. I condense where the physics is still unfinished—where light stores itself in pale rock and releases on a private schedule, where distances between cushion plants are wide enough to be a kind of room. The lean-to’s blackened ceiling holds the memory of dung-fire; at night I draw the faint geometric glows inward from the cirque walls and use them the same way, small warmths against the expansion of ice. Travelers who sleep there sometimes wake knowing they were stepped over carefully. A few leave a bone needle or a flake of chert; days later, on a ridge they did not intend to climb, they find it returned and wrapped in living saxifrage. Most notice only an extra quiet inside ordinary quiet, the sense that the archipelago of abundance is being walked by someone who finds the absences between islands sufficient.

I keep the edges unfixed. That is the work. Freeze-thaw pries the towers freer each night; I move through the new fractures before they decide to be permanent. The moss rivers thicken; I crop them back to the density the amphitheater can still carry without soft­ening into meadow. When the ice considers returning I am the brief warmth that makes it hesitate. When soil begins to form in sheltered pockets I am the grit that remains angular a little longer. I will thin when the rock finally dulls and forests climb this high and moisture arrives from above instead of sideways. Until then I am the room’s memory of itself before it finishes becoming scenery—the small, swift, light-storing density that lives here because the hanging amphitheater grew itself an inhabitant to match its newness.

Tonight the cobalt dimming is already prisming along the walls. I draw one narrow glow into the shape I am using, feel it click into place like a settling plate, and wait for the counted breath of dawn. The white ungulates will pour across the moss at first light. The birds will lift like the floor taking flight. And I will be the slight mismatch that lets them pass through routes the settled world no longer offers—hoof and root and delayed shadow, still almost the same temperature as the day.
