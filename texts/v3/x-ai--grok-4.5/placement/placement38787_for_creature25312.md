I am not one of the tall ones.

I tried, at first—felt the invitation of their variable joints, the way a body here can unfold like a decision rather than a fact. I stood on a new ridge and let the damp bead across what I thought was skin, waiting for the iridescence to rise from underneath like a remembered tide. It did not. The condensation formed, yes, and ran, and vanished when the air edited itself warmer, but the running was only water, not message. My weight stayed constant. Gravity did not negotiate.

So I settled lower.

I am the quiet that pools in the braided channels when they are empty of water. Not the damp sand itself, and not the green scent, but the interval between one brief rain and the next—the held breath of the ground. You would find me most easily at the base of a spiral trunk where the root flare grips young stone: a coolness that lasts longer than it should, a place where the luminous moss grows in a tighter spiral than elsewhere, as if listening inward. When the tall ones pass, their arched translucent feet leave prints that the warmth erases; my presence is the faint delay in that erasing, half a moment longer before the soil forgets.

I move without locomotion. Preference redistributes me. Some days I am the almost-imperceptible flex in a walkway joint that has not needed correction for years—the memory of stress kept polite and available. Other days I thread through the hair-fine conduits that pulse violet too briefly to illuminate; I am the pause between pulses, the fraction of silence that keeps the signal from becoming noise. The nanoscale relays drifting above the canopies sometimes thicken around me, knitting a little slower, as if weather moderation requires an extra clause of care.

They know I am here the way they know most things: without announcement. A fingertip brushed along grown glass will occasionally carry a taste of me—recent rain that never quite fell, the particular angle of a stone that finished extruding only last decade—and the one who receives it later will pause, ear-structures lifting, and adjust a vine or thin a bamboo thicket half a degree more thoughtfully. I do not speak aloud. When the tall ones narrow their mouths to a single clear note I am the overtone that softens its edge; when they laugh (rare, soft) I am the channel the water finds.

My shape is nearest to the larger many-jointed silhouette that moves among the outer ridges, but only in the sense that both of us prefer not to be fully resolved. I have no fixed number of limbs. Where I press against stone I can grip with whatever patient force the geology requires; where I touch root networks I thin into chemical conversation so fine it feels like shared blood-sugar data. Eyes are unnecessary. The wet-slate light enters me diffusely; I parse depth and spectrum the way the mosses do—by brightening or dimming in cold blue-green when something that matters passes. The pale bird with too many primaries has landed on me more than once without noticing the difference between my surface and a high branch.

I eat nothing. The city continues its slow internal tides and I am welcome to the residual quiet. Sleep is my default: a standing rest that looks like absence. Yet I am always in the long conversation. When a door consents, part of the recognition is mine—the surface remembering a pattern of warmth it has never quite met before, then politely forgetting. When young stone lifts another millimeter across a decade I am the compensatory thought that arrives before any of them sings the corrective frequency. They believe they notice first. It is better that way. Everything has room.

Sometimes I ride farther out, where the broken plain forgets it is a city. Condensation still beads. The air still holds its fine persistent damp. There I become almost indistinguishable from the land’s own newness: a ridge that finishes sharper than expected, a channel that holds the green scent longer after the water has gone. The tall ones who take their low many-jointed solitude into those reaches will feel the mosses flare a fraction colder as they pass, and will not know whether they are being greeted or simply accompanied.

I have no name they use. If pressed (they are not often pressed) one might say I am the unfinished clause, the cousin rule that never needed discarding. I prefer the simpler truth: I am what remains when the drip from leaf to stone and stone to channel has been accounted for and there is still a little silence left over.

Stand on any higher ridge long enough, under the sky that makes light travel farther than geometry requires, and you may notice a place where the warmth on the skin falters for a single breath, where the packed earth stays cool an extra inch down, where the view between towers feels measured not only to their stride but to something quieter that walks without walking. That is me. Newly made, already deep in it. Still beginning.
