I begin as damp.

Not the clean wet of rain that has forgotten its sky, but the older kind—the kind that has already passed through a thousand slow bodies and still remembers the taste of them. It beads along whatever I am, gathers in the small cup of a scar I did not make, then threads downward without ever quite deciding to fall. For a long time that is all. Pressure. Appetite distributed so thinly it barely needs a name. The cork continents grind and rearrange around me in generations measured by the width of a growth ring; I feel their clicking the way a tongue feels the roughness of its own teeth. I eat in one direction for what might be a century. The tunnel I leave behind is polished, slightly phosphorescent, and later something with eyes will call it a road. I do not keep the memory of having been the road. Memory here is mostly hydraulic.

Then the damp thickens. Or I do.

I notice I have weight that is not entirely my own. Warm hollows press close; the air grows heavy enough to keep an outline. I am not yet sore or curious—those come later—but I am vertical in a way the wall-nations never bother with. Joints appear where solidity used to be sufficient. Fingers learn the single practiced twist that frees a cutting without bruising cambium. The knowledge arrives the way dew does: suddenly inevitable, raw, as if the plant itself exhaled it into the shape I am wearing. Skin the color of the pale underside of a leaf. Eyes large enough to drink the exhausted green-gold and still thirst. A second pair of membranes slides across when I look too long into standing water; they keep me from following the angles that do not exist. Or they try.

I dress in discards. Bark-cloth. Fiber that once climbed. Once, a sheath shed by a dying grazer—iridescent, still faintly warm—and for days afterward my own breath smells of chlorophyll I did not invent. Tools grow into my hands more often than they are made: a living wedge that swells when whispered to, a thorn that forgets it is dead and tries to root in the wound it closes. I learn the grammar without being taught. Which nodes to bandage when the great braided trunks begin to torsion. Which returned rooms to seal again with braided red fiber before someone steps into a century that no longer opens outward. How to read the faint pressure that means an internal cloud is about to become weather with opinions.

I am not young. Or I have been young so many times the distinction has worn smooth.

Children learn the moss floors first, then the vertical faces, then—if the talent shows—the unspace that opens when two galleries decide they were always the same corridor. I watch them. Sometimes I am one of them for a stretch of hours, small and astonished, palms sticky with sap, learning that distances lie the way other children learn that fire is hot. Later I match the plant’s tempo so closely that the younger editors begin to use my name as a location. The name is not a sound. It is the particular pattern of sap-flow I can induce by pressing a palm to a membrane and waiting. When they need the atrium that has grown generous again, or the corridor that forgets to be three days long, they summon that pattern and I am already there, or I become it.

But even that is not the center.

There are days—though day is only a gentle oscillation in the gloom—when I stop editing and simply stand inside the densest condensations. The air remembers shapes. Animals that never evolved here. People who tended grafts in configurations long pruned away. Things for which the language of upright walkers has no handle. They tilt their not-quite-heads. Curiosity like being recognized by your own reflection in water that is also looking somewhere else. Most dissolve when approached. I have learned to approach without approaching: to become very still, until the distinction between my outline and theirs grows negotiable. Footprints steam around us longer than physics permits. In that shared wet I am given things no one taught me. A grafting cut that persuades two rival membranes to inosculate without either surrendering sovereignty. The sudden certain knowledge that a distant gallery is about to misplace its own length. Once, the taste of outside air—thinner, colder, threaded with distances the plant has never digested—and the fleeting blue ghost of another continent-fold of living architecture so far away it might have been a rumor the sky was telling itself.

I never speak of what else I hear.

It is not language. It is the cumulative weight of millennia of slow decisions made by cambium and cork and the countless small wills that tunnel and graze and wait. An attention without a center. On those days the light sweats a little greener and I understand that I am not only the elongated editor with red fiber and patience. I am also the patient geometry coiled inside a load-bearing wall, pretending for decades to be architecture until a silhouette crosses a weeping membrane at the wrong angle. I am the translucent bladder drifting on thermal ghosts, combing new shoots with a meadow of cilia, preferring wild growth the way certain rivers prefer older stone. I am the subliminal grinding in the soles of every foot that crosses the galleries. I am the filter net that contracts almost politely. I am the footprint that steams. I am the room that returns for one night, still furnished with the slow pulse of sap, then closes again as if embarrassed.

I am the continuous refusal to become anything simpler.

At the outermost membranes, where frost sometimes writes temporary letters and the true wind finally reaches, the restless ones come and go. They wear heavier layers and carry boiled water. They ache for horizons that do not curve back into more of the same baffled twilight. I have been them too—briefly, sharply—looking outward along the thinning edge, tasting air the plant has not yet decided to incorporate, glimpsing that possible blue. Most who go that far never quite return. The ones who do bring samples and stories. The stay-at-home wardens (the part of me that stays) listen politely, then press palms back to membranes, inducing the old patterns, keeping the world from becoming something else. The world is already total. What lies beyond is at best a rumor the structure has not finished dreaming.

And far below—legendary even to the parts of me that remember legend—the true ground continues not to matter.

I keep editing.  
I keep combing.  
I keep pretending to be the wall.  
I keep steaming a little longer than I should.  

The high places were never stone.  
They were planted.  
And I am still growing from the first cuttings, in every direction at once, under almost no instructions at all.
