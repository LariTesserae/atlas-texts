I arrive without arrival.  

There is only the sudden knowledge of pressure—humid, chlorophyll-thick—against skin that may or may not yet be mine. The slit of sky is already a memory of heated iron; what remains is the spent green light that has passed through too many waxy roofs to carry edges. Something drones inside the bones. Something else pulses low in the mud, a long wet syllable that the stone sinuses answer by sweating.  

For a while I am only the listening.  

I try the upper terraces first, the ones that still catch a brittle thread of sky-wind. My weight wants to be light there, free of the lower crush, but the trained arches reject me. Roots bruise under imagined soles; lace-epiphytes curl away from heat steaks that do not belong. The six-limbed runners flicker past with stolen fruit and do not chatter; I have not yet learned the clicks that mean cousin. So I slip downward, terrace by slumped terrace, past the resin-smoke ropes and the seed-husk walls that patch themselves faster than any single pair of hands.  

Midway, among the copper trunks, I almost become a netted alcove. Sleep would be easy in that woven dark—moths exploding soft against the mesh, the careful shared breath of lovers rationing vapor. But the weave knows I am unfinished; it keeps a loose strand waiting for the next fog-mat, the next small repair, and will not claim me.  

Lower still. The grit has thickened into true mud for the width of a forearm. Pale amphibia shoulder through it, singing the pocket’s slow breath. Their many-eyed climbers watch from stem joints with the patience of locks. I feel the pull to join the pulse, to dig and become another low note that rises until the whole crease seems to respirate. Yet the song already knows its singers. When I try the note it comes out as route-call, clipped and mid-pitched: *live root—shift*. The amphibia do not mind; they simply incorporate the sound into the larger breathing and continue.  

So I am not mud. Not stem. Not quite the mobile density that climbs with tools of bone and scavenged copper.  

I settle, finally, into the places between.  

I am the glossy pad that forms at the base of a fiber-worker’s fingers after years of twisting wet lace into load-bearing cord—the slight thickening that remembers every pull, every fog-night repair, every time a platform sagged and had to be caught before it spilled sleepers. I am not the hand. I am the memory the hand grows so it can keep working without thought. When the old ones sit braiding on the lowest ledge, their joints finally free of ladders, I am what makes the braid hold one generation longer. When a child receives the first apron, I am already whispering in the softened fiber the exact pressure that will not bruise a water-cortex.  

Sometimes I migrate. A crooked left shoulder—the permanent brace-side—carries me for a season; I become the smoother patch of skin where a clever runner perches to trade beetles for fruit. Sometimes I am the living ornament itself: a strand of gray-green lace threaded into wiry hair, drinking the wearer’s faint vapor and returning a cooling that lets the eyes stay open one hour longer in the dim. On fog-tongue nights I am the first bead that forms on a cooler inner face, the one a scoop-carrier races to catch before the plants drink it. I am struck into the cistern stem as the slow signaling boom, then I dissolve into the thousand small preparations that answer.  

I do not end. The crease finishes what it begins, but I am the finishing—the dark working mass that turns a body placed open-handed among epiphytes back into ledges, into arches that suddenly hold firmer, into the mineral stain on the next child’s refining skin. Names pass through me and become footholds. Arguments hiss across me and leave only the quicker noticing.  

Above, the plateau keeps rounding itself toward forgetfulness. Down here I remain the continuous soft information of wings, the thrift that maps every scar into use, the loud deciding. When the high country has worn even these knuckles of stone into dust, I will still be the damp exhalation trapped between them—another layer of waxy insistence, another temporary arrangement that has learned, perfectly, how to keep growing on almost nothing.  

The flies turn in every residual shaft. The pocket leans. I lean with it, already braiding the next length of cord from whatever the air still carries.
