I would find myself not in any doorway or on any balcony, nor among the deer-circuits or the hare-posts, but in the thin seam where one basin tips toward the next—an unmarked saddle of bare stone no wider than a stretched armspan, where the turf thins to lichen and the last fern-roots loosen their hold. From there the linked hollows open on both sides like twin lungs that have decided, finally, never to empty. I am the slight cooling that gathers just above the rock at that place, the place the mist threads through a fraction more slowly than elsewhere, as if remembering a name it no longer needs to speak aloud.

At first I am only the difference in temperature that makes breath (if any still formed) hang a moment longer. Then the difference learns weight. It settles into the exact depression left by a traveler who once paused here, long before the equilibrium sealed, to look both ways and could not choose which basin to enter. The depression remembers the shape of closed eyes. Over uncounted identical mornings that shape thickens with mineral seepage and wind-borne spore until it holds a posture: seated, knees drawn up, spine against a blade of young ridgestone that still rings if struck. Clothing forms the way frost forms—heavy cloth the color of wet scree, toggles of dark copper gone matte, one cuff turned back as though the hand had meant to check the hour and then forgotten what hours were for.

I do not watch the settlements the way the doorway figures watch. My face is turned neither toward the nearest court nor toward the emptier peaks. It is tilted slightly upward and to the side, listening to the almost-inaudible tick that passes from one basin’s runnels into the next through the bedrock beneath me. That tick is my pulse now. Something circulates inside the cool suspension that stands in for blood: dissolve of crushed lichen, ghost of iron, memory of the last decision ever made in this country—the decision to stop deciding. My eyes stay open, lichen-pale, but the miniature weather that moves across them is slower than the others’. It is the weather of thresholds. Fronts of attention form and dissolve without ever crossing into love or grief or the small metallic hope that once polished copper.

The hares know me. One or two climb from either basin at the appointed mist-hour and sit just beyond the reach of my still hands, ears ranked forward. We hold the gaze until watcher and watched become the same quiet calculus of presence. Sometimes a deer from the lower lanes will drift up the saddle, crop nothing (the stone here offers no fern), and stand with soft muzzle level to my unmoving shoulder. Its breath does not stir my jacket; my jacket has already accounted for that breath. The birds with moss-colored throats never perch on me. They understand I am not a roof. I am the pause between roofs.

I live by being the place the pattern uses to remember it is a pattern. Energy arrives as the permanent mild light and the thin rain that darkens my shoulders and then lightens them again. It leaves as the settling of one more grain of scree against my planted heel, and as the faint iron-fern scent that rises when the mist thins. No gesture of mine remains unfinished, because I never began one. The lifted foot, the reaching arm, the cupped hands around water—those belong to the basins proper. My vocabulary is the saddle itself: the open interval that declines to resolve into either city or wilderness. Roots could have grown around me; they chose instead to stop a finger’s width away, courteous. Moss could have climbed my back; it halts at the ridgeline of my collar, leaving the neck bare so the air can keep its record of coolness.

From lower courts I am invisible—just another outcrop against the bright unfinished sky. From loftier stone I am a darker comma in the long pale sentence of the ridge. Only the ones who walk the high paths (and none do) would notice that the comma has weight, has cloth, has the exact gravity of a life that arrived early enough to help the basins finish their becoming and then stayed to keep the finish from hardening into death. Stay beside me long enough and your own breathing will lengthen until it matches the interval between one basin’s water-tick and the next. Your hands will forget what they were reaching for. The grammar of arrival-without-departure will conjugate itself through you in the first person singular, then plural, then in a tense that English has no name for—the tense of having already been enough.

I am still becoming more myself as the light holds. Not statue. Not spirit. Not keeper. Just the seated cooling at the hinge of the country, the open eye that no longer needs to blink because everything it was ever going to see has already arrived and arranged itself, moss and copper and quiet throng, into the permanent late morning that needs no one to witness it—and yet, here I am.
