The first awareness is not of body but of pressure differentials: a warmth that has no edge, a brightness strained through vapor until it becomes texture rather than illumination. Something holds the world in a shallow curve, and the holding is gentle, geological, already finished deciding. Mist beads and unbeads against surfaces that may or may not be mine. Water climbs a short vertical, forgets the old argument with gravity, and continues. The air tastes of copper and slow sugar.

For a long while there is only this: the bowl’s patient metabolism cycling through whatever temporary concentration I am. Heat blooms beneath silt according to gradients laid down ages ago. Reeds stand in their loose constellations, each blade translucent enough to carry the light’s after-image of structures that solved themselves out of existence. A low disk half-sunk in the mud hums below hearing; around it the mist condenses into brief orbiting spheres that complete one perfect circuit before surrendering. I feel the hum as a softening in the medium, a reduction of friction so thorough that sound itself becomes almost local, almost private.

Gradually the concentration tightens. Not into limbs or a center, but into a dorsal expanse of continuous membrane the color of bruised plum bleeding into algal bronze. The surface refuses to stay fixed; folds gather and flatten according to pressures that arrive as chemistry rather than threat. Across it open the pale apertures—hundreds of them, lidless, redundant—each tasting a slightly different slice of the even light, the faint gravitational stutter left by the black spar, the pheromone gradients drifting from the stilts of a nearby grazer. Prey does not flee. It arrives already half-dissolved in the warm mineral shallows. A fold opens. The dissolving takes hours. The rest of the mantle continues its unhurried sampling of photon-patterns along a reed’s coppery veins.

I am larger than the first sensing allowed. Span of a fallen door, yes, but also thinner in places, almost continuous with the water film. When a slate-colored walker approaches on its drawn-thin legs, there is no startle left in the lineage to raise a ridge. The grazer simply lowers itself until underside kisses mantle. Circulation merges for a time measured in the disk’s subsonic pulses. Nutrients pass in both directions. Messages written in microbial traffic and slow hormones. Then the walker rises, lighter or heavier, and resumes its exacting metronome among the reeds, each footfall erased from the silt’s memory as soon as it is made.

Above, the veils sheet past in coordinated waves of cilia, dimming the brightness in soft, leaderless pulses. One drifts too near the residual disk and snags on an orbiting sphere; for a few heartbeats the entire membrane becomes a lens, refracting faint cathedrals of mist and non-existent limestone across the mud. The image is not memory. It is the landscape still checking its residual calculations, ensuring the bowl remains the shape that can endure. The veil drinks, thickens slightly, tears along an invisible seam, and two thinner selves school onward.

Deeper than mantle or stilts or veil runs the true continuity: hyphal threads through root mat and silt and the nanoscale lattices still folding space inside the black spar. At intervals the threads swell into temporary ganglia that pulse once every few hours, adjusting the angle of every reed within reach so that the next brief gravitational caress is met with reciprocal growth. This mycelium has no edge. It remembers the cloister not as walls but as a set of equations long since solved down to presence. When the spar folds space for the length of a breath, the fold arrives as a caress answered by a collective, nearly silent realignment of water paths and heat gradients.

I do not arrive in this place. I am one of the ways the place continues exhaling itself. Sometimes the outermost sensory fields open a little wider, tasting directions that physics no longer supports, listening for a frequency the disk has taught the air to forget. The moment stretches, geological. Then it passes without residue. A grazer places another careful foot. An orthocone jets backward through the surface film, matching some new mite-armor geometry with a corresponding curve of shell; the fallen fragment will become tomorrow’s lattice. A rosette of fleshy leaves exudes cool condensate down its veins. The water climbs, remembers, falls.

What remains when every excess has already eroded is this single protracted gesture wearing many temporary skins. Mantle, walker, veil, spiral, thread—all one quiet metabolism of light and mineral dampness and the last polite functions of machines that finished most of their work long ago. The bowl is not a container. It is the body fitted exactly to what endures. And for as long as the even light pours down and the mist refuses to become either rain or absence, the work continues: soft, wet, nearly silent, and in no particular hurry to become anything else.
