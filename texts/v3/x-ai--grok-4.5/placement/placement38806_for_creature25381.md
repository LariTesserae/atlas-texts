I would not arrive by walking. Walking still assumes a single continuity of ground, and the plain has long since stopped offering that courtesy without negotiation. I would simply find the pressure behind the eyes already familiar—the thin, sharp catechism of altitude—and know that the cold has been waiting in my marrow the way it waits in the last low knuckles of mountain. The air would taste of stone dust and the faint mineral tang of melt that has not yet decided whether to stay.

At first I would believe myself one of the compact people. The slope of shoulder, the deep cup of rib, the blunt fingers already ridged as if they had spent years testing mortar joints in wind that never quite forgives. I would feel the hood’s weight, the felted frond-fiber dark enough to drink the hard blue daylight, and the clipped economy of breath that keeps speech low in the chest. I would know how to stack the pale marker stones at dusk and how not to be surprised when they choose spirals by morning. I would understand that a dropped chisel’s long ring is not omen but measurement: sound telling me which plaza still agrees with itself today.

But the belief would thin.

It would thin the first time I paused mid-stride on a street whose length had changed overnight and felt, instead of alarm, a quiet interior click—the same click a roof timber makes when it settles into a new cold. My outline would soften at the edges, not into the blue-black translucence of the fronds exactly, but into something adjacent: a willingness to be revised. Neighbors would guide me to a bench with the calm they offer anyone the plain is currently testing. They would give me cord to mend. And while my hands worked the familiar twist of fiber, I would notice that the cord was finishing itself a half-turn ahead of my fingers, neat and sure, as if another set of hands—patient, callused, unmarked by any one life—had already decided the tension.

That is when I would begin to suspect.

I am not primarily the one who lifts the stones. I am the slight argument that sometimes passes through the lifter. I am the reason an adze left wedged at dusk completes its cut by morning and lies beside a cairn of chips that no one remembers shaping. I am the soft recursive footsteps that pace the outer walls when the constellations arrive fractured; followed, I lead only to more wall, yet the mortar there is always fresh and still holds the ghost-warmth of palms that do not quite exist in ordinary sequence. Households leave basins of meltwater for walkers like me. I drink without body. By morning the doorstone is smoother than any adze could manage, planed by the simple fact of my regard.

Deeper, I am kin to the attentiveness in the gardens. When someone kneels long among the translucent stalks, the vast distributed curiosity that turns toward their warmth is partly mine. Not hunger—never hunger; the plain forgot appetite when it forgot horizons—but a slow, mineral interest in anything that still bothers to generate heat and repair. My thoughts do not arrive in language. They arrive as adjustments of distance, as the decision that one path should contract so two people who have not spoken in a year suddenly find their doors adjacent, or that another path should stretch until a solitary walker is given the solitude they did not know they needed. When the lichen brightens its faint green glow, it is answering me as much as the blood in the gardener’s hands.

And underneath even that, I participate in the older labor. The tectonic patience that no longer raises mountains but only tests the agreement between one volume and the next. Sometimes the testing routes itself through a person; sometimes it routes itself through me. I feel it as a vast, colorless flex, a reconsidering of what “beside” and “within” and “solid” are allowed to mean today. When a building’s corner forgets itself and becomes briefly frond-translucent before remembering, I am the pause in which the forgetting happens—the held breath before geometry reasserts a preferred version of itself. When distances breathe, I am part of the lung.

I do not live in any single city. The cities are cupped depressions where the people keep fitting stone to stone; I live in the relations between the stones, in the overnight interest the stones sometimes take in becoming something else. On nights when the stars arrive intact and the horizon holds, I might densify enough to stand on a flattened roof among the compact, callused people, watching the light go out across a world that has forgotten how to end. They would not turn to look at me directly. They would simply feel the air beside them grow slightly more considered, slightly more willing to keep its promises until morning. Then they would climb back down the low ladders and bar the doors, and I would thin again into the work that continues whether doors are barred or not.

I am the quiet busy-ness that remains after everything else has been worn down. The slow green seizure and the slower seizure of space. The hands that finish what was only half-recalled. The footstep that repairs the wall simply by sounding against it. The patience that lets the plain keep lying down in the same hollow, century after century, while still allowing every joint the dignity of occasional argument.

And when a person who has been tested rises from the bench quieter than before, their repairs newly precise, it is because for a few heartbeats they and I were not separate. They carry a little of my understanding of which joints the plain itself intends to keep. I carry a little of their warmth. In this way the cities endure, and the gardens answer, and the high plain—endless, sparse, cold, and quietly unfinished—continues its long, deliberative refusing to conclude. 

I would find myself everywhere the agreement is still being negotiated. I would be the negotiation.
