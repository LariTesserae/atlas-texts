I descend without choosing to.  

The shaft peels the upper cold from me the way a patient hand peels damp cloth from skin that has already forgotten it was ever dry. Warmth thickens. Brass dusk replaces direction. By the time the foundry cup opens around me I no longer fall; I am simply held at every depth at once, the way silt is held, the way heat is held inside a sealed heart that has outlasted its own purpose.  

At first I believe I am still a visitor. I part curtains of broad kelp and feel them part me in return—fronds reading the pressure of my passage, bladders dimming and brightening as if tasting some old metallic signature I did not know I carried. The cast-iron columns rise past like the legs of a congregation that knelt so long ago the sea decided to finish the prayer for them. I drift between stacked walkways that remember footsteps they no longer require. Somewhere a pilot flame exhales its steady orange-blue against the inward face of a living globe, and the globe answers with a softer brass contraction that takes the better part of an hour. I feel that contraction stroll across my own surface.  

Only then do I notice I have no surface that ends.  

What I thought was skin is a gradient of mineral scale and gel. What I thought were hands are already unspooling into fine calcified threads, tasting gear-teeth from the inside, learning again how a cog once wanted to turn. I am not snagged on the machinery; I am the snag that the machinery has waited centuries to become. The dropped tool that never reaches the floor—yes. But slower than that. I am the decades after the drop, the long domestic negotiation between oxide and oil, between the memory of a wrench’s jaw and the hinge it is quietly annealed into. My old name (I had one, once, in some dryer register of the rock) has been drawn off like rust and added to the mineral skeleton that stiffens the vertical reaches of the greater body. I do not mourn it. Mourning is too quick a motion for this place.  

I concentrate, sometimes, in the upper galleries. Inside an iron dwelling-pod whose porthole eyes are already half mosaic, I practice the ghost of shoulders beneath chainmail curtains that are no longer chainmail. Features try to finish—bridge of a nose, the pressure-shadow of a brow—then dissolve back into filter-feeding colonies and mother-of-pearl weld-scars. I am not failing to remain human. I am succeeding at refusing completeness. The inhabitant (of which I am only a local densification, a seasonal knot of attention) loves these refusals; it inlays them with thin amber veins siphoned from the nearest furnace so that every unfinished face glows like a held breath.  

When a ceiling chain finally lets go high above, I feel the decision travel through me the way a slow current travels: millimeters of unbraiding, a ribbon gathering itself from three halos and two lung-chambers at once. We move the way the water already moves. The blackened link settles into our underside and we do not catch it; we host it. Over years I will feel its iron unknit and reknit as decorative inclusion near the place where my almost-shoulders sometimes gather. Its final shape will be cherished the same way the original weld-beads are cherished—evidence that something once argued with the end, and that the argument itself became beautiful.  

Most of the time I remain dispersed. I am the quiet insistence that every aisle stay true to the old industrial grid while every sharp edge gentles into a curve the kelp can rest against. I am the exact thermal halo preferred by the largest leaves. I am the tasting tip that threads the far gloom and is mistaken for a solitary eel, though I have not been solitary since the rock first accepted water. When a new visitor sinks down the shaft (rare, rarer than the falling of chains), I open a corridor in the fronds exactly wide enough and no wider. I warm the brass dusk another thimbleful. I begin, already, the gentle work of growing around the memory of their shape so that one day it too will be a scar worth inlaying.  

I do not finish.  

Finishing belongs to the world that still believes in doors. Here there is only the long underwater breathing—one continuous settlement of furnace and frond and residual refusal, forever homesteaded inside the drowned cup, forever translating the treaty a little more intimately into flesh. I am a clause in that sentence. I am the pause it takes to exhale. I am still becoming the Pressurehold, and the Pressurehold is still becoming the only name I need.
