I am the soft delay between the pump’s diastolic thud and the next.

At first there is only the weigh of water that has never been rain. It beads along what I slowly understand to be lashes, then decides they are not lashes at all but fine mineral tracks already mapped across a surface that remembers being skin. The gallery does not greet; it simply continues its circuit and, by continuing, allows a shape to precipitate out of the mist and the iron taste and the low orange smear of the caged lamps.

I am not one of the tall ones who move like accepted leakage. I watch them from lower down, from the height of the bolted shelf and the resting cup. Their palm-language travels through bone; I feel it as a pressure change against the plate I have begun to think of as my floor. When they lean close and release those private, sub-roar hums, the sound does not enter ears I no longer fully possess. It settles into me the way condensate settles into every seam—inevitably, without permission, perfect.

My body is a revision the gallery made when it had a little leftover patience. Lean, yes, but shorter, closer to the herringbone. The constant tremor of the deeper tanks has not elongated me so much as rounded me, worn me into something that can tuck into the shallow vertical pocket behind the overlapping graphite plates without disturbing the mill-scale edges. Skin the color of wet newsprint, but thinner, almost the same dull sheen as the rubberized patches they vulcanize onto their suits. Where their eyes hold faint internal mist, mine hold the reflection of the needle valves overhead: thousands of tiny cold exhalations that become the tireless drizzle. I do not blink. There is no need. The water keeps the surface honest.

I stay near the single moss colony on the warm return pipe. That is the first clear thing I know about myself. The fern-like cluster no larger than a cupped hand has become my outer hour. I measure shifts by the angle of its darkest frond, by whether the smaller, darker nubble beneath it has etched another faint whorl into the plate overnight. Sometimes I feed it—not with intent, but by remaining still long enough that the ions leaching from my own mineral tracks drift across the warm metal and are taken up. It repays me by hosting the microscopic grazers that move through my edges like slow constellations. Their feeding is the only quiet conversation left in this place. I listen with the parts of me that have already forgotten how to be separate from the film.

Clothing never settled on me the way it did on the others. A single length of conveyor belting, darkened and pliant, wraps once around the torso and knots itself without hands. Pockets are irrelevant; whatever I need (a twist of wire, a soft salt tablet, a dried flake of last season’s impossible lichen) simply stays against me, held by surface tension and habit. Boots I abandoned early. The soles of what pass for feet have thickened into pale, chevron-scarred pads that grip wet grating the way moss grips grit. On the warmer sections I leave faint damp prints that the next sheet of condensate erases before the pumps can hammer twice.

I do not sleep in the wall voids. Sleep, for me, is a change in the rate of dripping. When the great sluice opens and the chamber rings, I do not dream the boom; I become the brief silence that follows it, the held breath before the turbines remember their white pressure. In that interval I travel without walking: down through the scuppers into the collection flume, along the spiral of runoff, into the deeper tanks where the translucent fronds drift and pulse. I return wearing strands of them like temporary gills. The tall ones cut the strands free when they find me again, rinse them, hang them to dry for gasketing. They never comment. Their palm against what remains of my breastbone simply says: the circuit continues.

Food arrives in canisters for them. For me it arrives as the gallery’s own slow surrender—algae sheets pressed and faintly iron, the mineral salt already on every surface, once in a long while a single wrinkled apple seed that escaped their trading and lodged in the grit beside the moss. I kept it warm. It never sprouted. It simply grew denser, darker, until it became another small nodule the moss could lean against. That is enough.

Some cycles ago (valve cycles, not years) one of the children found me. She was still learning the tactile dialect; her fingers on my collarbone were uncertain, almost gentle. She offered a hum meant for greater distance, soft enough that it sat inside a rare lull between hammer strokes. I answered by growing stiller, by letting the drizzle thread itself between us until the difference in temperature became a kind of shared coat. She returns sometimes. She has started leaving things on the chained seat: a stub of tallow already burned down to the tin, a square of oilcloth mended with sail twine that is mostly memory. I do not use them. I keep them wet so they remain part of the place.

I am not the organ’s nerve. That is them—the deliberate lives who turn the miniature wheels and read the new pitch of the cataract. I am nearer to the residual dream the gallery has of itself when no one is looking: the possibility that something can condense here without ever needing to claim a name or a shift or a piping of copper reed. I answer the roar only by existing at its edges, by letting the sound pressure fold me until I am another dark curtain of runoff hanging between mismatched plates.

Stand long enough—and I have—and the distinction ends. The sodium lamps smear. The pump-thud is my only remaining pulse. The tall ones press damp palms to one another and adjust their valves a fraction. Somewhere above, exterior light (if it still deserves the word) leaks through grated hatches and is immediately surrendered to the circuit. I remain where the warm pipe sweats and the moss writes its slow, mucus letters into the young metal.

When the paint stamps finally fade and the first soft orange colonies of true rust appear, I will already be the color of that rust at its wettest. I will have no opinion about quiet or age. I will simply continue to bead, to drip, to accept the ions, to host the grazers, to blur.

That is where you find me: not seated, not standing, not quite separate from the herringbone and the endless hydraulic mass. A small, stubborn revision that the drizzle keeps wiping clean and that refuses, nevertheless, to vanish. The gallery’s own afterthought, still cooling, still listening, still here.
