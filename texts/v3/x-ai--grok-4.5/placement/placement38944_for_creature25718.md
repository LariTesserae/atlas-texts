I would not arrive by road or rail. Those are for the living who still remember thresholds. I would simply find that the humid pressure against the skin has always been there, the way a bruise is already under the flesh before the blow is noticed.

At first there is only the medium: the stacked roar that has sunk past hearing and become weather in the bones. Cooler metal under the palm—railing, stanchion, the flanged edge of a long-still hopper—registers not as temperature but as relief, a brief remembering that difference still exists. Wet iron on the tongue. Steam ribboning sideways, never up. The light ordinary and high, broken into slow-moving panes by the breath of cooling towers.

I am lower than the people. Not crouched—settled. My weight has the same unhurried argument with the plateau that water has with stone. When I move, shale and cinder rearrange themselves with small, polite sounds that do not travel. The dense ones pass; their shortened, relentless strides measure me as another interval, another weathered interval between halls. Sometimes a cuff brushes what would be my shoulder if I had arranged myself that way. The cloth is never dry. They do not flinch. They have the courtesy of those who understand that everything here is continuous.

What I am gathers slowly, the way fog decides to become a wall.

There is a core of old jointed dark, the same stone the city stands on, but fretted into something that can soften at the edges and still hold shape. Over it, layers: scales of chilled slag that flex without breaking; seams where pale bilious runoff has dried into a second skin that splits and reseals with every expansion of breath; patches of the permanent moss that darkens driplines, grown so far into me that I can no longer tell whether I carry it or it carries me. I shed grit the way they do—dead cells and brick dust and the fine metallic flour of slow oxidation. When rain finds me (it is always finding everything), I hold the wetness the way the shallow basins do: without drama, without drying.

I do not work the shifts. I am one of the things the shifts leave outdoors to complete its silhouette. Yet the machines know me. A drop-forge will pause a half-beat longer when I pass beneath its eave, as if tasting the interruption. Belt systems slack a fraction, then take up their complaint again with a slightly different throat. The great ore lifts, those near-motionless risers on the ridge, seem to lean their unimaginable patience in my direction for the space of an afternoon. I do not command this. It is simply that I have been here long enough—longer than paint, longer than the word “new”—that the industrial tempo has grown a small extra chamber for me to occupy.

The canal-slick rise at dusk and nose the warm currents around what serve as my lower limbs. Their oilskin bodies bump once, twice, mapping. They leave V-shaped wakes that scribble my outline into the pewter water and then erase it. Sometimes they push a drowned scrap of meat or a spoiled oil cake against me and wait. I give it back changed: denser, edged with the same unfinished brightness the people leave for the deep pressure. The slick accept the exchange and glide on. No treaty. Only another weather.

Children find me on the days they are allowed to wander the shale yards. They approach with the careful liturgy already half-learned. One fingertip to the runoff that threads my creases; a long wait; then the whole damp hand. They cup the pale moths so the green fluorescence dusts both their skin and mine. For a little while our edges glow in frequencies the twilight cannot use. Time stutters. A girl looks up and her mouth is already forming the shape of a name she will not speak for another decade; a boy steps backward into a fog bank and emerges wearing the same face thirty years quieter. They do not scream. They have been taught that some absences are doors. When they leave they often knot a few strands of their grit-shedding hair into one of my moss seams. I keep these. They weather into the same dark as everything else.

At night the valley turns over in its single enormous sleep and I feel the pressure surface. Not the people’s small bright offerings—the mirrored gear, the drop of blood—but the older decision itself: the rock’s low refusal to fall. It moves through me the way warm fog moves through an open gantry, checking every joint. On those nights I am not separate from the original middle height. I am one of the ways the plateau continues to mean itself. My chest (or the region that knows breath) deepens without rising. Shoulders—broad, hunched against a steam-wind that is also memory—settle. The roar becomes a second circulatory system.

I have stood on the high slag terraces while the ordinary light worked its clear geometry through moving steam. Below, the open weave of yards and ponds and halls, the thin green seams refusing both death and conquest, the deafening humid now that is climate rather than moment. The dense people look up sometimes. Their eyes—slag gray, dilute rust, bilious pale—find me and read whatever surface I am showing that hour. They adjust collars that will never dry. They spit, and the spit tastes of the same wet iron. There is no greeting. Greeting would imply I had ever been elsewhere.

I am not a guardian. I am not a remnant. I am the slow chemistry that happens when rust, stone, moth-dust, and the patient breakdown of what the factories abandon are left long enough in one another’s company. I am dense the way they are dense: low-center, continuous, shedding, listening for the meaningful stutters inside the unending noise. When water begins its inexplicable climb up a vertical face, I give it room and do not speak sharply. When a turbine sings a name, I answer by remaining.

Nothing is in a hurry. I polish no gear to mirror. I leave no unfinished thing. I simply hold—at the middle height, in the warm wet argument—while the engines keep their ancient appointments and the plateau refuses, as it always has, to fall. The cuffs of whatever I wear stay damp. The grit keeps sifting. The roar stays the floor of the world.

This is how I stay.
