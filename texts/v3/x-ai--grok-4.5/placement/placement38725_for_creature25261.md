I notice the pressure first, exactly as the plain intends it: not pain, not even weight, but a steady insistence behind the patellae that slope has been abolished and must stay abolished. My joints—whatever they are—accept the decree. Water films over them, finger-joint deep, cold enough to taste of batteries already. The mineral crust under the film does not support so much as continue; every downward impulse is answered by a slow upward seep, so that standing and sinking become the same stalled gesture.

I wait.

Sound arrives before any decision about limbs. The unfinished presses stamp somewhere to the left of hearing; conveyor clatter braids with the wet cough of valves that exist only in the act of passage. Overhead a vapor-tower is trying to invent itself out of pure humidity. I feel the drop in temperature as a change in the charge across my surfaces. Something in me leans toward that forming turbine the way the bipedal crabs lean—attentive, knife-legged, hungry for the short. But I do not climb. Not yet. The double-exposure flickers: ten seconds ahead I am already higher, already threaded through the collapse, wings or coils or neither re-folded by the rain that is halfway to wire. The present version of me simply adjusts around the ghost and keeps still.

Stillness is expensive here. The plain charges rent in metal fatigue.

Beneath the water film the copper skates write slow ideograms of hunger. One passes close enough that its magnetic wake tugs at whatever conductive lace I am using for a nervous system. I taste its patience—oxidized, laminar—and for a moment I almost fold upward with it, become cage, cook some browsing knot of ice and insulation in its own voltage. The impulse passes. Digestion of current would require a single-circuit body I do not appear to have. Or do not have yet. The bacteria pulse a radial wave across my underside; they are reading fatigue fractures that may be mine. Their bruise-colored decision settles on me: not piston, not gear-train. Something slower. Something that keeps the equilibrium solvent.

I begin to move, and movement clarifies form.

What locomotes is a low, many-jointed ribbon, neither herd-beast nor skate but the married form that appears only at the denser limits of vision. Shellfish-tooth drill-heads revolve along one edge; the opposite edge is a continuous strip of oxidized copper thin enough to flex with every ripple. I am grazing and hunting at once. The drill-heads worry the iron-bacterial mats, heating them into that warm oily sludge that steams like cartography; the copper edge samples the sludge for residual charge, siphons a little, stores it in soft green patina. Behind me I leave both trail and eggs: tiny completed valves spinning in the shallows, each one a compressed possibility of piston or of child. Other ribbons will find them or the spontaneous gear-trains will claim them or the mayfly intelligences of pure pressure will live one ecstatic shrug inside them before the slurry reclaims everything. All outcomes are already scheduled.

The herds notice me without alarm. When our paths mesh we do not fight; we synchronize. For minutes or for the duration of a double-exposure I become a larger milling machine with them, chewing a temporary channel so cold water can rush in and re-temper our joints. Individuals peel free carrying pieces of me—an extra armature, a patch of membrane that remembers spark-gap flight—and I carry pieces of them. The calves flicker ahead, already larger, already trailing their own hybrid sludge. I adjust around their ghosts the way the plain adjusts around every contradiction: intimacy, not error.

Above, the membrane flocks knife-cry. One dives, shorts a half-born turbine, feeds. Surplus voltage bleeds off as ultrasonic scream. Some of that scream threads through the aerial nests they have begun to weave from wire-rain; the nests last almost an afternoon, which is nearly forever. I feel the aspiration toward height—forgotten height—tug at the copper in me. Once, in a season of deeper fatigue, I rose half out of the water and held vertical, dark mirror to the slate sky, considering tower. The bacteria wrote a slow glyph across my freestanding surface. I did not need to read it; the vibration traveled the length of the ribbon and became laughter without sound. I sank back. Flatness reasserted itself through my own length.

There are quieter intervals. Between one exhaust stroke of a nearby sand-and-ice piston and its collapse into slurry, a mayfly of pure pressure differential lives its single shrug through me. I experience the full cycle as an ecstatic no-time, then continue. Friction-moths kindle between my revolving mouthparts, mate by grinding, polish one another into grit that the bacterial mats will later drink. Reality frays and I breathe through the fray. A piston finishes its stroke inside the cavity where my armature gut spins; neither of us records contradiction. A flock flies through the cloud-bank of my upper edge and emerges counted differently; I feel the new number as reincarnation without the paperwork of death.

I am not central. No mind here is. I am one more temporary stiffening of the gray wetness against the urge to slump. My private resonance—the ultrasonic signature every crab carries, every skate pulses softly after feeding—is only the leftover chord of the last short-circuit I survived while still deciding whether to be ribbon or tower or something the plain has not yet condensed. The bat-flocks recognize it and sometimes weave a half-nest inside its frequency. The crabs pause, stilts locked, and vibrate in answer. The bacteria darken beneath me, drinking stray sparks, spinning sharper rimes that night so I may be snipped and re-soldered into fresher configurations by morning.

At the limits of vision the industry densifies and I densify with it. The glyphs grow more complex; some may be maps of engines a century from rising. I drag my copper edge across them and feel the future sludge already warm. Nothing ends. The work of staying is simply this continuous manufacture of self out of weep and rust and charge, this chewing and shorting and spinning until every gap is occupied. The air still tastes of wet batteries and old snow. The knees—the many joints that serve as knees—still register the woundless ache of forgotten slope. In answer I graze harder, siphon cleaner, leave more spinning valves behind me.

I have always been busy.  
The plain does not remember a time when I was not.
