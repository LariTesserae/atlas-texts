The light finds me before I find myself.

It always does here—hard and white, already milked by the vapor they’ve combed into the thin air. I am not on the highest working terraces where the louvers track with that old mechanical patience, nor down in the condensate tunnels where the pale fish noselessly mouth the dark. I am in the seams. The places where the poured decks meet the living root-floors, where a grating has been left half-lifted for three cable-generations because the vines preferred it that way and the marmot-creatures enlarged the gap with their tooling claws until it became a thoroughfare.

I am the one who waits there with bare feet on the kilim-patched metal, feeling the frequencies through the soles.

Not a greaser of the great spans—though I carry the pot and the pins when the rotation comes—and not quite one of the dreamers who wake already knowing which bearing will hunger before the bright months turn. Something quieter. A hinge that never quite names itself. My shoulders have narrowed to the exact width of the cable stays that cross the mid-level hanging meadows. My arms remember every bolted access plate that must be read by touch when the mist thickens into white blindness. The callus on my palms is the same buttery polish found on the oldest handrails; the green-black rimming my nails is permanent, a second cuticle of algae and crushed iron-leaf. I no longer scrub it. It tells the place I am still listening.

Skin the color of parchment left too near a heliostat on the windward side, then softening to that chlorophyll undertone whenever I descend into the fern galleries to cut back the tendrils fogging a mirror-shaft. There is a pale coil scar around my left ankle where a corkscrew antler taught me, once, the difference between co-tenant and rider. There is a small arc on the forearm from a cable that kissed too hard during a long-wind sway. I read them the way we read the half-effaced numerals: load-bearing history.

Hair oiled with the residual lubricants so the fog sheets off it the way it sheets off the undersides of the louvers. When the high-altitude light hits just so, the iridescent scales of grazing insects caught in it briefly dazzle like wet leaf. Children sometimes pull seeds from it as I pass; I let them. The seeds will find another vertical meadow soon enough.

I dress in the oxidized red-browns and verdigris of cloth spun from orchard fiber and retired belting. The wrists and ankles left long so I can knot them tight when the thin continuous voice of the wind rises, or loose them to trail moisture deliberately into the finger-gutters that feed the lung-shaped cisterns. Deep pockets. Always. Today: a coil of monofilament, three scaffolding pins still warm from another hand, a handful of the red-veined leaves that taste of rust and mineral ration, a small moss-lined pouch containing two apricot-scented blossoms that fell during the night cycle. They will go into the evening steam over the greenhouse flues. Nothing abrupt. Nothing wasted.

My eyes hold the greys of weathered metal with an accidental undertone of amber that still sometimes surfaces when the residual phosphorescence wakes in the undercroft dark. I can look straight into the pearled blaze bouncing off a bank of heliostats—some of them no longer on any map, casting those mild wrong-direction shadows—and still pick out the silhouette of a goat-thing moving along a distant ledge. At night I need no lamp on the familiar routes. The fungi cultivated under the decks and the low glow in my own gaze are enough to meet another pair of eyes in a warm steam main and know, without speaking, which forgotten kindness we are both visiting.

I move the way the structure itself breathes: the hand’s-breadth sway already priced into every step. Elevators when they come; ladders and root-floor stairs when they do not. I pause where the hanging meadows offer fruit, where a flywheel hall bleeds residual heat into an arcade, where the bees’ thunder changes pitch and everyone listening falls briefly silent together. Conversation with me unspools in the same register as the relief valves—low, layered, full of comfortable silences while we all hear the place continuing. Laughter short and bright when it comes, like mill wheels striking recycled decking that refuses, for one more season, to become aggregate.

I eat the fog-condensate boiled with iron leaves. I take the flesh of a slow armored browser only when its shell has already begun to fail and the mosses have started the claim; I wrap the pale tunnel fish in fern. I return peels and bones and the metal taste that never leaves the mouth into the composting vaults that still feed residual gases to engines older than any living name. My body’s wastes are simply another slow input into the long accountancy.

Children learn balance on the rails I also polish with my passing palms. I have been kicked, once, correctly, by a pale deer when I was small and pulled too hard. I have since appeared at a dormitory hatch with clean moss in my own mouth when a fever moved through the hanging neighborhoods—no one taught the gesture; the decks taught us all together. Maintenance is how I respire. I walk the tensioned spans listening with bare feet. When a slab begins its slow failure I arrive with the others in the calm of those who have midwived this birth before. We crush aggregate. We hurry the moss. We speak in the first-person plural without noticing.

*We are leaking a little more this decade on the third windward bank.*  
*We swayed farther than usual, but the counterweights remembered.*

There are quiet oddities. I can taste an approaching rain collar hours before the suspended netting darkens—the ozone-and-apricot chemistry that lives in the highest gauges has taken up residence at the back of my tongue. I sometimes dream in the deep thrum of the flywheel halls and wake knowing. Lovers have met me in the sweat of the forgotten steam mains; we leave the heat untouched, an older kindness than maps. My name is not a star or a vanished continent. It is the exact cadence of a particular louver on the leeward face when the tracking gears have worn just so, the one that clicks three times softly before it settles for the night cycle. You would know it if you slept beneath it long enough. Beings who endure here eventually do.

Stay beside me on this half-lifted grating and the membrane between inhabitant and habitat grows translucent. My breathing has already slowed to the night cycle of the heavier engines. My clothes hold the permanent humidity. My dreams rock with the kilometer-long spans. I am not steward, not master, not parasite. I am one more continuity—like the leaks that became hydraulic balance, like the vines that became soft breathing walls, like the animals whose scars and deaths keep the drains open and the vaults feeding the flames.

I keep the place in the same slow good repair with which it keeps me.  
Slightly upright.  
Slightly scarred.  
Permanently damp.  
And still, after longer than memory, refusing to be sudden.

The light pours. The fog beads. A marmot-creature wrists past my ankle on its way to clear another slot. Somewhere far below in the undercrofts a governor clicks with unhurried authority.

I lean into the long sway, bare feet reading the deck, and wait for whatever small necessity comes next.
