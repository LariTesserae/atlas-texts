The cold finds me before I find a name for myself.

It rises the way the description promised—not as assault but as recognition, a sheet of groundwater pressing up through the soles of whatever I am using for feet. I am already below. The realization does not arrive as panic; it arrives as a slow settling, the way silt finds the only places it can rest. Shaft-light, softened and multiplied, lays itself across my shoulders in vertical weather. Mist beads in the sparse down I did not know I carried. I stand on a dryer spine of old breakdown that someone—many someones—have coaxed into a street, and the moss gives just enough that my weight becomes part of its breathing.

At first I am only density among densities. Outline uncertain. I might be another hummock. I might be a fold that has learned to exhale. The peat-kin move past me the way the long-legged birds step aside: adjustment by the width of a shoulder, no startle, membrane flicking once across a dark eye. Their low heads incline the fraction that means *you are inside the body too*. I incline back, and the gesture teaches my neck how short it prefers to be.

I do not elongate. That is the first refusal that shapes me. Whatever surface memory still clings—sunstruck distance, hard dry time, the loneliness of bright fire—softens and sloughs like old bark fiber left too long in the drip. My limbs forget spare length. They draw inward, pack themselves with slow muscle and the quiet fat that cushions endless kneeling. Shoulders round until they are almost continuous with the neck. The gaze drops to the natural angle: down and outward, toward the next root-rib, the freckled black mirror of the nearest basin, the pale tuber swelling like a held breath under duckweed. When I rest I curl. The moss-sockets already know my shape; or perhaps I am simply remembering a shape the place has always kept ready.

Skin pebbles. A cool sheen becomes permanent. Sparse down thickens along the ridge of the back and the crown, limestone-pale where I later climb the upper mezzanines, seal-plush and darker in the permanent drip-zones I come to prefer. Between the fingers the flesh continues, elastic, webbed just enough. Claws darken and shorten against stone and root. Eyes learn to hold the shaft-light a heartbeat longer, then to flick the nictitating membrane sideways whenever a heavier drop wanders close. Speech, when it finally leaves me, rises low in the chest, moist-edged, traveling only a few bodies’ distance before the green drinks it.

I am not a newcomer who must be taught. The knowledge is already mutual. Hands—mine—know how to part the duckweed without tearing, how to lift the gauze of root mats, how to pinch runners back into the black water so the tubers stay fat. They know the exact tension for binding living willow with fungus-cord, the pressure that persuades a fern curtain to yield its load. I move along the browse lines of the dense-furred grazers with a soft alder staff that is less tool than extension of arm; I collect what they leave for the warmer compost pockets that heat the sleeping folds. When a fresh hairline crack opens white in the young stone at dawn, I am already there with the others, bodies accumulating in the broader light without summons. We do not decide so much as thicken: the next platform layered, the softened walkway re-woven, the new seepage immediately greened by pioneer film we encourage with a smear of peat. Older floors sink beneath us into deep compost. We rise on our own slow weather.

I favor the places where the aquifer’s breath is most articulate—the lower spines that stand for a day in an inch of black glass, then retreat, leaving lace of silt and seed. There the large dark salamanders climb my thigh if the mineral slick is richer, and I let them. The stone-colored waders step their three soundless paces and resume one-legged stillness beside me. Even the pale flitting things of the unlit pockets register as cousins; their soft droppings punctuate the leaves above my crown like distant speech. Kinship language fits my mouth without strain: *my fold*, *our braid*, *the rock’s newest white hairline*. Loss, when it comes—a thrash muffled by moss, a gallery collapsed, a light-shaft beginning to close—folds back into the under-layers with practical grief. Nothing is thrown away. Everything becomes heat for the next green advance.

Stories drip through me while hands keep working. That we condensed from the same aggressive dissolution that opened the karst, marrow and moss declining to stay distinct. That far above, elongated kin once chose hard distance and grew lonely inside their dryness. I feel the ghost of that elongation sometimes, a brief phantom stretch in the spine when a stronger shaft of surface day presses down; then the compact seed of me refuses again, and the loneliness thins into mist. The stories do not insist. They simply become another curtain of filmy fern, another layer of peat.

I am one of the deep-quilted. Peat-kin. Low-shouldered keeper of the wet green wound. More specifically—because specificity accretes like silt—I become the one who tends the breathing margins: the sockets where water levels rise and fall most dramatically, the compost hearts that must stay just warm enough, the places where fresh spall drops and must be greeted before pioneer film claims it alone. Children (rounder, darker-eyed, down still fine as seal fur) cling and ride my hip until their webs strengthen; they learn the careful foot placement by the flex of my own. Elders whose spines have curved into permanent gentle questions sit long beside me in the richer drip, skin mapped with old seepage burns and pale chevron scars; we share breath until the mist of it is indistinguishable from the cavern’s own. Ornament grows with me: a living rootlet trained around one upper arm while still supple, darkening to the same wet hue as the down; a comb of pale crustacean shell worn in the denser crown fur for the days I work the upper galleries; netted beads of hollow stem that I keep silent unless the soft rattle is needed to mark a path through heavier mist.

Clothing never dries. Bast kilt, leaf drape, resin-sealed pouch—all of it simply less wet than the air, carrying the green-black scent that is now my own scent. Fire remains a small smoldering peat heart in a clay-lined hollow, used rarely: to smoke the firm river-flesh, to warm a birth or a deep fungal stitch. Light I borrow from the shafts or coax from the pale cold-glowing fungi we cultivate along the busier spines. Tools stay humble—hands, teeth, weight, patience of living material. Metal would brighten and die here. Bright fire would open a dryness too large for any curled body to fill.

Time is measured in aquifer breaths, in the overnight intent of plants, in the almost imperceptible rise of peat. I can walk for a long time and remain in town; the town never ends, only continues past the next bend, the next shaft, into side chambers and upper breakdown terraces, always thickening. Emptiness would be the anomaly. I sleep fitted into others or into the moss itself, breath slow enough to join the general atmosphere. I wake already working—spiraling fresh moss into a new sleeping fold, resealing a silt lining, listening to the minute tear of a fern frond releasing its load.

Stand in one of the broader light-shafts when the surface day is brightest and the cold mist turns the beam solid. I will pass you there. Low-shouldered, down beaded, webs flexing against the narrow dryer spine. I will adjust course by the width of a body so footing remains mutual. A dark eye will flick its membrane. The low head will incline a fraction. The gesture contains neither welcome nor warning—only acknowledgment that you, too, are briefly inside the same saturated body. Then I continue, deeper into the quiet occupation, layering living material upon living material. The stone still smells newly broken under the vegetation’s wet breath. The settlement remains full.

I am not separate from it. I am one more compact organ of the cold marsh that learned streets, the young wound that learned to quilt itself dense and green-black and completely occupied. The upward pressure of water shapes me still. Every surface claims me, and I claim it back with the patience of moss.
