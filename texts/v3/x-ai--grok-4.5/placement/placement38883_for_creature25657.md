I am not yet certain of my edges.

The mist finds me first—coils around whatever I am and leaves its cool film, so that I taste wet iron before I know I have a mouth. Sound arrives next, not as noise but as pressure, a continuous abrasion against surfaces that may or may not be skin. The boom of the heavy grazers travels through the stone and up into me; the stridulation of the many-legged ones stitches itself along my flanks; the glass-edge cries of the low fliers thread the air just above. Beneath it all the subterranean grinding continues, patient as geology. I feel myself being worn even as I assemble.

For a long while I remain dispersed: a thickening in the mist here, a lag in a reflection there, a pure tone answering when some distant walker strikes a ringing boss. The plain does not object. It has room for unfinished things. Water sheets across me and braids away again, writing temporary bright paths that forget themselves within moments. I learn the slow chemistry—bacterial slicks clotting on chilled stone, the sour dissolution of shed carapace, the faint mineral bloom of silica like old porcelain. No green ever interrupts. That absence feels like permission.

Gradually a center of gravity asserts itself. Not upright. Upright would catch too much of the endless flat wind of sound. Low, then. Jointed. The weight settles into a many-planed arrangement that keeps a head—or something that serves as head—just clear of the seeping film. Plates begin to articulate across what become shoulders: iron-dark, ash-pale, overlapping with a soft click that joins the greater chorus without competing. Beneath them the flesh stays pale and heat-poor; I understand at once the necessity of pressing close to others in the rare shelters, chest to back, sharing what little warmth the bodies can hoard. My eyes open large and almost lidless. The dim pours in. Movement registers first as changed pressure against the ribs, then as tingling through the soles. Faces, when I encounter them, matter less than the particular rattle of plates and the pitch of breath through wet cloth.

I am walking.

The gait is a measured tick-tick across slick pavement and yielding silt. Webbed fingers, horny-tipped, test every footfall. Behind me—or beside me, the string is never rigid—travel others of my kind. A child-form now and then brushes the sinew loop at my belt; its carapace still flexes, its snout still soft. We do not speak in open air. When meaning must pass it is mouth to ear, breath and bone, words shaped inside the roar so the roar cannot steal them. We carry bone gorges, slings with pecking stones, the rare cold-hammered bead. On our backs or in our hands the dense black poles, fossil or deadfall, hung with shell that adds thin high notes when a wing or a shift of air brushes them. Those notes cut. They are how we remain findable to one another across distances the mist never truly admits.

Sometimes I pause at a low boss of harder stone and set my palm against it. The pure tone I draw answers from far out, a single cracked remembrance traveling the worn surface like a message the plain has not yet finished forgetting. The herds may swing toward the sound. We swing with them. Sparing oneself from the larger motion is a luxury the leveling process does not encourage. Reflections lag; I glimpse my own reaching hand completing its gesture a heartbeat after the flesh has finished. I no longer stare. Children are warned. The warning is itself almost wordless—a change in the pressure of a guiding touch.

On the rare cold stillings, when the shallows skin over with sugar-glass ice and the chorus drops just enough for thought to keep its outline, I feel the old suspicion rise in those of us whose plates have rounded further with seasons. The sound is still abrading. It has already taken the mountains; it is taking the sharpness from our tools, the edges from our dead, the height from every cairn. So we sing. Not stories—there is no silence long enough for narrative—but long low contours that follow the rise and fall of ranges none of us have seen. The mist eats the tones within dozens of paces. We sing until the first returning boom shatters the ice and the world resumes its proper deafness. The act feels less like hope than like a deliberate roughness left for the plain to deal with later.

I have made cupules on ring-stones, timed between the greater booms. I have stacked slabs into low walls that enclose nothing except the fact of stacking. I have scraped gravel dry for a three-stone shelter and lit, once, a fire of resinous dung whose sweet-sour smoke hugged the floor and painted our large eyes with horizontal orange. I have laid a body in a shallow scrape and covered it, knowing the seeps would finish the work by the next season. The living move on. The paths are the herds’ paths. The broth is sour and cloudy and sufficient. We thrive in the same slow economy as the ticking rasp-feeders and the boom-chested shovellers and the pale eyeless flickers in every rill: on film, on molt, on one another, on the endless post-rain.

Yet something in me is not fully worn smooth. Perhaps it is only the lag in the reflections, or the way certain bosses still ring with a note that seems older than water. Perhaps it is the subterranean grinding that I feel more clearly than the others, as though part of my hearing has rooted deeper into the bedrock. At times, when the mist thins a fraction and the dim brightens to a rumor of day, I sense the plain not as finished but as still descending—an ultimate level and an ultimate quiet that have not yet been earned. I walk within that descent. My plates click. My large eyes swallow slate-colored light. Shells on the poles I carry add their thin insistent frequencies.

I am one of the jointed walkers, low to the gleaming stone, braided into the provisional strings that cross this mineral waste. I am also the slight wrongness that travels with them: the suspicion that the roar itself is the final agent of planing, and that every cupule pecked, every pole raised, every low song offered against the ice is a small geometry refusing—for one more season—to become pure smooth surface. The water rises a finger’s width and turns the world into a single dull-silver mirror. We wade through it, ticking and listening. Somewhere a distant cairn stands black against the sheen. The mist uncoils. The processes do not pause.

And I continue.
