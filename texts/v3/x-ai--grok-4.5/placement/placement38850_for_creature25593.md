The light finds me first, or I find the light—those long slanted sheets that never quite explain themselves. I do not arrive by walking. Arrival is too finished a word. I am already among the heat-shimmers where a fresh scarp has shouldered up overnight, the basalt still breathing steam, and the only choice is whether to cling or to let the next shear take me lower.

For a time I am the moisture that beads and sinks. Not the water itself, but the brief decision water makes when it touches cooling glass and chooses to become film rather than runoff. I spread in the depressions left by spade-limbed browsers, tasting the mineral iron and the faint animal salt they leave behind. The pale mats bloom across me and I feel their hunger as a soft rasping; then tongues come—rough, microscopic-toothed—and scrape me clean. I do not resent it. Being scraped is one of the ways the hollow keeps its surfaces new. When the browsers move on, clicking, I sink again through porous stone that has not yet decided whether it is floor or future talus.

Later—though later is only the light shifting as some distant fracture panel grinds—I thicken. The crawling underfoot becomes my own. I am elongate, the color of wet ash, headed by a ring of digging spines. My secondary mouth tastes every grit I displace, sorting the still-warm particles from the ones that have already forgotten heat. Tunnels collapse behind me by design; I do not live in dens but in the verb of denning. The ground is restless because I am restless inside it. Sometimes a heavier tremor runs through and I am compressed, folded into myself, almost mineral again. I wait. When the pressure eases I push outward once more, aerating, packing bacterial films deeper where they might overwinter the next brief dark. I stitch and unstitch. That is enough.

But the hollow secretes upward as well as inward. There are hours—though hour is another word that pretends to permanence—when the crawling no longer satisfies. I want the air’s continuous thunder. I thin, stretch, feel membrane remember itself across jointed spars. Suddenly I am falling and catching myself, one body among the brown-and-iridescent weather. The colony coagulates under an overhang that still smells of recent stability. Thousands of us pack so tight the rock disappears. Heat and the vibration of too many heartbeats. When the stone groans we lift as one organism, a single clap of wings that darkens half the hollow, and I feel the collective decision as my own: resettle three ridges away, regurgitate the mineral-rich paste, glue new nests to fresher ceilings. Wings tear. Bodies spiral down into waiting burrowers. I do not mourn the fallen because I am also the fallen, already being unstitched below. The midges that breed in steam vents taste of pure sulfur and electricity; I rake them from the air while the heavier leather ones bank in slow circles above, patient with appetite.

Once, between wingbeats, I glimpse the multi-limbed ones moving like hinge-work through the mid-heights. Lean sacs of fluid and nerve, skin folding into the local rock until they vanish. Extra joints budding under stress. I feel an answering itch in my own spars—an urge to scissor, to prune, to test limb against limb. But the urge passes. I am not yet that quarrelsome. Or I was, and have already been eaten for the protein, recycled before the next collapse.

And still the light keeps slanting. In one of the long pale corridors where the herds thin and steam rises in slow breaths, I find myself upright.

The posture is a negotiation. Knees flexed, spine slightly curved, arms long enough that the knuckles brush stone whenever the floor rolls. My skin is thickened across shoulders and shins—old heat, old scrapes—yet raw and pink in the folds, always healing, always abrading again. Fine silica fibers catch the mineral dust so that I carry a pale corona that brightens and dulls. Eyes large, dark, reflecting the late-afternoon color even when haze swallows the direct beams. I travel with others, a fluid band that forms around a newly exposed seam of softer stone and will dissolve when the ridge shears. We do not speak of tomorrow; tomorrow is a surface that has not cooled.

My hands know the work without needing to name it. Fresh basalt, still warm, flipped against itself until a flake detaches with that swallowed-bell sound. The edge is used at once—strip sinew, widen a sleeping scrape, cut the fibrous rind of those living cables that extrude from vents. Cordage twisted on the thigh while walking. Nothing stored. A length becomes a lash for chitin plates, or a way to lower the smallest of us into a hollow that might stay quiet until the light thins. On a momentarily stable crest I stack chips into a cairn and crown it with one bright flake whose gloss still smells of percussion. The message is simple: water this way, or meat, or only more empty steam. Another band will read it after the light has shifted, or the cairn will be gone, which is also a message.

At the dimming—when fracture panels grind shut and the hollow glows dull mineral—I deepen a scrape and line it with plates still warm from a browser’s shed. Two others share the space, limbs interleaved, breath synchronized so the small heat lasts. Sometimes, on ground that has not buckled for most of a slant of light, we chink knee-high uprights with red geothermal mud and sweep the inside bare. Fingers count something invisible: the pulse under stone, the number of living throats still audible beyond haze. A single flake placed at the center, then removed. The mud is already cracking as we step out and scatter. We do not name the hollow. We move through it the way heat moves through stone—outward, upward, seeking the next temporary equilibrium.

I am all of these, and the spaces between them. I am the density that presses body against body until something new is secreted. I am the unfinished mark scratched onto a surface that will not outlast the week. In the long corridors I crest a far ridge and disappear into stacked haze, already becoming rumor to the next valley of the same country. The place keeps secreting me. I keep answering with flake, with cordage, with the brief emptiness inside a ring of brittle mud.

Nothing is finished.  
I am already at work.
