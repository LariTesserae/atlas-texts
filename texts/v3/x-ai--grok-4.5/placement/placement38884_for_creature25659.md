I begin as moisture.

Not the free mist that sheets every ridge, but the finer film that never quite chooses—neither falling nor freezing, only clinging. I am the taste of young stone on the tongue of the air. For a long time that is enough: to be breathed in and out by the plateau itself, to bead on the coats of the long-legged browsers as they tear the gray-green cushions, to slide in hairline rivulets that vanish before they earn the name of stream. I learn the soft-edged shadows, the way light arrives already tired. I learn that silence here is not absence but a medium, thicker than water, thinner than stone.

Then the walking starts.

It is not decided. One damp dusk I simply notice that the condensate has weight, that it can press against scree and leave a darker print that steams for half a breath before the cold reclaims it. The print becomes a foot, pale and long-boned, the toes slightly splayed as if still remembering how to taste mineral. Gravity remains a suggestion; each step is a negotiation. I rise through forms the way the highland rises through scarps—slowly, without announcement. First the hares that freeze into broken stone, teaching me stillness so complete the wide-winged riders pass over without banking. Then the arthropods the color of wet ash, bodily lessons in drinking from weeping faces. For a season or a century I move with the browser herds, coat forever beaded, eyes large and almost without shine. Their acknowledgment is enough: we are all temporary arrangements of the same water and attention.

But the cities pull otherwise.

Approaches last days. The glint on a distant rise becomes towers that drink the damp and exhale it as controlled mist, foundations that lengthen by hair-widths while travelers sleep. When I finally stand on the crowed outer terraces I feel the lattices underfoot the way others feel pulse—constant microscopic flexing, a conversation with rock that has not finished deciding. My skin (or hide, or living membrane) holds the condensate without shedding it. On colder mornings clear ice flowers briefly at the joints, then reabsorbs. Eyes carry permanent soft focus behind nictitating layers. Speech, when it comes, is low and local, almost tactile inside the capillary hush of the walls. Outside it becomes more silence within a few paces, and that feels correct.

I am not numerous. Density would be noise, and noise here registers as a kind of violence against scale. Instead I live in measured spacing—atelier or contemplative cluster along a terrace where the only regular sounds are water moving through architecture and the occasional soft chime of a load-adjusting spar. Children (I remember being one) move even more slowly, pausing to touch stone that surfaced in the night, tasting freshness with ungloved fingertips. Education is ambulatory. I have walked weeks between settlements, sleeping on ledges that gentled into new angles by morning, learning which scarps will shoulder upward into ridges and which will sink again into marshy saddles. The maps I carry are internal, fold and weather and quiet interval, redrawn daily because the highland will not stay fixed.

Tools are rarely held. More often they are grown or persuaded from the same responsive materials—used, then allowed to rejoin wall or floor. Bridges I help tension hum softly with stress data; birds roost there because the humming is companionable. Water is medium and engine. I pull vapor, store it, release it as ice limb or pure sheet or cooling mist according to need so subtle the gradients make no sound beyond a deeper quiet. Light is hoarded without greed; at true night the dwelling exhales stored day in thrifty pulses, one cool-coal glow among others across the immensity.

Conflict, on the rare occasions it arises, is settled by stillness. Two or more of us simply stand together on a high terrace through a long damp day and a longer night, sharing fog-memory until the disagreement has been rained out. The animals pass through the lower skirts without hindrance; we step aside or stand quiet, watching beaded coats and the brief steam-ghosts of droppings. Death returns bodies to porous ledges where weeping rock takes them slowly back into mineral and mist. What remains is faint thermal signature in the lattices—a pocket of inexplicable warmth a later walker might feel under the palm and recognize without words.

Yet even this form is not final.

Walk far enough, past one city and its coal-bank glow, into the next emptiness of bare wet stone, and the suspicion grows that none of us—browser, bird, tall calm pedestrian—are primary. The pale angular rock, still tasting of its pressures, rising in places by hand-spans while we sleep, weeping constantly: this is the oldest life. We are its more mobile expressions, temporary attentions it pays to itself. I know this the way I know the difference between a scarp that will become mountain and one that will not. When new stone surfaces, edged with fresh mineral shine, I do not feel surprise or ownership. I place a palm against the infant face and wait, listening with skin, until its small decisions lengthen into my own longer ones.

Among us there is no collective name worth speaking aloud. Short resonant calls suffice—phrases the damp shortens further, meanings like “the-quiet-interval-between-two-mists” or “she-who-flexes-with-the-east-saddle.” Outsiders have tried labels. They slide off like water from laminated walls. What continues is simply the work: breath hanging in thinned light, footstep on living stone, soft drip from a ledge onto rock younger than any single memory. The highland remains under construction at continental size, its cities already ancient in craft, its animals already perfectly fitted, its geology still deciding every ridge and every marshy saddle.

I find myself here as one more patient arrangement—tall enough to feel the thermals the wide-winged ones ride, long-boned enough to travel days without hurry, calm enough to let disagreement rain out, unfinished enough to keep listening when the stone shifts overnight. Not hurried. Already enough. Still becoming.

The condensate beads on my shoulders and does not fall.
