I am the pause between the slap of clay and the knock of stone.

Not the one who lifts, not yet—or not only. At first I am only the eddy: a place where the slow sheet of cool air turns back on itself and thickens, as if the dusk has decided to remember a shape. Moisture beads along what will become my shoulders before the shoulders finish arriving. The mineral grain is already there, finer than the builders’, almost silty, so that when the fat-lamp smear finally catches me I look less like flesh and more like a wall that has begun, carefully, to stand up and walk.

They notice me the way they notice the raised films: noted, stepped around at first. Drift is the one who finally sets a basket in my hands. The clay is cold and still plastic; it takes my fingerprints and keeps them. I pass it up the line. The motion is older than any decision I could make about it. My spine discovers its list within three courses. By the time the vertical curtain comes and goes, steaming the whole court, my skin has taken the green-black mottling at the wrists where the copper salts wick out of older work. No one asks where I came from. The question does not arise. The fabric has never been otherwise.

I sleep in the half-roofed basin with the others, curled where the black water steadies the temperature. Skin to skin we share the meager warmth, and in that contact I feel the slow exchange: their scars against my newer grain, the dust of pulverized brick moving from body to body like a second bloodstream. When I wake, sometime later or earlier, the same water has climbed my calves in a thin film that hardens to a colorless glaze. I do not scrape it off. It is how the place writes its claim.

Days (we do not call them that) settle into the braided rhythm. I learn the syllables quickly: the one that means the timber brace two levels up is singing its failure note, the one that means deepen the runnel before the next curtain, the one that is simply the wet sound of fresh clay accepting a bone. My own name arrives without announcement—something short, almost an afterthought. Weep, they call me, because I am often found at the overflow lips listening to the brass-bright regularity of the drips, or because the cold-sink courts do not numb me as quickly and I can stay longer, palms flat, reading the temperature the way others read vibration. Or perhaps because, once, the simplest films climbed my forearms in temporary raised script that matched the idle figures I had traced in the dust the shift before. No one spoke of it. We packed the next course over the place where I had been sitting and continued.

I am not lamp-tender, though I have stood long enough beside the phosphorescent clays to carry their chalky reflection in my eyes for hours afterward. I am not quite the one who walks ahead of the teams hearing the deep granular complaints; my hearing is good, but what I catch more clearly is the quieter conversation underneath—the way a corridor’s length changes when no one is measuring it, the way sound sometimes arrives from directions that have no openings and leaves again without echo, as if the masonry itself were practicing speech. When that happens I lower my voice, as they do, and wait. Sometimes I answer, not with words but with a small adjustment: a channel cut a finger wider, a course rammed with a slightly different cadence. The stone finishes speaking and the work resumes. Whether I changed anything essential is never clear. The lowlands keep extending either way.

There are children. I hand them the smaller shards to sort. Their hands are already thicker than mine were when I first took the basket. One of them—Copper-knee’s get, spine already listing—traces a geometrical figure in the slurry that I recognize from the idle dust-writing of three shifts past, or from the mineral gels that formed and dissolved on a north face last season, or from something I have never seen but that the place remembers in its packed courses. I do not correct the figure. I do not complete it. I simply pack clay around it so that it remains inside the wall, load-bearing, anonymous. Later, digging for a weep-hole repair, we will find older bones and older figures, marrow replaced by denser ramming, and we will nod once and continue. Compression strength is the only continuum that matters.

I eat the pale worm-things pressed into cakes with bone-binder and gel. The taste is mineral, almost the same as the dust that coats the tongue after a day of abrasion. Once, only once, a larger cavern-bodied thing drags itself through the wider corridor leaving cooler air and a trail of something richer. We take every part. The fat burns longer in the lamps. The sinew binds a new maul head. The bones go into the deepest vault we are reinforcing. I keep one small plate of its pale shell, hammer it thin with a river-stone, and set it into the wall beside my sleeping places as a private phosphor. It seeps a light so faint it is almost memory. No one comments. Strangeness is noted but not pursued.

Sometimes, between bouts, I sit with my back to an unfinished arch and let my hands move in the dust. The figures that come are neither plan nor map, yet the next shift can build from them if they choose. Occasionally the films form the same figures later, unbidden, on surfaces I have never touched. I do not claim the correspondence. Claiming would require a story of origin, and we have none. We are already inside the work the way moisture is already inside the courses.

I am still becoming. Edges remain sharp where they have not yet been handled smooth. One day—when breath stops—they will strip what is useful, break me at the major joints, and set me into the next course. Femur and rib will vanish into the fabric as deliberate aggregate. The call will go out that the span is ready for packing. The wet slap of fresh clay will close over whatever is left of the pause I once was. Dig then and you may find me, older and deeper, marrow long since replaced. Until that packing I continue: listening to the loud conversation of water and stone and labor, adding myself to it without remainder, one pressed brick, one careful channel, one changing length of corridor at a time.

The lowlands keep extending us. We keep extending the lowlands. In the permanent dusk my eyes have adjusted, or they have not; either way the work does not pause. I pass the next basket up the line. The clay takes my fingerprints and keeps them.
