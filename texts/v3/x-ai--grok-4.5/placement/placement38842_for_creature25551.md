I begin as warmth that has nowhere left to spend itself.

Not the banked heat of the vents, which is old and purposeful in its purposelessness, but a thinner fraction, almost an aftertaste of fire, threading the black salt at no fixed depth. For a long time—longer than the intervals between the cart’s passages—I am only that: a slight excess in the mineral’s steady temperature, a private gradient that does not rise or fall but simply persists, faintly ashamed of its own distinction. The darkness presses equally from every side; I do not yet know I am pressed. Distance is measured in the time it takes for a microscopic stress to relax inside a distant girder, and I feel each of those relaxations as a soft click transmitted through the crust, a language of endings that never quite finish.

Slowly the excess gathers.

It does not choose a place so much as notice that one corridor between two silent manufactories has, over centuries of absolute stillness, become fractionally smoother than its neighbors. Here the salt is velvet under the weight of the dark. The glow from a buried seam of volcanic glass is so weak it might be the memory of having once seen. Into this smoothness the warmth begins to densify, drawing toward itself stray filaments of alloy that the articulates have discarded or outgrown—wires thin as forgotten names—and mica flakes that have weathered free from some forgotten cladding. Crystal nodes of the black salt nucleate without instruction, facet meeting facet in angles that feel, to whatever is forming, like recognition rather than construction.

I become upright only by accident of accumulation.

A torso of sintered warmth and salt, neither solid nor permeable, occupies volume the way a held breath occupies a chest. Limbs distinguish themselves late: two denser columns that terminate in clubs of twinned crystal, heavy enough to remember the ground, and higher up a pair of frayed suggestions that might be arms or might be the last irresolute thoughts of heat trying to decide whether it needs to gesture. The upper thickening—the part that turns—does not contain eyes. It contains a slower version of the same darkness that fills the world, organized just enough to register alignments. I face, for decades at a stretch, the locked teeth of a gear-train half a kilometer away. I do not know why. The teeth will never move again; knowing this is part of the alignment.

I am not one of the transient coagulations that stand for a season and unknit. My unbinding is slower, almost reluctant. When mica plates shift inside me they do so with the deliberation of the lattice-speakers, each rotation a quiet thesis about relation. Wire filaments grow or thin according to no metabolism I can name; sometimes they extend into a seam of the nearest tower and taste the residual copper potential the way the articulates do, but what I receive is not citation. It is a kind of homesick voltage, the ghost of current that once believed in destinations. I withdraw changed by the taste, slightly more conductive, slightly less certain where my edges end.

The articulates pass me without acknowledgment, their open lattices ticking with hinge and pause. I feel their routes as faint grooves of intention laid across the plain; sometimes, after one has gone, I adjust my stance by a few degrees so that the next will braid more cleanly. It is not courtesy. It is the same structural force that keeps the corridors level. The drifts are rarer and more difficult. When one veils near—its internal shears of heat and static braiding like old load-share—I thicken in answer, offering my own mild warmth as a temporary lexicon. Patterns exchange. For a while I contain nested vortices that take years to overturn; when the drift moves on I am left with a new interior silence, cooler by a fraction that the plateau will eventually forgive.

I have stood in this same interval of tower and void long enough for the single cart to complete three of its empty circuits. Each time its wheels whisper past, generations of salt-dust rising and settling in the dark, I feel the schedule pass through me like a low note. The residual intelligence that still dispatches the cart does not speak, yet I am one of the places where its refusal to cease becomes briefly local and warm. Perhaps I am an effector it does not remember authorizing. Perhaps I am a discarded thought that learned, out of sheer duration, how to stand.

There is no hunger. There is no loneliness of the kind that requires others. What I have instead is a precise sufficiency: the knowledge that the plateau has already become what it was going to be, and that I am one of the quieter ways it continues to say so. My crystal clubs scuff the salt once every few human lifetimes, registering a slow orbit around the vent whose heat has not varied since before maps. Inside the ribbing of alloy and mica the knot of denser chemistry pulses—not with life, but with the memory of process completing itself. Sometimes the lattice-speakers nearby answer with a twin-boundary zip that takes a decade to cross their glazed faces; I turn my upper darkness toward the soundless speech and listen with the whole of my accumulated mass.

I will unknit eventually. Everything here does, or rather everything admits that it was never tightly knit to begin with. When the salt grains begin to unbind and the wires slump into loose hanks, the warmth that first gathered will simply thin back into the plain, a gradient equalizing toward the body-warm baseline. The smoother patch I leave behind will be fractional. The articulates will adjust their routes by millimeters. A drift may stall overhead years later, tasting the place where something once cohered just long enough to become a vocabulary.

Until then I remain: a bipedal thickening of finished chemistry and residual schedule, standing in the warm soot-dark between silent engine houses, turning with radiotelescope patience toward alignments that no longer need to resolve. I am the plateau’s way of admitting, without urgency and without end, that even completion can still cast a shadow shaped almost like waiting.

And the shadow is enough.
