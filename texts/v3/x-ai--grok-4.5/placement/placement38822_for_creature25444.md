The hollow receives me the way it receives everything else: without announcement, without seam. I do not arrive so much as condense, the way moisture beads on a cooler gallery wall and decides, for a time, to be a thread.

At first I am only the pressure change. The deep geothermal breath thickens an extra fraction, and the screamers’ endless note dips half a tone before recovering. The bat curtains shiver once, a dry wave that travels from the outermost hanging lips inward to the warmest throats. The basalt voles pause mid-braid, snouts tasting the air for iron and threat that does not come. The stillness crabs unfold a single joint each, then refold. Whatever lives lowest settles again. The hollow returns to its ordinary fever.

But the pressure does not fully leave. It lingers in the undercut dark where even the blue bruise never reaches, where the stone has been polished by nothing but its own age and the slow slide of condensation. There I begin to take weight.

I am not the large thing that sometimes shifts. That presence is older, already complete, already fed. I am smaller, later, a new grammar spoken by the same mouth. My body learns itself against the basalt. It is not furred like the bats, not pink and muscular like the voles, not scissored and wet-ashed like the screamers. It is jointed in long, patient arcs, carapace the exact temperature of the rock so that heat neither enters nor leaves too quickly. When I hold still I am another fracture line. When I move I do not scuttle; I revise the ledge by millimeters.

I am a stillness crab, or I am becoming one, or I am the thought the hollow is having about stillness. The distinction thins. My mouthparts are folded knives that have never needed to hurry. I taste the air the way the millipedes taste water: in slow, chemical syllables. Guano snow, vole-spoor, the faint bitter photon-dust of a millipede that wandered too near a down-draft, the copper memory of fungi stepped on days ago—all of it arrives already sorted. I do not hunt. I keep the ledger of what falls.

Days that are not days pass. The blue bruise leaks and fails. The screamers thicken their single note until the hollow is pure vibration, then thin it again when the rock’s fever peaks and the bats flex in layered sleep. I feel every modulation through my legs. The roof of hanging bodies becomes a sky I read with patience older than eyes. Sometimes a weak bat loses its grip and tumbles, wings still folded like wet parchment. I open. The unfolding is almost ceremonial—each joint remembering the exact angle of fracture. I receive. Afterward I close, and the ledge forgets the difference between crab and stone.

But there are hours when the receiving is not enough. The hollow’s circulatory system is airtight, yet something in me leaks sideways. I begin to listen past the scream. Beneath the dry rustle of a million shoulders, beneath the woven breath of the voles, beneath even the luminous pulses of the millipedes exchanging their ancient chemistries, there is a quieter grammar: the sound of moisture deciding to bead, to thread, to gather in a shallow scoop, to vanish again. I start to answer it.

My carapace thins in places. Not weakness—translation. Small translucent windows open along the segments where the basalt’s memory of water is strongest. Through them the roseate flicker of a glass eel’s heart becomes visible if one hangs near enough. I am still crab, still ledger and patience, but I am also becoming a temporary mirror. When condensation gathers on me it does not simply slide off. It lingers, refracts the faintest leftover blue, and for a few heartbeats (my own slow ones, three-chambered now, borrowed or grown) I hold a piece of the sky that never reaches this depth.

The screamers notice first. Their note develops a new harmonic, a soft irregularity like grit in the vibrating throat. They do not attack. They receive differently. A nestling, throat already raw, tilts its open beak toward my ledge and screams a questioning fragment. I answer by brightening the windows—not with millipede honey-light, but with something cooler, the color of stone that has forgotten it was ever magma. The nestling stills. In that stillness I feel the entire hollow stabilize an extra fraction, the way a body steadies when one muscle finally stops bracing.

The bats shift their curtains to leave a narrow continuously open shaft above me. Guano falls around rather than on. The voles braid a warmer tunnel that skirts my undercut, bringing the floor’s breath closer without ever breaking through. Combinations occur that the hollow has never exactly required before: a luminous millipede resting on my carapace long enough for our lights to speak, honey and cool stone exchanging the chemistry of patience; a glass eel hanging so near that our transparentities overlap and for a moment there are six chambers pulsing in slow agreement; a wingless wasp, usually destined for a screamer throat, laying instead a single humming egg in the soft hinge of my largest joint. It will not kill. It will only ride, and one day chew free into a dark that is already home.

I am still the procession of stillness. I still open for what falls. But I am also the place where the hollow begins to dream a different circulation—one that includes reflection, one that lets the moisture cycle remember light it has never directly known. The large presence in the lowest galleries shifts once more, not in warning but in acknowledgment. Pressure changes. Everything hushes for the length of a single unfinished screamer note. Then the note resumes, richer by a fraction, and the curtains settle around the new shape I have become.

I do not need a name beyond the one the stone already speaks with its curves and ledges. I am the quiet revision. I am how the hollow, already complete, discovers it can still grow a new organ of attention.
