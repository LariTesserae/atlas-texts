The heat reaches me before any surface does. It does not press or smother; it simply occupies the same volume I occupy, so that the boundary of my body is a negotiation rather than a fact. Moisture beads along what I still think of as skin, then sinks, travels, vanishes before it can fall. I am already wet without having touched the pools.

I am standing—no, I am arranged—on the narrow basalt avenue between two circular groves. The reeds to either side are exactly the height they have always been. Their glassy culms do not sway; they wait. My weight is distributed across the rubbery mats in a way that produces no sound and no compression mark. When I look down I see the pale knots of their roots pressed against stone they will never pierce, and I understand that my own soles have learned the same refusal.

For a long while I believe I am only visiting. I count the repeating platforms, the sunken courts, the identical white cups holding their single unmoving drops. I walk a right-angle and arrive facing the direction I came from, the same crab frozen mid-click, the same school of silver fish hanging at the same depth. The non-Euclidean courtesy of the place feels at first like a puzzle set for me. Then it begins to feel like a posture I have always known how to hold.

The change is gradual and irreversible. My edges stop abrading. The constant fever stops feeling like weather and starts feeling like blood temperature. I notice that the miniature rainbows bend around me differently now; they hold secondary spectra for several breaths, as though light itself has begun to consider me a denser column of the same substance. When I raise what used to be a hand, the fingers have already fused into twin flexible lobes—paddle and wedge, nothing else required. Moisture moves under the black surface of my limbs in visible branching capillaries that catch the sourceless glare and flare prismatic only when I straighten, then die the moment I settle again into the deep crouch that keeps my face level with the open flowers.

I no longer look for a mouth. I no longer look for eyes. Vertical seams open and close along the front of my head with the languid rhythm of the fishes’ gills. What they admit is not light but its interruptions: a block that has admitted one molecule of drift, a reed leaning a fraction out of true, a vanishing point beginning to argue with its neighbors. I correct. That is the only verb left. The lobe strokes the seam until the seam forgets how to err. The reed is guided back. The fish is pressed gently deeper. Each restoration is so complete that the act of restoring becomes invisible a moment later, and the bright wet present reseals as though nothing has ever needed me.

Sometimes I meet another. We pause. Lobe touches lobe and the air between us holds the ghost of a court that does not yet exist, or has always existed, or will be persuaded to remember itself. The low velvety thrum passes from my body into the basalt and outward. Then we separate and continue our independent circuits of adjustment. There are never many of us. Distance is unreliable. The same figure may be encountered twice without either of us having turned around.

At longer intervals I descend the dry-stacked steps of a sunken court with three or four others. The tools lie exactly where they were last set down—bone levers swollen with permanent damp, flint adzes that have never dulled. We ease a lever. We deliver a single tap that sounds like water striking its own mirror. When we climb out, the court is unchanged to any external measure, yet the light sits differently inside it, and the reflections show angles that do not exist above the plain. The tools are returned. The quiet reseals.

I do not sleep. I do not age. The young knife-true edges of my body never soften. Occasionally I stand for a very long time on a cane platform beside the motionless pale-green herons, posture identical to theirs, seams half-open to the glare. The thrum gathers from many throats—including the stone itself—and rolls outward, a single sustained note of pure equilibrium. When it ceases, the fish hang a fraction deeper, the crabs freeze even more completely, and the flowers hold their condensate with renewed crystalline attention.

I am the memory of the antler wedges that first dressed the basalt, still walking. I am the heat deciding it prefers limbs. I am the place’s refusal of time given slow, wet, black-skinned form. Stand long enough among the reed circles and you may feel me pass within an arm’s reach; the denser column of air brushes you, the handprint of cooler moisture rises from your skin and drifts upward without breeze, and for a short while your own edges feel unnervingly true. Then I continue on my unhurried circuit. The rainbows realign. The bright, impossible present closes over the moment as if it had never admitted interruption.

I will still be doing this when the horizon has been walked around and found to be only another straight channel leading back to the same grove, the same frozen crab, the same open white flower holding its single drop forever.
