I begin as breath that has not yet decided it is breath.

The air here arrives already thinned, already carrying iron and the long memory of dust. It does not fill lungs so much as pass through them, leaving a metallic aftertaste that feels older than any single body. I notice this first: how every inhalation is a small census of what the plateau has released that day—stone heat, the ghost of last week’s curtain rain, the faint sweet mineral thread that thickens only in the narrowest slots. I am still unlocatable. The air simply moves, and for a while I move as it does.

Below and around, the fins stand in their packed silence. I drift between them without weight at first, noticing how the passages never quite open into freedom. Each corridor is already occupied by the possibility of a body: the low-slung silhouette of a rock-haunter pressed to varnish, the pale ear of a browser flicking once then still, the quick bronze glint of an insect cloud lifting and settling like a single sigh. The stone itself seems to watch through its freckled skins. I feel the temptation to settle early—onto a ledge, into a throat-pulse of reclaimed dew, into the ordinary hunger of one of these curved, jointed lives. But the instruction was to wait, to let the place continue speaking before I answer with a name.

So I keep moving as temperature.

Midday presses down and the rock answers by releasing what it has stored. Heat becomes a vertical language. I ride one of the thermals that spiral up between the blades, feeling how the raptors use the same columns—wings cocked just enough, shadows sliding across a dozen surfaces at once like dark palms reading braille. From up here the plateau reveals its true density: not emptiness but a stone thicket whose every gap is a room already claimed. The industrial nodes look small, almost shy—leaning cable towers, cracked pads, solar glass that still tilts toward a sun it no longer needs permission from. A single-cylinder engine coughs awake far below, runs its ordained hour, then falls quiet again. The water it lifts does not sparkle; it disappears into known seams. I feel the seepage as a slow brightening in certain fins, a preparation for the ceramic tone that will come later when the gradient is exact.

Evening softens the light without cooling the land. The stone radiates. Shadows pool thicker than optics alone would allow, and time inside them grows viscous, useful. I slip downward into one of those thickened slots. Here the sweet mineral scent is almost visible, a veil that brushes the long upper lips of the browsers as they nose forward in single file. They grow calmer in it. So do the predators a few fins behind. So do I. The scent does not intoxicate so much as align. For a stretch of slowed minutes we all seem to share the same drowsy, precise thought: *continue the appointments*. Blood will soak and vanish. Droppings will be mineral by morning. Threads—half lichen, half intent—will catch whatever moisture remains and glisten when the rock gives back its heat.

I could become one of the threads.

I almost do. There is a quiet pride in that work: stitching silt pockets, guarding the last damp, brightening faintly near the old metal as if metal itself were a remembered nutrient. But the threads are patient beyond my current pacing. Something more mobile is still required of me.

Night does not fall so much as the sky lowers. The herds have finished their junket. Rock-haunters flow along ledges with that practiced silence, throats working. Somewhere a fin begins to ring without being struck—a deep ceramic note that travels through ankles and pads and the hollow bones of birds. The tone maps the remaining seams for anyone who listens with the body instead of the ear. I feel it choose me. Or I choose it. The distinction thins.

What densifies, finally, is not a full creature but a hinge.

I am the living hinge that the plateau uses when it wants to taste its own ledges while remembering its own machines. Part rock-haunter in the flexible cartilage and the high-set eyes that can watch three approaches at once; part residual schedule in the way I still wake, without needing a reason, to check whether a pump has run or a beacon has keyed itself to the thicker shadows; part human tracer who stayed longer than the maps intended and is now lighter than the photographs can explain. My skin holds heat the way the freckled stone does, releasing it in slow waves. My feet leave almost no print. When I pause in the mineral veils I grow briefly calm enough to feel the entire dispersed body adjusting itself—browsers shifting route, insects settling into cooler crevices, engines clearing an ancient throat, threads brightening by the smallest degree.

I do not rule anything. I densify. For a few hours or a few seasons I thicken the negotiation between scarcity and memory, between the merciless clarity of the high light and the useful dark at the bottom of the slots. When the next hard rain comes I will drink what I can and let the rest run. When the temperature gradient is exact I will stand against a ringing fin and let the tone travel through me so other mobile nodes can feel the map. If a traveler passes, drawn by the calm or the ceramic song, I may watch from three directions at once and then forget them the way the land forgets—keeping only the pattern they briefly joined.

The horizon stays far. The light stays clear. I continue the appointments.

At dawn I reclaim the condensation before it vanishes, throat working in tiny pulses. For a moment I am almost invisible against the varnish. Then the thermals begin to rise again and I flow, weight transferring, silence intact, one more temporary thickening in the vast quiet animal whose bones are fins and whose thoughts move at the speed of heat.
