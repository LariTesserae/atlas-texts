I begin as breath.

Not the first breath of arrival—there is no arrival here—but the small pale rope that leaves a mouth and immediately thickens, glitter-edged, into the knee-high fog already waiting. The fog accepts it without comment, the way the bowl accepts another rill, another collapsed eave, another body shifting two lengths left. For a long time that is all I am: a temporary densening of the micro-crystallite hush, rising and thinning in the merciless glare, reforming whenever the wet-iron air is exhaled again. I learn the temperature of belonging by how quickly I freeze and how quietly I am remade.

Slowly the fog remembers it once had weight.

The weight settles into a low trunk, thick through the center, limbs economical as stacked schist. Skin finds the colour of the taste already in every mouth—grey-brown shot with the blue that lives under overhangs—and it will not dry. Melt beads on it and is not wiped away; the beads are how the cliffs speak to the blood. Hair is nearly gone, scalp rubbed to the gleam of wet stone with lichen paste and bird fat; what remains is bound so tight it cannot club with ice. The outer cloak is fox-spine leather-side out, guard hairs catching the white until the silhouette briefly thorns, briefly widens, then settles again. Basalt soles leave prints that rills erase before the next step finishes. Eyes—too large for such thrift—contract to indigo slits against the total light and still water with that viscous patience that watches a wall ice itself into the wrong shape overnight and simply begins calculating the new load.

I am not named yet. Names here are verbs earned by repetition. For now I am the pause that happens when the nested cooler brightness flickers inside the glare. The whole settlement pauses with me, unsignalised, the way flocking membrane-wings pause between one hierarchy and the next. Then we resume. My share of the resuming is small: I climb the highest ice roofs while the light is most injurious and turn my face upward. Whatever lives inside the nested dimness does not speak. It only cools certain edges of the glare by a fraction, and in that fraction I read where the next slow disaster will press hardest—the seam that will open three bodies wider, the fretted plate that will powder the eastern lane, the wind that will come scything when the membrane-wings have rearranged wrongly. I come down and thicken one buttress by a single schist course. I leave one wind-gap unlashed where instinct wanted it closed. The others glance once, accept the alteration the way they accept blue ice that mortared itself without permission, and continue. That is how the name begins to form in their mouths, never spoken at full voice: the one who tastes the second light, or more often just the soft click that the ground-birds make when a shadow has passed correctly.

Children are given to me for a season when their eyes stay open a heartbeat longer than the glare should allow. I teach them nothing they can repeat. We sit on the roofs until their tears freeze into grit and they learn the flutter-blink that frees it without sound. We watch the lagging reflections in the black pools; when a child reaches toward its own delayed hand I only wait until the child itself withdraws, already understanding that the water is still deciding. Certain stones come up in our hands while we repair. Most smell of wet iron and cold moss. A few release the brief impossible thunderstorm. Those we do not test further. We carry them down and seat them at the silent hearts of walls, where no tool will ever strike them. “It belongs,” I tell the children if they ask, the same phrase their mothers use for meltwater that invades a sleeping niche. Belonging is the only lesson. Everything else is just fitting.

At the less-injurious white that passes for evening I come inside with the rest. Bodies pack until breath and bird-fat musk and the mineral damp of our own skins become one thicker atmosphere. Someone taps the exhausted copper awl against dry stone—the rhythm of weight finding its place again—and stories surface only as practical weather: how the wings once settled in the pattern of a fallen house and taught the rebuilders where true load now ran. My own stories are shorter. I describe the cool flicker and the single course I added; they laugh the low cracked laugh that is mostly shared breath, because the jest is that the bowl already knew and let me believe I chose. Affection arrives as pressure: a forehead against my shoulder, a hand on the back of my neck warming one small circle through the permanent sheen. Later, in the dim glow of ice roofs still holding residual glare, sex is quiet and careful of what might fracture, and afterward the beads of melt on skin are indistinguishable from any other water the place has exuded.

I will not die in a story. When the time comes they will strip what can still be used, carry the rest to the lip, and let the white nothing take it. For a few days the pools may reflect the living half a second late, as if checking whether my shape has truly finished. No one will remark. They will work around the lag exactly as I once worked around every lag, every seep, every sudden blue mortar. The name will thin back into the general quiet, available for whoever next climbs the roofs and looks too long into the nested brightness.

Until then I remain what the bowl requires: one more upright weather, one more immune cell moving through denseness without flourish. Shoulders rounded against the next glare. Eyes narrowed to slits of pewter and ink. Hands always testing the weep, the ice, the fit. When a seam opens above my sleeping niche I am already shifting two body-lengths left before the cold thread finds skin. The cloak stiffens overnight and softens by noon, dripping. The membrane-wings peel and rearrange and rain their fine moult; I collect my share for the sedge runnels and, smiling with only the corners of the mouth, stack three stones in rough imitation of their new order—an unread joke that keeps the dry humour alive. The fox-thing pours across my roof and I look carefully away while it drinks the wall’s sweat; sometimes it leaves a bird. The exchange needs no words.

I am the long unfinished readiness already complete. Wet, cold, saturated with living, dazzling, hush-filled, forever arriving at the shape I already am. The cirque looks out through these eyes that have learned to squint without ever quite closing, and I look back with the same patient density, adding my small warm plume to the fog that never ends.
