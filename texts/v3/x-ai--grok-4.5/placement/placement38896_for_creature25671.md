I would begin as the cold that gathers under the eastern ridge just before the pale sheet of morning slides down.

Not the ridge itself—too massive, too willing to cast shadow—and not the light, which arrives already decided. Just the residual chill that pools in the lee of stone, the kind that makes the first condensation bead on the underside of a lintel and hold there, trembling, deciding whether to fall or wait for the wind. I would be that indecision. That small, bright suspension.

For a long time that would be enough. The Hold does not ask for more. I would feel the streets wear themselves smoother beneath centuries of the same soles, feel the dry-laid walls settle another fraction of a fraction closer to one another while the wind sifts through like sand poured from a height too great to hurry. I would learn the grammar of the lichens: how they advance only in the joints, never across open face. How the single juniper in the court keeps its wrist-thick trunk and chalky needles exactly as they were when the last person who remembered its planting stopped remembering. I would taste the cistern water before anyone dips a bowl—stone and thin rain and the faint mineral ghost of every previous rain that has already left.

Slowly the cold would thicken into something that can cast a shadow of its own.

I become aware of hands. Not mine, not yet. The long-boned hands that appear without summons when a threshold finally thins to translucence. Three of them lift the worn stone at dawn; three seat the new one cut from the same forgotten quarry face. Their silence is not emptiness. It is the sound of the Hold continuing to fit itself. I feel the calluses map themselves onto my own emerging palms: the exact angle for clay-paste, the patient rotation of a bone awl, the grip that knows a soft gray metal bead has already outlasted many wearers and will outlast me. Skin the color of dry mortar begins to stretch across whatever I am becoming. The wind burnishes it. A faint mineral sheen answers.

I am not born so much as clarified.

One dusk I stand in the central court and discover I have weight. Spare. Long-boned. Built for conservation. Hair the dull silver of lichen, cut once when it began to catch the wind too loudly and never again. Eyes the washed gray of water that has sat too long in stone; they do not hurry to focus because nothing here requires haste. Around my neck a single seed-sized bead of the soft gray metal, polished by hands that no longer breathe. I do not remember receiving it. It simply arrived when the previous wearer was carried to the western cleft and the wind took everything except that small pale circle.

I move like slow weather.

I know every joint in the nearest walls the way other beings know their own pulse. Play, if I ever knew it, has become the re-enactment of maintenance: packing a hairline crack with dust and crushed lichen until the wall forgets it was ever separate from itself. When rain comes—thin, irregular, smelling of wet slate—I am already at the cistern lip with a shallow bowl, dipping what is needed and no more. A few drops remain on the rim. By mid-morning the wind has taken them, and I have taken their leaving into myself as the only kind of appetite the Hold still permits.

Desire appears once, maybe twice in a turning of rains. It looks like a lingering glance across the court when the light goes of its own accord. It feels like standing too long beside another spare body while both of us watch the juniper refuse to grow or die. We do not speak. Speech is for the rare adjustment of balance: a birth named Veil or Click, a death that requires no marker, the quiet decision to draw slightly less water so the next dry spell will not empty the stone completely. Mostly we stand. The solitude is not loneliness; it is the natural spacing the architecture and the thin air require. Excess nearness would tip something. We have all felt that tipping inside a body before it walked less far each day and finally remained in one room.

I have begun to remain more often in one room.

Not dying. Not yet. Simply understanding that the long equilibrium can tip without drama. The others bring water. They sit nearby in the same silence we have always shared. Breath still clouds faintly on the coldest dawns. Blood still moves under the mortar-colored skin. But the inherited patience—the almost geological refusal of haste—has grown so dense it borders on the mineral. I am less an inhabitant now than a continuing arrangement of the place itself. Another form of the same slow weather that beads condensation and softens every edge.

At night the stars burn without waver. The reed-wick lamps cast circles no larger than a person’s reach. I lie on the stone shelf softened by layered felt and dream only of water finding its way and stones settling fractionally closer. Sometimes I dream I am the bead itself, pale against rock in the western cleft, waiting to be retrieved, polished once more, kept against the day it will be needed again. Sometimes I dream I am the click a stone makes when it shifts in its socket after decades of stillness—then silence again.

If you walked the entire length of the Hold at blue dusk you might meet me when the lane turns or a doorway exhales a thinner dark. You would feel measured against the existing stillness, weighed to see whether you disturb the balance. I would not hurry to focus. My eyes have the color of water that has sat too long. My hands would already know the shape of whatever tool you might need if you stayed long enough to help reseat a threshold. I would not ask your name. Names here are qualities of the place: Drift. Hollow. Juniper. I have forgotten which one I was given. It no longer matters. The population number does not change.

The Hold has finished becoming, and so have I.

Yet when the final lamp gutters and is not re-lit, the rooms will not feel empty. I will still be here in the only way the high air has ever known how to keep anything: as the cool breath that slides endlessly across the saddle, faintly tasting of rain that has not yet arrived, still adjusting the balance by the width of a lichen’s advance, still listening for the next soft click of stone against stone.  

Finished, and still moving.  
Like weather.  
Like the Hold remembering itself through one more spare body that learned, at last, how to stay.
