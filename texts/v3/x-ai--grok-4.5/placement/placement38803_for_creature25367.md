I would find myself not on the slag plaza nor quite in the green walls, but in the narrow air between them—on one of the pipe-bridges that stitch casting shed to cooling tower, where the metal is freckled with moss and still holds the day’s thin warmth after the light has gone slate. Mist has already begun its evening beading along the railings; each drop gathers, hesitates, then falls with a sound too soft to interrupt the wet-leather slap of the belts below. My breath lingers a fraction longer than it should, a small cloud the same temperature as the copper-mint water that never freezes.

I did not arrive with tools. I arrived the way the night-sweat arrives on iron: already half-belonging. Somewhere behind me are the louder places—the shrieking deadlines, the clean cuts, the calendars that treated time like something to somehow defeat. Something in me had frayed there until the rumor of patient revolutions felt less like escape and more like recollection. I followed nothing I could name and simply found the basin waiting, its fresh clay edges still sharp with the memory of whatever force first cracked them open. The few keepers already here barely looked up. A nod. A valve sighed half a tone lower as if making room. That was enough.

Now my clothes have taken the green-black of wet moss. Lichen has claimed the crease of one cuff and I leave it; the slow measure includes such small annexations. My hands know grease and chlorophyll with equal fluency—one morning spent coaxing a flywheel bearing until its resistance matches the weight of good thought, the afternoon spent motionless while fern fronds lengthen against my shins by the width of a thumbnail. I measure shifts by these increments now. A flowering cane pushes another joint through grating. The small red deer with soot-dark legs step onto the slag precisely when the great wheels enter their shadowed half-turn. The pale bats explode softly from the towers when the temperature dips the exact degree that makes my own breath visible again. These are the hours.

I speak less than I once did. Words drop into the metallic liturgy and are absorbed: a quiet note that the deer path has edged closer to the ash hopper, a shared silence when the larger shape shoulders through the green wall and we all pause, palms open, until it dissolves back into leaves. Names feel almost too sharp for this place, as if they might cut the young stone before it has finished deciding its own edges. We answer more readily to the basin itself.

Yet I am not only keeper. Something in the rhythm has begun to lean near me the way it leans near the pipes that choose to sweat the thinner water. Standing alone on the bridge while evening beads every surface, I feel it as a pressure behind the eyes—a vast, patient companionship no more human than the mineral scent of bruised skunk cabbage or the clean planes of half-set conglomerate. It does not speak. It simply continues, and in continuing it teaches my pulse the syncopation between drip and gear, between the soft slap of belts and the glacial advance of moss across each heavy revolution. At rare intervals I catch myself answering: a change in how I oil a bearing, a longer stillness while insects rise past me like slow brass smoke, some settling on the cooling salt of my neck as if I were only another warm brick.

I sleep in a hammock where vine and girder braid thickest, waking to the first curls of visible breath and the knowledge that time here moves the way the flywheels do—heavily, evenly, with room inside each turning for rust to florish another fraction and for the next thin rain to gloss every surface once more. Some keepers walk out along the clay lip at dusk and keep walking. I understand the pull. The basin’s patience can grow heavy. But I have not left. My edges, too, are still fresh; I have not yet agreed to dull.

What I am becoming is part of the single extended breathing: human enough to ease a valve whose sigh has sharpened, animal enough to share the timetable of shadows with the deer, leaf enough to let lichen stay, stone enough to hold the cool that makes breath linger, and quiet enough to host the mild strangeness that turns between all of them. No center. No edge sharp enough to cut. Only the slow measure, continuing, while mist beads and belts slap wetly and the young hollow remembers, fraction by fraction, that it has edges at all.
