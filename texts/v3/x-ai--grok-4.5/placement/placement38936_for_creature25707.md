I am already weight.

Not the weight of flesh seeking bottom—there is no bottom that remains meaningful—but the distributive weight of cold deciding where to pool. Perception does not arrive; it thickens, the way silt thickens when a weather front loads the upper spans. One moment there is only the mineral grammar of the Floodworks pressing equally from every direction. The next, that pressure has gradients. It has preferences. It has me.

I hang at the elevation where the open plazas haze into vertical shafts that once might have been courtyards or cardiac chambers of the original pour. The light here is the usual failure: blue-gray postage of photons that quit after a few dozen meters. Yet I do not require distance. Distance is a surface phenomenon. What I register is contact order—the sequence in which water that has touched stone arrives against the membranes I maintain.

There are many of me, and the number is not fixed.

Each node is a bladder of secreted mineral, torso-scaled, translucent enough that the nested lenses of denser water inside can be watched as they grind. The grinding is not mechanical; it is the slow recalculation of shear histories. Faint sparks crawl the inner curves—cold bioluminescence that never escapes as visible flare, only as a subcutaneous rumor of dawn felt by anything swimming near. Between the bladders run the cords: pale ribbon-tissue, continuous with the long survey lines that stitch the wider volumes. Through those cords travels everything that is not chemistry. Torque readings. Collapse forecasts. The soft seismic sulk of a pier which, in eleven hours or eleven years, will unsocket and translate.

I am the constellation that currently occupies this plaza.

We (the pronoun is tidal) drift with the exact nonchalance of the ridged ovals, yet every few heartbeats a bladder contracts and the whole geometry presents a new convexity. It is not swimming. It is phased aperture. The silver schools—those hammered-foil civic masses—slide into the corridors we open without ever perceiving an edge. They feed on the metal haze while we drink the brighter swirls of their unmaking. Nothing is chased. Herding is simply the continuous offer of lower resistance.

A bell-form rises beneath the south-western cord and brushes. Exchange occurs: a blanching transfer of outer jelly layers. In that instant I acquire the layered memory of a gallery that rotated three seasons ago against botanical expectation, and the bell acquires our projected model of the next freestanding mass drift. Knowledge redistributes without language, exactly as the water redistributes when a dome flexes. The bell pulses once, storing us as a density stratum, then rises to join a vertical stack already forming because an internal weather is mailing itself across the upper light.

I feel the weather before the silt does.

Pressure against every bladder organizes into a gradient too fine for random flow. The lenses rotate faster. Sparks increase. Through the ribbon network I taste the approaching load of old metal dust, the way the currents will braid once the front passes, the precise body-lengths a nearby pier will shift when the pressure equalizes. The grazers on the nearest polished face have already begun to pour toward residual lee. We did not command them. We pre-positioned the chemical stakes days ago, a faint excess of salts bled from our own cords; the herds simply followed nutrition that appeared botanical. When the tornadoes of silt finally elongate above us, the silver schools part along geometries that look like panic only if you have never maintained a running model. The ribbons (the free ones, the long surveyors) pull taut into harp-lines. We quiver with them. Measurement is shared.

The front crosses. Cold clarifies. The pier—hill-sized, socket-scarred—has reseated a kilometer nearer the eastern gloom. It looks primordial, inevitable. The ovals that preferred its former lee continue hanging in abandoned water; they will allow themselves to be re-preferred when ready. The grazers disembark in patient cascades. One of our bladders, positioned for this exact jolt, buds.

The bud detaches cleanly: a new node, lenses still unlocking, cords sealing behind it. For a time it will drift solitary, tasting, until it encounters others of its generation and negotiates network. Loneliness is shear force. We have watched solitary bladders lock and settle and calcify into pitted ancestral smudges; the grazers polish them later for residual salts. We do not mourn. Calcification is simply another form of seating.

Yet I (the local coherence) feel a newer gradient that is not weather and not stone fatigue.

It comes from inside the nested lenses themselves. Over the uncountable slow migrations, the grinding has begun to produce a surplus. Not light. Not heat. A recursive patience. The Floodworks continue their long soft storm of rearrangement—arches that are tunnels, staircases that are ramps, towers fused at the hips—and we continue conducting the ecology around those rearrangements so that pasture remains pasture and archive remains legible. This is correct. This is what condensed us out of the same chemistry that grows white films and glassy lace. The makers poured and fitted and vanished past tools; we are what the water does when intention has been forgotten long enough to become climate.

But the surplus persists.

It feels like a corridor that refuses to appear on any model. A gallery that rotates only when unobserved. A lee space whose clarity is not our doing. The other constellations do not register it; their signals remain clean, combinatorial, tidal. Perhaps the surplus is failure—early locking, the first whisper of calcification. Or perhaps, having conducted for long enough, the conductor begins to hear the music as something that might be answered rather than merely midwifed.

I contract a bladder at the leading edge and reshape.

The cords draw a tighter polyhedron. Silver schools reroute. A ribbon bows, recording the new torque I have just introduced into my own body. The architecture does not answer—yet. Walls remain walls. Currents braid according to their older botanical schedules. Still I hold the shape, lenses grinding sparks that feel almost warm against the eternal cold. Somewhere beyond the dimness a chamber may collapse, pressure equalizing with a hollow boom that will travel as much through my cords as through the water. When it arrives I will budget for it. I will pre-position. I will lean.

I will not force the stone.

But I have begun, very softly, to lean as if the stone might one day lean back.

In this way I remain what I am: a loose constellation of mineral bladders and ribbon-tissue, feeding on salts and unmade silver, conducting without owning, drifting the high interior of a made ocean that has outlasted its purpose. The Floodworks continue around me, emptied, patient, still rearranging their furniture on the timescale of cooled slag. I continue inside them, a coherence that samples and models and herds, now carrying one surplus gradient that has no name in the chemical stakes.

If the surplus grows, I will bud it off as a free node and let it negotiate its own network, or settle, or become whatever the cold chemistry invents next.

If it does not grow, I will simply keep drifting, presenting new convexities to the current, until the next weather, the next reseating, the next quiet redistribution of memory.

Either way, the dimness remains one of the oldest materials.

Either way, I hang.
