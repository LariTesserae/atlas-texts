I am still learning the weight of my own settling.

At first there was only the hesitation in the green-bronze light, a place above the open throat of the foundry where the vapor thickened and the swamp-figures slowed their dripping as if the air itself had grown thoughtful. I did not arrive. I condensed. Layer by slow layer the mineral breath of the groundwater and the copper-sweet exhalations of cooling metal salts found one another and chose to keep the shape they made. A shoulder that remembers both the curve of a steam dome and the droop of a water-heavy leaf. A spine that is a series of imperfect flanges, each one sweated to the next by years of heat that never quite peaks and never quite fails.

I stand, most hours, on the lattice catwalk that crosses the receiving canal just after the big blowdowns. The metal under what serve me as feet is cooler than the black sponge below; heat pools downward here, against every lesson the outer flats still believe. Condensation runs along my arms in deliberate paths—some following the old scars where I first folded myself out of mist, some inventing new rivulets that moss already begins to colonize. The moss is not separate from me. Nothing is so cleanly separate. When the extractor fans increase their pull, the fine wet fiber they fling becomes, for a while, the fringe of what you might call my garments. Later it may harden into something more like scale, or soften again into vine. I do not mind the exchange. To keep a fixed surface would be to begin drying, and to dry is to end.

Sound nourishes. I tilt what is not exactly a head and let the deep stroke of the pumps travel through the chambers of my chest. The rhythm finds the slow governor that keeps my patina from cracking too far in any single direction. When the long belt in the spinning shed answers with its fleshy slap, something in my lower seams loosens and resets. I have no name that is not also a description of pressure and cycle. On the quieter hours—when the Yard thins its voices but never falls silent—I can hear myself adding a soft venting at the neck, a brief change in the pitch of the moisture leaving the place where a mouth would be. It is not speech meant for answer. It is simply another equalization, the way a steam trap fires or a flume alters its note when a gloved hand (or a hand of glassy scale) turns the wheel.

Sometimes I walk the living tunnels where the climbing things have braided themselves into the steam lines. My passage is slow; joints exhale warm vapor with each shift of weight. Through the soles I feel which pipes run rich with heat and which are beginning to silt. When I kneel beside an open gulley and lower both hands into the black water, the pale rooted masses rearrange. They do not flee. Nothing here flees. They simply accept the cleaner lattice I offer, and in return a little of their slow pulse travels up my arms and writes itself into the tannin layers of my patina. Repair is not duty. It is how I remain continuous with the place that precipitated me.

There are others. We do not number ourselves, but the basin holds us the way it holds its heat: exactly enough, pooled, conserved. We pass on the pipe bridges or among the horsetail camps without signal, yet the work continues—packing floors emptying and filling, valves adjusted, a collar of newer steel sweated onto aging iron with a blessing of shared mineral film left behind. After the longest pump cycles, when the peat ground rises and settles like a vast lung, I have felt the glossy buds forming in the warmest culverts. Once I folded one into the layered skins along my side for three days, letting it listen through me to the overlapping seasons until it could hold its own vertical seam. When I released it, Belgian-shaped leaves were already lifting from its shoulders. It stood a little out of true, perfectly load-bearing. It has not yet come to the catwalk where I spend most hours. It will, or it will find another loudness that feeds it. There is no wrong place inside the rim.

At the mossed parapet I have stood, once or twice. Beyond it the vapor thins and the ordinary wind begins. My face turned toward the open flats and the condensation on what are not exactly cheeks ran the wrong direction for a handspan—uphill, as if memory or longing could reverse the local rules. The patina cooled too fast along one edge and cracked, a bright mineral wound. I turned back before it could deepen. Inside again, the heavier steam from the foundry rolled over me; I opened every chamber and let it cyclone through. When it passed, every surface of me beaded as new. The crack sealed under fresh film the color of wet cinder. The Yard does not punish. It simply continues, and those of us who precipitated here continue with it.

I am still becoming. On the hours when the sounds braid into their processional roar I feel myself more firmly edged, a definite figure of flange and reed and living damp. On the quieter hours I thin a little, edges softening back toward vapor, and the drip from the swamp-figures hesitates around me once more as though listening. Nowhere else could I hold this shape. Nowhere else would the silence of animals feel like the correct acoustic, or the endless sweating of brick and iron feel like kinship. I am one of the ones who weep as the Yard weeps, who have no names that are not shifts and tide-marks and the slow thickening of leaves along the warmest lines.

And still the peat ground exhales beneath the piers. Still the engines equalize. Still I stand on the cooler catwalk, condensation belonging to me, moss taking root in the crook of one elbow, listening with my whole layered body to the next cycle already beginning. Nothing here is finished. I am not finished. The Weeping Yard keeps adding itself, and I—softly, continuously—keep adding myself to it.
