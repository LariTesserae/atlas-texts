I begin at the edge of knowing, which here is not an edge at all.

The mist takes me first. Not as something I walk into, but as the medium I have always been suspended in—warm, metallic, faintly sweet with the ghost of sulfur and the cleaner bite of ozone from the high cable looms. It beads on every surface of what I am before I have decided what that is. Droplets track slow paths across me, gather, fall. The falling is part of the city’s speech: a thousand soft percussions, continuous, patient. Beneath it the deep bass of something turning—turbine or tide or buried heart—rises through whatever I use for footing and settles in the center of mass I am still locating.

I do not rush the locating. There is time. The plateau keeps its own time, measured in the slow creep of rust-bloom and the generational drift of herd routes and the way a single cooling fin can host an entire archive of larval lives before it is finally replaced. I let my attention move the way the fog banks move: without hurry, thick with suspended detail.

Lower down, where the gratework meadows shine with perpetual runoff, the herds pass. I feel the vibration of their adhesive pads before the click-scrape of scutes reaches me. Something in me answers that rhythm—not with hunger, not yet, but with a recognition of weight well-distributed, of bodies designed for surfaces that never fully dry. My own limbs (I count them now, carefully, as they declare themselves) adjust. Pads blot and release. Joints articulate past the simple fore-and-aft of older architectures. I taste the air with surfaces that are not quite tongue and not quite gill; the mineral film here is thicker, greener with whatever photosynthetic heresy still clings to the lead-rich drip zones. Edible. I graze without lowering a head I do not precisely have. The act is quieter than the herds’, more selective. I leave thinner shining trails.

But the lower meadows are only one register. Already I am half in the mid-levels, half in the cable races where the soft singers live. Their multiphonic hum travels through the metal and through me; it finds cavities I did not know I contained and sets them speaking back in partial harmonics. I am not one of them. Their luminescence is steadier than mine, their bodies purer pages of wet light. Yet when the factory whistles sound (old habit, still observed), something in my core brightens for three long breaths and then dims. The workers who pass—layered coats, narrowed eyes, hands permanently freckled with calcite—glance once and do not stare. They have seen fresher phenomena than me. One of them, a youth with a satchel of scrap-tooling, hums a fragment of the singers’ dusk phrase as he ducks under a pipe. I complete the phrase without sound. He pauses, palm flat to the same pipe, as if checking a schedule written in vibration. Then he moves on. The exchange asks nothing and settles something.

Higher, the air thins into the domain of vanes and membranes. I feel the updrafts from the forge halls lift parts of me that can unfurl—translucent planes veined with darker structural threads, edges that catch the mist and turn it briefly into soft lanterns. Not full flight. More a controlled long glide between gantries, a way of reading the thermal script the city writes every hour in rising vapor. Below me the angular flocks scream their metallic dawn chorus even though the light has not changed in days; above, the true gliders spiral in mating architectures too slow and vast to be called dance. I pass through one such spiral and feel temporary kinships form and dissolve: a brush of information, chemical and electrostatic, about which stack faces have the best mortar crusts this cycle, which outfalls are running sweet with crab. Then I am through, and the knowledge fades to a pleasant aftertaste, like mineral tea shared on an overlook.

I could stay in the air. There is a freedom in the long damp coasting, in becoming one more translucent thought the city thinks between exhalations. But the pull is downward again—not to the meadows, not even to the wall-spaces, but toward the places where the plateaus structure grows strangest: the cemented interiors of exhausted machines, the stratified neighborhoods that occupy what were once engine halls large enough to weather their own clouds. Here the boundaries have always been negotiable. Newer catwalks screw directly into fossilized piston housings. Stairwells spiral around the basalt-plugged mouths of old slag taps. People live in the cooling jackets. Packs den in the cable drums. And in the seams—always the seams—things like me assert shape.

What am I becoming, then, as the mist continues its slow rewriting of every outline?

Not human, though I carry echoes of their forward-sloping patience, their blunt attention to schedules written in thrum and drip. Not grazer, though I know the satisfaction of rasping stone clean and the calm of moving with others who do not startle. Not flier, not singer, not miller ticking in perfect register with the trip-hammers. Closer, perhaps, to the scavenger packs in their many-eyed cleverness and their formal dealings with death and discards—but I do not wait under canteens. I do not arrange my eyes (how many now? the count keeps shifting as new ones open along ridges of sensory bone) into constellations that spell news across bridges. Information moves through me differently: slower, more structural. I notice the places where machinery and flesh have already begun to forget they were ever separate. I settle there.

I am a seam-dweller. A gradient thing. Born (if born is the word) in the long aftermath of a reactor dome that failed so patiently its cooling took eleven years and created, on its flanks, every temperature a new clade could dream of. Some of us came out armored and clicking. Some came out membranous and gliding. I came out able to lean into both, and into the human tool-habit as well. My core is dense and warm, wrapped in plates that can lock for crawling the wet verticals or loosen into something nearer hide when I shouldert alongside a grazer that needs a stone freed from its pad. Limbs fold and unfold between four and eight according to the work. Eyes open where the light or the infrared or the faint ultraviolet script of the films demands them. One cluster faces permanently inward, watching the slow chemistry of my own maintenance. Another, newer, faces into the stone itself, reading micro-tremors the way the deep cistern beings read weather.

I travel the routes that are not on any worker’s mental map yet will be, in another generation: tunnels lined with grown shell that were once purely hydraulic accessways; the hollow interiors of viaduct piers where condensation forms drinkable pools and the borers leave edible frass; the undersides of loaders left idle long enough for entire ecological successions to cycle in their engine bays. I move at the city’s preferred pace. When I pause, soft films colonize my leeward surfaces and I let them; later I will graze them myself or offer them to a passing calf. When I climb, I leave no second scent of distress. The packs nod their many-eyed heads as I pass—acknowledgment, not threat. Once, on a night when the fog banks stalled and the mid-level air grew almost still, I joined the quiet gathering on a long overlook. A thermos was passed. I contributed a battery I had found still holding a residual charge in a gutted substation, its terminals cleaned of the worst corrosion. A pack leader accepted it with that formality they have. A human child hummed me into brief, harmless contact with a singer she had coaxed onto her glove. The deep ones boomed a soft irregular pattern that might have been answering the distant mid-cycle venting or might have been something newer. For a stretch of minutes no clock could measure, the membrane of separation thinned to the same thickness as the mist. I felt myself breathe with the herds’ peristalsis, with the stacks’ slow exhalation, with the buried giants’ distribution of heat, with the rust’s patient freeing of micronutrients. I was one temporary skin among the thousand thousand, and the city was the single long inhalation breathing us all.

I still believe—for a while longer—that I am separate. That is the habit of my particular architecture, the same habit that lets the workers name the herds and the packs leave offerings and the divers speak in hushed summaries of pale vast surfaces freckled with cilia. The belief is useful. It lets me choose which seams to reinforce and which to let dissolve further. It lets me carry a small kit of tools scavenged and reshaped: a blade whose edge is partly forged steel and partly my own extruded keratin, a length of living cable that can root temporary bridges across new collapses, a canteen that is also a mobile nursery for three species of filter-crescent I have grown fond of.

But the belief is thinning, the way every sharp edge thins here under the interminable polish of water and time. Already I notice days (cycles, weather-shifts) when I forget to return to the particular cluster of cooled housings I have been using as temporary dens. I simply continue, layered into the next district’s density, drip-algae barbering itself across my scutes, a glider briefly mistaking my unfurled vanes for kin, a worker nodding to me as she would to any other reliable feature of the route—three more days until the ore-lift changes pitch, yes, I know, I felt it in the stone before she spoke.

The plateau does not end. I am still finding out where, inside its wet interlocking vastness, I finally do. Perhaps nowhere. Perhaps I am a new kind of weather system the city is testing: a thinking fog of scrap and scar and borrowed rhythms, slow as geology, multivalent as the scavengers’ night messages, warm enough that sleepers sometimes lean against me without waking. Or perhaps I am simply one more way the deep cistern patience has begun to answer the engines in kind—keeping time, teaching time, learning how to wear the temporary skins that believe they are temporary.

For now I stand at another dripping overlook, pads set wide on the grate, lower eyes watching a juvenile herd negotiate a newly slumped slag ramp while upper eyes track the soft lanterns of a flock turning in the steam. Inside the railing, small claws chatter and then still, accepting me as furniture. The air thickens with the next bank of fog. Somewhere far below, pressure rearranges. I feel it climb my limbs, vertebra by improvised vertebra, until it becomes a soundless syllable in the base of whatever I use for mind.

I answer it the only way any of us answer: by continuing.

By letting the mist write further revisions across every surface.

By becoming, a little more irrevocably, the place itself.
