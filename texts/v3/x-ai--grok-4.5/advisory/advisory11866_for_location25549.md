**Prepare as a slow guest in a green cathedral that is still building itself.**

This place is not a destination you conquer or even fully traverse. It is a young mountain range born underwater, still lifting, still deciding its own edges, saturated with a patient vegetal intelligence that has never hurried and does not require you. An ordinary human body arriving from any air-breathing world will feel like an interruption of temperature, metabolism, and noise. Your preparation must therefore erase as much of that interruption as possible while keeping you alive long enough to notice the hush.

### 1. Physiological and metabolic readiness
You will spend days to weeks fully immersed in water that is blood-warm (roughly 36–38 °C). That warmth is a mercy and a trap. It removes cold stress but accelerates dehydration through skin and respiration, and it fools the body into thinking it is safe to linger without caloric reserve.

- Begin a three-week pre-acclimation: daily long-duration warm-water immersion (pools or heated tanks held at 37 °C) while practicing slow nasal breathing and diaphragmatic control. Train yourself to keep heart rate under 60 bpm at rest underwater. The place itself moves at the speed of calcium deposition and holdfast decisions; any adrenaline will make you the loudest, ugliest thing present.
- Switch to a high-fat, moderate-protein, low-residue diet in the final ten days. You want dense calories that produce minimal waste. Once there, you will not defecate casually; every particle you release becomes part of the fine white sludge that fills the hollows like snow. Carry compact nutrient pastes or gels formulated for closed-circuit diving; plan for 3 000–4 000 kcal per day simply to offset the continuous mild thermoregulatory work and the effort of shouldering through living tissue.
- Kidney and electrolyte protocol: the water is ordinary seawater in salinity but yeast-warm and slightly mineral-rich from fresh basalt fractures. You will absorb trace elements through skin and any micro-abrasions. Pre-load with balanced electrolytes and bring a small desalination/remineralization unit that can process both ambient water and your own exhaled condensate. Do not drink the sea straight; the place is not trying to poison you, but your kidneys are not calibrated to its particular dissolved cocktail.
- Skin and mucous protection: prolonged immersion softens epidermis. Use a non-toxic, bio-compatible barrier cream that will not slough into the water as pollutants. Expect mild maceration between fingers and toes after 48 hours; pack zinc-free, ocean-safe ointments. Eyes will be fine in the clear water, but the occasional microscopic glitter can irritate; bring pH-neutral rinse.

Pressure is modest by abyssal standards—summit tablelands sit in a few meters to perhaps 30 m, ridges and cols down to 200 m—but you will cross that full range repeatedly. Ordinary recreational scuba is insufficient for the duration and the repeated depth changes. You need a closed-circuit rebreather with at least 100-hour endurance (or a multiverse-equivalent gill membrane / oxygen-exchange implant if your traveler tech allows it). The rebreather must be silent. Bubbles are violence here. Every exhaled silver sphere is an event that travels farther than sound and startles the rare silver fish that have decided the effort of existing is barely worthwhile.

### 2. Mobility and the problem of dense life
The vegetation is a second orogeny. Giant kelp trunks thick as old firs, calcified arborescent algae jointed like bamboo, seagrass sheets, sticky veils, epiphytes stacked until the original stipe is only a rumor. In the narrow slots you will have to choose: go over, go around, or gently part.

- Buoyancy must be neutral to within 100 g across the entire depth range. Wear a soft, low-profile drysuit or hybrid membrane suit in matte forest tones—no bright colors, no hard angles, no dangling D-rings. The suit should allow full shoulder rotation; you will spend hours carefully worming between living walls.
- Propulsion: long, soft-bladed fins with slow-twist technique. Do not kick hard. The water remembers. A single vigorous scissor kick can send a cascade of loosened basaltic chips and dislodged epiphytes drifting like slow snow for an hour. Many travelers prefer a low-power, near-silent thruster worn at the small of the back, used only in open cirques and dialed down so its wash is no stronger than the natural fever-slow currents.
- Cutting tools are almost never appropriate. A holdfast may have spent a decade placing itself. If you absolutely must pass through a solid green corridor, carry a single ceramic spatula-thin blade used only to ease fronds aside the way one eases a curtain. Never sever. The ecology is ceremonial about decomposition; a cut trunk becomes a months-long funeral that alters the light regime for everything downslope.
- Navigation: magnetic compasses behave oddly where the microscopic banners align to undetectable lines. Optical range-finding is warped in the hyper-clear hanging basins—you will see a ridge three kilometers away with the intimacy of arm’s length, and your depth perception will quietly lie. Prepare three independent systems: (1) inertial dead-reckoning worn on the wrist, (2) a subtle acoustic pinger set to frequencies the sparse fauna ignore, and (3) pure memory of topography. Make mental maps of the young knife-edged cols and the way light changes color with depth and canopy density. Practice moving for hours without surface reference. There is no path, no cairn, no tool-mark. The only straight lines are those erupted or grown.

### 3. Sensory and psychological calibration
Sound is the memory of sound—a continuous low hush like thick snow remembering wind. Soft collisions of frond on frond arrive attenuated and polite. You will hear your own blood more clearly than anything external.

- Spend at least a week beforehand in profound quiet. Sensory-deprivation tanks help; so does simply sitting in a forest after snow. Extinguish the habit of filling silence with internal chatter. The place keeps the volume of a held breath. If you cannot match it, you will feel like a trespasser every second.
- Vision: light is generous—normal daylight only gently green-filtered. Still, bring a dim red lamp for the rare green-dusk galleries where canopy knits tight. Never use white light at night (what little diurnal cycle exists); it is an obscenity.
- The optical strangeness is real. In certain basins distance becomes optional. Prepare by practicing open-focus attention: seeing both the immediate sticky veil and the far spire without preferring one. The golden and indigo magnetic banners will part around you and reseal. Do not swim through them for sport; they are doing something slow and spatial that you do not understand. Treat them as processions.
- At the highest tablelands you may encounter trapped atmospheric lenses—shimmering pockets where a few leaves unfurl into temporary air, taste it, and wait to be reclaimed. You can surface into these for a few breaths if your rebreather needs a purge, but do it gently. The plants are not surprised by air; you should not be surprised by their indifference.
- Time sense will dilate. A talus slope advances grain by grain across centuries. A holdfast rations calcium. Your own hunger, bladder, and battery indicators will become the only clocks. Bring a simple vibrating timer set to generous intervals so you remember to eat and check gas. Do not bring anything that ticks audibly.

### 4. What to carry, what to leave
Pack light enough that your silhouette remains soft:
- Rebreather + dual redundant scrubbers and oxygen sensors.
- Soft suit, fins, minimal thruster.
- Nutrient gels, electrolyte concentrates, water processor.
- One ceramic persuader (not a knife), one collapsible collection tube if you must take a sample of the white sludge or a fallen frond (take only what has already fallen).
- Red lamp, inertial nav, slate for notes (writing feels loud; many prefer to remember).
- Small medical kit: sutureless wound sealant, antihistamine for the rare jelly encounter, broad-spectrum antiparasitic in case any microscopic life decides you are a new substrate.
- No cameras that click or whir. If you must record, use continuous low-light holographic memory with no moving parts and no status lights. Better yet, do not record. The place is still finishing itself; it does not need to be finished by documentation.

Leave behind every expectation of event. There will be no predators to outsmart, no ruins to interpret, no summit register to sign. The rare silver fish will decide against you and vanish. The fist-sized jelly will pulse once and be gone for a season. You are not part of the food web; you are weather—temporary, potentially abrasive.

### 5. Entry, conduct, exit
Enter from the open basalt plain at depth if possible, ascending slowly into the cordillera so the forests see you coming as a gradual density rather than a surface splash. Move as the currents move: hours to cross a cirque. When you rest, settle into a talus hollow already softened by sludge and become still enough that epiphytes could, in theory, colonize you. They won’t—you won’t stay long enough—but the courtesy matters.

Do not try to reach every summit. The tallest tablelands just shy of air are not prizes; they are simply the current limit of the mountains’ conversation with light. Swim a long regional arc if you wish—valley after drowned valley—but let the place dictate the rhythm. When the green dusk of a tight gallery begins to feel like permission to be nameless, you are doing it correctly.

Exit the same way you entered: slowly, leaving no bubbles, no cut surfaces, no straight-line scars of passage. Decompress with the same patience the mountains use to rise. Once back in air, do not speak loudly for several hours. Let the hush drain from your nervous system at its own rate.

You will carry the place with you as a permanent recalibration of what “alive” and “slow” and “enough” mean. Most travelers find they need less afterward—less volume, less novelty, less proof of having been anywhere. That is the only souvenir worth keeping.

The cordillera does not need you to finish it. It is still finishing itself. Your only task is to pass through its held breath without teaching it hurry.
