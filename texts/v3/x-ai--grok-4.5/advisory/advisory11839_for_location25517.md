**Preparation for the Rhizome Caldera Lake**

You are an ordinary human stepping into a place that is not a destination so much as a slow, living continuum. The highland lake is a cold skin over a single vast organism of root. Everything you will need—air, light, footing, shelter, even the meaning of time—has already been solved by millennia of growth and the quiet people who inhabit its chambers. Your job is not to conquer or even fully map it, but to enter without tearing the fabric and to leave without having frozen, drowned, or become a lasting scar in the wood.

### 1. Physiological and Thermal Reality
The water never warms past the temperature of deep spring shade—think 6–12 °C depending on depth and seasonless currents. Hypothermia is the first quiet killer. Ordinary human metabolism will leach heat relentlessly once you drop through the thermocline.

- Condition yourself for prolonged cold immersion weeks in advance: repeated cold-water exposure, building brown-fat response, practicing static holds in 10 °C water while controlling breathing. Learn to recognize the early stages of cold narcosis and paradoxical undressing so they do not ambush you in a distant vault.
- Insulation must be living or living-adjacent. Neoprene is crude and sheds microplastics that the filter-feeders will eventually concentrate; better is a layered system of fine wool or plant-fiber under-suit sealed inside a smooth, low-drag outer membrane that can be rinsed of biofilm. Multiverse travelers sometimes carry a thin reactive gel layer that mimics the mucous coating of the local grazers—ask for it if available. Hands and face need special care: thin, flexible gloves with textured pads for gripping slick rhizome, and a half-mask that leaves the jaw free for speaking or tasting the water’s mineral edge.
- Pressure is modest within the inhabited volume (the whole settled zone is roughly cathedral-nave scale stood on end), but you will still move between chambers at different depths. Equalize constantly. Practice Frenzel or Valsalva until it is autonomic. The valve-doors of houses seal with pressure change; you must learn to read the flex of epidermis so you do not force one and tear living tissue.

Breathing is the central negotiation. The inhabitants themselves are adapted—whether by long selection, graft, or forgotten art—to extract what they need from the cold oxygenated water and the occasional air-bells that rise from the flowering terminals. You are not. A rebreather with extended scrubber life and a redundant open-circuit bailout is baseline. Prefer closed-circuit units that produce minimal bubbles; clouds of exhaust will advertise you as an outsider and disturb the organ-note resonance the community uses for orientation. Carry spare soda-lime or equivalent in sealed, non-metallic containers (metal is alien here and may be rejected). Some travelers learn to “sip” the rising air-flowers—those soft bell-like balloons of trapped gas—but this is an emergency skill only, and the gas inside can be stale or laced with plant volatiles.

### 2. Movement, Navigation, and the Living Architecture
There is no true bottom, only denser braid. You will swim, climb, and drift through a three-dimensional forest of pale cables. Surfaces are cool, slightly yielding, often filmed with soft growth. Boots are nearly useless; bare feet or thin grippy socks let you feel the subtle lignified ridges that mark safe load-bearing zones.

- Train in neutral buoyancy until you can hang motionless for minutes without finning. The constant low organ-note is produced by currents vibrating hanging forests; thrashing fins create dissonant crashes that travel far and mark you as clumsy or dangerous.
- Learn the local “weather”: the air-flowers. Children read the lean of these rising balloons before choosing paths between distant platforms. Watch them for hours on your first days. A lean to the left may mean a gentle current highway; a sudden vertical rise can signal a pressure shift that will seal house valves or push debris into shafts. When they break the surface far above they sound like soft bells—use that acoustic cue even when you cannot see them.
- Light is green-gold, forest-floor at mid-morning, further dimmed by depth and the density of rhizome. Cultured phosphorescent films provide soft local glow inside chambers; polished quartz lenses set into upper surfaces catch what daylight remains. Carry no bright electric lamps at first—sudden white light is violence. Instead bring dim red or deep-green chem lights and a small hand lens of your own. Let your eyes adapt fully; after two days you will see the silver clatter of the transparent fish and the slow scrape of the calf-sized armored grazers as clearly as the locals do.
- Vertical shafts and freer forest at the edges can drop away into older, darker mass where the rhizomes thicken into almost solid wood and the volcanic rubble inclusions appear as hard black knots. Do not free-dive these alone on early visits. The “occasional drifting leaf-scale of something larger farther down” is not metaphor.

Houses are hollowed swellings. Floors are tissue allowed to lignify just enough. Doors are flexible epidermis valves. Entering is a courtesy and a pressure skill: approach slowly, let the valve sense you, wait for the soft parting. Never cut. The people prune and freeze-graft. If you must make a temporary hold-fast, use bone wedges or reed spikes that can be withdrawn without leaving a wound that bleeds sap for years.

### 3. Tools, Materials, and the Ethic of Living Timber
Your multiverse kit should be stripped to near-nothing that cannot be explained as bone, fire-hardened reed, mussel-shell abrasive, or polished stone. Metal knives will find the old volcanic inclusions and screech; worse, they introduce a hardness and a smell the rhizome remembers as wound. 

- Primary tools: a selection of shell scrapers and bone awls, several lengths of tough plant-fiber cordage you have already dyed and softened in cold water, a small fire-hardened reed drill (even if you cannot make open flame underwater, the hard tip is useful). A pocket full of smooth quartz pebbles for trade or lens work.
- Grafting knowledge is more valuable than cutting knowledge. Observe how the inhabitants redirect growth with a single wedge or a binding. If you are staying long enough to need a sleeping niche, you will be taught (or must request) a freeze-graft protocol—essentially chilling a chosen swelling so it densifies without dying. Practice on fallen leaf-scales first.
- Light and warmth without smoke: master the culture of the phosphorescent films. They can be transferred carefully on shell spatulas. Your own body heat is a resource; sleeping curled against a thick rhizome trunk that has been gently warmed by community breath or slow sap flow is common.
- Waste and purity: everything returns to the water. Defecation and urination must be done down-current of settlements and preferably in the freer forest where filter-feeders can process it over multi-day rhythms. Menstruation or open wounds require sealed collection; blood in the water is a loud scent-signal to grazers and worse things lower down.

### 4. Sensory and Temporal Recalibration
Sound is rumor and architecture. The foundational organ-note never resolves into a chord; it is the place breathing. Over it: metallic clatter of nearly transparent fish schools, the distant stone-cutting rasp of armored grazers, the wet-canvas tear of long-necked divers surfacing, the ringing of bubble-chains against plant walls. Silence is not absence—it is the moment before a new harmonic arrives.

- Spend the first 48 hours mostly listening. Lie against a vibrating cable and feel how information moves. Speech among the inhabitants is low, sparse, often timed to the troughs of the organ-note so words carry. Shouting is almost physically painful to the medium and socially unforgivable.
- Time dilates. Colonies of filter-feeders open and close on multi-day cycles. Structures lean a finger’s width over decades. Your ordinary human sense of schedule will feel frantic. Bring no ticking watches; they are noise. If you need to mark duration, watch the air-flowers or the slow pulse of phosphorescent films.
- Touch is constant cool. Everything is cool. The pleasure of warmth becomes the rare sun-warmed surface water you may visit briefly, or the shared body-heat of a sleeping chamber. Sexual or affectionate contact, if it occurs, is slow and careful of buoyancy and pressure seals.
- Taste and smell: the water carries mineral iron from old basalt, sweetish sap where rhizomes have been recently pruned, and the clean iodine of mussel beds. Your rebreather will mute this; when you safely surface or sip an air-bell, pay attention. Off-flavors can warn of deeper stagnation or of something large moving below.

### 5. Social Entry, Gift, and Risk
The settlement is sparse—dozens of chambers and bridges separated by silent water. People appear and vanish like thoughts. They have no memory of a time before the water; the volcanic stone exists only as dark hardness inside living wood. You are therefore not a visitor from “outside” in any historical sense; you are a temporary density in the continuum.

- Approach the first inhabited chamber with empty open hands and a small gift that belongs to their material world: a particularly fine mussel shell already shaped into a blade, a polished quartz lens better than average, a length of unusually strong reed cordage, or a living cutting from some compatible highland plant you have kept cold and viable. Present it near a phosphorescent film so it can be examined without bright light.
- Speech: learn a few low-frequency carriers and the etiquette of waiting for the organ-note’s trough. Questions about “before” or “surface history” are often met with polite incomprehension or gentle redirection to the wood itself. They will teach you to read lean of air-flowers, safe graft points, and which grazers may be approached.
- Children move freely between platforms; they are the best early teachers and the most honest mirrors. If a child offers to show you a flowering terminal, follow slowly and copy their exact finning or climbing style.
- Conflict is almost unknown because space and resource are not scarce in the way surface peoples understand. The real dangers are clumsiness (damaging a load-bearing braid), introducing heat or toxin, or attracting something from the deeper freer forest by too much noise or blood. If you feel the low note change pitch or the fish schools vanish, freeze and drift with the current until the place settles again.

### 6. Long-Stay and Exit Considerations
For stays beyond a few days you must either resupply through multiverse means at carefully chosen surface intervals or begin partial metabolic adaptation—controlled breath-hold expansion, gradual acceptance of more cold, learning which local tissues can be safely grazed or filtered. Some travelers report that after weeks the constant vibration and green-gold light recalibrate circadian rhythm into something closer to the multi-day filter-feeder cycle; sleep becomes polyphasic and shallow.

When you leave, do not take living wood if it can be avoided. A single pruned cutting kept in cold water may be permitted as memory if you have contributed a successful graft or repaired a damaged shaft. Rinse every piece of your gear thoroughly in the surface layer before translating out; biofilm and seed-like plantlets have a way of following travelers and attempting to root in other worlds’ water systems.

Most importantly: accept that you will not fully understand the place. The rhizome remembers the volcano in its hard inclusions and has turned catastrophe into architecture. The people have turned architecture into quiet continuity. An ordinary human can only visit as a respectful density of warm blood and borrowed air, moving slowly enough that the organ-note accepts the new vibration and then, when the time comes, releases it again without a scar.

Prepare as if you are about to enter a library whose books are still growing and whose librarians breathe water. Everything else—gear lists, contingency caches at the ridge line, emergency surface signals—follows from that single attitude. The lake will do the rest.
