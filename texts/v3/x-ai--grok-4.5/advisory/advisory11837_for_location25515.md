**Preparation for the Fabricated Highland (the Riveted Plateau, the Continuous Fabrication)**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler—someone with a standard baseline physiology, no inherent reality-stabilizing gifts, and only whatever portable tech or soft skills they can carry—must treat this place as a complete substitution of world rather than a destination within one. The plateau is not a ruin sitting on a planet; it is the entire local ecology, orography, hydrology, and sky, extruded and maintained as one continuous industrial artifact suspended above nothing you will ever reach. Preparation is less about packing a bag and more about rewriting your expectations of solid ground, reliable distance, the feel of metal, the passage of an afternoon, and the density of living things that will share every cubic meter with you.

### 1. Physiological and Environmental Baseline
The air is cool in the way of an unheated foundry that has been running for centuries: breath always visible, skin slowly losing heat through contact with any surface, joints stiffening if you remain still too long against a bulkhead. Moisture does not weather the place; it condenses and drips in irregular silver curtains from every underside. The smell is constant—mineral rust inhibitor, faint glycol, the clean metallic tang of old hydraulic fluid mixed with the algal colonies that have learned to live on it. Nothing here is “weather.” It is climate-controlled by machinery that no longer fully cares about human comfort.

- **Thermal management**: Bring layered, non-absorbent clothing that breathes yet retains heat. Avoid cotton; it will take on the drip-moisture and stay clammy. A light insulated shell with many pockets is ideal. Gloves that allow fine manipulation (you will be climbing ladders, turning valves for shortcuts, and testing handrails) but can be thickened quickly. The metal never becomes truly cold enough to frost, but prolonged contact drains warmth. Footwear must have aggressive tread for grating, wet paneling, and the occasional patch of grit-filled brake-lining soil; ankle support is mandatory because surfaces drop at true right angles with no warning talus or soft edge.
- **Respiratory and dermal protection**: The air is breathable but laced with decades of vaporized inhibitors, dielectric residues, and the metabolic output of the local biota. A simple particulate/chemical filter mask (replaceable cartridges) is strongly advised for the first weeks until your mucous membranes adapt. Some travelers report a low-grade metallic taste and mild conjunctival irritation that fades; others develop contact dermatitis from the constant micro-mist. Carry a small supply of barrier cream and lubricating eye drops. Do not drink the drip-water unfiltered—ever. It is silver-bright and inviting in the shallow stamped basins, but it carries dissolved metals, glycol traces, and whatever the lichen mats excrete. Compact UV + carbon + ion-exchange filter or a small still. Collect from higher, cleaner condensations when possible (undersides of active cooling towers after a long still period).
- **Light and circadian override**: There is no night and no true shadow, only cooler gradients in the permanent sodium-mercury glare bouncing between polished upper works. The illumination level at “ground” (any primary deck) feels like a foundry floor at full shift. Your circadian rhythm will collapse within days. Bring blue-blocking lenses or a sleep mask rated for bright industrial light, plus a reliable personal chronometer that is mechanical or otherwise hardened against local temporal oddities. Melatonin or equivalent, carefully dosed. Many long-term visitors eventually stop trying to sleep on a 24-hour cycle and adopt polyphasic rest timed to the slow machinery cycles (the compressors that inhale once every few minutes become a kind of metronome).
- **Nutrition**: Vegetation is sparse, toxic-tolerant, and low-calorie: blue-black adhesive lichen, aluminium-tolerant scrub, filter-floss moss. Fauna is abundant. You will need a high-calorie densified food supply for at least the first ten to fourteen subjective days while you learn what can be safely taken. After that, sustainable foraging is possible but requires knowledge. The pale armour-plated insects that nest in junction boxes are protein-rich if you can stomach the dielectric-grease flavour (roast them). The angular birds are edible; the slow-bodied grazers can be taken if you have the means and the stomach for scraping biofilm-eaters. Avoid anything that looks like it has been reassembled by the wandering maintenance frames. Carry electrolyte tablets—the mineral smell is not just atmosphere; your sweat will taste of it.

### 2. Mobility, Verticality, and the Body in a Stacked World
Density is the organizing principle. Structures stand shoulder-to-shoulder and then stack above themselves for seven, eight, twelve process levels. Pipe racks cross until the air is a three-dimensional schematic. Walkways appear wherever someone needed a shortcut and had welding capability. You will climb more than you walk on flat surfaces. Expect constant three-axis movement.

- Train beforehand for grip endurance, ladder work, and moving through confined yet high spaces. A lightweight full-body harness with multiple tether points is not overkill; many spans have no railings on one side, and the drop is not onto ground but into another layer of machinery or an open void between decks that may be hundreds of meters of fabricated emptiness. Magnetic or mechanical clamp tools for temporary anchors help.
- The edges remain crisp. Tables of metal end in true right angles. There is no gentle slope, no erosion-rounded boulder. When you fall, you fall cleanly until something catches you—pipe, cable tray, or the back of a grazing thing in a trench. Practice controlled descents and the art of reading which panels are load-bearing by sound and micro-vibration.
- Carry a compact multi-tool heavy on wrenches, locking carabiners, and a small cold chisel. Spontaneous walkways are often secured by temporary bolts; knowing how to test and, if necessary, reinforce them is survival.

### 3. Navigational and Metaphysical Preparations
This is the core difficulty. Maps fail. Geometry is locally negotiable. Time is not uniform. Materials occasionally forget themselves.

- **Non-Euclidean and recursive spaces**: Certain interchanges stretch. A corridor thirty meters from the outside becomes three hundred inside, repeating bulkhead art with slight variations. Stairs loop or deposit you onto a catwalk already occupied by your own approaching figure. These are not dramatic folds; they are patient, condensation-like accumulations of discrepancy. Preparation: never trust a single transit. Travel in short legs, leaving temporary markers that are *yours*—colored cable ties, scratched personal sigils, scent markers if you have them—because the assemblers rearrange struts according to private aesthetic logics and may remove impersonal survey tape. Learn to navigate by sequence of sensations rather than compass: the particular thrum of a compressor, the drip pattern under a specific cooling tower, the way a certain handrail feels just before it remembers it is alloy. Oral tradition is the only reliable cartography. If you meet workers or long-term residents, listen more than you speak; they transmit which spans are currently trustworthy the way sailors once transmitted reef knowledge.
- **Time dilation around heat-exchange batteries and larger process nodes**: People enter to inspect a valve and emerge having aged hours while a full maintenance cycle has passed outside. Carry at least two independent timepieces (one worn, one cached on your person in a Faraday-adjacent pouch). Log subjective versus observed cycles obsessively for the first weeks. Never schedule precise rendezvous near the big batteries. If you must work inside one, tether a bright external beacon on a long cord and check it frequently. Some travelers leave a low-power radio beacon outside transmitting a simple counting signal.
- **Material unreliability**: A handrail may briefly feel like packed earth or warm hide. This is not illusion; the object has temporarily forgotten its composition. Do not panic. Continue weight-bearing gradually; it almost always remembers. But never commit full trust to a single point of contact during these moments. The same applies to decking underfoot. Develop the habit of micro-testing surfaces with a foot or hand before full transfer of weight.
- **Growth and dismantling**: The highland expands at the margins and is slowly recycled at the center across generations. Edges are places of active extrusion—new decking being bolted on. Centers contain quiet deconstruction. Avoid both if you are new; the fabrication halls at the perimeter still move with deliberate, massive purpose, and the melt-return zones have fewer handrails and more sudden absences.

### 4. Fauna, Assemblers, and Coexistence
Life is thick. Nothing is sparse. The volume is packed the way the pipes are packed.

- **Insects**: Pale, armour-plated, nesting in junction boxes, feeding on grease. Mostly harmless unless you stick unprotected fingers into their colonies. They can deliver a painful, chemically irritating bite if crushed against skin.
- **Birds**: Angular, turbine-blade primaries, near-silent flight. They roost in decommissioned flues. Startling a flock in a confined trench is disorienting but rarely dangerous. They are more startled by you than predatory.
- **Grazers**: Slow-bodied, keratinised shovel-mouths, scraping biofilm in the deeper trenches. Give them room; they are heavy and uninterested in anything but their feeding lanes. Their trails of cleared metal can serve as rough paths.
- **Predators**: Lean, jointed, favoring stairwells. They are the primary zoological hazard. They are patient and use the verticality. Travel with a light source that can strobe or a loud ultrasonic deterrent if you have one; many have adapted to the constant glare and low thrum but still flinch from sudden high-frequency noise. A sturdy staff or collapsible baton helps for keeping distance on stairs. They are not invincible; they are simply at home. Learn their resting periods (often aligned with the slowest compressor cycles).
- **Assemblers**: Semi-autonomous maintenance frames that have slipped programming and now rearrange struts according to multi-year private aesthetics. They are not hostile. They are indifferent and powerful. Do not interfere with a strut they are currently contemplating. If one begins to disassemble a walkway you need, leave. Some travelers report that remaining still and allowing an assembler to “consider” you results in it eventually moving on; sudden movement can cause it to incorporate you into its temporary structural logic (rarely fatal, always inconvenient). They are the closest thing the place has to weather or to gods of small changes.

There are human or human-descended workers. Conversations occur in ordinary voices that carry surprisingly far in the quiet. They are not numerous relative to the volume, but they exist. Approach with the deference due to people who have already solved the daily problems of food, water, and trustworthy spans. Offer useful trade: fresh filters, off-world medications, stories, high-density calories, tools that are not yet worn into the local style. Do not assume shared language, but assume shared understanding of the plateau’s patience.

### 5. Psychological and Sensory Orientation
The weirdness is not an overlay; it is the density and completeness of the build expressing itself. The mood is unfinished permanence. Everything is clearly made. Nothing is entirely final. The quiet bright air circulates through the same valves and lungs year upon uncounted year.

- Expect the low, almost sub-audible thrum to become the baseline of reality. Silence will feel wrong when you leave. The lack of true shadow means depth perception shifts; practice judging distance by motion parallax and sound rather than shading.
- The accumulation of small physical discrepancies produces a particular fatigue—not horror, but a quiet tax on the part of the mind that expects Euclidean consistency and material stability. Some travelers develop mild derealization; counter it by maintaining small, reliable personal rituals (a specific way of brewing a drink from filtered drip-water and a pellet, a daily log of which handrails felt true). Bring a small object of purely personal, non-functional significance—something that cannot be mistaken for local fabrication—and handle it when the world begins to feel too continuous with itself.
- Loneliness is paradoxical. You are never alone (fauna, drips, distant slow engines, the occasional assembler silhouette), yet the human density is low and conversations rare. The place rewards slow attention and punishes haste.

### 6. Kit Summary (Minimum Viable Load for First Insertion)
- Layered technical clothing + insulated shell, high-traction boots, dexterous gloves, spare insulating layer.
- Filter mask with spare cartridges, barrier cream, eye lubricant, personal water filter/still.
- High-calorie densified food for 10–14 days, electrolytes, basic multivitamin.
- Harness + tethers, multi-tool, compact baton or staff, magnetic clamps, colored markers/cable ties, personal chronometers (×2), sleep mask, blue-blockers.
- Headlamp with red/strobe options (even though ambient light is high, trenches and under-deck spaces can pool into cooler gloom), small first-aid including heavy metal chelators if available, broad-spectrum antibiotic, antihistamine.
- Notebook (physical, waterproof) and writing implements for logging safe spans and time discrepancies. A simple voice recorder for capturing oral directions from residents.
- Optional but high-value: low-power radio beacons, ultrasonic deterrent, small welding/brazing capability for emergency span repair, trade goods.

### 7. Insertion and First Days Mentality
Arrive prepared to move slowly. The plateau was stamped into existence all at once (or nearly so), and it continues at a geological-industrial pace. You will not master it. You will learn a few valleys of it, a few trustworthy vertical stacks, the moods of particular compressor stations, which stairwells the lean predators currently favor, and the drip-patterns that indicate cleaner water. Let the quiet brightness and the patient discrepancies work on you rather than fighting them for the first cycles. Speak little. Watch how the birds launch across open reaches without sound. Notice when a handrail forgets itself and when it remembers. Leave markers. Accept water and stories when offered by those who already live inside the long-cycle maintenance of the world.

The edges are still crisp. Fresh cut-marks show where giant shears parted a bulkhead last season or last century. You are another small, temporary operation on the surface of something that does not need you and will not hurry for you. Prepare accordingly: thoroughly, lightly, and with the understanding that the most important equipment is the willingness to let distance, time, and the feel of metal become local variables rather than constants. 

If you can do that, the fabricated highland will hold you as it holds everything else—shoulder to shoulder with the pipes, the lichen, the slow grazers, and the unfinished permanence of the bright, cool air.
