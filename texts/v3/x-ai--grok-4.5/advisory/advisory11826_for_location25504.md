**Preparation for the Salt-Pocket City (the Folded Depression, the Wind-Kiln Settlement)**

You are an ordinary human. You have a body that sweats, ears that bleed if pressed too hard, eyes that water and burn, lungs that reject fine mineral dust, a mind that panics when language dies and silence itself becomes a blow. This place does not hate you. It simply does not contain any of the soft permissions your biology evolved under. Prepare as if you are about to step into a living kiln that sings, a library whose only remaining text is abrasion and heat-drinking architecture. The pocket opens only when you are already inside it; the flats give almost no warning. Once down, the world above becomes theoretical.

### 1. Mental and Temporal Orientation
Understand the central contradiction before you go: the settlement is urban in ambition and pocket-scale in fact. You can cross it in an hour of hard walking if the wind permits; you can also lose days moving between leaf-shadows that never cool. Time here is the “long middle.” Nothing is ruined, nothing is new. The builders are gone. The wind, the copper-pistachio columns, the rare hard-shelled scrapers, and the stone itself have reached equilibrium. You are a temporary fourth party. Do not arrive expecting narrative, rescue, water, night, conversation, or forgiveness for disruption.

Cultivate two internal stances:
- Radical smallness. The place will not notice you unless you force it (by breaking a plant skin, by trying to shout over the wind, by lingering in the central chord-mosaic as if it owes you revelation). 
- Acceptance of sensory absolutism. Light never softens. Heat never drops. Sound is the true weather—continuous, physical, tooth-and-joint. The three-heartbeat silences are not relief; they are amplified absence that can make your ears ring as if struck. Train for this. Sit in the loudest industrial space or desert wind tunnel you can find, then suddenly have the noise cut. Practice letting thought arrive already half-shredded without panicking.

If you carry grief, unfinished speech, or a need for quiet contemplation, leave them topside. The wind will shred them into pollen anyway. If you require meaning, know that the only meaning on offer is endurance and the strange courtesy the four long residents (air, stone, heat-plants, scarce fauna) extend to one another. You may borrow that courtesy for a short while.

### 2. Physiological Conditioning (Start Weeks Earlier)
- Heat acclimation: Daily progressive exposure to dry heat well above comfort. You must be able to function when your core feels like “a body that has been running” with no prospect of cooling. Sauna + movement + deliberate dehydration drills (under medical supervision) help, but nothing fully prepares you for heat that does not cycle.
- Desiccation tolerance: Practice strict water discipline. Your body will demand liquid the moment the lime-and-dust air hits your tongue. There is none. There has never been any in living or stone memory. Pre-hydrate heavily, then taper. Learn the early signs of your own heat exhaustion and delirium so you can retreat before they become decisive.
- Auditory fortitude: Prolonged exposure to high-volume broadband noise (industrial fans, recorded canyon wind, white-noise generators at damaging-but-monitored levels). Pair with sudden silence drills. Soft foam is useless here; the howl is structural and bone-conducted. You need to remain cognitively intact when conversation is impossible and internal monologue frays.
- Visual and dust resilience: Bright-light training (glare, UV, albedo bounce). Practice moving and working with eyes narrowed to slits. Respiratory conditioning for fine, alkaline dust and the dry pollen that never settles.
- Vestibular and spatial: The descent is shallow steps that feel accidental; once inside, free-standing walls, exterior staircases ending in platforms over emptiness, and green-gold tunnels of overlapping fronds create repeated scale shocks. Practice navigating sparse, high-contrast, high-echo environments while partially blinded and deafened.

If you have any pre-existing respiratory, auditory, ocular, renal, or thermoregulatory vulnerability, this location will exploit it without malice. Consider whether “ordinary human” in your case still includes the trip.

### 3. Entry Protocol — Finding the Throat
From the mineral flats: white unrelenting sky, salt crust stretching forever, heat shimmer. The marker is almost nothing—an imperceptible sag, a faint change in shimmer. Walk methodically. When the ground begins to drop in steps so shallow they feel accidental, stop. Do not rush the descent. The pocket “opens only when you are already inside it”; treat the weathered-stone throat as a one-way sensory airlock.

Just before dropping fully out of the flats’ silence:
- Final topside water and electrolyte load.
- Seal all skin you can. 
- Activate eye and ear protection.
- Start your time-and-physiology log (you will not trust subjective duration once the roar takes you).
- Accept that the flats above will remain empty and silent while the world below insists. Re-ascent is possible but will feel like clawing out of a living instrument back into a vacuum of sound and meaning.

Move down deliberately. The air will change taste immediately—lime, superheated dust. Light will intensify by bounce. The first real blast of funneled wind will hit like a physical gate.

### 4. Essential Gear (Everything Must Survive Alkaline Dust, Continuous Abrasion, and Heat That Never Relents)
**Water and metabolism (non-negotiable core)**
- Carry every drop you will drink, plus emergency reserve, plus the water required for your exit climb. There are no wells, cisterns, stains of damp, or extractable moisture from the plants (they abandoned liquid long ago; their thick segmented skins hold heat-metabolized chemistry, not juice you can use). Closed-system hydration bladder with bite valve you can work while half-blind and buffeted. Electrolyte concentrates. Do not plan to “find” anything. Calculate stay length by water weight you can still carry when half-deafened and heat-stupid.
- High-calorie, non-thirst-provoking rations that do not crumble into the dust. Eating will coat your mouth in lime taste; accept it.

**Thermal and dermal armor**
- Loose, full-coverage, highly reflective outer layer (pale, metallized, or desert-albedo fabric) over a wicking base. The goal is to reject radiant load from the white sky and every bone-colored surface. The air itself is luminous; shade under the densest fronds still requires squinting and still does not cool.
- Sealed gaiters, gloves, neck gaiter or balaclava. Every seam is an entry point for superfine dust and pollen that never settles. The plants shed constantly; the wind redistributes.
- Sturdy, closed, heat-resistant footwear with aggressive tread for salt crust, loose mineral flats, cracked plazas, and polished-smooth ancient stone. Exterior staircases and platforms have no rails.

**Sensory protection (the difference between visit and damage)**
- Eyes: High-coverage, sealed goggles with true UV/IR rejection and photochromic or fixed extreme-neutral density. Multiple backups. The light comes straight down and bounces until the air seems luminous; shadows are only thin vibrating edges. Pollen and dust will find any gap. Have saline rinse in sealed vials.
- Ears: Beyond ordinary plugs. You need a system that reduces the tearing shriek and the resonant instrument-howl of walls, arches, and unfinished arcades without isolating you so completely that you lose the ability to feel wind shifts (those shifts matter for movement and for not being thrown). Consider custom-molded high-attenuation plugs plus over-ear that can be vented, and accept that bone conduction will still deliver the roar into your joints and teeth. Train with the exact kit. The occasional total drop into silence must not disorient you into removing protection.
- Respiratory: A tight-seal respirator rated for fine alkaline dust and organic pollen, with heat-tolerant filters you can change with gloves on. The air tastes of lime and superheated dust; your lungs will try to reject it. Mouth breathing when the wind forces you will sandpaper your throat raw without protection.
- Skin: Barrier balm for any exposed face (alkaline dust + wind = micro-abrasion). The heat is the temperature of sustained exertion; sweat evaporates instantly and salt crusts on you.

**Navigation, time, documentation, safety**
- Dead-reckoning tools that do not rely on electronics you cannot hear or see clearly (the luminous air and roar interfere with casual device use). Pre-drawn sketch map of major courts, solitary towers, green-gold tunnels, and the central open circular structure. Mark the ring of denser, taller vegetation around the mosaic as a sonic and spatial landmark—the wind plays that space as a deep fluctuating chord felt more than heard.
- Multiple ways to track elapsed time and physiological state (simple mechanical, body-check protocol). Subjective time compresses or stretches under continuous roar and unrelenting brightness.
- Bright, heat-tolerant marker system for your entry throat so you can find the way back when the wind is shredding orientation. Do not rely on memory of “almost imperceptible sag.”
- Lightweight, non-flaking note method if you must record (most pens fail; dust destroys paper). Better: decide beforehand what you actually need to remember. Many travelers return with the wind still in their teeth and little coherent narrative.
- Emergency signaling that works in white-out glare and howling noise (high-visibility panels, pure-tone devices tuned outside the wind’s dominant frequencies if possible). Assume no one is coming.

**Minimalist respect kit**
- Nothing that sheds microplastics, oils, or seeds. 
- No drilling, scraping for samples, or “taking a piece of the mosaic.” The equilibrium is old. Dust settles into carving grooves and is scraped clean by the next gale; walls lose a grain at a time yet keep shape. Match that pace. Look, do not harvest.
- Soft brushes or air bulb if you must clear pollen from your own gear; never from the plants or carvings.

Weight is the enemy. Every extra kilogram is carried in heat that does not forgive. Yet water and protection are not optional. Ruthlessly edit.

### 5. On-Site Conduct — How to Be a Guest in the Long Middle
Move during whatever passes for lower wind if you can detect the pattern; the roar is rarely uniform. Use the coppery forests as directional cues and limited glare filters—they stand so densely in places that streets become green-gold tunnels of overlapping, rubbing fronds. Elsewhere they thin to solitary giants. Their skins are thick as leather, segmented, faintly translucent in hard light. They drink heat and afternoon mineral vapor. Do not cut, lean heavily, or break segments; the pollen cloud you create will be worse for you than for them.

Buildings: low doorways, heavy lintels, rooms the size of a body alternating with voids that swallow sound (or would, if the wind ever stopped). Exterior stairs end on platforms overlooking more emptiness. Treat every surface as load-bearing history fitted without metal—local pale conglomerate, stone-tool dressed, mortar of burned shell and dust now harder than the stone. Your presence is already an abrasion. Walk the wide courts and free-standing walls as if the vanished procession might still need the space.

The central low circular structure: open to the sky, floor a perfect dark-and-light spiral mosaic. The wind’s chord here is different—deeper, fluctuating, felt in the chest. The vegetation grows in a perfect listening ring, denser and taller. Approach with the same courtesy you would a sleeping instrument or a shrine whose rite is purely acoustic and thermal. Sit the rim if you must; do not occupy the center unless you understand you are adding a dampening body to a long resonance. Many feel an unnameable recognition here. Let it pass through; do not try to name it over the sound.

Animal life is almost absent—an occasional pale hard-shelled thumb-sized scuttler, rarer still a single dark wing-shape high in the white glare. Do not chase. You are more transient than they are.

Communication with any companion: agree beforehand on touch signals and simple gesture. Speech is impossible most of the time. Thought arrives half-shredded. If you travel alone (recommended for first visits), accept the absolute solitude. The city was finished, abandoned, then re-inhabited by wind, plants, and scarce fauna until understanding was reached. You are walking through that understanding.

Watch your physiology on a fixed schedule: water sips even when the lime taste nauseates, electrolyte, eye rinses, skin checks, heat-stupid test (can you still do simple arithmetic or recite a fixed poem?). When performance drops, move toward exit. The place will not stop you from staying until you fail; it simply continues.

### 6. Exit and Aftercare
Re-ascent is the reverse sensory shock: from insistence back to the empty silent flats. The roar falls away, the light changes character, the heat remains in your clothes and bones. Do not remove protection until you are well clear and have rinsed. Your ears will ring; your eyes will ache in what suddenly feels like dimness; thirst will announce itself with interest.

Immediate aftercare:
- Controlled rehydration and cooling (as much as ordinary environments allow). 
- Full dust-off and filter disposal before you contaminate other spaces or your lungs further.
- Hearing and vision check. Soft sound and ordinary night may feel luxurious or unreal for days.
- Psychological decompression. The wind can stay in the nervous system—phantom howl, heightened startle to silence, a lingering sense that ordinary rooms are too soft and too dead. Journal if it helps, or simply sit with the fact that you were inside a pocket that should not exist, a city that measures emptiness carefully and keeps its long middle without you.

Some travelers report that afterward ordinary wind never sounds the same, that heat carries a faint lime memory, that dense plantings feel like they might begin to drink light. This is normal. You went into a place where the ecology that matters is slower than blood and louder than speech. You returned.

### Final Intuitive Note
Visiting is less exploration than temporary admission into an argument already concluded among stone, air, heat, and the tall ribbed columns. The beauty is severe: bone-and-clay surfaces, tarnished-copper forests, luminous air, the city as resonant instrument. The danger is not dramatic predation but the steady erasure of your margins—water, hearing, sight, coherent thought, the illusion that the world must accommodate you. Prepare so thoroughly that once inside you can stop protecting yourself every second and simply witness the folded city under its white sky, the fronds rubbing, the deep chord at the center, the careful emptiness. Then leave while you still can, carrying only what the wind did not take.

An ordinary human can do this. Many should not. Those who do it well come back quieter, dustier, and permanently recalibrated to the possibility that some places insist without needing an audience. Travel lightly, protect the vessel you arrived in, and let the pocket close behind you without souvenir or scar beyond the ones you knowingly accepted.
