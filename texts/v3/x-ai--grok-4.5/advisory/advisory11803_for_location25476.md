**Preparation for the Pocket Settlement (the Cold Green Bowl, the Finished Courtyard, the Sealed Breath)**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler—no enhanced physiology, no phase gear beyond a basic transit token or anchor, no monomolecular anything—must treat this place as a living, self-sealed organism that has already perfected itself and will not rearrange for you. You are entering a room-scale world that behaves like a town, a cliff, a lung, and a closed meteorological cell all at once. The overriding principle is **minimum disruption + maximum quiet endurance**. The pocket does not hate visitors; it simply has no unused volume left for clumsiness, noise, waste, or unfinished intentions. Everything you bring and everything you do must already feel half-worn and necessary.

### 1. Mental and Temporal Orientation
Spend at least three days beforehand practicing the internal clock of “already completed.” Sit in a cool, dim, slightly damp room with no screens and only the smallest necessary objects. Move only when movement is required. Speak only when the words are edged and few. The settlement operates in an unchanging hour; your sense of linear progress will feel vulgar and tiring to the place and, soon, to you. Accept that exterior seasons and exterior weather cease to matter once you step between the pale stone shoulders. The sky you see from the highest moss terrace is real but progressively less connected; do not keep checking it for proof of an outside. That habit generates a low-grade panic that the pocket absorbs poorly.

Cultivate two complementary mindsets:
- **Guest of a finished form.** You are temporary moisture on stone that has decided its shape. You do not improve, clear, catalog for extraction, or leave marks.
- **Participant in slow circulation.** Moving through the dens is less locomotion than respiration. People (the few long-practiced residents) and the small fauna treat the architecture as continuous tissue. Your body must learn the same economy: no wasted gestures, no loud footwear, no trailing straps.

Expect a mild ontological pressure. Paths that “should” exit curve gently inward. Shadows are only denser greens. Light is high, white, and essentially shadowless, originating as much from the stone as from above. Depth perception will stutter for the first hours. Some travelers report a growing reluctance to leave, not from enchantment but from the sheer rightness of the equilibrium—an emotional gravity. Decide your exit window in advance and bind it to a physical cue (a specific number of touches to the central basin, a worn notch you make on a personal tally stick that you will not leave behind).

### 2. Physical Conditioning
The cold is mineral and deep; it lives in the stone and migrates into fingertips, knees, and the base of the skull. It is not dramatic freezing—it is the chill of bone left on a windowsill. Combined with permanent cling-damp (never quite rain, never quite dry), it produces steady heat loss. Condition yourself with prolonged cool-damp exposure: cool showers left to air-dry, sitting on stone or tile floors, barefoot walks on wet moss if available. Build tolerance for slippery, irregular, living surfaces.

Strength and mobility priorities:
- Grip endurance and precise foot placement (polished concavities, moss cushions, cut footholds, lashed ladders).
- Hip and shoulder flexibility for twisting through abutting rooms and under sagging lintels that have forgotten sharpness.
- Core stability for three-dimensional movement—up, down, and lateral—without swinging packs or elbows.
- Balance on rounded, beaded, slightly yielding surfaces. The moss and the ice-leaf plants are load-bearing tissue; crushing them is both rude and structurally inconsiderate.

Lung capacity and quiet breathing matter. Sound carries strangely: a single drip three levels down is an event. Your breathing, swallowing, and fabric rustle will be audible. Practice nasal, slow respiration. If you are a noisy sleeper, rehearse still rest.

Skin and extremities: constant wet iron + leaf mold air and surface moisture invite maceration, fungal issues, and cracked cuticles. Begin a pre-trip regimen of barrier oils (plain, unscented, minimal) and keep nails short.

### 3. Clothing and Body Covering
Everything must be quiet, close-fitting without constriction, quick to dampen and slow to feel miserable, and free of hard edges or synthetic squeak.

- **Base layers:** Merino or equivalent high-quality wool blends, thin. Multiple thin layers beat one thick. Color: muted earth, black-green, bronze, soft grey—nothing that shouts against the pale stone and dark moss. Avoid pure waterproof membranes that crackle or refuse to breathe; the damp will condense inside them anyway. A tightly woven wool or wool-silk outer that sheds the worst beads while accepting the ambient moisture is better.
- **Insulation:** A compact mid-layer that still allows climbing. Down is risky when wet; treated down or synthetic equivalent rated for cool damp, not arctic extremes. The temperature does not swing wildly.
- **Shell:** Minimal. A short, soft, matte anorak or smock that can be worn or packed. No loud zippers—use ties, toggled loops, or horn/wood toggles if possible. In a pinch, high-quality waxed cotton that has already been broken in.
- **Lower body:** Close trousers with articulated knees, reinforced quietly. Gaiters or simple wraps to keep debris and excess moisture from boot tops, but nothing that will snag ferns.
- **Footwear:** The critical item. Soft-soled, high-friction, flexible boots or shoes with minimal tread aggression—you need grip on polished stone and wet moss without tearing the living surfaces. Think approach shoes or barefoot-style hikers with a thin, sticky rubber that has been scuffed and quieted. Many travelers go with a primary pair and a softer moccasin-style secondary for upper terraces and indoor shelves. Break them in until silent. Socks: wool, multiple pairs, rotated and dried against your body if needed.
- **Hand and head:** Thin wool gloves that still allow two-finger touch to the basin and fine tool use. A close hood or soft cap—ears stay cold. No dangling drawstrings.
- **Extras:** A simple wrap or spare length of beaten-fiber-like cloth (linen or hemp) that can serve as extra layer, seat pad, or modest covering. Everything should be repairable with bone needle and bark cordage logic.

Do not bring bright logos, reflective strips, or anything that needs to “stay dry at all costs.” Accept that you will be damp. The goal is thermal neutrality and silence, not wilderness-magazine dryness.

### 4. Gear and Burden
Total carried weight should feel embarrassing by normal expedition standards. Density is total; you will circulate inside a single intricate lung. A low-profile, soft-body pack or rolled bundle that can be passed through narrow living frames is mandatory. No external hard frames, no clanking pots.

Essential categories:
- **Water discipline:** The seeps and basins are the water. The central circular basin at the lowest point is always half-full of water so clear it is nearly invisible. Residents touch two fingers to it in passing—greeting, measurement, confirmation. You will do the same. Carry a small personal cup or folded leaf-catcher only if needed; better to learn to drink as they do. A compact filter or purification drops for the first day while your gut adjusts to the wet-iron mineral taste; after that, trust the equilibrium unless your body clearly rejects it. Do not soap, wash clothes, or dump anything into basins.
- **Food:** Compact, non-perishable, low-odor, low-packaging. Dense cakes of fat + grain + dried meat or fish, nut pastes, hard cheeses if tolerated, dried fruit in moderation. Nothing that crumbs extensively or requires open fire. The pocket has no spare fuel culture you can see; residents’ tools are hand-scale and ancient. Eat small, clean, over moss-felt or personal cloth. Pack out every scrap of wrapper if you foolishly brought any. Better: re-usable cloth wraps. Calories need not be high—activity is steady but not frantic, and the cold is constant rather than storm-spiked—but the damp increases metabolic demand quietly. Include something salty and something bitter for the iron taste that will coat your mouth.
- **Tools (hand-scale only):** 
  - Small, already-resharpened knife (simple carbon or copper-compatible; the place still knows copper fishhooks the size of fingernails).
  - Bone or wood needle + strong fine cordage (bark-fiber style or modern equivalent that looks humble).
  - Tiny copper or bone awl.
  - Soft brush or bunch of moss-equivalent for cleaning grit from your own gear without stripping living surfaces.
  - Personal tally stick or cord for exit-count and day-sense.
  - Minimal medical: blister care, antifungal dust or ointment, strong pain tabs, electrolyte powder, any personal meds in waterproof but quiet containers. Suture kit if you are competent; the residents have bone needles and infinite reuse logic—do not assume emergency surgery is available.
  - Light: the ambient light is relentless and shadowless. A tiny shielded warm-LED or tallow-style stub for the rare deeper undercut is enough. No headlamp beams raking across quiet faces.
  - Recording: if you must, a small notebook of rag paper and graphite or a quiet mechanical pencil. Camera only if silent and without flash or shutter slap; better to memorize. The place is not for extraction of images so much as for being inside the breath.
- **Sleep system:** Compact quilt or bag rated for cool damp, compressible. A thin closed-cell pad or the ability to accept moss shelves and sleeping niches. Residents use shelves and platforms that abut everything else; you may be offered floor felt or a niche. Do not unroll a giant colorful inflatable palace. A simple hammock is usually wrong—vertical space is already stitched with ladders and living frames.
- **Waste:** Ultimate pack-out or ultra-minimal catholes only in any rare outward fringe if the pocket even permits it. Most travelers hold and manage with extreme care, using sealable bags and carrying out. There is nowhere to discard. Internalize this before entry.

Everything you carry should already look as if it could have been mended for decades. New glossy plastic is an insult to the dull gleam of infinite reuse.

### 5. Skills to Rehearse
- Silent three-dimensional movement: ladders of lashed wood, cut footholds, moss handrails that have replaced carved ones, doorways narrowed by living frames.
- Two-finger basin greeting, performed without flourish.
- Reading condensation and seep patterns as the only weather report.
- Identifying the small fauna so you do not startle or step wrong: frost-tipped rodents on moss highways, thumb-sized nearly soundless birds in fern pockets, flat grey-green frogs in lowest basins, translucent-winged beetles on algae films. Nothing large, nothing loud. You are the largest uncertain animal.
- Basic greenstone/antler/bone tool sympathy even if you never touch theirs—understand that resharpening is a moral act here.
- Navigation by texture and slope rather than landmarks or sky. Everything ultimately slopes to the central basin. When disoriented, descend quietly toward denser damp and the felt-worn moss of centuries of kneeling.
- Exit discipline. The bowl rejects the idea of elsewhere with gentle curves. Your transit anchor may feel muffled. Some travelers need to climb to the rim, look out, then deliberately re-enter and reverse a remembered sequence of hollows to “remind” the pocket they are not permanent tissue. Practice short forays and returns if your multiverse method allows.

### 6. Entry Protocol
Approach the shallow bowl from the colder, barer country without announcing yourself. The pale stone shoulders are easy to miss from a hundred paces; a thickening of green that does not belong is the tell. Step between them and accept the collapse of scale. Pause just inside. Let your eyes adjust to the shadowless clarity and your skin to the immediate mineral chill and cling. Breathe the wet iron and leaf mold. Do not speak. Touch nothing for the first minutes except necessary footholds.

If residents are visible, still your body and wait. They move with the economy of those who have never known empty space. A nod, an open empty hand, or simply beginning to circulate at their pace is usually enough. Mimic the two-finger basin touch the first time you reach the lowest point. Kneel on the dark resilient felt if it feels right; many do.

Do not map ostentatiously. Do not ask the name of the place if none is offered—the whole of it is the whole of it.

### 7. Daily Existence Inside
- Move as circulation, not tourism. Sleep when the quiet deepens, eat small, repair anything of yours that frays.
- Contribute nothing permanent and remove nothing living. If you sharpen a tool, catch the filings. If you must wash, use a scrap cloth and personal water away from seeps, then evaporate or carry the dirt out.
- Speak rarely and low. A pure brief note (grass blade on stone) is already an event; your conversation should be rarer.
- Accept the light. It does not angle with any sun you know. Rest your eyes on moss and lichen fractals.
- Monitor your core temperature and skin integrity. The chill never quite leaves the fingertips—that is normal. Shivering that will not stop or white patches are not. Add layers or increase gentle movement through the levels.
- Social calibration: the people are few and long-practiced. Offer small useful quiet help only if it is obviously welcome (passing a lashed ladder item, holding a cord). Do not gift shiny new objects. Shared silence is the main hospitality.

### 8. Risks and Contingencies
- **Hypothermia and maceration:** Layer, keep moving gently, dry socks against your torso, air skin when possible in less-beaded micro-spots. Antifungal and skin barrier are not optional.
- **Disorientation and exit failure:** The closed meteorology and curving paths can produce a soft amnesia of elsewhere. Your pre-bound exit cue is vital. If the sky starts to feel decorative, enact the cue.
- **Damage to tissue:** Stepping through moss mats or snapping fern frames weakens the single load-bearing green-stone body. Move as if the handrail is alive, because it is.
- **Social rupture:** Loudness, waste, attempts to “clear” a space, or treating tools as disposable will mark you as unfinished and unwelcome. The pocket has no dungeon; it simply makes circulation harder and the damp heavier until you leave.
- **Health unknowns:** The water is ancient and mineral. The air is pure in its way but spore-rich. Carry your meds. If you fall ill, descend to the basin level—the air moves least there but help, if any, will find you by the same slope that everything follows.
- **Psychological absorption:** The finishedness is seductive. Travelers have lingered until their own edges softened and their tools took on the dull gleam. Set the tally. Leave while you still remember the colder barer country as real.

### 9. Exit and Aftermath
When your count is reached, make the final basin touch, retrieve every thread of your presence, and climb toward the rim by a remembered reverse sequence. Do not hurry; haste makes noise and slips. Step out between the shoulders and keep walking into the ordinary cold until the pocket’s self-contained brilliance fades behind you. You will still feel the mineral chill in your fingertips for hours or days. The taste of wet iron lingers. Ordinary wind will seem extravagant. Ordinary waste will seem criminal.

Dry, clean, and mend your gear with the same care the place taught. Journal while the memory is exact—the shadowless light, the way wall becomes stair becomes roof, the felt under centuries of knees, the invisible meniscus of the central water. Then let the equilibrium stay where it is. You were a brief additional condensation. The pocket remains finished.

Travel light enough that the settlement never has to notice you as a burden. Be quiet enough that a drip remains the louder event. Be finished enough in your own intentions that the bright, damp, ancient breath of the place can include you without strain for the short time you circulate inside it. That is the entire preparation.
