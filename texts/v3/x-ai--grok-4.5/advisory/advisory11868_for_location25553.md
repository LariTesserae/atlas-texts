**Prepare as if you are inserting yourself into a living geothermal lung that has only recently consented to host humans.**

This place—the Rooms, Below, the unfinished twin chambers—is not a destination you visit so much as a medium you enter. The air is already half water. The stone is still negotiating its final shape. Heat is ambient and intimate, the temperature of blood and breath. Life is not background; it is the primary architecture, cohabiting every surface, interval, and vibration with the iron and the brick. An ordinary human body can survive here, but only if it stops fighting the wet and the slow and begins to move at the pace of mineral deposition and forty-second piston strokes.

### 1. Physiological and Material Reality First

**Thermoregulation and the wet heat**  
The water is body-hot. The air never drops. Sweat does not evaporate; it becomes another layer of the chamber’s condensate. You will feel continually filmed.  
- Clothing must be minimal, loose, quick-shedding synthetics or treated merino that can be wrung out and still function while damp. Multiple ultra-light layers rather than one substantial garment. Dark colors hide the inevitable mineral staining and bioluminescent smear.  
- Footwear: soft-soled, high-traction boots with drainage ports and replaceable insoles. The stone can be plastic within hours of a fresh seep; hard cleats gouge and are resented. Many long-term residents go barefoot on the upper walkways once calluses form, but newcomers should not.  
- Skin care is survival. Antifungal powders, barrier creams that resist constant re-wetting, and a personal “dry kit” sealed in multiple nested dry-bags: silica packets, a microfiber towel that will never fully dry but can be wrung, isopropyl for feet and folds. Expect rashes, soft nails, and a permanent low-grade prickle. Treat every crease of the body as a potential reef.  
- Hydration and electrolytes become continuous micro-dosing. Local water is mineral-heavy and warm; it can be drunk after cooling and basic filtration, but it tastes of iron and deep time and will loosen the bowels until you adapt. Bring concentrated electrolytes and a small still or chemical treatment if you are staying beyond a few cycles of the engines. Waste heat is currency—any device that cools water or your body core can be traded for favors, food, or a better cube berth.

**Breathing and the air**  
The air settles and re-settles. It carries steam, mineral aerosol, the faint sweet-chitin smell of the swarms, and the rubber-metal exhalation of the engines. Most lungs adjust in two or three sleep periods. A simple moisture-wicking scarf or soft respirator helps the first days when the midges densify around warm updrafts. Do not bring sealed gas masks as primary gear; they fog instantly and mark you as transient and distrustful.

**Light and vision**  
Illumination is sparse yellow bulbs, dull red engine glow, and the soft continuous sheen of the bioluminescent film. Your eyes will recalibrate to a world of silhouettes and wet reflections. Bring a low-lumen headlamp with multiple red/amber filters and physical switches (touchscreens fail). Waterproofing must be absolute—assume total immersion risk every time you move. The flying membranous things and the upper vaults are easier to track by sound than by sight after the first day.

**Sleep and the mesh**  
You will sleep under fine mesh or not sleep at all. The susurrus of chitin, the wet click of mouths, the leather flap of gliders, and the eternal drip become a single brown noise. Earplugs help some; others learn to treat it as heartbeat. Living cubes are raised metal boxes on stilts—cramped, sweating walls, shared or solitary. Bring a compact hammock or cot that keeps you off the floor plates, plus your own mesh enclosure. Laundry never fully dries; accept a permanent state of damp cloth and rotate what little you have.

### 2. Moving Through a Place That Is Still Arriving

The ledge, the walkways, the short corridor, the twin chamber—that is the entire map that matters. Beyond is rock that has not finished deciding.  
- Walk as if every surface is already occupied, because it is. Crabs carpet supports in slow migrations; shrimp curtains part and reform; the film coats what you touch. Step slowly, weight distributed. Sudden movement is the closest thing to rudeness here.  
- Never drill, hammer, or core deeper than three meters. The stone at depth is plastic and will close on tools like clay around a finger. Surface fastening is tolerated; deep penetration is how people lose bits and sometimes worse.  
- When the engines’ cadence changes, freeze. A new vent has opened. Wait while the heat plume rises and the entire biota rearranges—shrimp, fish, crabs, gliders, midges. It takes minutes. Then resume. This is not ritual; it is traffic management in a shared body.  
- The eastern corridor is already half-choked; treat any further exploration as geological rather than geographical. Soft stone records boot prints and then begins to erase or immortalize them with silica. Leave only what you intend to become part of the walls.

Iron walkways are freckled with rust and slick with condensate and film. Handrails grow mineral crust at a visible weekly rate. Move with three points of contact when the steam thickens. Falling into the pools is unlikely to kill by heat alone (body-hot, not boiling), but the shock, the thrashing shrimp, and the difficulty of climbing out slick iron while weighted with wet clothes make it serious.

### 3. The Ecology Is Not Backdrop

You are not visiting a wilderness with some animals. You are inserting a large, warm, clumsy mammal into a dense vent community that has accepted industrial iron as just another substrate.  
- The bioluminescent film is left untouched. Scrub it and the midges become a torment instead of a weather. It is both light source and ecological regulator.  
- Midges never land on you if you are still; they orbit heat. Sudden swatting is useless and marks you as nervous.  
- Shrimp curtains are beautiful and slightly unnerving—the water goes grainy with their bodies. Do not trail hands idly; they will investigate.  
- Coin-sized crabs rasp and migrate. Larger flattened milk-eyed fish surface for insects. The gliders above are heard more than seen. None of it is hostile. Hostility is almost a surface concept. Overwhelm is the real vector. The totality can produce a low-grade existential vertigo: you are never alone, never unobserved by a thousand small minds, never the dominant process.  
- Food: the settlers eat what they bring, what they culture in cooled side-streams, and selectively the fauna. Pale shrimp can be netted and cooked; some do. Taste is clean mineral with a sweet aftertaste. Bring dense calorie bars, vacuum-sealed proteins, and spices that survive humidity. Fresh surface food is a rare luxury and powerful gift.  
- Do not introduce outside biota. A single mold, insect, or seed that thrives could shift the balance. The ivory ferns are the only native larger flora and they are sparse and precious.

Treat every tool, bag, and garment as a potential raft for hitchhikers. Inspect and clean before entry if you can.

### 4. Social Physics and the Thick Time

There are perhaps a few dozen permanent or semi-permanent residents across the cubes. The settlement has the density of a large family that has stopped needing to speak every thought aloud.  
- Speech is soft because the chamber is already loud with its own wet industrial and biological choir. Conversations pause for engine cycles, for vent events, for long stretches of comfortable silence, then resume hours later without apology. You must learn to hold threads across time. A courtship or a dispute can unfold across weeks of slow accretion.  
- Waste heat is the local soft currency. Anything that helps cool the reciprocating engines or their pipe lattice—better heat exchangers, clever baffles, labor on the cooling snake—earns standing. Information about surface weather (theoretical though it feels) or new materials that resist mineral creep also trades well.  
- Children learn the engine cadence the way surface children learn traffic lights. Follow their lead.  
- Names for the place remain temporary. Do not try to impose a permanent one. Do not speak of “conquering,” “taming,” or “developing” the Rooms. The stone is still arriving; humans are guests of a process.  
- Privacy is acoustic and visual more than spatial. Cubes are close enough that dampness and engine thump travel; far enough that each has a radius of empty dripping dark. Knock softly on metal. Wait.  
- Grudges and affections both mineralize. Behave as if your actions will be visible in the silica decades from now.

Bring small, useful gifts that acknowledge the environment: sealed dry socks (legendary), corrosion inhibitors, better mesh, thermochromic paint, fiction printed on waterproof stock, music that incorporates long drones and heartbeat rhythms. Avoid anything that requires true dryness or complex electronics without redundancy.

### 5. Psychological and Sensory Calibration

Time thickens. The forty-second engine cycle becomes a heartbeat you feel in the catwalks. Mineral crust grows while you watch if you are patient. The place breathes—heated rock expanding, water table rising almost imperceptibly, collective biota pulsing. Stand on the central catwalk long enough and you will match it.  

Sensory load is high and continuous yet low-frequency: drip percussion, piston thump, chitin susurrus, steam collapse, distant leather flap. Many travelers report an initial period of mild depersonalization followed by a strange tenderness. You are inside something unfinished and vital. Surface concepts (daylight, wind, dry cloth, silence, finished stone) recede into mythology. This is not horror; it is intimacy with process.  

Prepare by practicing long stillness. Sit in a hot bathroom with the shower running until the mirror disappears and the floor is wet and simply wait without needing the discomfort to resolve. Listen to recordings of rain on metal mixed with slow machinery and distant insect swarms. Accept that you will not be clean, dry, or fully alone for the duration.  

The unfinished quality can be existentially soothing or quietly terrifying depending on temperament. The stone has not decided. Neither have you, once you are inside. Finishing is not the point.

### 6. What to Pack (Minimal, Redundant, Humble)

- Multiple sets of quick-dry base layers and one slightly warmer wrap for the rare cooler seep.  
- Drainage boots + soft camp shoes.  
- Nested dry-bags within dry-bags; assume failure.  
- Fine sleeping mesh + compact off-floor sleep system.  
- Low-light waterproof illumination (red-heavy).  
- Comprehensive skin/foot antifungal and barrier kit.  
- Electrolytes, dense food, basic water treatment.  
- Soft tools only: fasteners that bite shallow, scrapers, brushes for delicate cleaning of gear (never the film).  
- Notebook of waterproof paper or voice notes accepted by the humidity; pens that write wet.  
- Small trade goods oriented toward cooling, drying, or comfort in damp.  
- Basic medical: heat management, wound care (cuts mineralize and heal wet), antihistamine if you react to the film or swarms, something for GI adjustment.  
- One completely sealed “surface relic” (a dry photograph, a wind-up music box, a sealed vial of actual rain) as personal talisman; it helps when the theoretical country above feels too far.

Leave behind: deep-drilling tools, strong biocides, heavy cotton, delicate electronics, rigid schedules, expectations of quiet or privacy or dryness, any plan that requires the place to stay as it is.

### 7. Entry, Cadence, Exit

Arrive through the damp throats slowly. Pause at each change in temperature and sound to let your body and the midges adjust. Announce yourself softly on the first walkway; someone will appear. Offer heat-help or simple labor before asking for a cube.  

Stay by the rhythm of engines and vents rather than clocks. A “day” is a dimming of the bulbs and a thickening of the biological chorus.  

When you leave, you will carry the wet in your clothes and the thump in your blood for a while. Surface air will feel violently thin and dry and empty. Showers will seem wasteful. Silence will sound like something missing. Some travelers return. The Rooms do not care, and that is part of the mercy.

You are not going to a ruin or a factory or a cave. You are going into a sentence the planet is still speaking, where a handful of humans have built iron punctuation marks and learned to breathe in time with it. Prepare your body for the wet heat and the film. Prepare your habits for stillness and soft voice. Prepare your mind for the realization that unfinished can be a stable, breathing condition. Then step onto the ledge. The stone will take your print. The engines will keep their unhurried count. The swarms will part and reform around your heat. Nothing down here is finished. That is the only promise the place has ever made.
