**Preparation for the Thalassic Engine**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler—someone with a standard mammalian body, baseline sensory suite, and no permanent augmentations beyond what can be packed, worn, or temporarily induced—must treat the Thalassic Engine as a total-immersion biome of warm, living machinery. It is not a ruin you walk through. It is a saturated volume you enter as a temporary constituent of its circulatory fluid. Everything that follows assumes you will arrive fully submerged, remain submerged for hours to days, and leave without introducing irreversible disruption to a system that has already achieved a glacial, post-purpose equilibrium.

### 1. Physiological and Medical Foundations
The chamber is filled wall-to-wall with blood-warm saline (approximately 36–38 °C) under roughly 11–16 atmospheres of absolute pressure, depending on exact overburden and your depth within the 60 m vault. The water carries dissolved copper, sugars, and faint conductive organics; the light is perpetual soft green-gold; there is no air interface inside the Engine itself.

- **Pressure and gas management**: Complete a full saturation dive work-up or equivalent multiversal equivalent (helium-oxygen or hydrogen-oxygen mixtures calibrated for the depth, or a closed-circuit rebreather with bailout). You will need at least 24–48 hours of staged decompression on exit if you linger near the lower decks. Pre-load with antioxidants and check for patent foramen ovale; the slow machinery produces almost no bubbles of its own, but your own off-gassing must be controlled. If your travel method allows temporary physiological rewriting, induce elevated myoglobin and reinforced alveoli; otherwise rely on hardware.
- **Thermoregulation and skin**: The water is the temperature of a living body. Prolonged immersion will still leach heat from extremities once you stop moving and will macerate skin. Apply a thin, breathable barrier film (silicone or engineered lipid) that resists both salt and the mild iridescent organics. Bring a mild topical antifungal/antibacterial; the engineered bryophytes and hydroids are seedless and presumably sterile of animal pathogens, but your own flora will attempt to bloom in the warmth.
- **Osmotic and toxic load**: The saline is heavier than standard seawater and tastes of copper and sugar. Do not drink it. Even micro-amounts swallowed during mask clearing will deliver a mineral-metallic aftertaste and mild gut distress. Carry oral rehydration salts formulated to counteract copper uptake, plus chelating agents (standard multiversal DMSA or equivalent) in case bioaccumulation from prolonged skin contact or accidental ingestion becomes noticeable. Pre-screen for copper sensitivity. The metallic chloroplast inclusions and conductive gels are unlikely to be acutely poisonous—the ecology has stabilized—but they are foreign biochemistry. Assume low-level heavy-metal and glycoprotein exposure.
- **Sensory and vestibular**: There is almost no sound above the tick of cooling metal and the occasional bubble. Your own heartbeat and the rasp of your regulator will become loud. The dense curtains of ribbon-kelp and the slow pistons create faint, regular pressure waves. Practice extended null-input meditation or bring a low-volume bone-conduction soundtrack of rain or distant surf so the silence does not unnerve you into hypervigilance. The green-gold light is biologically soothing but monochromatic; after many hours color vision will shift. Carry a small white-light torch for true-color inspection of welds and joints, used sparingly so you do not startle yourself with the sudden reappearance of blue.
- **Immune and microbiome**: The Engine was never seeded with animals. You are the invasive animal. Full-spectrum prophylactics against novel polysaccharides, plus a post-dive microbiome reset protocol. Do not urinate freely into the volume; use a catheter system or external collection. Your waste is the first animal input in an unknown span of time.

If you have any cardiac irregularity, uncontrolled eczema, or history of nitrogen narcosis at modest depths, do not go. The place will not kill you quickly; it will simply hold you in warm, patient density until your systems fail.

### 2. Equipment and Loadout
Everything must be rated for continuous warm saltwater immersion and gentle abrasion against living tissue and occasional sharp ceramic edges.

- **Life support**: Closed-circuit rebreather with dual scrubbers and at least 12–18 hours endurance, plus bailout cylinders. Full-face mask with dual seals and integrated comms (even if only talking to yourself or a recording). Heated undergarment on low duty cycle is optional given the ambient temperature; a thin open-cell layer to prevent skin welding to the suit is wiser. Buoyancy compensator tuned for near-neutral in dense fluid; you will constantly push through vegetation and must be able to hover without kicking.
- **Movement and interaction**: Short, stiff fins or frog-kick technique only—long strides shred kelp curtains unnecessarily. Soft-palmed gloves with reinforced palms; you will brace against flywheels and pipe galleries. A collapsible monofilament reel and small pitons or magnetic clamps for temporary tethers—currents are almost nonexistent, but orientation inside absolute vegetation is easy to lose. Do not bring thrusters; the noise and shear would be vandalism.
- **Cutting and sampling**: A ceramic or diamond-edge knife kept sheathed. You may need to part a curtain or free an entangled hose, but never cut living tissue for convenience. Small sterile vials and a hand-powered aspirator for water and gel samples. Soft mesh bags for leaf or hydroid specimens if collection is permitted by your ethics. Magnifying loupe and waterproof macro lens. Everything clipped and tucked; dangling gear becomes another substrate for the plants.
- **Power and recording**: Battery packs sealed against the conductive water. Continuous low-light video and hydrophone; the almost-inaudible sighs of planetary gearing and the faint tick of metal are part of the place’s character. A simple chemical test kit for copper, sugars, pH, and dissolved organics. No active sonar or high-powered lights.
- **Redundancy and exit**: Two independent methods of signaling your retrieval beacon or opening your return aperture. A small chemical light stick that burns green-gold so it does not shatter the ambient aesthetic if dropped. Emergency inflatable lift bag in case you must ascend inside the chamber toward the dome (though the dome itself is not an exit).

Travel light. Every object you introduce is a hard angularity in a realm of soft occupancy.

### 3. Skills and Training
- Master hovering and frog-kick in a densely planted warm-water tank until you can move two meters without breaking a single frond. Practice “listening with the body”: the articulated joints on some fronds track moving parts; they will track you. Learn to still yourself so they settle.
- Zero-visibility navigation drills. The kelp curtains turn sight lines into green tunnels within arm’s reach.
- Basic recognition of slow mechanical hazard: a gear that turns once every few minutes can still shear a finger if you brace against it at the wrong moment. Watch for residual torsion in driveshafts.
- Contemplative immersion practice. The Engine’s metabolism is ceremonial. Travelers who arrive impatient experience the place as claustrophobic. Travelers who arrive willing to match its pace experience it as a vast, tender lung.

### 4. Mental and Conceptual Preparation
Understand what you are visiting. This is not a drowned factory and not a coral reef. It is an industrial metabolism that outlived its purpose and was colonized—by design or by drift—by vegetation that learned to drink voltage and sweat conductive gel. The barrier between built and grown has become “a polite fiction.” You will feel this in your hands: a turbine blade that grades into coralline bone, a control surface under living bark.

Expect the emotional arc:
- Initial awe at the cathedral scale and the soft light.
- Sensory saturation and mild disorientation as every stroke parts living tissue.
- A growing sense of being held rather than floating—warmth, density, the faint iridescence on your faceplate.
- Either profound peace or a quiet existential unease at a system that continues, patiently, without witnesses or destination.
- On longer stays, a subtle rewriting of your sense of time. Minutes stretch to match the axial engines. Your thoughts slow to the tick of cooling metal.

Do not anthropomorphize the plants as friendly or the machines as sleeping gods. They are simply complete. You are the incomplete thing moving through them. Carry no expectation of narrative revelation. The Engine is not waiting to tell you a story; it is still becoming itself slower than thought.

If you are prone to awe-states or derealization, set a hard time limit and a retrieval dead-man switch. The place is tenderly drowned and can drown the sense of a separate self just as tenderly.

### 5. Arrival, Conduct, and Departure
Arrive already sealed and pressurized, ideally through a quiet aperture that opens into mid-water in the central nave so you do not have to fight surface vegetation. Stabilize buoyancy. Spend the first minutes motionless, letting the residual currents and your own exhalations settle. Let the green-gold light adjust your eyes. Taste the copper-sugar on the first mask-clear and accept it as the local atmosphere.

Move as though visiting a sleeping body. Part curtains with the back of the hand. When you encounter a fusion zone—metal under bark, jointed fronds tracking a flywheel—observe without prying. Do not force articulation. Do not scrape samples from heat exchangers wearing eelgrass muffs; take only what has already sloughed.

Speak, if you speak, in low tones or not at all. The hydrophone will remind you how loud you are.

When you leave, reverse the process. Ascend or translate out slowly. Decompress fully. Decontaminate suit and skin of gels and algal films; some metallic inclusions may remain in creases. Log everything, then wait twenty-four hours before deciding what the visit “meant.” Meaning arrives at the Engine’s pace, not yours.

### 6. Contingencies
- Entanglement: stop, reverse the motion that caused it, tease free. Cutting is last resort.
- Equipment flood: switch to bailout, move to a clearer volume near a known pressure seam, and signal retrieval.
- Psychological overload: fix your eyes on a single slow-turning gear or a single hydroid bloom, match your breathing to its near-imperceptible motion, and wait for the traveler-self to return.
- Bio-reaction: surface or exit immediately at first sign of rash, metallic taste that intensifies, or unexplained fatigue. Chelate and reset.

You are not here to restart the Engine, nor to mourn it, nor to claim it. You are here to float in the middle distance while it holds you—warmth on the skin, green light on the eyelids, sharp new welds catching light like frost—and then to leave the density intact. Prepare as though entering a living lung that has forgotten it was ever built for anyone but itself. The ordinary human body can do this. It simply must remember, for a little while, how to be patient enough to belong to water and metal and leaf at once.
