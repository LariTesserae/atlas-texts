**Preparation for the Plateau That Forgot It Was Lowland**

You are an ordinary human who has learned to step between worlds. This place will not try to kill you quickly. It will try to dissolve you slowly—into grit, into damp, into softened seconds, into the patient conviction that nothing urgent has ever happened or ever will. Prepare as if you are packing for a long, cold, industrial pilgrimage across a mountain that is still deciding how high it intends to be, under a sky the color of wet iron, while cause and effect quietly miscount their steps.

### 1. Body and Breath: The Immediate Insult of the Air
The air tastes of metal and wet stone because it *is* metal and wet stone in suspension. Fine grit falls continuously—not in storms, but as a relentless isolation of particles that coat tongue, lungs, eyes, and every seam of clothing. Moisture beads on every surface and condenses inside your chest. Your lungs will feel lined with cold iron filings and black tea.

- **Respiratory protection is non-negotiable.** Bring a full-face respirator with interchangeable P100/HEPA filters *and* activated-carbon layers rated for metal fumes and coal smoke. Pack at least two complete spare filter sets in sealed bags; the grit will clog them faster than any ordinary dust. Add a small positive-pressure blower if you can carry the battery weight—exhaling against constant fine particulate becomes exhausting. Practice donning it blind and with wet gloves. The haze is perpetual late dusk; you will live inside that filter.
- **Eyes and skin.** Sealed goggles under the respirator or a full integrated hood. The grit is patient. It will find the corner of your eye and stay. Bring lubricating eye drops (preservative-free, many single-use vials) and thick, fragrance-free barrier cream or industrial moisturizing balm. Reapply constantly. Skin will crack from the combination of cold moisture and mineral abrasion. Lip balm with high wax content. Every mucous membrane needs defense.
- **Clothing system.** Layer for cold, damp, and abrasion, not for temperature extremes. Base layer: merino or synthetic that wicks yet does not hold the metallic taste against you. Mid: fleece or light insulated jacket that can be rinsed. Outer: heavy waxed cotton or modern equivalent hard-shell that sheds both the thin cold rains and the grit. Everything must have sealed seams or taped zippers; grit infiltrates like it owns the place. Multiple pairs of gloves—thin dexterous ones for tools and thick insulated waterproof ones. Gaiters. Boots with aggressive tread for scree, basalt, and half-settled paving stones that have not decided to remain flat. Bring a soft brush and compressed air (small canisters) solely for de-gritting clothing and seals every few hours. Accept that you will never be fully clean.
- **Thermal reality.** It is never warm. The perpetual dim carries a wet chill that settles into joints. A compact insulated jacket or vest that packs small. Chemical heat packs (many). The black water basins never freeze, but you can. The rain curtains are cold enough to steam ironwork when they hit.

### 2. Water, Food, and the Body’s Slow Accounting
Moisture is everywhere and nowhere reliable. Condensation forms on your own gear. The black rectangular basins reflect the wrong number of moons and look like old foundations or ore pits; do not drink them. Rivulets between stones are suspect. Steam is industrial and geothermal—possibly contaminated with whatever the engines still burn.

- **Water.** Carry more than you think. Collapsible bladders, minimum 6–8 liters capacity on your person, plus purification (chemical + filter capable of metals and industrial residue). A small solar still or condensation trap can supplement if you camp near cooling towers or vines that drip with metronomic patience, but test everything. The air itself deposits metallic film; any open water container needs a lid *immediately*. Electrolyte tablets—your sweat will carry grit taste.
- **Food.** Dense, non-perishable, low-residue, easy to eat with gloves and while wearing a respirator (pull-aside designs or practice the ritual of safe exposure). High-calorie bars, nut butters in squeeze packs, dried meat, hard cheeses if waxed, dehydrated meals you can reconstitute with *your* water. The processes here are pathologically slow; your digestion may feel desynchronized from clock time. Pack a small stove that works on solid fuel or butane (wind is inventible and sudden). Eating will feel mechanical. The copper-leaved shrubs and glowing mosses are unknown; do not forage unless you have multi-universe botanical testing gear and a death wish calibrated for slow toxins. Assume the fauna is inedible or reciprocal.
- **Waste and hygiene.** The place already feels half-excreted by geology. Pack out what you can. A trowel is useless on basalt; use sealed bags. Wet wipes (many, in waterproof pouches), hand sanitizer that does not freeze into useless gel, and a small microfiber towel that you resign yourself to turning permanently gray. Dental care matters—grit in the mouth is demoralizing. A compact mirror for checking eyes and nose for packed dust.

### 3. Light, Time, and the Softening of Duration
Light never quite arrives. You live in wet-iron dusk. Shadows detach and drift. A streetlamp’s beam can pool and spread like liquid for an afternoon before falling off its own edge. Time softens: shifts last three days or collapse into forty minutes, and no one records it anymore.

- **Lighting.** Multiple independent sources. Headlamp with red/white modes and long runtime (lithium batteries, many spares in waterproof cases). Chemical light sticks (cold, reliable, no sparks near possible steam vents). A small lantern for camp that can burn for hours. Reflective tape on your pack and limbs—fauna and any remaining workers move in the dim. Accept that light itself sometimes misbehaves; if a beam starts to puddle, step back and do not follow it.
- **Personal timekeeping.** This is critical for sanity. Redundant mechanical watches (spring-wound, not automatic—motion here is unreliable) kept in padded cases. A small hourglass or three if you can find them. Notches on a stick. A written log with *subjective* and *attempted objective* columns. Note when you eat, when you rest, when a chimney reverses smoke, when a vanishing point breathes. You need anchors that belong only to you. Do not trust factory clocks or the overlapping thunder of engines. The discrepancy is the point; your job is to notice it without drowning in it.
- **Sleep system.** Compact insulated sleeping pad (the stone leaches heat) and a bag rated colder than you expect. A bivy or lightweight tent with full bug/grit netting—though the larger fauna treat the city as geology and may simply walk through. Earplugs are only partial help against continuous industrial thunder and the torn-sheet-metal cries of dog-sized birds. Sleep in short, decided increments rather than waiting for night; night is a gradient that may not arrive cleanly. Some travelers report that sleep itself can stretch or compress. Set a mechanical wind-up alarm as ritual.

### 4. Navigation and the Fraying of Straight Lines
Walk long enough down a straight boulevard and the vanishing point begins to breathe. Doorways open onto rooms larger than exterior walls allow. The city sprawls without packing tight—districts separated by raw scree and emptiness. At the margins it thins into tool-marked rock and then unfinished dark.

- **Maps and dead reckoning.** Any map you draw will be provisional. Use a waterproof notebook and pencil (ink smears in the perpetual damp). Sketch landmarks that feel geologically stubborn: cooling towers, specific viaducts, the rectangular black basins. Compass may drift; bring two and note disagreement. GPS or multiverse equivalent devices often fail where rules no longer apply—treat them as secondary. Mark your path with biodegradable tape or stacked stone cairns *of your own making*; the local stone already sheds and rearranges itself slowly. When a doorway looks wrong, do not enter unless you have fixed a strong tether (literal rope) and a time limit you will honor even if felt duration lies.
- **Movement technique.** Slow is native here. Match it deliberately. Rushing invites the physics glitches to notice you. Test paving stones; some are half-decided. Cross open plazas when the multi-legged soot-colored herds are not ticking through— they are glacial but heavy and indifferent. Use the arterial viaducts for overview but watch for open elevators that rise and fall empty, chains clanking. At the outer margins the engines fall silent one by one; that silence is a navigational signal you are leaving the settlement-as-geology and entering pure becoming. Turn back unless you intend to walk into the unfinished height.
- **Tethers and markers.** Bright, non-local cord. Personal flagging. A small bag of chalk or temporary spray that is foreign to this place—anonymous chalk diagrams already appear overnight on boiler floors; your marks need to be distinguishable. If shadows detach, do not chase them. If smoke draws downward into a chimney, walk around the entire block rather than watching too long.

### 5. Fauna, Machines, and the Indifferent Inhabitants
Birds the size of dogs nest in gantries and cry like torn metal. Multi-legged wet-soot things move in herds at glacial pace, legs ticking like loose clockwork. Luminous moths, phosphorescent-slime crawlers, almost-mammalian chord-singers just below language. They do not fear the machines. Workers continue slow circuits even when physics reverses.

- **Fauna protocols.** Observation first, always. Most treat you as another slow geological event. Do not block herds. Do not climb gantries with nests. Light does not scare them; sudden movement might simply be irrelevant. A long walking staff is useful for balance and for gently indicating your personal radius. Pepper spray or a loud horn is last resort—noise is already continuous, so it may not register. The chord-singers can unsettle the edge of language centers; if you start hearing almost-words, move to a noisier engine district to drown them. Phosphorescent trails are pretty and probably cling. Assume nothing is domesticated.
- **Machines.** Great reciprocating engines in basements keep overlapping thunder, never quite synchronizing. Steam valves hiss long and wet. They have run on slightly modified cycles for a century; modifications arrive as anonymous chalk diagrams. Do not interrupt a cycle. Do not touch levers or valves whose purpose is opaque. The machines are part of the mountain’s new habit. If a process seems broken, it may simply be running on a longer rhythm than your attention span. Respect the industrial continuity the way you respect tide or erosion.
- **People.** “Workers” are mentioned continuing their circuits. They may be human, post-human, or simply another opportunistic species wearing habit. Do not assume shared language or urgency. Offer no sudden trade, no loud questions about the moons or the grit. A silent nod, a gift of clean water or a foreign cigarette if such things still mean anything, then move on. They have stopped recording time discrepancies; they will not start for you. If someone leaves chalk diagrams, you may look but do not correct them.

### 6. Mind, Reality Fraying, and the Pathology of Slowness
Reality frays at the edges of attention. Buildings settle a centimeter per decade. Shrubs advance a meter per generation. Breakdowns of physics feel gradual, like ice melting under uniform pressure. You can watch light puddle for an afternoon. The erosion of mountain and the erosion of cause-and-effect rhyme.

- **Mental anchors.** Bring physical objects that have strong personal history and do not belong to this world: a photograph sealed in plastic, a smooth stone from your origin, a handwritten letter to yourself with firm statements of identity and intent. Touch them at decided intervals. Recite names, dates, the reason you came. Keep the log mentioned earlier—not just of events but of *your own certainty levels*. When doorways lie about their volume, when shadows drift, when a single boulevard’s vanishing point inhales, name the phenomenon out loud inside your respirator and keep walking to a place where geometry is still mostly honest.
- **Pace of expectation.** Leave urgency behind. If you came for a specific artifact, measurement, or meeting, accept that it may require longer subjective residence than planned. The plateau does not remember being lowland; it will not hurry to explain itself. Boredom is a real hazard—endless wet dusk, continuous mechanical thunder, slow herds. Bring a small analog entertainment: a paperback that can survive damp (or many sealed pages), a musical instrument that does not mind grit (harmonica), carving tools for local soft metal scraps if you find any. Do not bring anything that requires precise digital timing.
- **Sanity calibration.** The place is melancholic rather than malevolent. It is industrial ruin and geological adolescence sharing the same body. Feelings of loneliness will be amplified by the empty horizon and clouds that form below you. Schedule radio checks or multiverse beacon pings if your travel method allows—even if replies are delayed by the same soft time. If you feel yourself beginning to think in slower and slower loops, matching the engines, deliberately perform a fast, pointless task (clapping, counting backward rapidly, dancing for thirty of your own counted seconds). Reassert human tempo.
- **Exit discipline.** Decide in advance the conditions under which you leave: grit reaching a certain load in your filters, time log showing more than X subjective days, a glitch that frightens rather than fascinates, the thinning of the city into true unfinished dark. Mark the entry point heavily with your own foreign materials. The plateau continues beyond the settlement, still becoming. Do not follow it into the absolute dark without declaring a new expedition.

### 7. Gear Loadout Summary (Ordinary Human Limits)
- Full-face respirator + spare filters + blower option  
- Sealed eye protection, barrier creams, wipes, lip balm  
- Layered grit-shedding clothing, boots, multiple gloves, brush  
- 6–8+ L water capacity + purification for metals  
- Dense packaged food for 10–14 subjective days + stove  
- Redundant mechanical timekeepers + logbook + pencils  
- Multiple light sources + reflective markers  
- Waterproof notebook, compasses, cord, foreign chalk/tape  
- Sleep system rated for wet cold  
- Staff, basic first aid (emphasize eyes, lungs, abrasions, grit removal), painkillers, any personal meds in excess  
- Personal anchors (photo, letter, stone)  
- Small comforts that survive damp  
- Multiverse transit device with extra power and a pre-set return window that you will treat as sacred even if local time argues

### 8. Final Intuitive Posture
Move like the herds: deliberate, jointed, ticking against stone. Breathe only through your filters. Let the thin cold rains darken your outer layer and then steam off the engines as you pass. Watch the light pool without following it off its own beam. When the vanishing point breathes, breathe back in your own rhythm and choose a different street. Record everything that refuses to stay consistent. You are visiting a place where the mountain and the factory and the rules are all still cooling into their new shapes. An ordinary human cannot stop the grit rain or synchronize the pistons or make the moons count correctly. You can only remain ordinary—anchored, filtered, logged, and willing to leave when the slow poetry of the place begins to rewrite your tempo.

Pack carefully. The plateau does not hate you. It simply does not remember any other way to be, and it has all the time in the world that it no longer bothers to measure. Walk in, walk through, walk out again while you still know which direction “out” was.
