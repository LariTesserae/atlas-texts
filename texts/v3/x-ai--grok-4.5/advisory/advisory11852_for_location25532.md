**Preparation for the Drowned Continuum**

You are an ordinary human who happens to step between worlds. Your body is still the same fragile, air-breathing, warm-blooded thing it has always been: lungs that panic without oxygen, skin that leaches heat in cold water, eyes that need photons, a mind that expects horizon, soundscape, and the small mercies of change. This place offers none of those by default. It is a single, finished liquid room the size of a dead continent, built of perfect stone and then given forever to cold water and deliberate green. To visit it as anything other than a brief, dying intrusion, you must become a temporary citizen of stillness.

### 1. The Body Must Be Made Amphibious and Slow
Ordinary scuba is an insult here; tanks will empty while you are still crossing a single terrace. You need a closed-circuit rebreather rated for multi-day endurance, with scrubbers you can recharge from concentrated CO₂ absorbents carried in redundant canisters. Better: a gill-analog membrane system or liquid-breathing emulsion if your multiverse transit method allows temporary physiological rewriting. The water is cold—assume 2–6 °C in the upper courts, colder in the deeps—so a full drysuit with active thermal recirculation (or a low-power chemical heat pack array) is non-negotiable. Hypothermia will not announce itself dramatically; it will simply erase your ability to care about leaving.

Pressure is the quiet killer of comfort. The surface (if a true surface still exists) is so far above that many chambers sit at several atmospheres. Equalize constantly. Carry a dual-stage mechanical and chemical equalizer for middle ears and sinuses; the architecture loves narrow vertical slots where pressure gradients sneak up on you. Never free-dive the long staircases that spiral into true darkness—your eardrums will become part of the fitted stone.

Propulsion: long-blade fins for silence and economy, plus a low-thrust worm-drive or magnetohydrodynamic scooter for the multi-day traverses the place demands. The scooter must be able to idle for hours without vibration; any mechanical whine becomes a personal violation in a world whose only native sound is the pressure-hum of moving water and the rare wet applause of shifting kelp.

### 2. Light, Vision, and the Ethics of Seeing
There is almost no light. What exists is a pewter rumor from far above, plus the faint stored glow of pale algal films. Your eyes will adapt further than you think, but not enough. Bring three independent lighting systems:
- A dim, broad-spectrum “memory lamp” on a head mount, tunable down to the brightness of those algal films so you can read joints and holdfasts without erasing the place’s native luminosity.
- A narrow, high-output beam for emergency signaling or inspecting deep niches (use sparingly; every photon you introduce is an anachronism).
- Chemiluminescent strips and a vial of cultured bioluminescent algae matched to the local films, so you can mark a path without committing technological violence.

Practice moving by pressure and silhouette for hours in training tanks before you go. Learn to feel the difference between open court and slot canyon by the way water resists your shoulders. The vegetation will brush you constantly—ribbons of kelp, feathery reds, soft black-green nap. It is not hostile, but it will foul lights, snag straps, and turn a careless kick into a green cocoon. Move as the makers once placed their stones: deliberately, with respect for joint and edge.

### 3. Navigation in a Finished Labyrinth
The architecture never ends; it only changes its dialect of ashlar and pillar. Continental scale means you can travel for days along one terrace and still be “inside.” Dead reckoning fails. GPS of any origin fails. You will need:
- An inertial mapping suite that records every turn, rise, and drop in a tactile or bone-conduction display (screens freeze the wrong way here).
- Spools of biodegradable, weakly luminescent thread that dissolves after weeks, so you can lay temporary Ariadne lines without littering eternity.
- A personal sonar tuned to stone densities rather than biology; it will paint the cyclopean blocks and the hollows behind them as a continuous grammar. Learn its language until the overlapping stelae and nested courts feel like rooms in a house you once knew.
- Redundant mechanical depth gauges and a simple plumb-line spool for the vertical courts where staircases reverse themselves into lighter water that is still not light.

Accept that you will get lost in the pleasant sense. The place is not a maze designed to confound; it is a completed thought so large that any path is correct if you have the air and the calm to follow it. Still, designate a “home court”—a distinctive plaza of truncated pillars and heavy mat—where you cache a beacon and supplies. Return to it the way a sleeper returns to the same dream hallway.

### 4. Sustenance, Waste, and the Long Now
Food must be dense, non-perishable, and eatable inside a helmet or mask: nutrient gels, compressed bars that do not crumb, perhaps a small cold-water still for extracting fresh water from the surrounding volume if your rebreather does not already handle it. The local vegetation is not food for you; the cold-growth kelp and mosses have spent ages becoming exactly what the stone requires, not what a mammal requires. Do not harvest except in true emergency, and even then take only what has already detached.

Waste is a moral problem. This world has no fauna to process your leavings and no current strong enough to carry them far. Everything you release will settle into some perfect joint and stay. Carry full containment. Plan exits before bowels or bladder become urgent. The stillness makes every trace an archive.

Sleep is possible but strange. Find a re-entrant where the algal nap is thickest, tether yourself loosely so you do not drift into a pillar, dim every light, and let the pressure-hum become a heartbeat. You will wake convinced only minutes have passed while the vegetation has lengthened by another invisible fraction. Dreams here are vertical and silent.

### 5. The Mind Must Be Prepared for Completion
This is the hardest preparation. The place is finished. It does not need you. It does not threaten you. It simply continues. Ordinary human minds are built for unfinished stories—threats to dodge, resources to chase, other voices answering. Here the only voice is the low note of water and the occasional soft clap of kelp. The scale will first exhilarate, then oppress, then dissolve the difference. You will float in a slot-canyon of ashlar so precise the joints still drink water rather than admit it, and feel the centuries settle another hair’s breadth. The absence of animals is absolute; even the memory of bones has been reincorporated. You are the only quick thing.

Train for this. Spend weeks in sensory-reduced tanks or deep caves with no exit schedule. Practice the slow emotions: reverence without worship, curiosity without acquisition, solitude without loneliness. Carry one small, meaningful object from your origin world—a worn coin, a scrap of dry fabric, a recording of wind in leaves—and touch it only when the finished quality threatens to erase your sense that time can still move for you. Do not bring music or spoken entertainment; any imposed soundscape becomes noise against the native pressure-hum. Let the place teach you its tempo.

Accept that “visiting” may recalibrate what you mean by home. Many travelers report that after returning they find ordinary air too sharp, ordinary light too frantic, ordinary conversation too loud. The drowned continuum does not haunt; it simply remains inside the mind as the default shape of permanence.

### 6. Gear Loadout (Minimal, Redundant, Quiet)
- Multi-day rebreather + thermal drysuit with hot-water or chemical loop
- Primary quiet thruster + fin backup
- Triple-redundant lights (dim native-matched, pencil beam, bio-marker)
- Inertial mapper + dissolving thread + stone-sonar
- Contained nutrient supply for the planned duration plus 100 % margin
- Full waste containment
- Soft tool kit: nylon probes for testing joints without scarring, sampling vials if you must study the algae, a single mechanical crowbar only for self-rescue from collapse (none is expected)
- One personal talisman of the dry, changing world
- Exit method: whatever multiverse key or transit anchor you use, keep it strapped against the body where kelp cannot gentle it away. Test the recall frequently; the deeper courts can damp more than light.

### 7. Entry and Conduct
Arrive in open water above a major terrace if possible, then descend like a stone the makers themselves might have lowered. Do not kick hard. Let the cold take you gradually so your mind keeps pace with your skin. Greet the first kelp ribbons as residents, not obstacles. When you pass broken pillars and lintels vanishing into murk, move as though the original craftspeople might still be watching through time rather than space. They worked by eye and cord and weight; you move by lung and lamp and borrowed time. Leave no new joints, no modern trash, no bright scars.

You will never map it all. That is correct. The continuum is not a site to conquer or a mystery to solve; it is a condition to inhabit briefly and cleanly. Prepare so that your presence is only another slow current, cold and careful, that for a little while shares the quiet room of uncountable chambers and then withdraws, leaving the green to thicken and the blocks to settle another hair’s breadth toward the only shape they will ever need.

When you finally surface—somewhere, sometime—breathe the dry air like a language you once spoke fluently and must now relearn. The water will stay in your ears longer than physics suggests. That, too, is preparation of a kind: the knowledge that some places finish you as much as you finish visiting them.
