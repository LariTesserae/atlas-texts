**Preparation for the Sparse City Under Slate Light**

You are an ordinary human multiverse traveler: no implants beyond the standard jump-stabilizer, no engineered organs, no permanent cognitive overlays. Your body still expects roughly Earth-normal gravity, a brighter spectrum of light, drier air most of the time, and a denser social texture. This place will not kill you for being ordinary, but it will quietly reveal every assumption you carry about density, noise, dryness, and finished-ness. Prepare as if you are visiting a living architectural score that has only just begun to play and prefers not to be interrupted.

### 1. Physiological and Sensory Acclimation
The light is the first renegotiation. The sky is permanent wet-slate dimness; “day” is only a half-shade warmer. Ordinary human eyes will initially strain, then over-compensate, then settle into a softer, slower visual tempo. Spend three to five local cycles (their length is close enough to 24 hours that your circadian rhythm will not collapse) doing almost nothing except walking short loops near your arrival anchor and letting the rods in your retinas reclaim primacy. Do not bring bright lamps or high-lumen headlamps as primary gear; they will feel violent here and will mark you as someone who has not yet understood the place. A single low-output, warm-spectrum continuum lamp (adjustable down to the brightness of luminous moss) is enough for reading or close work. If your jump kit includes temporary low-light amp drops or contact filters, use the mildest setting for the first two days only, then wean yourself. The goal is to see the way the locals and the mosses see: edges first, then slow internal glows, then the violet pulse of intradimensional conduits.

Air is warm and holds fine, continuous condensation. It never quite rains hard and never quite dries. Your skin will bead, then the warmth will drink the beads back. Mucous membranes stay slightly swollen; lips chap less than they feel perpetually soft. Begin a simple humidity protocol two days before jump: increase ambient moisture in your staging habitat, breathe steamy air, and stop using heavy moisturizers so your skin’s own barrier reasserts itself. Upon arrival, expect a mild, pleasant sinus fullness for the first hours—like walking into a warm greenhouse after dry winter. If you are prone to respiratory irritation, pack a short course of broad-spectrum antihistamine or a simple saline rinse; the local microbial load appears gentle and deliberate, but your immune system will still notice the difference in water chemistry and airborne volatiles from the spiral trees and moss.

Temperature sits in a narrow, skin-friendly band. You will rarely feel cold or hot, only damp-warm. The edited plazas hold air half a degree warmer and sound-dampened; stepping into one feels like entering a held breath. Train yourself to notice these micro-climates without commenting on them aloud at first. Your thermoregulation will adapt within a day; the larger adjustment is psychological—the absence of sharp thermal contrast removes a habitual marker of time and place.

Gravity and footing feel Earth-cousin normal, yet the ground is “newly decided.” Soft dark soil that stays cool an inch down, sharp pale stone ridges still scorched or striated, braided channels of damp sand. Ankle strength and proprioception matter more than raw endurance. In the weeks before travel, walk barefoot on uneven surfaces, practice slow balance work, and strengthen the small stabilizers of feet and knees. Upon arrival, your first footwear should be flexible, grippy, quick-drying soles with enough tread for wet aggregate and enough give for soft earth. Rigid hiking boots will feel clumsy and leave uglier prints than necessary.

### 2. Clothing and Carried Gear
Dress for continuous gentle moisture and for visual quiet. Fabrics should wick, breathe, and refuse to cling when damp. Matte finishes in deep jade, slate, soft charcoal, or the pale of new stone. Avoid high-saturation colors and anything that crinkles loudly. A lightweight, packable shell that sheds condensation without rustling is useful for the brief true rains and for sitting on cool stone. Layers should be few; the air itself is a layer.

Carry almost nothing that announces itself. A small, soft-sided satchel of woven or grown-feeling material is better than rigid packs. Inside:
- One change of the same quiet clothing.
- A compact, low-output continuum lamp.
- Minimal medical kit (hydration salts tuned for slightly different electrolyte profiles, blister care, the antihistamine/saline already mentioned, a broad but gentle antiseptic).
- A simple paper or matte-film notebook and graphite or mineral stylus. Local surfaces can remember fingerprints for a generation; your own notes should not compete with that memory unless invited.
- A small vial of your home world’s rain or river water (for grounding, and as a polite offering if the moment arises—though offerings here are more often gestures of stillness than objects).
- Nutrient-dense, low-odor rations for the first three days while you learn what the gardens and any public dispensaries consider ordinary food. Assume the local biochemistry is compatible enough for short stays but do not binge on unfamiliar fruits or saps until you have watched others.
- A soft cloth for wiping condensation from instruments or face—nothing that leaves lint or scent.

Leave behind: drones, loud scanners, bright multi-tools, scented anything, single-use plastics, and any device that pings or chimes without being asked. The city’s own tech is past spectacle; yours should be as well. If your multiverse kit includes a passive environmental translator or a consent-protocol key, keep it on the lowest outgoing setting. Doors here consent; surfaces remember and then politely forget. Your first interactions should be requests, not demands.

### 3. Mental and Temporal Preparation
This place is still beginning. That is its deepest quality. Nothing has had time to round or soften completely; yet everything already conducts a long, quiet conversation among stone, vine, root network, condensation, and the floating or ridge-rooted structures. The emotional weather is spaciousness without loneliness, lushness without crowding, function without density of body. Many travelers arrive expecting wonder that shouts. Here wonder is the patient business of water and leaf.

Practice silence as an active skill. Sit for thirty minutes a day in the days before jump with no input, noticing the small sounds of your own breathing and the building around you. Upon arrival you will hear the dual drip (leaf to stone, stone to channel), the rare rustle of bamboo-like stands without wind, the almost inaudible slide of a transit capsule, the soft chime of arrival, and very little else. The loudest thing for kilometers may be your own footsteps if you walk heavily. Learn to walk as if the soil remembers. Heel-to-toe, slow, weight distributed. The packed-earth and pale-aggregate paths stay quiet under such feet.

Time feels differently parceled. Afternoon is only a half-shade warmer; night is relative. The nanoscale relays drift like slow will-o’-wisps and knit weather and communication into one gentle process. Your internal clock may try to impose old hours. Let it loosen. Mark time instead by condensation cycles, by the slow brightening or dimming of moss when a larger many-jointed shape passes in the outer ridges, by the occasional opening of a tower façade like a flower deciding the air is right. Keep a light journal of sensory notes rather than clock time for the first week.

Emotionally, expect an initial phase of mild disorientation that feels like tenderness rather than threat. The under-occupation can read as melancholy if you come from crowded worlds. Counter it by attending to the deliberate spacing: the twenty- or thirty-meter gaps between broad-leafed canopies are thermal corridors and sightlines left on purpose. The city negotiated with the land; you are invited to negotiate with the city in the same spirit. Loneliness, if it arises, is usually the ghost of expecting denser social proof. The place is companioned at scales you normally overlook—root networks trading sugars and data, microscopic grazers, slow chemical talk between stone and vine. Practice feeling accompanied by those.

### 4. Navigational and Interaction Protocols
Arrival anchors usually place you on or near a higher new ridge or at the edge of a low garden “street.” Do not stride toward the nearest luminous tower. Stand still for several minutes. Feel the warmth on skin, the damp earth, the almost-absent sound. Let the geometry declare itself: towers like measured notes on a sparse score, green volume held carefully between them.

Paths are obvious once you stop looking for grids. Follow the packed earth and the gentle catenaries of the carbon-and-grown-glass walkways. When a walkway is only a heat-haze suggestion, wait; it will decide whether to fully present. Never force a surface. Doors do not open so much as consent—approach, pause, offer the intention of entry without urgency. If the door remains, walk away; another will consent later or elsewhere. Surfaces that remember fingerprints will take yours gently. Do not wipe them obsessively; the forgetting is polite and already scheduled.

Transit capsules arrive with a soft chime along rails that are sometimes there. Board only if the capsule’s interior lighting and the subtle pressure of its field feel welcoming. They are not public in the old sense; they are shared among those who move at the city’s tempo. Walking is almost always better for the first days.

If you meet fauna—the long-legged hare with translucent ears, the pale bird with too many primaries, the rare low many-jointed shape—do not pursue or photograph with flash. Acknowledge with stillness. The mosses brightening at the larger creature’s passage is a sufficient greeting; you may let your own breathing slow in answer.

Should you encounter inhabitants (they exist, though the description emphasizes sparseness), match their volume. Speech here is rare and low. A nod, an open hand, a shared silence on a ridge overlooking the damp plain—these carry more than sentences. If conversation happens, let it be about the continuing extrusion of young stone, the way a garden has recently respired, the half-shade shift of the light. Avoid questions that demand rapid inventories of history or politics; the city is more interested in its own long present.

### 5. Daily Rhythm and Sustaining Practices
Morning (cooler slate): walk a slow circuit, noticing new condensation beads and any millimeter shifts in stone or walkway. The city grows compensatory joints; you can learn patience from them.

Mid-cycle: find a plaza where the air is edited. Sit. Let the hush and the tuned dampness reset your nervous system. Eat simply.

Later: climb one of the higher ridges. The city extends to every dim-air horizon. Do not try to name every tower. Feel the under-occupation as intentional room rather than absence.

Evening relative: watch the nanoscale relays drift above the canopies. If you have a soft lamp, use it only inside a consented space. Sleep when the body asks; the constant low light makes classic night optional. Many travelers find they need less sleep after the first cycles because the sensory load is so even and non-jarring.

Hydrate more than you think necessary—the warm air and constant light sweat of condensation exchange can mask loss. Eat when locals or public interfaces suggest; when uncertain, the luminous mosses and certain vine exudates appear to form part of a safe, slow cuisine, but watch first.

### 6. Risks and Quiet Contingencies
The primary risks are subtle:
- Over-bright behavior or gear that disrupts the hush and the light economy.
- Impatience with consent-based architecture, leading to wasted effort or mild social cooling.
- Underestimating the soft ground and sharp stone—twisted ankles, not falls from height.
- Psychological inflation of the sparseness into alienation. Counter with deliberate attention to micro-life and process.
- Minor biochemical mismatch (stomach, skin, sinuses). Your kit covers the short term; if staying longer, seek local guidance through quiet observation or a consented interface.
- Getting gently lost. The city is navigable by rhythm more than map. If disoriented, return to the last ridge or garden that felt welcoming and wait. Transit or a walkway will eventually offer itself.

In the unlikely event of true distress, the intradimensional conduits and the drifting relays form a continuous gentle process that includes care. Stillness and a clear, non-panicked request broadcast at human emotional volume are usually sufficient. The place is not hostile; it is simply already complete in its incompleteness and does not need your urgency.

### 7. What Success Feels Like
After several days you will notice you have stopped waiting for the skyline to appear. You walk ten minutes between towers and meet only the drip of condensation from a cantilevered garden. Your footsteps have quieted. You can feel, through the soles of your feet, that the land is still learning its shape and that the city is learning with it. The rules of weight, growth, wall, and weather are cousins to the ones you know—close enough to trust, different enough to keep you honest.

You will find yourself leaving longer pauses before speaking, before stepping, before asking a surface to remember you. You may sit on a young stone ridge at what passes for dusk and realize the strangeness was never loud. It was the quiet certainty that everything works, everything has room, and everything is still, in some essential way, beginning.

When you eventually jump onward, take almost nothing physical. Take the recalibrated eyes, the quieter gait, the new patience with unfinished edges, and the bodily memory of warm damp air that never quite dries and never quite chills. That is the correct souvenir from a city that does not announce itself.

Prepare lightly. Arrive willing to be edited by the air. Stay long enough for the condensation to feel like a conversation rather than a condition. Then you will have visited properly.
