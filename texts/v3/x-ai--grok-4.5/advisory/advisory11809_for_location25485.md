**Prepare as if you are about to become a temporary organ of a living mountain that has not finished deciding to be dry land.**

The Shelf of Unfinished Stone is not a destination you arrive at so much as a medium you must consent to enter. An ordinary human body from a surface world—lungs, skin that expects air, eyes calibrated to directional sunlight, a sense of time measured in hours and ownership measured in vertical plots—will experience the place as a continuous, gentle violation of every unspoken assumption. Preparation is therefore less about packing clever gadgets and more about re-negotiating your own ontology before you ever touch the water.

### 1. Physiological Reconfiguration (Do This First, and Do It Slowly)

You will live under approximately ten atmospheres of pressure in water that never quite grows cold and never quite grows bright. The pressure is “only a constant companion,” but companions can still kill the unprepared.

- **Breathing and gas management.** Ordinary free-diving capacity is useless for residence. You need either a multiverse-grade closed-circuit rebreather tuned for long-duration mid-water work (scrubbers that handle the faint mineral outgassing, no bubbles if you can help it—bubbles are rude and expensive), or a temporary biological graft: a set of external gill filaments or an internal hemoglobin/myoglobin upgrade that lets you extract oxygen from the warm, slightly oxygen-poor water the way the locals do. The graft is preferable for cultural immersion; mechanical systems mark you as transient and invite questions you may not want. Either way, train for weeks in a pressure tank that replicates both the absolute pressure and the slow, warm current. Learn to equalize without conscious thought. Nitrogen narcosis at this depth is mild but insidious—it makes time feel even more viscous than it already is. If you keep residual nitrogen, you will never surface again without a carefully staged decompression chamber or a reverse-pressure transition gate. Most travelers who fall in love with the place simply never leave; plan for that possibility.

- **Skin and mucosa.** Constant immersion softens everything. Your epidermis will macerate, then toughen if you give it time and the right mineral exposure. Begin daily soaks in warm saline spiked with silica and the greener unidentified precipitate weeks in advance. Add dilute iodine and the metallic sweetness of cooling basalt if you can synthesize it. Your eyes must learn to gather the milked-down silver and the foxfire without straining; spend hours in near-dark rooms lit only by bioluminescent cultures. Eyelids become almost ceremonial. Many travelers grow a nictitating membrane or simply accept permanent slight myopia underwater. Ear canals and sinuses must be conditioned against the low-frequency rock-song that vibrates through everything; untreated, it produces a permanent low-grade vertigo that the locals experience as ordinary silence.

- **Thermoregulation and metabolism.** The water is warm by abyssal standards, but your core will still leach heat over days. A thin layer of subcutaneous fat or a mild thyroid adjustment helps. You will eat less in absolute calories because movement is gliding rather than walking, yet you will crave the iodine-rich tissues of the macroalgae and the soft parts of the mineral-farming crabs. Start a diet of seaweed, shellfish, and bone broth now so your gut flora begin the shift. Metal is myth here; if you carry a titanium or steel knife it will be an object of quiet wonder and possible ritual confiscation. Replace every tool with stone, bone, fire-hardened mangrove, or the hard outer tissues of certain algae. Practice striking the chemical heat-pair stones until you can boil a bladder of soup without looking.

- **Buoyancy and posture.** You will spend most of your time at neutral buoyancy. Practice the slow pole-and-glide of the coracle sleds weighted with stones. Learn to hang vertically in the water column for hours the way the gardeners do. Your neck and lower back will thank you if you strengthen the deep stabilizers now. The density of the city means you can trail fingertips along three walls in one breath; train your proprioception so that constant near-contact feels intimate rather than claustrophobic.

### 2. Sensory and Cognitive Retuning

Light never brightens past perpetual late-afternoon underwater. At the deeper indigo that passes for night, the mineral skins glow cold green and map the half-born architecture. Spend a month living by bioluminescent dials and red-shifted lamps. Directional sunlight will feel aggressive and almost obscene when (if) you return.

Sound is mostly absence plus the held-note of adjusting rock and the soft click-hum of speech. Every syllable is metabolically expensive in water; verbosity is a surface-world vulgarity. Begin practicing economical communication: short clicks for attention, chest-resonant hums for affection or agreement, silence for most everything else. Laugh without bubbles. Children here master it early; you will look like a tourist if you cannot. Record and loop the low-frequency geology so your nervous system stops startling at the mountain’s self-adjustments.

Time itself feels syrupy. A house may rise fifteen feet in a generation. A street may simply ascend into gloom and be replaced from below. Stop wearing clocks. Stop thinking in deadlines. Practice tasks that cannot be hurried: stacking stones that must be accepted by living basalt, waiting for mineral skins to precipitate overnight, pruning kelp with the unhurried economy of people who have never known seasons that hurry. If you arrive still addicted to urgency, the place will gently erase you.

### 3. Material Culture and What to Bring (and What to Leave)

Leave almost all metal, plastics that off-gas, and anything that expects to stay dry. Bring:

- A small set of flaked-stone knives, bone awls, and blades of fire-hardened mangrove already seasoned in salt water.
- Twisted gut and algal fiber cordage.
- A collapsible flat-bottomed coracle frame you can weight with local stone; the locals will teach you the rest.
- Sealed bladders and the two-stone heat pairs (synthesize or source equivalents that work underwater).
- A few offerings for the stone: crushed shell, bone meal, the correct red algae that encourage cementation. These are both practical and polite. New buildings are midwifed, not constructed; you may be invited to help claim a fresh swell.
- Bioluminescent starter cultures compatible with the local worm-sigils and siphons, in case you need to read or be read at night.
- A compact library of the oldest songs about rain that falls upward and light that hurts—purely for private grief or wonder. Do not sing them aloud until you understand the weight.

Clothing is minimal and often unnecessary once your skin adapts; when worn it is dyed with the faintly luminous reds and indigos of the rooftop gardens. Learn to tie fronds and harvest the softer tissues. You will be expected to garden if you stay more than a few tidal cycles (there are no true tides, only the slow pulse of the rising dome).

Currency is negotiation, lateral inheritance, and demonstrated patience with lithification. No one owns the vertical. Internalize this before you arrive or you will commit unconscious sacrilege by looking upward possessively.

### 4. Cultural and Etiquette Immersion

The people treat the growth of stone the way other cultures treat weather. Watch it. Scrape it. Build around it. Sometimes sing to it. Never try to dominate it. When a lesser giant—the pale, house-roof-sized cartilaginous ghost—moves through the outer channels, become still with the respectful stillness given to ambulatory weather. Do not point. Do not chase.

Speech is soft. Touch is constant and casual because everything is near enough to touch. Privacy is achieved by angle and attention rather than distance. Children laugh carefully. Adults prune kelp with flaked-stone knives and tie new fronds with gut. Join them. The density is saturating without crowdedness; it is the density of a kelp forest that learned masonry. Move as though you are already part of the interval rather than a visitor occupying space.

If a family is midwifing a house on a new dough-like swell, you may be asked to place an offering or stack a course. Do it slowly. Wait to see whether the mountain accepts the weight. Sometimes it does not. That, too, is ordinary.

### 5. Psychological and Existential Readiness

This is the part most surface-dwellers underestimate. There is no sky. There has been no sky within living memory. The oldest songs speak of it the way we speak of mythic floods. The dim silver is a remembered rumor of sunlight. You will feel the absence first as a pressure behind the eyes, then as a quiet panic, then—if you stay long enough—as a relief so profound it rearranges your sense of what a world is for.

The nearly imperceptible upwelling of the world itself becomes the only real motion. Geology becoming landscape becoming home, one patient inch at a time. You will start to feel your own thoughts precipitating like mineral skins across the new faces of your mind. Some travelers report that after a few months the distinction between self and shelf grows thin. You may dream you are basalt still exhaling thin curtains of heat. You may wake certain that your bones are negotiating with lithification.

Prepare by spending long periods in sensory-reduced environments: flotation tanks, deep caves, silent retreats. Practice the meditation of unfinished things—projects left deliberately incomplete, sentences that trail into quiet. Make peace with the possibility that you will not want to leave, and with the equal possibility that the mountain will someday rise far enough that the city must thicken itself anew or be left behind in the upper gloom. Inheritance is lateral. No one owns the vertical. That includes you.

### 6. Arrival, First Days, and Contingencies

Cross into the Shelf at the outer channels where the pillow lava is freshest and the kelp forests thin. Enter slowly—pressure, light, silence. Make no large movements. Let the translucent crabs and the phosphorescent worm-sigils notice you before the people do. When you meet the first gardeners, offer a small sealed bladder of something warm and a handful of crushed shell. Speak in single hums. Wait.

Sleep in the niches they offer; the insides are often dyed luminous. Navigate at night by the foxfire skins that outline every new ledge. When you trail your hand along three different walls in a single breath, notice that the walls are still warm and still growing.

If you must leave, you will need a staged decompression path or a multiverse gate that equalizes pressure and gas loads gradually. Surface too fast and the mountain will keep pieces of you as nitrogen bubbles and memory. Most who prepare well enough to thrive do not leave quickly. Some do not leave at all. The Shelf keeps rising—slow as coral, slower than regret—and the warm water keeps covering it, and the city keeps thickening itself across the unfinished stone like a thought the mountain has not yet finished having.

You are preparing to become part of that thought for a while. Pack patience measured in generations. Pack the willingness to be lifted or left below as the dome decides. Pack the understanding that home, here, is not a place that finished, but a place that is still, patiently, becoming.

That is enough. The rest the stone will teach you, one soft mineral skin at a time.
