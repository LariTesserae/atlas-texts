**Preparation for the Wet Metal Night (the Self-Making Industrial Underworld)**

You are an ordinary human. You bruise, you chill, you tire, you disorient, you need calories and clean water and sleep that is not merely unconsciousness. This place was never built for you. It was poured, bolted, and welded for throughput, for the slow ecstasy of its own expansion. It does not hate you; it simply does not contain the concept of you except as a possible bipedal interruption in a pump cycle. Prepare accordingly. Over-prepare. The penalties for under-preparation are hypothermia laced with industrial pathogens, disorientation so complete you walk in slow circles for days, structural revision while you are standing on the thing being revised, and a quiet psychological fraying that feels at first like awe.

### 1. Thermoregulation and the Metal Cold
The cold is conductive and personal. It climbs through boot soles, through gloved palms when you steady yourself on a railing, through any damp fabric that touches skin. It is not the clean bite of winter air; it is the deep, wet insistence of refrigerated mass.

- Wear a base layer of hydrophobic merino or synthetic that wicks even when saturated. Over it, a mid-layer of high-loft synthetic insulation (down collapses and dies here). Outermost shell must be fully waterproof *and* breathable enough that you do not drown in your own sweat when you hit a furnace-bleed corridor ten meters from a frosting brine pipe. The shell needs pit zips, full side zips, and a hood that cinches over a helmet.
- Carry a compact vapor-barrier liner system for sleep or static waits: a thin plastic or coated suit you can don so that your own moisture stays inside and your insulation stays dry. You will use it.
- Gloves: multiple pairs. Heavy waterproof work gloves with grip for climbing and rail work, thinner dexterous pairs for gear, and chemical-resistant disposables. Metal leaches heat instantly; never touch bare structure with bare skin for more than a second.
- Head: balaclava + insulated waterproof hood + hard hat or climbing helmet with mounting bracket. The helmet is non-negotiable—low pipes, swinging loads, sudden sparks, unfinished overheads.
- Test every seam of your clothing in a cold shower while wearing a loaded pack before you ever translate. If water reaches skin, redesign.

### 2. The Water Problem (It Is Everywhere It Should Not Be)
You will walk in ankle-deep black film. You will be rained on by condensate and by ruptured mains. Fog will condense on you and become new water.

- Boots: insulated, waterproof, mid-calf or higher, aggressive tread that works on wet grating, wet plate, and silt. Vibram or equivalent with deep lugs. Redundant gaskets at the tongue. Carry neoprene socks and a full spare pair of boots sealed in dry bags; one long wade through a warm runoff channel contaminated with whatever the pale jointed things live in and you will want to destroy the first pair.
- Gaiters that seal *over* the boot and to the pant shell.
- Dry bags nested inside dry bags inside a waterproof pack with a roll-top and welded seams. Assume the pack will be submerged or sprayed for hours. All electronics, sleep system, calories, and medical go inside.
- Personal water: do not drink the water. Not the condensate, not the channels, not the “clean” looking drips from high manifolds. Bring a high-capacity filter + chemical backup + UV pen, but treat them as emergency only. Primary plan is carried water in collapsible bladders and rigid bottles, plus a small still if you have power and time (you can distill in a pinch using machine heat carefully). Plan for 4–6 liters per day minimum; the air is wet but your lungs and skin still lose fluid, and stress drinking is real.
- Anti-fungal powder, waterproof skin barrier cream, and spare socks changed on a schedule even if they do not feel wet yet. Trench foot and exotic dermatitis will end a trip faster than most monsters.

### 3. Footing, Verticality, and the Unfinished Body of the Place
Grating that wants to twist ankles. Plate that is glass-slick. Catwalks that end in air. Staircases hanging mid-flight. Entire walls that become bays between one visit to a sightline and the next. Multiple arguing verticals.

- Harness: full body or at least a solid seat harness you can live in. Cow’s tail lanyards, multiple locking carabiners, short lengths of dynamic rope or energy-absorbing lanyards. You will clip to railings, to beams, to anything solid when you rest or when a crane starts moving the world nearby.
- Lightweight adjustable climbing crampons or micro-spikes that can bite wet metal and concrete. Test them; some coatings are treacherous.
- A compact grappling hook or throw bag with thin strong cord for snagging higher gratings or dangling structure when stairs simply stop.
- Work the edges. Move like the place is revising itself under you—because it is. Never sprint. Never trust a surface you have not weight-tested. When sparks fall like orange snow, stop and look up; something is being cut or welded and the cut piece may not know you are below.
- Mark your passage without assuming permanence. Chem-light sticks (military grade, long duration), biodegradable flagging that still survives damp, and RFID or Bluetooth beacons if your multiverse kit includes them. Chalk washes away. Paint may be mistaken for process markings and painted over by automated systems. Your own glowing trail of spent chem-lights in a dark reach is oddly comforting and can save your life.

### 4. Light, Vision, and the Tired Amber
Sodium-vapor spiders that never reach full burn. Long darknesses broken only by weak green bioluminescent film on black water. Sudden absolute black in failed districts.

- Primary: high-lumen headlamp with flood and spot, redundant batteries or a generator system that can sip power from the environment carefully (inductive coils near heavy cables, thermoelectric if you find reliable heat differentials—common here). Carry more batteries than arrogance says you need.
- Secondary: handheld torch, helmet-mounted backup, and a belt light.
- Passive: a stack of high-intensity chem-lights in multiple colors. Use one color for “safe path back,” another for “danger / do not re-enter,” another for personal camp.
- Night vision monocular (gen 2+ or digital) with infrared illuminator. The bioluminescent film is real but faint; IR can reveal structure and the pale jointed things before they decide you are interesting.
- Avoid staring into the green film for long periods in total dark. It rewrites itself. After enough hours your brain will try to read it as language. That way lies a particular fatigue.

### 5. Sound as Climate and the Need for Selective Silence
Compressors in stacked octaves, drop-forges, steam barks, water in a thousand keys, the grid hum that never sleeps. Quiet is the unnatural thing.

- High-quality filtered earplugs or electronic earpro that compress dangerous peaks but allow speech frequencies and the particular stutter of a failing pump (which may signal a route about to flood or a crane about to move). You need to hear the place; you also need to not be neurologically sandblasted by it.
- After long exposure you will feel the silence of any sealed refuge as a physical pressure. Prepare for that inversion. Some travelers carry a small device that plays back filtered industrial hum at low volume so sleep does not feel like falling off the world.

### 6. Air, Fog, and Whatever the Machines Exhale
Industrial fog is the breath of the process. Occasional chemical notes, welding fumes, possible cryogenic off-gassing, warm stagnant pockets in lower sweeps.

- A solid half-face respirator with interchangeable cartridges (organic vapor, acid gas, P100 particulate) and sealed spare cartridges in dry storage. In heavy fog or after a main rupture, full face or powered air if you can carry the weight.
- CO and multi-gas detector clipped to harness, with alarm you can feel (haptic) because you may not hear it.
- Practice bottle breathing or rebreather skills if your traveler loadout allows a small closed-circuit unit for the worst pockets. Most corridors are breathable; the exceptions are abrupt.

### 7. Navigation in a Place That Refuses Single Up and Single Map
Vast continuous made world. Sightlines that become pipe forests that become plazas the size of town squares. Geometry fused from different load-bearing logics. Expansion and drowning of older floors.

- Do not trust magnetic compasses near the big electrical trunks or ferrous mass. Use gyroscopic or strapdown inertial navigation if available. Otherwise, meticulous dead reckoning: pace count, timed legs, sketched map on waterproof paper or digital slate updated every few hundred meters.
- Altimeter / barometer is useful for relative levels even though “up” argues with itself; note every climb and descent.
- Personal locator beacons or breadcrumb trail devices that can survive the damp. If you have any multiverse-anchored coordinate system that still functions, calibrate it repeatedly against fixed heavy structure (the oldest, most heavily bolted cores move least).
- Accept that the far end of every vista is more of the same. Psychological navigation matters as much as physical: mark not only location but *emotional waypoints*—“this cooling plaza felt safe,” “this pipe forest where the metal bats nested,” “the brine pipe corridor that skins the air with frost.” When the place revises itself, memory of feeling can still orient you.

### 8. Sustenance, Shelter, and the Long Shift
No sun. No circadian gift. Time is pump-cycle and shift-change (even if you never see the shifters).

- Calories: dense, boring, high-fat and high-protein rations that tolerate cold and damp. Hot food is a morale weapon—carry a reliable stove that can burn in oxygen-variable air and a pot that nests. Machine heat can sometimes be used, but never trust a surface temperature; use a thermometer.
- Sleep system: suspended if possible. A compact hammock or portaledge variant that clips to catwalk rails or beams keeps you above the black film and above casual runoff. Under-quilt and over-sheet rated for wet cold. Earpro and eye mask (the amber never fully dies, and sparks fall at irregular hours).
- You will not find “campgrounds.” You will create temporary parasites on the structure: clipped in, condensed, lights out, earpro in, one person always half-watching if you are not alone. Sleep in short blocks aligned to quieter pump cycles if you learn to feel them.

### 9. Medical, Damage, and the Body’s Quiet Revolts
Cuts from fresh metal edges and unfinished welds. Blunt trauma from slips. Hypothermia. Ingestion or skin contact with industrial soup. Rare fauna (bites or simply startling you into a fall). Psychological load.

- Trauma kit weighted toward bleeding control, wound irrigation (lots of sterile flush), broad-spectrum antibiotics, strong anti-inflammatories, and blister/trench-foot management. Waterproof packaging.
- Hypothermia wrap and chemical heat packs in quantity.
- Mental: short-acting anxiolytic if you tolerate it, for the moments when the arguing verticals and the sense that the complex is a slow machine producing more of itself press too hard. Journaling on waterproof paper helps some travelers; the act of naming (“this is fog from the cooling tower overperforming,” “this is only condensate”) keeps the quieter strangeness from metastasizing into dread.
- Know the signs of your own fraying: reading the green film as text, deciding the pale jointed things are messengers, feeling that the drop-forge is speaking your name in rhythm. When that starts, clip in somewhere solid, eat, warm the core, and reduce stimulus for a full sleep cycle.

### 10. Fauna, Process, and the Sparse Other
Pale jointed things in warmer runoff—avoid the warm channels when the big pumps cycle down. Sheet-metal bats in high dead spaces—earpro already helps; a light lashing upward usually discourages them. Tracks in silt that lead nowhere sensible—do not follow them at night.

If there are shift workers or maintainers (rumor varies), they will be adapted: louder, wetter, less interested in your explanations. High-visibility markings on your shell can prevent you from being mistaken for debris or saboteur. A simple offering of intact tools, dry cigarettes if they still mean anything, or just staying out of active crane envelopes earns more goodwill than speeches.

Mostly you will be alone with the process. Treat moving machinery as weather of steel. Stop, look, listen, clip in, wait for the revision to finish or the path to reopen.

### 11. Multiverse-Specific Considerations
- Anchor: carry at least two independent ways to re-lock onto your exit translation signature. The metal mass and electrical grid can scramble weaker methods. Place a physical beacon in a stable, older core district if you can identify one—somewhere high, dry by local standards, and unlikely to be scrapped in the next cycle.
- Contamination: you will bring metal dust, biofilm, and the smell of wet iron back with you. Have a full decontamination protocol at the far end: strip, seal contaminated gear in multiple bags, skin wash, mucosal rinse. Some travelers burn the outer shell.
- Time slippage: subjective time is shift and cycle. Mechanical or atomic watch, plus a simple paper log of “hours since entry” updated whenever you eat. Do not trust pure feeling.
- Exit discipline: decide in advance the conditions under which you *will* leave even if curiosity sings (core temperature dropping, wind of infection, navigation confidence below threshold, mental reading of the green film). The place is open enough and continuous enough that “just a little farther” is the most common last thought.

### 12. The Intuitive Core — What It Will Actually Feel Like
You will step through and the first thing is the cold climbing through your soles and the sound already inside your ribs. The amber light makes every wet surface a dull mirror. You will walk an hour and realize the “horizon” is only another freight basin, and the fog smells faintly of ozone and old rain that never fell from sky. Sparks will hiss out in the black film like brief lives. You will lay your gloved hand on a railing and feel vibration that is not one machine but a civilization of machines arguing in frequencies.

The strangeness is not cinematic. It is the patient knowledge that the floor you stand on was not there a decade ago and may be a retention roof a decade hence. It is the soft vertical of a pale leafless switch of growth in a cracked joint—the only apology the place offers for being endless hard verticals. It is the moment the lights die and the water writes weak green script and for a few heartbeats you feel the complex noticing, not you, but the space you occupy, as a temporary eddy in its becoming.

Preparation does not make you native. It makes you a careful guest who can leave. Pack the harness, the dry layers, the light, the water you will not refill here, the earpro, the willingness to stop and clip in when the cranes wake. Move slowly. Drink before you are thirsty. When the drop-forge tests its rhythm against a hull plate, feel it in your sternum and keep walking the grated path between one unfinished cathedral of pipe and the next.

The far end of every vista is only more weeping industrial night. That is not a threat. It is the geography. Go anyway if you must—but go as someone who intends to return with all their fingers, their core temperature, and their ability to dream of sun.
