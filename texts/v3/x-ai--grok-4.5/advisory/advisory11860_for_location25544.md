**Prepare as if you are about to enter a sealed lung that has forgotten how to exhale.**

This pocket is not a destination you stumble into. It is a discontinuity you must already know how to touch—an improper fold three vertical kilometers above any useful map, where the old lift-cables dissolve into rust lace and the basalt scaffold simply stops offering further purchase. An ordinary human body arriving unready will experience it as a simultaneous assault of thinness, density, permanence, and quiet violence against every expectation of change. Preparation is therefore less about conquest and more about calibrated surrender: making your presence small enough, slow enough, and temporary enough that the place does not notice you as a gradient.

### Physiological Hardening
The air is permanently cool and thin enough to taste of powdered iron. It will not sustain unassisted human metabolism for long. Begin acclimatization weeks in advance with progressive hypoxia training—sleep in altitude tents ratcheting toward the equivalent of 4,000–5,000 m, practice controlled hyperventilation followed by long breath-holds, and condition your chemoreceptors so the metallic tang does not trigger panic. Carry a closed-circuit rebreather or at minimum a compact oxygen concentrator with scrubber, rated for multi-hour use in dust-laden air. The suspended microscopic scales (silica and metal-salt frond sheddings) never settle; they form a permanent, almost invisible snow. Without fine particulate filtration (P100 or better, preferably positive-pressure), your alveoli will accumulate an abrasive, bio-inert glitter that produces progressive dryness, micro-tears, and a delayed inflammatory response. Expect the first deep breath inside to coat your tongue and throat with the flavor of old blood and battery terminals.

Temperature is stable and cool—never drifting—but the thin air steals heat efficiently from skin and breath. Layer a base of moisture-wicking fabric beneath a light insulating shell that remains flexible; avoid bulk. The environment will not freeze you, but prolonged immobility while crouched or half-suspended in the frond mass will leach warmth until your core registers the chill as a philosophical condition rather than a physical one. Hydrate aggressively beforehand; the air is desiccating and there is no liquid water. Do not plan to drink inside. The fronds draw current, not moisture, and introducing water risks local electrochemical oddities you cannot predict.

Your inner ear and balance system must be primed. The floor is a single sprung mattress of braided silica-metal fronds over basalt grit and flake rust. Every step answers like a restrained trampoline. The entire woven mass transmits vibration. Practice walking on unstable, elastic surfaces (netting, thick moss beds, ship decks in swell) while carrying pack weight. Strengthen ankles and proprioception. The constant mid-register roar from the sourceless flywheel—multiplied by basalt shell and vibrating fronds—arrives as pressure on the eardrums before it registers as sound. After minutes the mind reclassifies it as silence, but the physical load remains. Carry high-quality earplugs or adaptive electronic muffs that can flatten the roar without creating total isolation; sudden removal later can produce vertigo. Some travelers report a low-grade bone conduction hum that settles into the jaw and sternum. Accept it.

### Sensory and Cognitive Calibration
Light inside is a saturated, unwavering green-white bio-phosphorescence, dim as aquarium glass at night. It is enough to see by once rhodopsin adapts, but depth perception collapses among the translucent, blade-thin fronds that lean and braid until they fill every cubic handspan. Train your eyes with low-light obstacle courses and translucent maze work. Do not bring bright artificial lights; they are both useless against the ambient glow and culturally rude to the equilibrium. A single dim red or deep-green personal marker light for your own hands and exit path is the maximum.

The density is total. You will move by parting, crouching, sometimes crawling or swimming through the fronds as much as walking. The fronds are laminated silica and dried metal salts—translucent like old bone, edge-thin, springy, and faintly conductive where they tap the buried conduits. They are not viciously sharp in the razor sense, but repeated contact abrades skin and fabric. Wear close-fitting, abrasion-resistant gloves and full-coverage clothing with smooth exterior surfaces so scales do not snag and tear. Soft-soled shoes or specialized grip socks that still protect against grit are better than rigid boots; you need to feel the trampoline response to stay upright. Expect the woven mass to shift subtly under you as a single organism. There are no animals, no insects, no scavengers. The ecology completed itself and locked. This absence is its own pressure: no scurrying, no decay smell, no soft life sounds—only the mechanical cough of the flywheel becoming oceanic roar becoming silence.

Psychologically, the place is saturated time. Gradient has been spent. Nothing accelerates, nothing stops, nothing brightens or dims. The mind, evolved for change detection, will first search frantically for narrative and then, if you stay too long, begin to dissolve its own edges. Prepare with meditation on stillness, sensory deprivation sessions, and exposure to drone or continuous mechanical sound until your threat-assessment circuitry stops flagging constancy as danger. Some travelers practice “duration fasting”—short voluntary periods in featureless environments—to inoculate against the seductive sense that you could simply remain and become another stanchion. Decide your maximum sojourn in advance (two to four hours is wise for a first visit) and enforce it with a redundant timer that cannot be silenced by the roar. The pocket does not remember the last technician. It has no further need of memory. You must supply your own.

### Entry, Movement, and Exit Protocol
You must already know the seam. No amount of scanning from below will reveal an improper fold this subtle. Approach the uppermost rim with climbing gear rated for rotten basalt and rust-lace cable remnants; the final meters are exposed and the drop is absolute. The entrance is a shoulder-wide wound half-hidden by outermost fronds. Crouch. Do not force it. Let the fronds brush you as a threshold rite. Once inside, move with deliberate weight distribution. Test each placement. The mass will answer. Avoid grabbing living fronds for balance if possible; they conduct faint current and dislike sudden shear. Thread yourself through the industrial ghosts—cast-iron stanchions, scraps of grated walkway, frozen valves, the patient flywheel—as if they are bones in a body you are visiting rather than a ruin you are looting. Do not attempt to stop or accelerate the flywheel. Its motion is now equilibrium. Interfering is the closest thing this place has to sacrilege.

Mark your path invisibly if you must (biodegradable thread of a color that vanishes in the green-white light, or pure mnemonic discipline). The braids create corridors that close behind you. Disorientation is not spatial so much as temporal; after twenty minutes the unchanging light and sound can erase the sequence of turns. Keep the entrance slit as a psychological anchor—the place where planetary curvature and the ash-colored salt pans and knife-edged ridges remain visible. When you crouch there to look out, you are still half in the pocket’s density and half in the oceanic absence. Use that contrast to recalibrate.

### Gear Philosophy and What to Leave Behind
Bring only what preserves your ability to leave. Oxygen/filter system, minimal insulation, abrasion layer, ear adaptation, dim personal marker, timer, water for before/after, basic first aid emphasizing respiratory and abrasion care, and a small notebook or voice recorder if you must externalize memory (the roar will eat audio clarity). A compact multi-tool is acceptable for emergency cable or fabric issues, nothing more. Do not bring food that crumbs, open flame, strong scents, seed stock, liquids in quantity, or anything that introduces new chemical gradients. Do not bring weapons; there is nothing to fight and the attempt would be embarrassing. Do not bring measurement devices that emit pulses or bright light unless you are a specialist prepared for the ethical weight of disturbing a closed system that has been static for centuries.

Document by observation and restrained touch. The cracked ceramic insulator nestled like a skull, the short run of narrow-gauge rail that surfaces and vanishes, the half-turn valves—these are afterthoughts or bones. Treat them as such. The fronds have knitted the grit into one mattress; your footprints will spring back, but repeated traffic could eventually leave a compressed memory the place does not want.

### Decompression and Aftercare
Exit slowly. The return to weather, drip, and true silence (or ordinary wind) can feel like a sudden vacuum. Reacclimatize oxygen carefully; the powdered-iron taste may linger on the palate for hours. Shower with attention to hair and skin folds where scales hide. Monitor for delayed respiratory irritation. Psychologically, expect a hangover of stillness: ordinary time will seem vulgar and hurried for a day or two. Some travelers report mild grief—that the pocket did not need them, that it continues its patient roar and green-white crowding exactly as before. This is correct. You were a brief, soft anomaly.

If you ever return, you will find it unchanged. That is the point. An ordinary human multiverse traveler prepares not to master the pocket but to be briefly permitted inside a system that finished its arguments long ago and now simply persists—cool, dense, luminous, and complete. Move as if the trampoline of fronds is remembering you back. Breathe filtered. Listen until the roar becomes silence. Then leave before you begin to envy the flywheel.
