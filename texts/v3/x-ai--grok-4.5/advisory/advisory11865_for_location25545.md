**Prepare as if you are about to enter a living machine that has been breathing damp iron for geological time and has never once considered you.**

This place—the endless plateau city of weeping stone, slow pistons, and saturated ecologies—does not care that you are human, ordinary, or a traveler. It will not try to kill you quickly. It will try to dissolve your sense of edges: the edge between wet and dry, between machine and animal, between “here” and “farther,” between an hour and a season. Your preparations must therefore be dual: keep your body intact inside a warm, metallic, forever-damp industrial biome that out-masses and out-numbers you, and keep your mind from quietly accepting that the city does not end and that this is fine.

### 1. Mindset and Temporal Recalibration
Accept slowness as the primary physics. Rust advances by decades. Herds rise and fall on maintenance calendars. A reservoir flood takes a month to renegotiate the districts. Your ordinary human urgency will feel like a high-pitched whine against the industrial bass and the subsonic boom of deep cisterns. Practice long-duration stillness before you go. Sit in heavy rain under a metal roof for hours. Listen to overlapping mechanical and animal rhythms until your nervous system stops demanding silence or climax. 

Cultivate a working indifference to infinity. From any overlook the city continues until it becomes weather, and the weather is alive. If you need a psychological anchor, choose something small and portable that is unmistakably *yours* and not of this place: a dry stone from home, a scrap of true green leaf sealed in glass, a watch that still believes in hours. Touch it when the mid-level bridges at night become constellations of scavenger eyes and you feel the plateau trying to rewrite your scale.

You will hear factory whistles answered by evening choruses of wall-life that continue long after the whistles stop. Do not anthropomorphize this as welcome or warning. It is simply the folded ecology keeping time. Learn to move through sound the way the locals move through mist—without needing it to resolve.

### 2. Physiological and Medical Preparation
The air is warm, heavy, permanently misted, and carries a faint metallic taste. It is breathable but saturating. Your lungs and sinuses will run. Expect a low-grade, chronic dampness in every mucosal surface. Pre-treat with whatever barrier methods your multiverse pharmacology allows: saline rinses, mild antifungal/antibacterial prophylaxis aimed at warm wet metal environments, and something for the inevitable low-level heavy-metal exposure (the mist, the films, the dust of slag and sulfur crusts). 

Hydration is paradoxical. Water is everywhere—beading, sheeting, pooling, rising as vapor—yet it is industrial water, mineral-laden, possibly laden with the dull greasy films that are the only non-flesh softness. Carry a serious filtration and distillation setup that can handle dissolved metals, sulfur compounds, and biologicals that are neither plant nor quite fungus. Do not drink the open sumps or the cooling-pond runoff until you have tested and treated. The deep warm cisterns under old foundries are calving grounds; their water is not for you.

Temperature is mild but the wet bulb is relentless. You will not freeze; you will never fully dry. Skin maceration, fungal issues, and slow chafing are the everyday medical threats. Toughen your skin with repeated wet abrasion training. Get used to the feeling of clothes that never quite leave your body. 

Vaccinate or prepare broad-spectrum countermeasures for whatever zoonoses arise when clever many-eyed scavengers, hide-bound grazers, angular diving birds, pale crabs, soft-bodied dusk-singers, and translucent updraft gliders all share the same layered metal habitat with humans and never stop. Wounds go septic in interesting ways here. Carry aggressive wound care and assume every surface is coated in something that wants to colonize warmth.

The low tremble of distant press-halls comes up through stone and bone. Some travelers develop a persistent internal vibration sense. It is not harmful; it is the city reminding you it is running. If you are prone to motion sickness or infrasound sensitivity, condition yourself.

### 3. Clothing, Pack, and Material Culture
Everything you wear must tolerate perpetual warm wet, abrasive mineral beards (calcite, rust-bloom, pale sulfur crusts that flake like old paint), riveted plate, gratework, and the occasional climb of something soft-bodied or clawed. 

- Base layers: quick-drainage synthetic or treated natural fibers that do not hold stink forever. You will smell of warm metal, animal musk, wet stone, and your own damp skin. Accept it.
- Shell: not fully waterproof in the sealed sense (you will overheat and the mist is already inside everything), but highly water-resistant, breathable, and abrasion-tough. Multiple thin layers beat one heavy coat. You need to vent steam from your own body while shedding the city’s condensate.
- Footwear: aggressive tread that still works when clogged with greasy film and sulfur flakes. Ankle support for wet gratework, slag heaps, and the soft troughs that centuries of feet have worn into streets. Waterproof-breathable with the understanding that “waterproof” is aspirational. Spare socks are sacred; keep a dry pair in a true dry-bag for psychological rescue.
- Hand and knee protection: surfaces are cold, rough, and often slimy. Gloves that grip wet metal and can be rinsed. 
- Head: brim or hood that keeps sheet-water off your face without blinding you in mist. The light is thin, even, diffused; shadows are soft. You will not need sun protection so much as perpetual drip management.

Pack light but redundant. The city has power, workshops, and materials beyond counting, yet little that is clean, dry, or sized for an outsider who still thinks in single lifetimes. Essentials:

- True dry storage for medications, documents, the personal anchor object, and one change of soft clothes.
- Filtration/distillation as above.
- A compact mechanical toolkit (the layered ruins reward people who can unbolt, lever, and jury-rig).
- Light that works in whiteout mist and does not rely solely on the city’s humming grid (headlamp, chemical light, hand-crank). At night the animal eyes provide constellation light but not reading light.
- Climbing/traversing aids: the verticality is constant—gantries, viaducts at every height, stairwells spiraling around forgotten presses, hollow tower legs full of life. A lightweight harness, gloves, and the skill to move on wet riveted plate.
- Signal and navigation: dead-reckoning tools that do not assume endpoints. Maps are local and temporary; the plateau does not end. Mark your route with something that survives wet and curious animals (metal tags, carved slag, scent markers the scavengers will not immediately rewrite). GPS or equivalent will eventually laugh at you.
- Food strategy: the city runs on ore, scrap, cooled glass, barreled chemicals, and the animals. Humans eat. There will be shift canteens, protein from the herds or the crabs or the things that never surface, possibly cultured or rendered industrial byproducts. Bring concentrated calories and spices for the first long stretch while you learn what is safe and how to trade labor or salvage for it. Do not eat the greasy films.
- Sleep system: something that can hang, wedge into a warm dynamo housing niche, or claim a dry-ish pocket inside a cooled machine interior. Earplugs that reduce the industrial bass and the dusk screaming without isolating you from klaxons or the click of something investigating your perch. The city never goes quiet; it only changes key.

Power is ubiquitous—open substations, cable looms thick as the trunks of trees no one here has seen, warm dynamo housings. You can recharge, warm yourself, or sleep against machines. Bring adapters and the humility to ask before you tap a loom that is also a migratory route.

### 4. Skills to Acquire Before Crossing
- Wet industrial movement: balance on gratework and rail beds that shine with water, confidence on ladders and gantries while something angular wheels in the steam above or something many-jointed clicks below. 
- Animal literacy without dominance: the beasts outweigh the people. Great slow herds use lower gantry “meadows” of drip-algae and mineral film. Scavenger packs know shift changes better than most workers. Soft-bodied singers, armored millers clicking in time with engines, translucent gliders on forge updrafts—treat corridors, pipes, and slag heaps as their infrastructure first. Learn calm passage, which food wastes attract which clades, and when to yield a bridge. Do not startle the herds on narrow viaducts. 
- Layer-reading: construction is accreted. Newer gantries stand on cemented ruins; neighborhoods live inside cooled machines. Learn to see which pipes are still pressurized, which cisterns are calving grounds, which humming substations are safe to approach, which sulfur-crusted depressions are merely old and which are actively chemical.
- Slow-time labor: if you stay more than days you will need to fit into the patient economy—ore, scrap, glass, chemicals, maintenance of engines whose cycles are measured in seasons. Basic mechanical sympathy and the willingness to work a long shift in warm mist will buy you food, directions, and tolerance.
- Sound navigation: the layered noise is information. Turbines, trip-hammers on weekly cycle, ore-lifts groaning, sheet-water from a thousand lips of stone and steel, gutter spill, vapor release, dawn flock-screaming, midday wall-horn click-and-rustle, evening choruses. Locals hear the weather and the shifts in it. Train your ears.

### 5. Daily Realities and Risk Landscape
You will wake (if you slept) already slightly damp. The air has weight. A fine warm mist that never quite burns off softens every distance. Metal gleams without sharp glare. Your first lungful tastes of iron and wet stone and distant animals. 

Movement is vertical and braided. Rail viaducts at every height carry cages, ore, scrap. You will share them with herds, flocks, packs. Crossing an open gallery, you look out and the layered mass continues until haze. There is no “edge of town.” There is only heavier weather that is still city.

Threats are mostly patient or infrastructural:
- Slip and fall into sumps, cooling ponds, or the soft mineral beards.
- Being in the wrong place when a piston the size of a house completes its stroke or an ore-lift groans into motion.
- Scavengers testing whether you are weakly claimed or edible salvage.
- Gradual metal and biological loading.
- Disorientation. The same density continues. Streets worn into soft troughs. Handrails polished by centuries of shoulders. You can walk for days and the vocabulary of basalt, poured slag, riveted plate, kiln-glazed brick, cooling towers, foundries, and animal life does not thin.
- Catastrophe on the city’s schedule: overtopped reservoirs finding new paths over a month, failed reactor domes cooling over years while scavenger clades recolonize the warmth gradient. You can outwalk many disasters if you read the signs early.

People are present—workers who know the engines, the shifts, the animal calendars. They move at human speed inside a place that does not hurry. Interaction is possible, even welcoming if you are useful and not loud about other worlds. They have never needed the concept of a city that ends.

### 6. Sensory and Emotional Load Management
The sound never rests. Industrial bass under water sounds under fauna. Voices and bootsteps and klaxons are bright threads. You will either learn to let it become the floor of perception or you will exhaust yourself fighting for silence that does not exist.

Smell: warm wet metal, animal dens in the walls, sulfur, oil, the clean mineral scent of constant condensate, occasional chemical bite from barreled loads or forge exhalations.

Touch: everything cold and wet or warm and humming. Railing polish. The slight give of greasy film. Vibration through soles.

Vision: soft-edged, mist-veiled, endless layered mass. At night, eyes. 

Emotionally, the place can induce a profound calm or a slow-motion panic of insignificance. Both are reasonable. Schedule deliberate “smallness” rituals: a hot drink in a sealed cup, a conversation with one person about something finite, a few minutes with your dry anchor object, a climb to a warm dynamo housing where you can feel power without being asked to interpret the infinite. 

If you feel the city beginning to seem inevitable and correct in its endlessness, that is the moment to mark your map, check your exit window, or deliberately do something petty and human that the plateau did not schedule.

### 7. Exit and Return Considerations
Leaving is not dramatic. You travel outward until the layered mass thins into haze that is no longer architecture, or you find a multiverse aperture that still answers you. The difficulty is internal. After enough time the ordinary world will feel shockingly dry, quiet, finite, and green. Plants will seem like an overstatement. Silence will ring. You may find yourself missing the low tremble and the knowledge that past the next stacks the same density continues.

Bring back nothing biological that is still alive and complex without containment. The films, the small clawed things, the eggs of angular birds—these belong to a closed, patient system. Do not become the vector that teaches some other world the taste of this plateau’s damp.

You are ordinary. That is useful. Ordinary humans have worked these districts for longer than the maps remember, sleeping against dynamos, learning the shift-change eyes, walking the wet shining rails, listening to the city become weather. Prepare thoroughly, move with the patience the place teaches, keep one dry true thing that is yours, and you can walk the plateau without being erased by it. The engines will still be turning when you leave. The herds will still click across the gantries. The mist will still rise. The city does not end.
