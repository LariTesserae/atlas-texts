**Preparation for the Embryonic Gorge-Station**

You are an ordinary human: lungs that expect roughly 21% oxygen at sea-level pressure, skin that prunes and chafes, a vestibular system that hates unexpected drops, a gut that needs calories every few hours, and a mind that still flinches at the uncanny. This place will not kill you quickly if you are careless, but it will slowly unmake your comfort, your sense of scale, and your assumption that “outside” exists. Prepare as if you are entering a vertical greenhouse that is also a dry-dock, a lung, and a half-finished cathedral, all held at tropical bath temperature inside a pressure membrane that pretends to be sky.

### 1. Physiological Baseline and Acclimatization
The air is thick, warm, and saturated. Pressure domes keep it denser than the external altitude would allow, so you will not gasp, but the partial pressure of water vapor is extreme. Your lungs will feel like they are breathing soup. 

- **Hydration and electrolyte strategy**: You will sweat continuously and invisibly. The mist keeps skin wet; sweat cannot evaporate properly. Carry a sealed hydration bladder with a high-electrolyte mix (sodium, potassium, magnesium) and sip on a timer, not according to thirst. Plain water will dilute you into cramps and headache within hours. Add a mild diuretic schedule for the first day so your body recalibrates fluid retention; otherwise you will bloat.
- **Respiratory prep**: Pack a simple heat-and-moisture exchange mask (the kind climbers use in deep cold, worn reversed) for the first 6–8 hours. It will feel counter-intuitive in the heat, but it gives your mucosa time to stop overproducing mucus in panic. After that, go bare-faced; the air is clean of dust and industrial toxins beyond a faint, pleasant oil-and-sap scent when engine-room doors cycle.
- **Thermal load**: Core temperature will want to creep upward. The ambient is deliberately bathwater (roughly 34–36 °C). You cannot dump heat by radiation or convection easily. Train for two weeks beforehand in a humid sauna or tropical glasshouse while doing light vertical work (ladder intervals). Learn to recognize the early signs of heat exhaustion when sweat no longer cools you: mild euphoria, then irritability, then a glassy calm. That calm is dangerous here because the place itself feels calm.
- **Skin and infection**: Constant wetness plus micro-abrasions from rivets and young binder-stone will invite fungal and bacterial bloom on *you*. Start a prophylactic antifungal regimen three days before entry (terbinafine or equivalent). Pack a zinc-oxide barrier cream for every crease of the body and a pH-balanced rinse. The local plants are not hostile, but their root sleeves pulse nutrient solution; if you nick yourself on a conduit and it drips into the cut, treat it as a dirty wound until proven otherwise.
- **Sleep architecture**: There is no true night, only a soft diurnal slide of filtered light. Bring blackout goggles and a reliable timed melatonin micro-dose. The turbines’ low-frequency murmur is actually excellent for sleep once you stop listening for danger, but the occasional polite clank of a new deck locking into place will jolt you until your brain files it under “weather.”

### 2. Clothing and Load-Bearing Gear
Dress for a warm rain that never quite falls, for climbing that is mostly catwalks and ladders, and for surfaces that are simultaneously slick and sharp.

- Base layer: thin, antimicrobial merino or synthetic that wicks *and* does not hold stink when wet for days. Avoid cotton entirely.
- Mid layer: a short-sleeved shirt of densely woven, quick-shear fabric with a high collar you can flip up when mist turns momentarily heavier. Elbows and knees need reinforcement; you will kneel on grate and young stone constantly.
- Outer shell: a lightweight, highly breathable rain shell that is *not* waterproof-sealed in the classic sense. You want something that sheds bulk water but allows vapor out, otherwise you steam yourself. Pit zips are mandatory. The shell should be a muted moss or oxidized-copper color; high-visibility orange feels obscene here and is unnecessary—there is almost no one to see you.
- Hand and foot protection: thin leather or synthetic gloves with high wet-grip palms (the micro-channelled plates are treacherous). Boots with aggressive siping and a mid-cut that protects ankles from the inevitable slip on wet grating. Bring gaiters that seal to the boot; condensation runs down vertical surfaces and will fill any open cuff.
- Harness and vertical kit: a full-body light harness rated for industrial work, not just climbing. Anchor points exist everywhere—girders, risers, new cladding eyelets—but many are still curing or temporary. Carry two lanyards with shock absorbers and a short cow’s-tail for positioning. A compact ascender/descender pair and 30 m of lightweight static rope live in your pack even if you plan never to leave the main catwalks; the structure grows outward and upward without warning, and yesterday’s safe balcony may today end in empty air.
- Pack: 30–40 L, waterproof liner, worn high and tight so it does not snag vines or catch the slow sweep of broad leaves. Everything inside double-bagged.

### 3. Navigation and Spatial Literacy
Horizontal travel is trivial; the long axis is under three minutes. Vertical and temporal travel are the traps.

- The place has no natural horizon and no outside. Your inner map will try to impose “up-canyon / down-canyon.” Resist. Think in terms of pressure levels, turbine loops, and growth fronts. The catch-basin is the only reliable “ground zero.” From there, count risers (they are also structural columns and water returns).
- Light is even and shadowless enough that depth perception softens. Phosphorescent strips exist in the undercroft but are easy to miss when your eyes are full of green. Carry a small, warm-tint headlamp with a red mode; white light feels violent against the dome’s soft radiance.
- Mark your path with biodegradable, plant-safe tags or simply with twists of local vine (they regrow so fast that a broken stem is a clear sign for days). Do not trust chalk; the sheet flow erases it.
- Growth is active. A deck that was not there at breakfast may exist by afternoon, extruded on hydraulic rams. Conversely, a temporary scaffold may be retracted. Never assume a route remains. Re-check three points of contact and your retreat every time you hear a clank.

### 4. Sustenance and Metabolism
The ecology is almost purely vegetable and mechanical. Nothing here is packaged for you.

- Calories: dense, non-perishable, high-fat bars and freeze-dried meals that tolerate moisture. Cooking is possible but socially odd; the heat exchangers run hot enough that you can warm a metal flask against certain sealed housings if you time the door cycles. Open flame is unnecessary and feels wrong.
- Water: technically abundant, but do not drink the sheet flow or the catch-basin without treatment. The water is cycled through turbines and nutrient sleeves; it is warm, slightly mineral, and may carry plant exudates or microscopic machine lubricants. Use a UV pen or chemical tabs, then add electrolytes. Condensate wicked by the capillary felts is cleaner but harder to collect in volume; you can press a clean cloth against the felts and wring it if desperate.
- Foraging: the plants are decorative-industrial, not a food forest. Some leaves look succulent; most are locked into nutrient contracts with the sleeves and will taste of metal and fertilizer. Do not experiment unless you have a full testing kit and several safe days to observe reactions. The pale hand-sized moths are probably not toxic, but they are few and the act of eating one would feel like a desecration of the slow choreography. Leave them.
- Waste: pack out everything solid. Liquid waste can go into the catch-basin only after the local system has been observed long enough to confirm it already handles biological input (the turbines and filters almost certainly do, but verify). The place is self-contained; it does not need your nitrogen spike.

### 5. Tools, Spares, and Quiet Technology
- Multitool with pliers, wire cutters, and a sharp blade for vines that grow across handrails overnight.
- Small roll of industrial tape and a tube of two-part epoxy that cures in wet conditions; you may need to stabilize a loose grating or seal a weeping seam that is misting too aggressively near your resting spot.
- Compact moisture meter and a simple infrared thermometer. Knowing whether a plate is merely wet or still cooling from recent placement can save your hands.
- Earplugs of the musician type (flat attenuation). The continuous hiss is soothing until you want silence; the turbines never stop.
- Power: a small hand-crank or kinetic charger. Solar is useless under the mesh dome’s diffusion. Many local systems are hydraulic or inductive; with patience you can top up from the pulse of a nutrient sleeve or the low bleed of a heat exchanger, but do not splice without understanding the schedule—disrupting a cycle is the closest thing this place has to sacrilege.
- Recording: a waterproof notebook and pencil (ink runs). Cameras fog. Your memory will try to smooth the place into “beautiful wet canyon” or “creepy factory”; the notes keep the double vision intact.

### 6. Psychological and Behavioral Calibration
This is the deepest preparation.

The strangeness is not spectacle; it is substitution so complete that your pattern-matching quietly panics and then goes still. Every cliff is a bulkhead. Every spring is a pump. Weather is a maintenance schedule. Yet it is lush, warm, growing, and at peace with itself. Ordinary humans experience a cluster of predictable responses:

- **Awe that curdles into grief**: You will stand on a newly extruded balcony, watch water sheet down a plate that was white-hot weeks ago, and feel something like homesickness for a planet that never existed. Let it happen. Do not force cheer.
- **Hyper-vigilance fatigue**: The construction never fully stops. Your threat-detection system will stay online looking for the workers who are no longer present, or for the animal life that never arrived. After 24–36 hours this exhausts people. Counter it by deliberately sitting still for one full turbine cycle (you will learn the rhythm) and naming every layer of green you can see. There are usually seven. Counting them is a prayer that works.
- **Scale vertigo**: Horizontally intimate, vertically endless, and still lengthening. If you feel the sudden urge to climb until you reach “the top,” stop. There may not be a top, only a temporary growth front. Fix your eyes on the catch-basin or on a single moth. Scale returns.
- **Social hunger**: Voices are rare. The place prefers its own metabolism. If you are used to human chatter you will start talking to the vines or the occasional jointed service mite. This is fine. They do not answer, which is better than a lie. If you encounter another traveler or a lingering automated caretaker, match the local volume: low, sparse, polite. Loud speech echoes once and is swallowed; it feels like shouting in a library that is also a rainforest.
- **Time dilation**: Growth is visible. Vines lengthen by the hour. Decks advance. Your own sense of a “day” will slip. Anchor yourself with a watch that shows external time even if it is meaningless here, and with strict meal and sleep rituals.

Do not try to help the construction. Do not try to halt it. You are not a builder and you are not a wrecker. You are a guest inside a body that is still knitting its own bones and skin. Move the way the moths move: slowly, without sudden starts, landing only on surfaces that have already decided to hold weight.

### 7. Entry, Duration, and Exit
Approach through whatever gate or membrane transient the multiverse left you. Pause under the dome until your breathing matches the soft hiss of the water films. That is the handshake.

Plan for no less than 36 hours and no more than 10 days on a first visit. Shorter and you leave with only postcard impressions. Longer and the place begins to feel like the only real climate, making return to ordinary thin air and hard shadows briefly painful.

When you leave, do it cleanly. Coil your rope. Retrieve every tag. Let the last mist dry on your shell as you pass back through the membrane. You will carry the smell of wet iron and green glass for days. Your dreams will drip. That is normal. The gorge-station does not need you to remember it, but you will.

You are ordinary. That is enough. The place was built—or grew—to be inhabited by the deliberate and the quiet. Walk its catwalks as if you are visiting the inside of a living throat that chose to become a garden instead of a scream. Keep your harness snug, your electrolytes high, your voice low, and your wonder undiminished but disciplined. The rest is just weather, and here the weather is always on purpose.
