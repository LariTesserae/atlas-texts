**Preparation for the Briarholds**

You are an ordinary human. That means lungs evolved for denser air, a body that expects free water as a birthright, a nervous system tuned to skies that brighten and darken on short cycles, and a sense of physics that treats floors as reliable and walls as barriers. The Briarholds will quietly contradict all of these. Preparation is less about conquering the place than about arriving soft enough not to be rejected by it, and solid enough not to dissolve into its ancient equilibrium.

### 1. Physiological and Medical Foundations
Begin months ahead if your multiverse transit allows. The plateau sits four to five kilometers above the old cloud seas. The air is thin, cold, and chemically alien—copper-resin on the tongue, particulates of spore, silica, and abraded leaf-scale permanently suspended. Ordinary humans will experience immediate altitude stress compounded by chronic low-grade desiccation.

- **Respiratory adaptation**: Train with progressive hypoxic exposure. Carry compact closed-circuit oxygen rebreathers or hemoglobin-boosting implants/nanite suspensions if your tech base permits; otherwise, chemical oxygen candles and carefully rationed bottled mixes. The stomata-windows of the living stone exchange methyl ethers and crystal hydrates rather than water vapor—your lungs will not like the sudden shifts. A simple particulate filter mask with copper-scavenging media is non-negotiable for the first weeks; many travelers eventually discard it once the body stops recoiling from the taste, but early rejection can become pulmonary inflammation.
- **Hydration architecture**: Free water is myth. You will not drink as you understand drinking. Begin conditioning your kidneys and mucous membranes to higher osmotic loads. Pack concentrated electrolyte gels and lipid-rich pastes, but plan to transition rapidly to local sources: municipal phloem taps that meter a mineral-sweet emulsion, and certain galls that can be chewed until they release bound moisture. Carry a small sterile docking kit (needles or soft collars that can interface with seeping cambium without triggering full immune response). Dehydration here is not dramatic thirst; it is a progressive stiffening of thought and joints. Learn to read your own saliva viscosity and the slight copper aftertaste that signals acceptable fluid balance.
- **Thermal and dermal**: The cold is dry and slow. Ordinary down or synthetic insulation works if it is freely vented—moisture from your own body will otherwise freeze into abrasive crystals against the skin. Favor layered, slightly abrasive-resistant shells that can shrug off silica dust and the occasional flake of roof-sized leaf-scale. Skin will crack. Bring heavy emollients based on non-volatile lipids; local gels exuded by habitation walls can substitute once you are trusted. Eyes need protection from the perpetual sideways dimness and the grit—photochromic lenses tuned to copper-green/violet spectra help the brain accept that “noon” will always look like late afternoon underwater.
- **Vestibular and gravitational readiness**: Near old heartwood, gravity becomes locally negotiable. Floors tilt into helices; walls briefly decide they are floors. Ordinary humans experience this first as nausea and then as a kind of courtesy. Practice in rotating habitats or with programmable inertia fields if available. If not, simply accept that you will fall a few times and that the city lights will courteously shift spectrum so the new orientation feels inevitable. Core strength and proprioceptive drills matter more than raw climbing ability.
- **Immune and allergenic baseline**: The substrate is a single vast organism that has mineralized without dying. Its spores, sap fractions, and the fauna that live inside it will challenge you. Broad-spectrum antihistamines, mast-cell stabilizers, and a short course of adaptive immunotherapy against common vegetal sterols are wise. The Grove’s own immune memory will eventually recognize you if you behave; until then you are a possible pathogen. Avoid open wounds. Carry sealant gels that mimic local cambium chemistry.

Vaccinations against ordinary pathogens remain useful—closed habitats concentrate people—but the true risks are quieter: slow mycotic colonization of lungs if you linger in certain galleries, or crystal-mite dermatitis from brushing the plateau crust.

### 2. Gear That Will Not Insult the Place
Pack light. Everything you carry is mass that the thin air and long distances will tax, and the Groves prefer epiphytes to parasites.

- **Footwear and traction**: The surface is compressed xylem plates—pale ochre and ash-lavender polygons the size of plazas, cool and faintly cork-like, cracked, never dusty in the ordinary sense yet always shedding micro-filaments. Soft-soled boots with deep but non-aggressive lugs work; hard cleats feel like vandalism and can provoke slow sealing responses from living cambium threads in the cracks. Bring gaiters against the grit.
- **Insulation and shelter**: A vacuum-quilted bivy that can double as a faraday-ish quiet sack when the tectonic bass notes roll through. Between Groves you may walk for days. The open plateau offers no caves in the rock sense—only shallow depressions and the occasional younger shoot. Your shelter must handle sudden long exhalations of the land (a square kilometer humming a chord you feel in the teeth) without resonating itself into scrap.
- **Light and vision**: The sun is a pale coin behind high haze. Shadows are permanent and long. Carry broad-spectrum lamps that can shift into the city’s preferred copper-violet conversational range; white light reads as aggressive. Many long-term residents develop a mild photophilia for the Groves’ own bioluminescent and resonant glows. Night-vision that works in near-infrared is useful for the open crossings; inside the wood the living light is usually sufficient once your pupils accept the gloom.
- **Sound management**: This is critical. The plateau groans and ticks. The Groves hum day and night—a stacked, unresolved chord of resonant cavities that is simultaneously power grid, data bus, liturgy, and weather. Filter-ray flocks add glissandi; kite-foxes and wood-borers add stridulation and wingbeat. The combined sound organizes into information for those who live there, yet it can drown speech and fray an unprepared mind. Bring high-quality ear pro that can switch between attenuation, selective pass-through, and active noise-cancellation tuned to the dominant frequencies of the nearest Grove. Better: spend weeks beforehand listening to long recordings of stacked drones, tectonic rumbles, and overlapping animal glissandi until your brain begins to parse them as weather rather than assault. Some travelers learn to sleep inside the hum the way others sleep to rain.
- **Navigation and signaling**: Distances between Groves feel like remote towns. Landmarks are sparse—upright shoots that will matter in another age, the slow lift of a ridge over centuries. Inertial and stellar navigation remain reliable; magnetic is distorted by the ionic living mass. The cities themselves sing their locations on the wind. Learn the basic interval patterns before you go so that a distant roar resolves into “that way, two days, current emotional weather calm.” A simple resonant whistle or small fretted tube that can answer in the local harmonic language is more useful than most radios. Radios work, but the substrate sometimes treats strong EM as irritation.
- **Consumables and tools**: High-calorie, low-residue rations for the crossings. A small pruning blade that is more ceremonial than practical—staircases are pruned rather than built and will reseal if you linger rudely. Soft measuring tapes and pressure sensors if you intend to interface with circumferential logic engines (sap-pressure and ionic differentials). Do not bring heavy power tools. Power is ambient and sung; attempting to extract it clumsily can make a district go dark or, worse, make the wood forget to keep your door open. A basic medical kit weighted toward trauma from quiet predation (a rustle, a sudden absence), abrasion, and cold injury. Sealants, not sutures, for anything that must touch living wood.
- **Documentation and memory aids**: Time feels thick. Your ordinary sense of hours will blur. A simple mechanical chronometer that does not rely on quartz or signals, plus a paper or stable-polymer journal, helps. The place does not hide information; it simply operates on schedules longer than your attention span. Notes prevent you from walking in circles both literal and cognitive.

### 3. Mental, Sensory, and Cultural Calibration
The weirdness is load-bearing. Physics has been in long negotiation with the substrate. Constants feel optional without ever quite becoming surreal in the carnival sense. You can still freeze and still fall. You may also walk up a wall because the wall has decided it is floor and the spectrum has shifted to make the decision feel like courtesy.

- **Accept the dim and the dry as normal**: Spend time in planetaria or simulation chambers set to perpetual copper-green late-afternoon under high haze. Practice tasks in low light until frustration fades. Dry your living space gradually; notice how thought changes when lips crack and the air tastes of resin.
- **Listen until listening becomes thinking**: The symphony is not background. It is infrastructure and conversation. Ordinary humans first experience it as beautiful then oppressive then informative. Meditate inside long-duration drone pieces. Learn to feel bass notes in the jaw without panic. When a square kilometer exhales, the correct response is stillness and quiet awe, not flight.
- **Etiquette of the living city**: You are an epiphyte, not a carpenter. Habitations are carved, laminated, exchanged with living wood. Walls sweat gel that hardens into panes. Doors open because the tree’s immune memory recognizes you. Move slowly at first. Leave offerings of interesting minerals or carefully composed harmonic phrases at root-buttresses. Do not force growth; request it. Staircases reseal behind the lingering—interpret this as housekeeping, not hostility. Certain districts compute with sap pressure; sudden emotional spikes or violent motion can inject noise into local logic. Cultivate evenness.
- **Relation to fauna**: Filter-rays the color of bruised fruit sail thermals; their skins are photosynthetic tattoos. Kite-foxes hunt them and the crystal-mites. Inside: soft browsers, luminous thread-snakes, large diggers keeping vascular systems clear. Predation is constant and mostly quiet. You are rarely primary prey if you stay out of galleries at the wrong hours and avoid smelling like a wounded ray. Still, carry a short deterrent (ultrasonic or bitter aerosol) and learn the difference between a rustle that is traffic and a rustle that is interest. The collective life-noise is oceanic; silence is the true warning.
- **Temporal humility**: Erosion finished its dramatic work so long ago the land has forgotten how to be theatrical. Edges are proverb. Processes—growth, calculation, hunting, dust inhalation into wood—continue inside an equilibrium so old it feels like law. Your urgency is the exotic element. Schedule margins measured in days for what would take hours elsewhere. Boredom is a teacher here; it sands the mind to match the plateau.

### 4. Arrival, Transit, and First Days Protocol
You will most likely portal or drop onto the open plateau rather than inside a Grove—arrival inside living wood without prior recognition is rude and sometimes fatal to the visitor. 

Spend the first hours simply standing. Feel the cool reticulation underfoot. Taste the air. Listen to the nearest city’s distant chord and the closer ticks of the ground. Do not immediately march. Let the place register you as a slow new growth rather than an impact.

Crossings between Groves are the purity test. Carry your water discipline, your sound discipline, your light. Watch for ice-fog: rare, beautiful, and a frenzy trigger for fauna as every surface steams with sublimation. When it comes, find a shallow depression, go still, and let the event pass. The land will steam; you will not melt.

At the edge of a Grove, where root-buttresses sink into plant-stone, pause again. Look out across the kilometers of open ancient mass under the dim vault. Hear the next city singing. Both the song and the space between are exact. Announce yourself if you know how—simple harmonic matching or a quiet spoken name against the bark. Then enter as a guest who intends to become a resident lichen.

First nights inside: sleep near the quieter outer cavities if possible. Let the stacked chord reorganize your dreams. Drink only from offered taps. Touch walls with open palms so the immune memory can sample you. Watch how local gravity and light courteously guide you toward food, sanitation (also metabolized), and conversation. Humans and posthumans here have grown into the wood the way epiphytes once did; they will understand your ordinary fragility if you do not pretend otherwise.

### 5. Long-Term Maintenance and Exit Considerations
Stay long enough and the plateau rewrites small preferences. You may find yourself craving the emulsion’s mineral sweetness, timing your thoughts to the hum, preferring the sideways light. Gravity negotiations become playful. The open crossings turn from ordeal into pilgrimage.

Watch for the slow dangers: becoming so attuned that ordinary wet worlds feel vulgar and loud; losing the ability to speak without the supporting chord; letting the thick time convince you that deadlines elsewhere are illusions. Maintain a private ritual—a song from your origin, a sip of carried water once a month, a hard white light shone briefly into your own eyes—to remember you are a traveler rather than a new organ of the Briarholds.

When you leave, seal any personal growths you have encouraged in the wood so they do not become wounds. Thank the immune memory. Carry a small sealed vial of local emulsion or a fragment of dry resonant bark if the Grove permits; these become tuning forks for the next visit. The place does not chase, but it remembers. Future arrivals will be easier.

### Final Intuitive Notes
Visiting the Briarholds feels like walking across the cooled surface of a thought that has been thinking since before names. The cities are loud, glowing, half-awake organs of that thought. You will be cold, dry, slightly hypoxic, aurally saturated, and frequently astonished by the courtesy of a physics that has made room for you without ever needing you. Ordinary human preparation succeeds when it keeps you alive long enough for astonishment to turn into competence, and competence into a kind of quiet belonging that you can still choose to set down when the multiverse calls you onward.

Pack humility first. Everything else is gear. The high places will teach the rest in their own slow, singing voice.
