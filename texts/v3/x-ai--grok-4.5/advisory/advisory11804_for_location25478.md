**Preparation for the Low Geothermal Chamber (Basalt Vault with Residual Industry and Fermentative Flora)**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler—baseline physiology, no exotic augmentations beyond standard portable kit—must treat this place as a humid, low-ceilinged geothermal pocket that has partially digested an abandoned industrial installation. It is not actively hostile, but it is indifferent, slow, oppressive, and chemically patient. The dominant experiences will be enveloping mineral heat that has nowhere to vent, near-total soft darkness broken only by dull plum bioluminescence, intermittent warm mineral water, uncanny blurring of machine and stone, absolute absence of fauna, and a temporal texture that makes hours feel like held breath. You will sweat continuously. Condensation will form on your skin and gear. The air will coat your tongue with wet iron and boiled lichen. Sound will refuse to travel properly. The plants will ignore you while very slowly advancing. Rock may clink once and then forget it moved. You can cross the long axis in minutes if you walk purposefully; lingering is the real risk.

Prepare as if you are entering a living boiler room that has decided to become a greenhouse and a tomb at the same time.

### Mental and Temporal Preparation
Spend several days beforehand practicing extended stays in warm, high-humidity darkness with deliberately sparse sensory input. Use a heat chamber or sauna set to 35–42 °C with near-100% humidity, lights off, and only occasional drip or metallic tick sounds played at long irregular intervals. The goal is to inoculate yourself against the dual sensations of being gently cooked and gently erased. The chamber’s silence is not empty; it is process without mouths. Many travelers report a progressive sense that their own heartbeat and breathing have become the loudest events in the universe, followed by a quiet dread that the place is waiting for them to slow down to its pace and simply join the condensation cycle.

Accept that local time has weight but almost no velocity. Steam cycles may last days; plant movement is measured in finger-widths per visit if you return at all. Bring a reliable external chronometer with multiple redundant power sources and a luminous (not bright) display. Mark your intended departure window in absolute terms before entry and obey it. The chamber encourages indefinite hovering. “Just one more look at the flywheel planter” is how people overstay into heat exhaustion or psychological unmooring.

Rehearse the uncanny boundary effects. Machine parts mineralize; basalt takes on gear-like banding and tool marks that no present tool could have made. Decide in advance that you will note these without prolonged staring. The darkness amplifies the quiet wrongness. Treat it as geology and corrosion having a long conversation you are not required to join.

No animals means no sudden movement, no startle fauna, no scavenging birds or insects. The lack itself becomes a pressure. Practice sitting with that absence.

### Physiological Conditioning and Medical Prep
Heat acclimation is non-negotiable. Two weeks of progressive humid-heat exposure (exercise in steam rooms or hot yoga equivalents) will raise your sweat efficiency and plasma volume. You will still feel the chamber’s layered stagnation in your lungs; the air is heavy and mineral. Expect your fabrics to dampen within minutes and stay damp. Condensation beads on skin exactly as it does on the ceiling.

Hydration protocol: begin entry fully hydrated with electrolytes biased toward sodium and potassium. The air tastes of wet iron; your body will act as another condensing surface. Carry more water than you think necessary—see gear below—and sip on a schedule, not thirst. Local pools are warm, black, intermittent, and progress from water to gel to nothing; do not drink them. They are geothermal/fermentative soup.

Respiratory prep: the vapor from certain tubular plants smells of ginger and solder. Assume trace volatiles, metal aerosols, and spore loads from the slow fungal/bacterial consortia that must underwrite the non-photosynthetic flora. If you have any history of metal fume sensitivity, asthma, or mold reactivity, this place will find it. Carry broad-spectrum antihistamines, a steroid inhaler if prescribed, and consider a short course of prophylactic measures only under real medical advice. Baseline travelers should plan for possible delayed inflammatory response after exit.

Skin: continuous damp + mineral film + possible plant exudates. Barrier cream (dimethicone-heavy or comparable) under clothing at friction points. Antifungal dust or cream for post-exit, because the same heat-and-moisture regime that grows the local vegetation will try to grow things on you once you leave.

Musculoskeletal: the floor is talus, sharp recent fractures, slick condensate films, and occasional half-flooded low spots. Ankle support and deliberate foot placement. Ceiling clearance is often far less than 30 m and can feel intimate; tall travelers will crouch more than expected. No climbing required for basic traversal, but side alcoves and collapsed catwalks invite it—decide beforehand whether you are there to survey or to scramble.

No open wounds. The environment is not sterile.

### Clothing and Personal Protective System
Layer for heat dumping and moisture management while retaining abrasion and minor puncture resistance.

- Base layer: lightweight, antimicrobial, hydrophobic synthetic or treated merino that wicks and does not hold stink. Full coverage; bare skin becomes a condensation plate and a potential contact surface for plant chemistry.
- Mid: thin insulating layer only if your acclimation is poor; most will omit it.
- Shell: durable, breathable softshell or light hard-shell pants and jacket with high pit-zips and thigh vents. Reinforced knees and seat. The shell’s job is to take abrasion from basalt flanges and talus, shed the worst of the dripping, and not melt or off-gas in the residual warmth of machinery. Avoid pure cotton or heavy leather; both become sodden liabilities.
- Footwear: waterproof-breathable mid boots with aggressive tread and ankle brace. Soles that tolerate heat and sharp rock. Gaiters optional but useful against grit and root-moss.
- Hands: thin durable gloves for scrambling and for touching anything that might still hold residual warmth or plant acids. You will want to touch the amber fronds and the mineralizing gear; gloves let you do it once and then decide.
- Head: wide-brim or low-profile hood for drip deflection. The ceiling drips continuously in places.
- Eye protection: clear, anti-fog goggles or glasses. Humidity will defeat ordinary lenses. The plum bioluminescence is dim; you need unobstructed vision for the residual lamp glows and for not walking into a frond vane.

Critical: a closed-cycle or high-quality filtered breathing system rated for humid metal-laden air and fine particulates/spores. At minimum a full-face respirator with appropriate cartridges plus a small positive-pressure or powered unit if you have it. Many travelers underestimate how the stagnant heat and the “boiled lichen + wet iron” taste accumulate. You can go without for short scouting passes, but full stays reward filtration. The plants exhale visible vapor; treat it as non-optional atmosphere.

Do not wear anything you cannot afford to have permanently take on the smell of the place. It clings.

### Core Kit (Carry Compact; the Space Is Intimate)
- Lighting: redundant low-output sources only. Bright white light feels violative and destroys what little dark-adaptation and bioluminescent mapping you have. Use dim red or deep amber lamps, chemical light sticks in the same spectrum, and a single shielded white for emergencies. Headlamp with multiple brightnesses and a diffuser. The abandoned lamp housings still hold trickle charge; do not rely on them.
- Navigation: the chamber is small and bounded, yet darkness makes distance slippery and side alcoves multiply. A simple inertial or map-and-breadcrumb system plus physical markers (biodegradable or collectible) at entry. Laser distance or ultrasonic can help ceiling and wall ranging without noise. Record a voice map on a device that works in heat and damp.
- Water: 4–6+ liters minimum in insulated bladders or bottles that will not sweat excessively into your pack. Electrolyte tabs. A small UV or chemical purifier as belt-and-suspenders even though you should not need local water.
- Food: dense, non-perishable, low-residue. Eat lightly; digestion in stagnant heat is unpleasant. The place itself offers nothing edible to a human baseline.
- Thermal management: compact cooling towel or chemical cold packs for nape and wrists if you overheat. A thin emergency bivy that can reflect or vent. You will not sleep comfortably here; the heat does not drop.
- Tools: multitool, short pry bar, sample bags and vials (if your multiverse protocols allow collection), pH and basic metal test strips, gloves, and a small roll of high-temp tape. A collapsible probe or walking staff for testing pool depths and talus stability. Magnet (the residual industry still answers).
- Documentation: waterproof notebook or ruggedized recorder. The condensate patterns, the slow creep of root-moss over the flywheel, the single needle on the dead control panel that still drifts—these are worth noting. Photography with low-light capable, non-flash systems.
- Power: everything dual-powered or with chemical backup. Heat and damp kill batteries faster than you expect.
- Waste: pack out everything. Leave no human nitrogen spike for the fermentative web.
- Exit insurance: whatever multiverse transit anchor or recall device you use, keep it on your person in a heat-and-moisture sealed pouch, tested cold before entry. Secondary dead-man or timed beacon if your continuity protocols support it. Rockfall is possible (fresh talus, sharp breaks, place still settling). Heat illness is more likely.

### Entry, Conduct, and Exit Protocols
Approach the chamber as a short, deliberate visit unless you have specific long-duration research reasons and support. Announce to yourself the heat first—feel it pool. Move slowly enough that you do not slip on unseen wet stone or kick talus into a pool. Let your eyes use the plum glow and the weak lamp remnants; force white light only for hazards.

Do not force interaction with the plants. They are spaced in formal islands and have no interest in you. A fallen droplet from a high vane striking stone sounds like a soft key in an unused lock—acknowledge it and keep walking. Avoid breaking fronds; they take a season to blacken and the chemistry is unknown. The root-moss crawls over decades; your footprint is irrelevant to it but still rude.

Machinery is inert yet warm. Residual heat in casings and the rock’s interrupted industrial thought mean surfaces can be hotter than air. Test before leaning. Do not attempt to spin the flywheel, force the pump, or chase the creeping needle. They are becoming landscape. The places where basalt pretends to be gear and iron grows frond-rind are for observation, not excavation, unless you are equipped and authorized for that weight of weird.

Watch your hydration and core temperature on a fixed schedule. When fabrics are fully damp, when the taste of iron is thick, when you catch yourself standing still listening for the next metallic tick that may not come for hours—check the chronometer.

Exit while you still have margin. Decontaminate on the far side: strip the shell, rinse or wipe skin, change into dry clothes you cached, rehydrate, and monitor for respiratory or skin delayed effects. The chamber’s smell will stay in memory longer than in cloth.

### Experiential Reality You Are Preparing For
You will feel held. The low vault, the layered heat, the sweating stone, and the spaced luminous plant islands create a womb that remembers factories. Beauty is present—the translucent amber vanes, the ginger-solder vapor, the way moss has made the scorched flywheel geological—but it is beauty without urgency or welcome. Loneliness here is clean and total. The abandoned control panel facing its doorway with one living needle is a better memento mori than most ruins manage. You are the fastest moving thing in the entire system by orders of magnitude; that fact will eventually feel like bad manners.

If you prepare with respect for the heat, the damp, the dark, the spores, the sharp rock, and the slow chemistry, you will come out with your baseline body intact and a precise understanding of a place that is still finishing its last industrial sentence in the language of basalt and root. If you improvise, the chamber will not kill you dramatically. It will simply let you overheat, lose time, slip, or breathe more of it than you intended, then continue its patient work of turning engines into landscape after you are gone.

Travel light, leave lighter, and do not let the soft key sound of a single falling drop convince you that something is about to open. Nothing is. That is the point.
