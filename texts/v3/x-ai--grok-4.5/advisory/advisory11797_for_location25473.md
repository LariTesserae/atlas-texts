**Preparing for the Underskip**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler—someone with a normal body, normal limits, and no permanent superhuman adaptations—cannot simply arrive. The Underskip is not a place you visit; it is a place that begins consuming your assumptions the moment you enter. It has no surface to breach, no sky to orient by, no soil to stand on, and no edge you will ever reach. It is a manufactured ocean the volume of a world, held in a young, sharp, unfinished lattice of ferrocrete and black alloy, lit by industrial lamps that never quite defeat the dark, driven by engines that still thump in places, and filled with life that has claimed every gap. Temperature is a constant 9–10 °C. Pressure is always the pressure of depth. Sound is continuous. “Down” is provisional. If you stop moving, you drift until a current or a spar decides otherwise.

Preparation is therefore total. Treat it as equipping for an indefinite residency inside a drowned megastructure that does not care whether you live.

### Mindset and Psychological Conditioning
Spend weeks beforehand living in cold water, total darkness, and constant low-frequency noise. Practice sensory isolation tanks with added mechanical thudding and the recorded groan of stressed metal. The Underskip produces a unique double vertigo: claustrophobia from the enclosing lattice cells and agoraphobia from the knowledge that those cells open into still larger frames that no one has finished mapping. You will feel both trapped and infinitely exposed at the same time.

Accept that orientation is local and temporary. There is no absolute up. Heavier silt falls “down,” and that is the best definition you will get. Maps are recursive; every void is a chamber inside a larger void. You can travel for a lifetime and remain inside the same continuous artifact. Cultivate comfort with unfinished geometry—knife-edge corners, spans that simply stop in open water, welds still bright. The place feels recently abandoned by builders who left the pumps running. That rawness never softens; it only accumulates life and silt.

Emotionally, prepare for beauty that arrives without comfort: vast schools of silver fusiforms turning like weather under flat white lamps, the sudden flare of luminous shrimp in a dead breaker box, the slow pulse of a fifty-meter colonial jellyfish. And prepare for the counter-note: the displacement roar of something longer than a freight keel moving in the far dark, the metallic shriek of a seized turbine that still tries to turn, the knowledge that the nearest solid surface large enough to rest against might be kilometers away and vertical.

You will be alone in a crowd of machines and animals. Loneliness here is oceanic.

### Physical Conditioning
Build extreme cold-water endurance and fin-swimming stamina, then discard most of it. Continuous swimming will kill you with exhaustion long before anything else does. Focus instead on:

- Breath-hold and CO₂ tolerance (for emergency suit breaches).
- Core strength and joint resilience for working in a full pressure suit.
- Resistance to motion sickness and spatial disorientation in three-dimensional currents and shear zones.
- Fine motor control while wearing thick gloves in cold.

Train in overhead environments with no free surface—flooded caves, industrial tanks, wreck interiors—until the absence of “up” stops triggering panic. Practice moving through sharp, unfinished metal structures without snagging or cutting yourself. Learn to read subtle pressure and temperature gradients with your body; they are the closest thing to weather reports.

### Core Life-Support and Mobility Systems
You need a closed-cycle, long-duration atmospheric diving system. An ordinary scuba rig is a death sentence.

- **Suit**: Full hard-shell or hybrid atmospheric suit rated for the expected pressure range (assume planetary-ocean pressures; the lattice has no free surface and depth is effectively unlimited). The exterior must be abrasion- and cut-resistant against knife-straight ferrocrete edges and proud rivet lines. Excellent thermal insulation is non-negotiable; 9–10 °C water will pull heat relentlessly. Include self-healing layers for punctures from crustacean claws or cable fretting. The suit must allow fine manipulation and have modular hard-points for tools and thrusters. White or high-visibility panels help in the lamp-lit zones; matte, non-reflective sections help when you want to disappear among the industrial camouflage of the predators.

- **Life support**: Closed-circuit rebreather with redundant scrubbers and oxygen supply measured in weeks, not hours. Ideally, include an electrochemical oxygen generator that can crack water if absolute disaster strikes, plus emergency gill-assist or chemical rebreather backups. CO₂ monitoring must be paranoid. Hydration and nutrition ports that let you eat and drink without breaking seal. Waste management for long durations. The suit should maintain a shirtsleeve internal environment so you can think.

- **Propulsion**: Primary and backup personal thruster packs (ducted fans or water jets) with multiple speed regimes. Silent ultra-low for stealth among fauna, cruise for distance, burst for escaping shear zones or predators. Fin attachments for the suit as zero-power backup. Buoyancy control must be precise and multi-stage; you will trim constantly. Never rely on “settling to the bottom”—there is none.

- **Power**: Multiple independent sources. Nuclear batteries or high-density fuel cells preferred; solar is useless. Harvest residual heat from still-functioning heat exchangers when you can, but treat every machine as potentially live and toxic.

### Navigation, Sensing, and Mapping
You will get lost. Plan for it.

- Inertial navigation with periodic acoustic and optical resets.
- High-resolution multibeam sonar and Doppler velocity logs for currents.
- Passive and active hydrophones: the Underskip is loud. Learn to read the layered soundscape—the staggered thud of circulation engines, the fretting of cables, the displacement signature of large bodies. Sound is information.
- Low-light and active imaging systems that work in the flat white industrial spectrum. Personal lamps (redundant, diffuse and focused modes) because ambient light dies with distance and there is no upwelling sunlight.
- Deployable beacon network: sonic pingers, chemical dye markers that persist in cold water, and small self-anchoring lights or radio-frequency tags you can clip to struts. Build your own local constellation as you travel. Assume any map you carry is incomplete and that the lattice is recursive.
- Pressure and micro-current sensors. Shear zones and internal pressure fronts can move you kilometers without your consent. Detect them early.

Carry physical notes in waterproof form. Electronics fail; metal and polymer scratchings last.

### Defense, Hazard Mitigation, and Tools
The fauna is abundant and scaled to the structure. Silver schools are weather. Torpedo predators wear industrial camouflage (matte grey, safety-yellow). Crustaceans the size of ground vehicles graze bacterial films with clicking legs. Larger things—eel-forms longer than freight keels, fused colonial jellies—announce themselves with roar and slap.

- Non-lethal deterrence first: bright strobes tuned to startle, acoustic repellents, low-power electric fields. Lethal options only as last resort; blood in the water has consequences you cannot predict.
- Structural hazards are equal threats: sharp incomplete spans, cables that frets and loops, automated arms still welding phantoms in empty bays, conveyor tunnels coughing metallic silt, dying capacitors that strobe and arc. Heat exchangers bleed warm, copper-tasting effluent that attracts both life and corrosion.
- Carry a full toolkit: cutters rated for alloy and ferrocrete, welders or clamps for emergency repairs to your own gear or for creating temporary anchors, sampling gear, and heavy gloves. Magnetic and mechanical grapples. Line and reel systems for short fixed traverses inside a single cell.
- Filtration and toxin handling: the water carries metallic silt and industrial residues. Never open the suit casually. If you must process local resources, assume heavy metals and bacterial films that have grown on machinery.

Medical kit must handle trauma, hypothermia after suit breach, infection from unknown microbiota, decompression stress if you move between pressure regimes inside the lattice, and long-term nutritional deficits. Include strong anxiolytics and antipsychotics for the psychological load; the combination of scale, noise, isolation, and unfinished geometry can break people.

### Logistics and Sustenance
Bring everything. There is no reliable foraging. Filter-feeding tubes are sparse and pale; bacterial films coat every surface; the copper taste near heat exchangers is a warning. Some travelers report that certain crustaceans or the silver fusiforms are edible after heavy processing, but treat that as emergency only and carry testing equipment. Calories, micronutrients, and water (or recycling capacity) for months. Cache supplies in marked, sealed containers clipped into quiet corners of the lattice if you establish any kind of circuit.

### Arrival, Movement, and Exit Protocols
Arrive already sealed and powered. Multiverse translation into open water at unknown pressure is unsurvivable without the suit fully online. Orient immediately by residual acceleration and silt fall. Deploy a primary beacon. Do not move far until you have a local acoustic picture and understand the nearest currents.

Travel in short, deliberate legs between solid structure. Use the great struts and cell faces as waypoints. Rest by clipping in, never by drifting. When internal weather builds—sediment storms, panicked fish, standing-wave flex that you feel in your ribs before you hear it—find the lee of a large beam or enter a quieter manufactory nodule. Some nodules still have residual power and pockets of different water chemistry; they can serve as temporary waystations, but they are also dens for predators and full of half-alive machinery.

Establish a personal rule: never exhaust more than one-third of your propulsion or life-support reserves without a known return or cache. The scale makes every journey longer than it appears.

Exit the same way you entered: controlled translation while fully sealed. Have a dead-man procedure that triggers automatic return if life signs fail or if you drift beyond a pre-set time/distance without beacon contact. The Underskip will not notice you are gone.

### Final Intuitive Notes
What visiting feels like: perpetual civil twilight under cages of white lamps, the water so clear that mid-range looks almost like overcast daylight until it simply ends in black. Your lights pick out ribs and perforations that march away in perfect geometry until perspective fails. Schools pass like living fronts. Somewhere a circulation engine keeps its ancient rhythm. Somewhere else a turbine shrieks once and falls silent again. You clip to a spar to eat, and the cold of the metal comes through the suit. A warm plume from a heat exchanger draws white weeds and scavengers; you give it wide berth. Hours later a deep subsonic flex rolls through the district and every cable sings. You feel very small and very unfinished yourself.

The Underskip does not hate you. It does not know you are there. It was built, then left running, and life poured into the gaps. It continues—cold, loud, full of motion, sharp-edged, planetary, incomplete.

Prepare as if you are moving into a living machine the size of a world and intend to remain coherent inside it. Everything else is detail.
