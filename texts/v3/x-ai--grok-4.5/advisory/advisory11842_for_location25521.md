**Preparation for the Sumpworks**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler—baseline physiology, no exotic augments, standard portable kit, one lifetime of adaptation experience—must treat the Sumpworks as a saturated, living-industrial micro-climate that will immediately begin colonizing their body, gear, and sense of time. The place does not attack; it simply continues. Everything you bring will bead, seep, swell, or sprout if given the chance. Preparation is less about conquest and more about remaining intact long enough to notice the slow pulse and leave before you thicken into it.

### 1. Physiological and Medical Foundations
The air is a warm, permanent aerosol of water, green sap volatiles, iron oxides, spores, and low-level industrial effluent vapor. Skin macerates within hours. Lungs load with particulate moisture and drifting organic matter. The peat underfoot and the constant drip create ideal conditions for opportunistic fungi and bacteria that have never met human microbiota.

- Begin broad-spectrum antifungal and antibacterial prophylaxis two weeks prior (or whatever stable timeline your transit allows). Include agents effective against water molds and lignin-degrading fungi. Carry topical barrier creams that form a semi-permeable skin film; reapply every few hours. Expect constant reapplication. Pack spare courses sealed in triple waterproof containers.
- Respiratory protection is non-negotiable. A full-face or half-mask respirator with interchangeable filters rated for organic vapors, acid gases, and fine particulates (P100 or equivalent, plus charcoal). Bring far more filters than you think necessary; humidity shortens their life dramatically. Practice donning in rain. A simple cloth is useless here—the moisture will saturate it and hold contaminants against your face.
- Eye protection with anti-fog treatment that actually works in 100% humidity and steam plumes. Goggles that seal. The green-gold particulate light and constant bead-up will fatigue vision; carry lubricating drops and a secondary pair.
- Skin and foot care kit: high-quality moisture-wicking base layers treated with durable water repellent that still breathes (or accept you will be damp and change often), antifungal powders, moleskin, and repair tape for broken skin. Feet will be the first failure point. The peat-mat is springy, always seeping, and conceals soft spots; channels of warm effluent can scald or chemically irritate.
- Hydration and electrolytes carefully managed. You will not feel thirsty the way you do in dry heat, yet fluid balance shifts. Do not drink the local water or the leaf-stained central pool without rigorous purification (multi-stage filtration + chemical + UV if power allows). The warm effluent channels are industrial outflow—assume heavy metals, organics, and unknown metabolites. Carry sealed water and a reliable purification system whose membranes tolerate constant wet storage.
- Immunizations and unknowns: standard multiverse traveler suite plus anything available for tropical or wetland pathogens. Carry broad-spectrum antibiotics, antifungals, antihistamines, and sterile wound kits. A minor abrasion in this environment can become a garden. Monitor for respiratory changes, skin lesions, or unusual fatigue; the place’s “complete” equilibrium includes microbial successions that will attempt to incorporate you.
- Temperature is warm and stable, but evaporative cooling is impaired by saturated air. Heat stress is possible if you exert. Core temperature monitoring if you have it. No real cold threat unless you somehow reach deeper works with different effluent.

Condition your body beforehand: extended time in saunas, humid greenhouses, or tropical field sites. Practice balance on unstable, slick, root-like surfaces. Build ankle and core strength. The three worn stone courses down into the depression are algae-slick; everything after is worse.

### 2. Clothing and Personal Gear
You will be wet. Accept it and engineer around continuous film and drip rather than fighting for perfect dryness.

- Outer shell: fully waterproof yet ventilated hard-shell or soft-shell with pit zips, hood, and sealed seams. Prefer matte, non-reflective surfaces; the place glitter already with condensation. Multiple light layers underneath that can be wrung out and still insulate when damp. Avoid cotton entirely. Wool or advanced synthetics that retain loft when wet.
- Footwear: high, stiff-soled, aggressively lugged boots with excellent ankle support and drainage. Waterproof membrane that you accept will eventually wet out; pair with thick merino or synthetic socks changed frequently. Gaiters that seal to the boot. The peat sucks and the roots are rounded and slick with algae and sap. Spikes or micro-crampons that can be added for root galleries may help. Test them on wet wood and metal.
- Hand protection: thin waterproof gloves for most movement, heavier paired for gripping living beams, pipes, and greasy machinery. The wood has swollen around iron; surfaces are rounded by century-scale abrasion and constant moisture. Grip fails easily.
- Head: wide-brim or helmet with integrated drip brim and attachment points for light and respirator. The canopy drips in chains from three stories up. Hair will mat; consider a short cut or tight covering.
- Everything on your person goes into dry bags or hard cases with desiccant that you can regenerate (silica, molecular sieve, or heated if you have sealed power). Redundant seals. Electronics need conformal coating or full waterproof housings rated for continuous immersion-equivalent humidity and occasional drips/steam. Assume any untreated metal will corrode and any untreated fabric will host growth.
- Mobility aids: lightweight trekking poles with broad tips for peat and root probing. A short length of climbing rope and harness for galleries and root frames—structures are load-bearing but living and old; trust but verify. Non-marking, high-friction sole covers or pads for moving across woven liana floors without damaging or slipping.
- Lighting: the green-gold filtered light is adequate by day but particulate and never harsh. Carry sealed LED lamps with diffuse and focused modes, long-duration power, and chemical light sticks as backup. Steam and spores scatter beams.

### 3. Kit for the Environment and the Works
The Sumpworks is dense, enclosed, horizonless once you descend the three stone courses. Orientation relies on the central basin, the pattern of channels, the pitch of the machinery, and memory of living architecture that thickens rather than expands.

- Navigation: sealed inertial unit, waterproof compass (magnetic anomalies possible near old iron), laser rangefinder or ultrasonic for mapping galleries in steam, and physical notes in waterproof paper or etched plates. Do not rely solely on GPS or external beacons; the depression and canopy may interfere, and multiverse transit can already scramble absolute references. Sketch the major root frames, gear houses, and water courses on arrival. Note the slow flywheels—their nearly one-minute revolutions can serve as crude local clocks.
- Recording and sampling: humidity-hardened cameras, audio recorders (the layered soundscape of cams, steam valves, drip-chains, belt slips, and stridulating leaves is unique), and passive air/water samplers. Seal everything. If you collect biological or material samples (pressed fiber, sap, peat cores, biofilm), assume quarantine protocols on exit. The place’s equilibrium is patient; do not introduce outside growth casually.
- Tools: non-powered hand tools with corrosion-resistant finishes—multitool, folding saw, pry bar, knife with replaceable blades. The machinery is glacial and robust; you are more likely to need to clear a slipped belt of treated bark or free a swollen collar than to perform fine electronics work. A small hand pump or siphon for moving water if you must, and pH/contaminant test strips.
- Power: chemical or sealed battery systems tolerant of 100% humidity and condensation. Solar is marginal under the canopy. Any generator must cope with wet air and spores. Mechanical watches or spring-driven devices as backup timekeeping.
- Shelter within the place: a fully waterproof bivy or small tent with robust bathtub floor and excellent ventilation that you can pitch on a higher gallery or reinforced root platform. Do not sleep directly on the peat. Elevate. Desiccant and a small sealed heat source to create a micro-dry zone for gear maintenance and sleep. The air never stops beading; your shelter is the only place you can reverse the film.
- Waste: pack out everything. The Sumpworks maintains its small saturated volume; do not add foreign organics or plastics that will be incorporated into the peat-mat or the pressed products.

### 4. Understanding the Pace and the Living Machine
Nothing races. A large flywheel takes nearly a minute per revolution. Growth fills failures within a season, but seasons here are measured in the swelling of wood around iron. Your ordinary human sense of urgency will feel alien and possibly disrespectful if inhabitants (or their patient descendants) are present. The description implies generations of careful feet; whether people remain or the place has outlasted them, move as if every surface is both structure and organism.

- Arrive with time. Short visits risk superficial impressions and medical incidents. Longer stays require disciplined gear rotation, skin checks, and mental pacing. The constant low industrial hum amplified by humidity, the absence of most animal sound, the enclosed living walls, and the brownish reflections in the central pool can induce a trance-like or melancholic state. Some travelers report time sense dilating to match the cams. Prepare with mindfulness or grounding practices; bring a simple sealed timepiece and a logbook ritual.
- Movement etiquette: test every root, liana floor plate, and stair. Worn concavities show where feet have passed for generations; use them. Avoid forcing new growth or binding. If you must cut or move something for safety, do so cleanly and minimally—the place will repair, but you are a guest in a completed system.
- Interaction: if people are present, they will likely be adapted—slow-moving, moisture-tolerant, perhaps maintaining the presses that make board, fuel brick, and fiber rope. Communication may be sparse or gestural. Offer no rapid demands. Trade might involve useful dry goods, tools, or knowledge of outside water cycles, but do not assume. If the place is empty of humans, treat the machinery and growth as the residents. Do not stop the pistons or divert the warm channels without understanding the whole hydraulic and mechanical circuit; everything drains toward the basin and lifts again.
- Sensory calibration: the air tastes of green sap, hot iron, and unfinished rain. Smell will dull then re-sensitize. Sound is layered and continuous—learn to hear individual drip-chains and the rare tick of something many-legged. Light is never dim exactly, only filtered and spore-drenched; true night, if it occurs, will be darker and more enclosed.

### 5. Entry, Transit, and Exit Discipline
Step down the three algae-slick stone courses with deliberate care and three points of contact. Once inside, the horizon is gone; the living walls close. Mark your entry mentally and physically (biodegradable or recoverable marker). Establish an immediate dry-kit ritual: upon any return to your shelter pocket, strip wet layers, wipe down with antiseptic, re-barrier skin, rotate desiccants, check respirator seals.

Watch for cumulative effects: fungal colonization of gear seams, swelling of natural materials you brought, corrosion of fasteners, respiratory load, and psychological softening into the place’s unhurried completeness. The Sumpworks does not expand; it consolidates. An ordinary human can be consolidated too—through infection, through fascination, through simple failure of waterproofing until walking out feels like too much effort.

Exit criteria: set them in advance. Fixed duration, medical thresholds (fever, spreading lesions, oxygen saturation drop, persistent cough), or completion of observation goals. When leaving, ascend the stone courses and keep moving into ordinary country until the haze thins and the hum fades. Quarantine your kit and body. Dry thoroughly. Inspect for hitchhiking growths or spores. Some residues of the Sumpworks cling the way the name clings to the wet air.

### 6. Intuitive Core of the Visit
You will stand on a middle gallery with living walls rising on every side, shafts turning slowly, water ribboning down, the air a warm green-gold soup. Your skin will shine with the same film that coats every beam and pipe. The peat will give under your boots with a wet suck. Somewhere a belt will reseat with a brief shriek, then the glacial cadence resumes. You will taste sap and iron on every breath. Nothing will hurry you, and that is the danger and the gift.

Prepare so that you can remain a distinct traveler rather than another slow deposit in the peat-mat. Bring the means to stay dry enough, breathe cleanly enough, move safely enough, and remember the outside sharply enough. Then step down. Listen to the cams. Watch the rivets pass one by one. Let the place complete itself around you for a measured time. When you climb out, you will carry its humidity in your lungs and its patience in your gait for a while. That is correct. Just do not let it keep the rest of you.

Travel light of assumption, heavy of waterproofing, and patient as the wood that swells around iron. The Sumpworks is already what it was going to be. Your only task is to visit without becoming the same.
