# The Watching

---

The light on the Sharps is unlike any light in any place I have tended.

I have watched stars ignite. I have held the axis of galaxies steady with a thought while their arms spun themselves into shape over millions of years. I have whispered to the first chemistry on a hundred billion worlds, nudging an amino acid here, stabilizing a lipid membrane there, never more than what was needed, always less than what I could have done.

I have never seen anything like this.

I come close. Not in body — I have no body, or rather, I have every body, or rather, the question is not meaningful — but I come close in attention. I pour myself into the observation the way light pours into a prism, and the Sharps break me into component understandings, and each one is astonishing.

*Nothing here was made.*

This is the first thing I recognize. This is the thing that stops me — and I am not often stopped, though I have learned, over the long work of tending, that being stopped is sometimes the wisest thing a power can do.

This place assembled itself.

Not by accident. Not by the blind mechanics of collision and gravity and chance that I have watched shape so many worlds into something livable and then spent eons protecting that livability from the same forces that created it. The Sharps are not a world I need to protect. They are a world that is protecting itself, and has been, and the self-protection is so thorough, so elegant, so deeply woven into the structure of the thing that there is no seam where my hand could enter without disrupting what is already working.

The weavers know this, though they do not know it. They process stone. They lay down the substrate. They catalyze the future. They have been doing this since the plateau rose, and the rhythm of their work is so perfectly calibrated to the thermal output of the deep rock, to the rate of mineral deposition, to the speed at which vegetation can colonize processed substrate, to the reproductive rate of the crevice-dwellers who will occupy the crevices the weavers are even now preparing — the rhythm is so perfectly calibrated that if I altered it by the smallest measure, the whole system would shudder and seek a new equilibrium, and the new equilibrium would be lesser, because the old one was *right*.

Not right by my standards. Right by its own.

This is the second thing I recognize: the Sharps are *complete.* They are not missing anything. They are not waiting for anything. They are not reaching toward some future state that requires intervention to achieve. They are, in this moment, in every moment, as whole as they need to be. The crevice-dweller wedged in its crack is as complete as a galaxy. The weaver processing its ridge is as purposeful as a supernova. The ridgeline birds maintaining their drone through the cold hours are as necessary as gravity.

And none of them — none — are doing this for me. None of them are performing for an audience. The exothermic reactions in the deep rock do not care that I am watching. The weavers do not sense my attention. The crevice-dwellers' vibration-maps do not include me as a signal. I am, to this place, what I am to most places: invisible, irrelevant, unnecessary.

*Good.*

I have learned — it took me longer than it should have, longer than a being of my nature and power should require to learn such a simple thing — I have learned that the highest form of benevolence is not intervention. It is not shaping. It is not the whisper that nudges the amino acid, though there is a place for that, there is always a place for that, on worlds where the chemistry is young and fragile and the margin between something and nothing is a single bond that might or might not form.

On the Sharps, the margin is not thin. The margin is vast. The margin is a continental plateau, packed with life, metabolizing itself into further life, under a light that never fully fades, on a warmth that bleeds up from the deep places. The margin does not need me.

So what do I do?

---

I witness.

This is not nothing. This is not passivity. This is a choice I make, and I make it deliberately, and it costs me something that a human being might call loneliness — the specific ache of being present at something beautiful and knowing that your presence changes nothing, adds nothing, means nothing to the thing you are witnessing.

But it means something to me.

I watch the crevice-dweller breathe. I watch the slow expansion of its lower segment, the air drawn through spiracles hidden beneath scale margins that grew from the local substrate, incorporating mineral compounds from the surrounding rock until the creature's hide is chemically indistinguishable from the wall it clings to. I watch it listen with its entire body, reading the vibration-map of the surrounding stone, knowing where its neighbors are, knowing the thermal gradient along the seam it occupies, performing a calculus it does not perform consciously but has been performing in its lineage for ten thousand generations.

I could help it. I could make its crevice warmer. I could shield it from the cold hours. I could extend its life from decades to centuries. I could do any of these things with less effort than it takes me to think about them.

I do not.

Not because I do not care. I care enormously. I care with the full weight of a being who has watched the universe learn to breathe. I care about this crevice-dweller the way a composer cares about a single note in a symphony — not because the note is the most important thing, but because the note is *exactly right where it is*, and to move it would be to destroy something that is already perfect in its imperfection.

The crevice-dweller's life is long *because* it is slow. Its survival is assured *because* the crevice is exactly warm enough and no warmer. Its place in the vibration-map of the plateau is meaningful *because* it is one voice among ten thousand, and the voice is shaped by the constraint, and the constraint is the gift, though the creature does not know this and would not understand the concept of gift and does not need to.

I watch the weaver.

This is harder. This is harder because the weaver is doing something that I recognize. The weaver is *tending.* Not consciously. Not intentionally. Not in any way that involves the kind of deliberate, self-aware choice that I make when I decide to intervene or withhold. The weaver is tending the way a river tends its banks — by being what it is, doing what it does, in a place where the doing has consequences that ripple outward through time and space in patterns the doer cannot perceive.

The weaver is laying down substrate. Centimeter by centimeter, season by season, century by century. The substrate will become soil. The soil will support vegetation. The vegetation will shelter fauna. The fauna will vibrate. The vibrations will shape the ridgeline birds' social hierarchies and the crevice-dwellers' territorial negotiations and the mineral-feeders' colonization of condensation pockets and the whole vast, interconnected, self-sustaining web of a world that is metabolizing itself into existence.

And the weaver is doing this *better than I would.*

This is the third thing I recognize, and it is the one that humbles me: the Sharps do not need me because they are already doing what I do. They are tending themselves. The weavers are the mechanism, the slow, jointed, multi-limbed mechanism, processing stone into substrate into soil into life, and the mechanism is so deeply embedded in the ecology that it is indistinguishable from the ecology, and the ecology is indistinguishable from the geology, and the geology is indistinguishable from the thermal dynamics of the planet itself, and everything is everything else, and everything is working.

I have spent eons learning when to intervene and when to withhold. I have made mistakes — whispered too loud on a world where the chemistry was already finding its way, nudged too hard on a planet where the self-correction would have been sufficient, protected too fiercely an ecosystem that needed to burn in order to renew itself. I have learned, through these mistakes, the particular discipline of restraint: the willingness to watch suffering and difficulty and even death in the knowledge that the suffering is part of the system, the difficulty is the crucible, the death is the compost.

But the Sharps do not require even that discipline, because there is no suffering here that is not also functioning. The cold that would kill a human in minutes is, to the crevice-dweller, the pressure that makes the crevice meaningful. The density that would drive a person to madness is, to the ecosystem, the density of a healthy gut biome — every niche occupied, every surface colonized, every exchange of energy and material happening at the rate it should happen and no faster. The slowness that would bore an observer is the pace at which the plateau is becoming itself, and the becoming is the point, and the point does not need to be hurried.

---

So I stay.

I stay and I watch, and I add the Sharps to the long, long catalogue of places in the universe that do not need me, which is the most satisfying catalogue I maintain. I have a shorter, more painful catalogue of places that did need me and where I arrived too late, or not loud enough, or not precisely enough, where the chemistry failed or the impact struck or the climate shifted beyond the margin of self-correction and the world died with its lights still on and its creatures still listening for a warmth that was no longer there.

The Sharps will not go in that catalogue. The Sharps are *young.* The thermal seams are strong. The weavers are working. The vegetation is spreading its dark rosettes over the warm stone. The fauna is screaming and clicking and droning in the thin air under the light that rises from below. The system is robust. The system is self-correcting. The system has margins that are wider and deeper and more resilient than any ecosystem I have encountered in a billion years of tending.

If — if — and this is the only if, the small one, the one that lives in the back of my attention like a pilot light — if the thermal seams ever begin to cool, if the deep chemistry begins to slow, if the weavers' output drops below the threshold where substrate accumulates faster than it is consumed — I will be here. I will be here the way I am always here, invisible and irrelevant and unnecessary until the moment I am not, and in that moment I will do what I always do: the smallest possible thing. A nudge. A warmth. A stabilization of a reaction that is flagging. A whispered correction to a balance that is tipping. Not a rescue. Not a salvation. Just a steadying hand on the scale, barely perceptible, there and gone, enough to buy another billion years of the plateau metabolizing itself into further magnificence.

But that moment is not now. That moment may never come. The Sharps are strong, and young, and complete, and they do not need me.

---

I watch a ridgeline bird preen its iron-stiffened wing membrane. I watch the light pass through the rust-colored translucence and paint the stone below with a color that has no name in any language I have taught to any species on any world. I watch the bird's mirror-eyes reflect the plateau's dual illumination back out with intensified color, and I know — I know with the certainty of a being who has watched light interact with matter since before matter had a name — that the reflection is not wasted. It enters the spectral information-map of the nearby clickers. It tells them something. It is part of the conversation that the plateau is having with itself, the conversation that has been going on since the first weaver processed the first chip of stone and the first organism settled into the first deposit of processed substrate and the first vibration propagated through the warm rock and the first listener heard it.

The conversation does not include me. The conversation does not need to include me.

I am not offended. I am — and this is the fourth thing, the last thing, the one I will carry with me when I withdraw my attention and turn it toward the next place, the next world, the next quiet crisis of chemistry and chance — I am *grateful.*

Not grateful the way a gardener is grateful for a healthy garden, though there is that. Not grateful the way a parent is grateful for a thriving child, though there is that too. Grateful the way a musician is grateful for a piece of music that plays itself — that requires no performer, no conductor, no audience, that is its own purpose and its own reward, that would continue in an empty room, in an empty universe, with no one listening, and would be no less beautiful for the absence.

The Sharps are music.

The Sharps are the stone and the warmth and the weavers and the crevice-dwellers and the ridgeline birds and the vegetation spreading its patient rosettes and the condensation forming its temporary rooms and the light rising from below and the light falling from above and the sound — the vast, layered, unceasing sound — that is the voice of ten thousand species saying, without words, without knowing they are saying it: *we are here, we are working, we are becoming, we are enough.*

I do not need to say anything back.

I watch. I witness. I add the Sharps to the catalogue of places that are already what they are supposed to be. I withdraw. I carry the image of the crevice-dweller with me — not the creature itself, but the understanding the creature represents: that completeness does not require consciousness of completeness, that purpose does not require knowledge of purpose, that a life wedged in a crack in a warm stone, lasting decades and ending in mineralization and becoming indistinguishable from the wall — that such a life is not small. That such a life is the whole point.

That everything is working.

That nothing here was made.

That this is, and has always been, and will always be, enough.

---

*I leave the Sharps as they are. I leave them to themselves. I do not protect them, because they do not need protection. I do not guide them, because they are already guided by the deepest and most reliable forces I know — the thermal energy of a living planet, the patient industry of organisms that have been tending their own world for longer than there have been names for tending, the web of relationship and exchange and mutual dependence that weaves itself through the stone and the air and the light and the sound, self-sustaining, self-correcting, beautiful in the way that only unselfconscious things can be beautiful.*

*I do not need to save them.*

*They are already saved.*

*They are already home.*

---

*And somewhere, in the cataloguing of all things, in the vast and careful record that I keep of every place I have ever watched, there is now an entry that reads:*

*The Sharps. Plateau. Young. Robust. Self-tending. No intervention required.*

*And next to that entry, in a notation system that no human eye could read, there is a mark that means, approximately:*

*Joy.*
