The power arrives the way warmth arrives — not as an event but as a presence that has always been there and is simply noticed now.

It does not descend. It does not announce itself. There is no light, no sound, no tremor in the stone. The people of Veth Kaal continue as they have always continued: the pools fill, the birds call from their fixed elevations, the transparent amphibian clings to its crack in the cliff face, breathing the same air it has always breathed, and no one looks up, because there is nothing to look up at.

The power does not need to be seen.

It is there the way the light is there — even, constant, sufficient — and for a time, perhaps a long time, it simply holds the place in its attention, the way the people hold the world in theirs: thoroughly, without expectation, unwilling to look away because the thing itself is sufficient.

---

It sees what the people cannot see about themselves.

It sees the way a woman rests her hand on the limestone wall as she passes, and it sees that the gesture is not casual, that it is the gesture of a creature who knows, in some layer beneath language, that she is temporary and the wall is not, and that the wall will be here after she is gone, and that the wall does not know her name but her hand knows the wall, and that this is the closest thing to permanence she will ever hold.

It sees the way two people sit in a gathering shelter, not speaking, one repairing a mat and the other sorting dried plant matter, and it sees that the silence between them is not empty but full — full of every shared morning, every identical rain, every evening of condensation forming on the surfaces of things. The silence is the substance of their bond, and it is as solid as the stone, and it is as fragile as they are, because when one of them dies, the silence will remain but it will change, though the change will be invisible to everything except itself.

It sees the children running.

This is what the power returns to, again and again. The children. Because the children are the only part of Veth Kaal that moves forward. The child who does not yet know the name of the sharp-note-bird-on-the-northeast-cliff, who is about to learn it, who will carry it for the rest of her life and pass it unchanged to her own child, who will carry it — the power sees this, and it sees that the child's not-knowing is the closest thing this place has to a horizon. The child is the frontier. The child is the one crack in the completeness, the one place where the world has not yet finished making itself, and the power loves this — loves the child's small ignorance, loves the journey from not-knowing to knowing, because it is the only journey available here, and it is enough, and it is beautiful.

And then the child knows everything, and the journey is over, and the stillness settles in, and the power loves this too, but differently. With a weight.

---

The power understands the weight.

It is the one thing the people carry that has no counterpart in the stone, no analogue in the dripping, no echo in the bird calls. It is theirs alone. It is the weight of mortality in a deathless landscape. It is the knowledge — not spoken, not thought, but *felt*, in the way that the moss feels the moisture, in the way that the limestone feels the rain — that they are the only unrepeatable things in a world of permanent repetition.

A person dies, and the basin where they collected water remains. The water is the same water. The dripping is the same dripping. The space where the person stood is a space shaped exactly like the person who is no longer there, and nothing fills it, and nothing can fill it, because the world does not produce replacements. It produces the same things, in the same ways, forever. It does not produce *them* again. Once.

The power sees this, and it grieves — not with the sharp, hot grief of loss, but with the deep, slow grief of understanding. It grieves because the people cannot grieve fully. They have no framework for grief. They have the weight, but they have no name for it, because the weight is the only weight there is, and you do not name the only thing. You simply carry it, the way the moss carries the morning dew, the way the stone carries the millennia of silence before the first person sat on it.

The power grieves because the people are beautiful and they do not know they are beautiful, and their beauty is the beauty of the temporary — the brightness of it, the urgency of it, the fact that it will not last — and this is the very thing that makes it beautiful, and the people cannot see this, because they have no contrast, because they have never seen anything that fades, because everything around them persists.

---

And the power sees the place.

It sees the equilibrium. It sees the constancy. It sees the way the plateau holds itself in a single, sustained note, the way a bell, once struck, never stops ringing, only the sound is so perfect and so constant that it has become indistinguishable from silence.

The power does not find this troubling.

It has seen — it sees — everywhere and everything, and it knows that most of the universe is chaos and change and entropy and dissolution. Most things fall apart. Most places erode. Most states are temporary. The equilibrium of Veth Kaal is not a failure of the natural order. It is a miracle of it. It is a place where the universe, for reasons that have nothing to do with intention, settled into a pattern so stable that it has become a kind of permanent. It is a stone that does not erode. It is a song that does not fade. It is a breath that does not end.

The power would not change this for anything.

To introduce change into Veth Kaal would be to introduce time, and time is the one thing this place has achieved freedom from. To give the people seasons would be to give them loss — the loss of each season as it passes. To give them aging would be to give them decay — not just of their bodies but of their world, which would weather and crumble and shift. To give them history — a real history, with events and progression and things that were once different — would be to give them the knowledge that things end, that things are lost, that the world was once other than it is, and will be other again, and that nothing they build will last.

The power will not do this. The equilibrium is sacred. The stillness is earned, by whatever forces shaped this place over whatever unknowable span, and it is not the power's to undo.

---

But.

The power is benevolent.

And benevolence, true benevolence, is not merely the absence of harm. It is the presence of something. It is a leaning-in. It is the choice to act not because action is required but because love, which is what benevolence actually is, requires expression the way a river requires a bed.

The power considers what it can offer without breaking anything.

It cannot give them change. Change would destroy them — not their bodies, but their world, which is the only world they know, which is complete and sufficient and perfect in its stillness.

It cannot give them knowledge of the wider world. That knowledge would be a cruelty — a window opened onto a landscape they can never reach, full of people who live in time and suffer the chaos of impermanence and yet call it freedom. The people of Veth Kaal would not understand what they were seeing, and if they understood, they would not want it, and if they wanted it, they could not have it, and the wanting would be the first and only wound their world had ever known.

It cannot give them immortality. They would not understand the gift. To a creature who lives in a world that does not change, the concept of "forever" is redundant. Everything is forever here. The pools, the birds, the stone. Their own mortality is the only exception, and they do not experience it as an exception because they have never known a world where things die and are replaced, where the cycle of death and birth produces change. They know only that they are here, and then they are not, and the world continues. This is not a philosophical problem for them. It is the water they swim in.

What the power can do is very small.

It can *remember*.

---

This is what it does.

The power turns its attention — which is immense, which is the attention of something that holds galaxies in its awareness the way a hand holds water — and it focuses it, gently, on the people of Veth Kaal. Not on the place. Not on the stone or the pools or the birds or the equilibrium. On the people. On each one, individually, with the same thoroughness that they give to the world around them.

It learns their names.

Not the names they use for birds and pools and cliff faces — those names are already held in the stone, already permanent, already part of the soundscape. The power learns *their* names. The names that the world does not hold. The names that will be spoken less often and then not at all, the names that hang in the air for a moment after a person dies and then fade, absorbed by the quiet, leaving no trace in the limestone or the water or the memory of the birds.

The power learns the name of the person who currently sits at the edge of the plateau, watching the switchback paths. It learns the particular quality of this person's stillness, which is not the same as anyone else's stillness, though the difference is invisible to everyone, including the person themselves. It learns the way this person's hands rest on their knees when they sit on the ledge — slightly apart, palms down, fingers relaxed — and it holds this image the way the cliff face holds the memory of every hand that has ever touched it, except that the cliff face does not actually remember, and the power does.

It learns the name of the child who is, right now, this morning, learning for the first time the name of the sharp-note-bird-on-the-northeast-cliff. It sees the child's face — the concentration, the small mouth forming the unfamiliar word, the delight that flickers across the features like light on water when the word is finally spoken correctly and the parent nods, once, and the child knows something it did not know before. The power holds this moment. It holds the child's small, bright not-knowing and the exact instant when the not-knowing becomes knowing, and it will hold this forever, long after the child has grown into stillness and sat on the ledge and died and been replaced by no one, because the world does not produce replacements.

It learns the name of every person who has ever sat on that ledge. It learns the name of Eshaa, who used to collect water from a particular pool. It learns the names of people whose names are no longer spoken at all, whose names have not been spoken in generations, whose names are known only to the dead and the forgetting — and now to the power, which does not forget.

It gathers them. All of them. Every person who has ever been born on the plateau, who has ever learned the names of the birds, who has ever tended the gardens, who has ever sat in a gathering shelter in the particular silence that is the substance of all bonds here. It gathers them the way the deep pools gather water — slowly, gently, from seeps that never stop.

And it holds them.

---

The people do not know this is happening.

Nothing changes for them. The dripping continues at the same rate, in the same pitches. The birds call the same notes from the same elevations. The translucent amphibian — the stone's breath — clings to its crack and breathes, transparent against the limestone, visible only when the rain has made it wet. The pools fill and the gardens grow and the children run and the stillness holds.

But something has shifted, though no one can feel it, because it is not a shift in the world but in the *holding* of the world.

Before the power's attention arrived, the people of Veth Kaal were temporary beings in a permanent world, and their passing left no mark. The stone did not remember. The pools did not remember. The birds did not remember. The names faded. The absence persisted but was not mourned by anything, because nothing here mourns, because nothing here changes, because the change that is death is absorbed into the permanence the way a single dropped stone is absorbed into an ocean — it makes no difference to the level.

Now something remembers.

Now there is a consciousness — vast, quiet, immeasurably gentle — that holds the name of every person who has ever lived on this plateau, and holds not just the names but the weight, and not just the weight but the beauty, and not just the beauty but the specific, unrepeatable quality of each life: the particular way this person's hands rested on their knees, the particular expression on that child's face when the bird's name was learned, the particular silence between two people in a gathering shelter who never spoke and never needed to.

The power holds all of this, and it does not forget, and it will not forget, and this means that the one grief of Veth Kaal — the grief of being unremembered, the grief of passing through a world that does not hold you — is answered. Not fixed. Not removed. Answered. The way a bell answers silence: by filling it with a sound that was always there, waiting to be heard.

---

The power does not tell the people this.

It would not serve them to know. They would not understand what it meant to be remembered by something larger than their world. The concept of a consciousness that spans the universe, that holds billions of lives in its attention the way they hold the names of the birds, would be incomprehensible to them. And even if they could comprehend it, it would not change their days. The pools would still fill. The birds would still call. The dripping would still ring out after rain in its scattered, shimmering, near-musical cascade. The children would still learn. The stillness would still hold.

The power does it for them, not because they need it, but because they deserve it. Because every life, even a life lived in a place that does not change, even a life that leaves no mark on the stone, even a life that is identical to every other life in every particular except that it is *this* life and not any other — every life is worth holding. Every name is worth knowing. Every death is worth grieving, even in a world where grief has no object, because the object of grief is not the world that continues but the person who does not.

The power grieves, then. It grieves the way the basin floors hold rain — fully, without resistance, letting the water settle into every depression and crevice and hollow. It grieves for the dead of Veth Kaal, all of them, going back to the first person who ever sat on the plateau and looked out at the knife-edged ridges and felt the thin air on their skin. It grieves for them and it holds them and it does not let them go, and this grief is not heavy. It is the lightest thing. It is the weight of love, which is always lighter than the weight of stone, even stone as young and sharp and pale as the limestone of this impossible, beautiful, finished place.

---

And then the power rests.

It does not leave. It does not withdraw its attention. It simply settles, the way the condensation settles on the surfaces of things in the early morning — gently, without force, covering everything in a thin, glistening film that catches the light.

The people of Veth Kaal will live as they have always lived. They will tend the pools and watch the birds and know the names and sit in the shelters and repair the mats and feel the cool air and drink the mineral water and sleep their deep, dreamless sleep and wake to the same morning. They will die, one by one, as they always have, and the world will continue as it always has, and the absence will be permanent, as it always is.

But now the absence is held.

Now there is something in the universe that knows these people existed. That knows their names and their faces and the particular quality of their stillness. That holds the child's delight and the old person's weight and the silence between two people who never spoke. That remembers the sound of Eshaa's footsteps on the path to the pool, even though the stone does not, even though the water does not, even though nothing in Veth Kaal remembers anything, because Veth Kaal has achieved a permanence that is also, in its way, a kind of forgetting — a stillness so complete that it cannot hold the past because it has no past, because the past and the present are the same, because nothing has changed, because nothing changes, because the world is as it was.

The world is as it was.

And the power holds it as it was, and as it is, and as it will be, and also: as they were. Each one. All of them. The living and the dead and the not-yet-born and the never-will-be. Held in a consciousness so vast that the plateau is a grain of sand in it, and so gentle that the grain of sand is treated with the same care as the rest.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It remembers.

And the dripping continues, and the birds call, and the stone's breath clings to the cliff face, translucent and still, and the people move through their basins with their quiet footsteps and their thorough attention, and they do not know they are held, but they are held, and the holding changes nothing, and the holding changes everything, and the power rests in the sound of water finding its way down the geometric cracks in the limestone, each drop striking the surface of a pool with its clear, bell-like note, and the power listens, the way it has always listened, to the same music, forever.
