# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would arrive not as light — they have no use for light — but as attention. A quality in the air. A shift in the composite tone that the Listener has spent a lifetime learning to read. The Listener would notice. Of course the Listener would notice. That is what the Listener does.

And then — nothing. Not immediately. The wise power would do what it does first, which is what the spiders do, what the water does, what the stone itself does: it would listen.

---

It would listen for a long time.

It would learn the drip-rate before it moved. It would learn the quarter-tone shift of the pump. It would learn the difference between the sound of bats merely breathing and the sound of bats deciding to move — that half-second of collective intake before the ribbon pours — and it would learn this not because it needed to but because it respected the difference. Because the difference mattered here. Because everything here matters in its smallness, in its specificity, in its refusal to be anything other than what it is.

The wise power would count the workers. Not the way the workers avoid counting — carefully, guiltily, in approximate numbers — but precisely, once, and then it would forget the number again, on purpose, the way you forget the name of a song you've already learned by heart. It would count because knowing is a form of attention and attention is a form of love. It would forget because love does not keep a ledger.

It would watch a child fall.

This is important. The child — four years old, maybe five, old enough to carry a small tool but not old enough to know which surfaces are slick — would stumble on a shelf of exposed bedrock and go down hard, and the sound of the fall would be swallowed by the machinery, would barely register above the hum of the conveyor, and the child would cry — not loudly, because children of the Hollow learn early that loud sounds attract bats and bat-attention is disorienting — and the child's parent or keeper would come and lift them and carry them to the Sealer, and the Sealer would pack the cut with calcite and mycelium, and the white crust would harden, and the child would learn. The child would learn which surfaces are slick.

The wise power would watch all of this and feel — *feel*, which is not a word usually applied to such powers but which is the only word that fits — something that a surface person might call grief but which is not quite grief, because grief implies that something has been lost, and nothing has been lost here. Nothing has been taken. The child was born into this place, shaped by it, fed by it, sheltered by it. The cut will heal. The lesson will hold. The child will grow into the stoop and the ochre skin and the attunement and the patience, and will live a life that is complete in every way that a life can be complete within the parameters of its world.

But.

The wise power would sit with the *but*. It would hold it the way the Sealer holds the poultice — carefully, with both hands, knowing that what it contains is medicine and also a kind of damage, and that you cannot separate the two, and that the act of packing the wound is simultaneously an act of closing and an act of binding.

The *but* is this: the child will never see the sky. Not because the sky is hidden from them, not because someone has decided they shouldn't see it, but because the concept does not exist in the architecture of their experience. The question will not arise. The question *cannot* arise, because the question requires a framework that the Hollow does not provide and cannot provide, because the Hollow is complete, because completeness is what the Hollow *is*.

And the wise power would understand, with the deep understanding that comes from being wise and benevolent and ancient, that completeness and freedom are not the same thing. That a life can be full and also narrow. That a system can be coherent and also closed. That the children's contentment is real — absolutely, genuinely real — and also, in some sense that cannot be articulated without violating it, a kind of beautiful, perfect cage.

Not a cage with bars. Not a cage with a door that could be opened. A cage made of the absence of the question. The most complete cage there is, because its inhabitants do not know they are in it, and would not understand the concept if it were explained, and would not want to leave if they did understand, and would not find anything if they went.

The wise power would know all of this. And it would know something else, something more difficult: that this is not a problem to be solved. That the Hollow is not broken. That the people in it are not suffering, not trapped, not in need of rescue. That they are *adapted*, deeply and beautifully and completely, to a world that is genuinely theirs. That to introduce the sky — to crack open the completeness, to plant the seed of the question — would not liberate them but would wound them. Would give them a longing for something they cannot have and would not want if they could. Would replace a whole, small, dark world with a half, large, bright one, and the half would not be enough, because halves are never enough.

And the wise power would recognize this as the oldest dilemma. The tension at the root of all benevolence: *Do I love them as they are, or as I believe they could be?*

---

It would choose the former. Of course it would choose the former. It is wise.

But it would not leave entirely untouched.

Here is what it would do, and it would do it so gently that no one would know. Not the Listener, not the Sealer, not the children with their wide pupils and their stone-colored skin and their sure, sure feet on the uneven ground. No one would know, because the thing the wise power would do is this:

It would make the Hollow slightly more itself.

That is all. Not better. Not brighter. Not more spacious or more plentiful or more anything. Just *more itself* — more fully and completely and precisely what it already is, with the volume turned up by the smallest possible fraction. The way you might, if you were cleaning a lens, remove not the glass but the nearly invisible film on the glass, so that what you see through it was always there but is now seen with a clarity that wasn't before.

The water would drip, and the drip would carry a slightly richer mineral load — not enough to change the taste, not enough to alter the calcification in the workers' throats, but enough that the calcite deposits on the walls would grow a fraction faster, a fraction thicker, and in a hundred years — or two hundred, what is time to the stone? — the collapsed end of the chamber would fill in more densely, and the new passages carved by water would be shaped differently, would open onto different hollows, would create different possibilities. Not better possibilities. Just *other* ones.

The bats would breathe, and in their breathing there would be a frequency — ultrasonic, below human hearing, below even the spiders' vibration-sense, in a range that only the stone could hear — and the stone would hear it, and in hearing it the stone would respond the way stone responds to vibration: it would shift. Microscopically. Grain by grain. The hairline fractures in the newest walls would close a little faster, or open in slightly different directions, creating passages that hadn't been there before — not that anyone would walk through them, not yet, perhaps not ever, but they would *be* there, and their being there would mean that the Hollow was larger than it thought it was, and that the map it had of itself — the map the bats drew, the map the workers read, the map the Listener heard in the composite tone — was incomplete. Was always incomplete. Was, in fact, growing.

The mushrooms would fruit a little more freely. Not in the farm — that was human work, human cultivation, a system within a system and not to be touched — but in the wild clusters on the walls, the pale translucent ones that reached toward the light with desperate patience. There would be one more cluster, or two, in a crevice where none had been before, and they would be slightly larger, slightly more vigorous, and the insects would find them, and the beetles would eat them, and the cycle would turn a little more easily, a little more generously, and no one would notice because no one counted the mushrooms.

And the Listener, sleeping in the carved alcove near the center of the chamber, would dream.

Not a dream with images — the Listener had no images to dream with, had never seen a sky or a tree or a horizon. The dream would be a sound. A new sound. The composite tone — pump, press, water, bats, stone — would incorporate a new note, a note that had always been there but that the Listener had not yet learned to hear, because it was so low, so deep, so slow that it required not ears but something more like patience, more like geology, and the Listener would hear it and would not know what it meant, would not be able to name it, would only know that the Hollow was *more* than the Listener had understood, that the composite tone had always contained more notes than the Listener had been able to distinguish, and that this was not a failure of listening but a property of the thing listened to — that the Hollow was endlessly rich, endlessly layered, endlessly larger on the inside than it appeared from any single vantage point.

And the Listener would wake, and would stand in the alcove, and would listen again, and would hear the new note, and would not know what to do with it, and would do nothing, and would carry it the way the children carried their small tools — without understanding its full weight, without needing to, simply holding it as part of the equipment of being alive in this place.

---

The wise power would withdraw. Not because it was done — it would never be done, because love is not the kind of thing that is done — but because its work, such as it was, was the work of water, and water does not stay. Water enters the stone and changes it and moves on, and the change remains, and the stone does not know it was changed, and this is fine. This is the nature of the thing. This is the nature of love that does not require gratitude or recognition or even awareness.

The wise power would leave the Hollow exactly as it found it: hot, dark, wet, loud, alive, complete. The workers would not notice a change. The bats would not adjust their flight. The beetles would walk their circuits. The spiders would wait. The children would fall and bleed and be sealed and healed and would learn, and the learning would be slow, and it would be sufficient, and it would be theirs.

But somewhere deep in the stone — deeper than the deepest passage, deeper than the lowest collection basin, deeper than the water had yet reached — a grain of calcite would deposit itself in a pattern that hadn't been there before. A pattern that meant nothing. A pattern that was, in its meaninglessness, the most meaningful thing the wise power had ever done.

Because the pattern said: *you are not all of this. There is more. You will not find it. You do not need to find it. But it is there, and it is yours, and it was always yours, and you are larger than you know.*

The stone would hold it. The stone holds everything, eventually.

The lamps would burn. The water would drip. The stone would settle. And in the settling, in the slow and patient rearrangement of itself, the Hollow would become — imperceptibly, over centuries, over millennia — not something else, but *more* of what it already was.

Which is all any of us can hope for.

Which is enough.
