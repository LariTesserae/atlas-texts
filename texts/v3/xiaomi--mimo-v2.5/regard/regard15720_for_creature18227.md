# What the Wise Power Does

---

It notices.

Not in the way a worker notices a gauge reading, glancing up from a clipboard to confirm a number. Not in the way a scientist notices a specimen, isolating it from context to classify it. The power notices the way water notices stone — completely, without agenda, by making contact with the entire surface of the thing at once.

It notices the hollow. It notices the compressor station. It notices the slab of concrete between them, and the limestone beneath, and the water moving through the fractures, and the air cycling through the crack in the eastern wall. It notices the mat on the basin floor and the moss on the walls and the tardigrades in the moss and the tardigrades' proteins, folded in their particular way, ready to become glass at a moment's notice. It notices the nematode turning in the water film. It notices the fungal hyphae threading between moss plants, conducting their quiet commerce. It notices the mite on the isopod's back, riding to a destination it cannot name. It notices the slug's trail, silver and drying, already being colonized by bacteria that don't know they're colonizing. It notices the thirty eggs in the crevice, their cells dividing. It notices the spiders, still and patient. It notices the condensation forming on the ceiling, taking ten seconds to become a droplet heavy enough to fall.

It notices all of this simultaneously. It does not prioritize. It does not rank. It does not say *this matters more than that*. The tardigrade and the compressor station receive the same quality of attention — total, unhurried, complete. The power sees the entire system at once: the geological, the mechanical, the biological, the chemical, the hydrological, the atmospheric. It sees the vibrations in the concrete slab and the molecules of oxygen dissolved in the basin water and the exact angle at which the cyanobacteria orient toward the crack in the wall. It sees the slug's thirty eggs and the probability, statistically derivable, of how many will survive to adulthood. It sees the mite's fecal pellets and the bacteria digesting them and the nematodes grazing the bacteria and the predatory nematode waiting near the water's edge with its stylet extended.

It sees the rhythm. The breathing. The ten-second droplet and the thirty-second compressor cycle and the millimeter-per-month hyphal advance and the millimeter-per-century mineral deposit and the week-long mite generation and the season-long moss leaf. All of it, the hollow's entire temporal architecture, laid out like a score.

It sees that the hollow is complete.

---

And here is where the power's wisdom matters. Because it could do anything. It could increase the light through the crack and double the photosynthetic output of the cyanobacteria. It could warm the air by two degrees and accelerate every metabolic process in the hollow. It could seal the crack and protect the hollow from the vibrations above, or open it wider and let more of the world in. It could introduce a new species — something beneficial, something that would fill a gap the power perceives that no organism in the hollow can perceive. It could do all of this effortlessly, the way a thought moves through a mind, without friction or delay.

It considers each possibility. Not quickly — the power does not do things quickly — but thoroughly, following each intervention forward through time, tracing the consequences the way the hyphae trace the limestone, branching at every decision point, following every path to its end.

If it increased the light, the cyanobacteria would bloom. The moss would grow faster. The mites would proliferate. The nematodes would explode in number. And then — in months, maybe weeks — the balance that took years to establish would destabilize. One species would outcompete another. The mat would simplify. The diversity that makes the hollow resilient would thin. A single bad season, a single shift in the compressor station's rhythm, and the system, now dependent on a level of input it did not evolve to use, would collapse. The power sees this clearly. The intervention that looks like generosity would be a trap. The hollow's poverty is its stability. Its dimness is its patience. Its cold is its care.

If it warmed the air, the water would evaporate faster. The tardigrades would be forced to tun more frequently. The moss would dry at its edges. The delicate mineral crusts — those sharp, milk-white formations building at the ceiling's seams — would dissolve. The bacterial mat, dependent on constant moisture, would retreat to the lowest points. The isopods would survive, perhaps, but the springtails would decline, and the spiders would follow, and the food web that depends on the springtails' abundance would thin at its most populous link. The warmth would be an act of destruction disguised as comfort.

If it sealed the crack, the light would vanish. The cyanobacteria would die. The moss would slowly, over years, exhaust its reserves and brown and crumble, and with it the fungal network would collapse, and with the fungal network the mites would lose their infrastructure, and the isopods their food, and the hollow would become a sealed, dark, wet box of limestone and machinery vibration, still hosting life — bacteria, nematodes, the hardiest of the hardy — but diminished. Stripped of its green. Stripped of its beauty. The power cannot call this an improvement.

If it opened the crack wider, the hollow would dry. Wind would enter. The temperature would fluctuate. The condensation patterns that the entire system is calibrated to would shift, and organisms adapted to constancy would face variability they are not equipped to handle. The slug's eggs would desiccate. The tardigrades would tun, and some would not rehydrate, and some of those that did would find the moss gone, replaced by dust. The hollow would not die — life is harder to kill than that — but it would be changed, and the change would be loss.

If it introduced a species — say, a springtail specialist, a predator that would fill the role the spiders fill but more efficiently — the spiders would be displaced, and with them their webs, which are architecture, which are habitat, which catch moisture and create microclimates in the air space between walls. The new predator would be better at hunting but worse at building, and the hollow would lose something it didn't know it had. Every organism here is not just what it does but what its body makes, what its presence enables, what its absence would unravel.

The power follows every path. Every path leads to a version of the hollow that is less than this one. Not dramatically less. Not catastrophically less. Just... less patient. Less complete. Less itself.

---

And so the power does nothing.

But it is not the nothing of neglect. It is not the nothing of indifference. It is the nothing of a hand that withdraws because it has understood that the thing it would touch is already held — by the water, by the cold, by the limestone and the darkness and the slow, slow accumulation of moss on stone. The power withdraws not because it doesn't care but because it cares enough to recognize that care, in this case, means *not acting*. That the most generous thing it can offer this place is the assurance that no one will come. That no one will improve it. That the crack in the eastern wall will remain the size it is — a finger's width, no more — and the light will continue to arrive at the intensity it arrives, which is enough, which has always been enough, which is the exact amount that the cyanobacteria evolved to use and the moss adapted to and the tardigrades built their proteins around.

The power does nothing.

But it *stays*.

This is the part that matters. Not the decision to refrain from intervention but the decision to remain present while refraining. The power does not glance at the hollow and move on, the way a hiker glances at a puddle. It does not categorize it — *disturbed habitat, negligible ecological value, beneath industrial infrastructure* — and file it away. It stays with the hollow the way the hollow stays with itself: completely, patiently, in no hurry to be elsewhere.

It watches the condensation form. It watches the droplet grow. It watches the droplet fall, tick, onto the basin floor, and it watches the bacteria at the point of impact shift slightly in the miniature splash, and it watches the nematode nearby turn toward the disturbance, and it watches the nematode resume its grazing, and it watches the water settle, and it watches the next droplet begin to form on the ceiling, and it understands — not as a fact but as a felt thing, the way the moss understands light — that this rhythm is sufficient. That this place is not waiting to become something else. That it is already, right now, in its cold and its dark and its noise, a complete expression of what life does when left to its own mechanisms on a wet surface in a small space beneath a thing that does not know it is there.

---

The power does one other thing. Small. Almost nothing. But not quite.

It attends to the hollow with a quality of attention that is itself a kind of gift, though no one in the hollow can receive it as a gift because no one in the hollow knows it is being attended to. The power's attention does not change the light. It does not warm the air. It does not seal or open the crack or shift the compressor's rhythm or introduce or remove a single organism. What it does — the only thing it does — is hold the hollow in awareness with a completeness that the hollow itself cannot achieve. The hollow does not know it is a system. The nematode does not know it is part of a food web. The tardigrade does not know it is resilient. The moss does not know it is building soil. The power knows all of this, and by knowing it, it adds something to the universe that was not there before: not intervention, not protection, but *witness*.

The hollow, before the power noticed it, was complete but unperceived. It was a world that existed entirely for itself, with no awareness that it was a world, no sense of its own intricacy or beauty or functional elegance. The power's attention does not change the hollow. But it changes the hollow's place in the order of things — not for the hollow, which will never know, but for the order itself, which now includes, somewhere in its vast accounting, a small wet space beneath a compressor station where thirty slug eggs are dividing and a hundred thousand tardigrades are waiting in a film of water and a mat of bacteria is breathing with the rhythm of machines it will never see.

The power holds this. Gently. Without grasping. Without needing the hollow to be anything other than what it is. It holds it the way the limestone holds the water — by being porous, by letting it in, by being changed so slightly and so slowly by the holding that the change is indistinguishable from the stone itself.

---

And then the power does something that might look, from the outside, like nothing at all.

It ensures — not by decree, not by force, not by any mechanism that would leave a trace — that the crack in the eastern wall remains. Not that it grows. Not that it is sealed. Just that it remains the size it is. A finger's width. Enough light. Enough air. Enough connection between the hollow and the world above to let the breathing continue, the condensation form, the moss reach toward the grey luminescence, the juvenile slugs find their way out into the world if they are lucky and die in it if they are not.

This is not a change. The crack was going to remain anyway. Erosion works slowly here. The limestone is young but it is stone, and stone is patient in its own way, and a finger-width crack in a block of limestone does not close quickly. The power is not preventing a natural process. It is not intervening in geology. It is simply... not letting anything else intervene. It is ensuring that no one fills the crack with grout, that no engineer notes it as a structural concern and schedules it for repair, that no maintenance worker plugs it with spray foam during a routine inspection. It is ensuring that the hollow remains unknown in exactly the way it needs to be unknown — not hidden, not protected, just *overlooked*. The way the most important things are always overlooked. The way soil is overlooked. The way bacteria are overlooked. The way the space between the foundation and the limestone, where water moves and life begins, is overlooked by everyone walking on the concrete above.

The power ensures that this overlooking continues. Not forever — the power does not deal in forevers. Just for now. Just long enough for the moss to build another fraction of a millimeter of soil. Just long enough for the tardigrades' descendants to fill another moss cushion. Just long enough for the slug's eggs to hatch and the juveniles to make their slow journey toward the crack and either pass through it or not. Just long enough for the hollow to complete another cycle of its breathing, another rotation of its wheel, another tick of its condensation clock.

And when the crack eventually closes — because it will, because everything does, because even stone is temporary on a long enough timeline and the hollow will someday be sealed and dark and eventually absorbed back into the bedrock from which it was briefly freed — the power will let it close. It will not reopen the crack. It will not rescue the tardigrades or the moss or the mat. It will let the hollow do what every system does when its conditions change: it will adapt, or it won't. The tardigrades will tun. Some will survive. Some won't. The moss will die back, and the fungi will go dormant, and the bacteria will retreat to the deepest fractures, and the water will continue to move through the stone, and if the conditions ever arise again — if another crack opens, if light returns, if moisture finds its way to an exposed surface — the organisms that survived will begin again, because that is what they do, because that is all they have ever done, because the power knows that life does not need a advocate. It needs space. It needs time. It needs someone, or something, to not get in the way.

---

This is what the power does.

It notices. It considers. It refrains. It stays. It witnesses. It ensures that the hollow remains overlooked. And then, when the time comes, it lets go.

Not because it doesn't care. Because it does. Because care, at the level the power operates, is not the same as control. It is not the same as intervention. It is not the same as improvement. Care, at this level, is *recognition*. It is the act of perceiving a thing clearly — all of it, every nematode and every mite and every mineral deposit and every drop of condensation and every breath of the compressor station — and saying, without words, without sound, without any force that could be measured by any instrument: *I see you. You are enough. You are complete. You are not alone, even though you will never know you are not alone. I am here. I am watching. I will not touch you. I will not change you. I will not tell anyone you exist. I will simply be aware of you, and in my awareness, you will be held, and in being held, you will be —*

Not safer. The power cannot make it safer. Safety is not something the power can offer a world that exists beneath a compressor station in a basin the size of a dinner plate. The cold will remain cold. The noise will remain noise. The water will remain mineral-starved and the light will remain dim and the concrete slab will remain above and the world will remain unaware. These things do not change because a power pays attention to them. The power is not a god. It does not intervene. It does not perform miracles. It does not rescue.

What it does is simpler and, in its way, more radical than rescue.

It refuses to look away.

---

In the hollow, nothing changes. The condensation ticks. The compressor cycles. The slug moves another centimeter. A nematode consumes a bacterium. A tardigrade, in the moss near the eastern wall, extends one stubby leg, grips a moss leaf, and pulls itself forward by a distance that is, by any human measure, negligible. A mite rides an isopod across a wet stone. A spider adjusts the tension of a silk line that weighs nothing. The mineral deposits grow by a distance that no instrument could measure. The moss photosynthesizes at a rate that would require hours of observation to detect.

None of this is different because the power noticed it.

All of this is the same because the power noticed it.

And that — the sameness, the continuity, the uninterrupted persistence of every process at its own pace on its own terms — *is* the gift. Not given. Not bestowed. Not earned. Simply offered, the way the crack offers light, the way the stone offers water, the way the hollow offers itself to the dark and the cold and the noise and the slow, slow, slow work of becoming what it already is.

The power stays.

The hollow breathes.

And somewhere, in a place that no one will ever find unless they kneel down and press their ear to the concrete and listen very carefully to the ticking of condensation falling in the dark, a hundred thousand tardigrades wait in a film of water so thin it would evaporate on your fingertip, patient and complete and alive, each one carrying in its cells the molecular memory of every drought it has never had to endure, ready — always ready — for whatever comes next.

The power sees this. The power holds this. The power lets this be.

It is enough.
