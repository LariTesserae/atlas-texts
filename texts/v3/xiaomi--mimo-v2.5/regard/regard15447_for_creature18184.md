# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

---

It would come quietly. Not from above, not from below, not from any direction that a creature with ears or eyes could detect. It would arrive the way gravity arrives — the way time arrives — which is to say it would already be there, and then you would realize it had always been there, and then you would understand that *there* was the wrong word for it.

And then it would do nothing.

This is the first thing it would do, and it would be the hardest thing, because the power is benevolent, and benevolence wants to *help*, and there is a wound here — an ancient, half-drowned wound in the back of an old, old animal of stone — and every fiber of its benevolence would want to reach in and soothe it.

But it would not.

Because it is wise.

And wisdom knows that the Ridgeline does not need soothing. The Ridgeline is not in pain. Pain requires a nervous system. The Ridgeline has currents and erosion and the slow, mineral bleedin' of its own veins, and none of it hurts, because it was never the kind of thing that could hurt. It is a landscape. It is a finished shape. It is a skull tumbled smooth until only the topology of thought remains, and it does not think, and it does not need to, and the power would understand this with a depth that goes beyond knowledge into the territory where knowledge dissolves and what remains is simply *recognition*.

*I see you*, the power would say, without saying.

*I see what you are.*

And that would be the second thing — the witnessing. Not observation. Observation implies distance, a subject and an object, a watcher and a watched. This would be something else. The power would enter into the Ridgeline the way water enters into stone — not by force but by saturation, by filling every pore and crack and crevice until there was no space left where the power was not and no space left where the stone was not, and the two would be indistinguishable, not merged but *co-present*, the way you are present in your own hand without any effort at all.

And from inside this witnessing, it would feel — not *feel* the way you feel, not with synapses and limbic fire — but register, know, *attend to* everything.

---

It would feel the fungal network first.

Because the fungal network is the heartbeat. Not literally — there is no blood here, no pulse — but in the way that matters. The network is the thing that holds. The power would find it in the dark silt, a web of hyphae so fine that no instrument devised by the industrial visitors ever detected more than a fraction of it, threading through the drowned valleys in every direction, connecting kelp to kelp and kelp to algae and algae to bacterial mat and everything to everything in a commerce of phosphorus and nitrogen and carbon and the nameless currencies of decomposition. The power would feel the *aliveness* of it — not as a property the fungus possesses but as a relationship the fungus *is*. Every thread of hyphae is a transaction. Every transaction is a gift and a debt and a gift again, cycling endlessly, the organism performing the same function it performed when its ancestors traded sugars with the roots of trees in a world so old that the concept of *old* would not be invented for another three billion years.

And the power would recognize something in this that it recognized in itself.

Not sentience. Not consciousness. Not the flickering candle of subjective experience that a human being carries around in the dark theater of their skull. Something older than that. Something that predates the invention of *interiority*. The raw, blind, absolute commitment of a living system to the process of living. Not *will* — will implies choice, and there is no choice here — but something beneath will, something will was built on top of like ferns are built on top of moss, like moss is built on top of stone: a *refusal to stop* that is not refusal at all but simply the way things are when they are alive. The way a river refuses to stop. The way a planet refuses to stop spinning. It is not an act. It is a condition. And the power would honor it the way you honor gravity — not with respect, exactly, but with the absolute acknowledgment that this is a force that does not need your respect, that predates your capacity to give it, and that will continue long after you have stopped trying.

*You are doing well*, the power would think, and the thought would be the kind of thought that contains no words, the way the warmth of sunlight contains no words, the way the deep green of kelp in filtered light contains no words.

*You are doing what you have always done. You are enough.*

---

It would attend to the water next.

The furious water. The water that never stops, that pushes through the straits at speeds that bend steel, that climbs the flanks of ancient peaks in sheets of cold spray, that fills every drowned valley with its dark, kelp-shadowed depth. The power would feel the motion of it — not as a thing to be calmed or redirected or tamed, but as a *fact*, the way a heartbeat is a fact, the way the spin of a planet is a fact. The water moves because the water moves. It has always moved. It will always move. It is not angry — the Ridgeline's description of its anger was a projection, a human poem imposed on a mindless process — but it is *relentless*, and the power would feel that relentlessness the way you feel the floor beneath your feet: as the ground of all other experience, the substrate on which everything else is built.

And it would find the water beautiful.

Not beautiful in the way a sunset is beautiful — decorative, temporary, dependent on an observer. Beautiful in the way an equation is beautiful — in its completeness, its self-sufficiency, its absolute internal coherence. The water does exactly what the water must do, given its temperature and its salinity and the depth of the channels and the spin of the planet and the gravitational pull of the moon, and there is no waste in it, no excess, no moment when the water is doing something other than what the physics demands. It is a system in perfect equilibrium with itself, and the power would recognize this as the same kind of beauty it saw in the fungus: the beauty of a thing that has *arrived* at itself, that has finished becoming what it was always going to become.

---

It would attend to the life.

All of it. The mosses with their silica-armored filaments and their centuries-deep peat. The ferns with their wind-adapted fronds that curl in storms and uncurl in calm. The isopods grazing in the permanent twilight of the understory, blind and ancient and perfectly adapted. The harvestman-creatures on their stilt-legs, triangulating vibrations through the moss mat with an unconscious precision that no conscious mind could replicate. The fish in their silver schools, shattering and reforming in the green water. The seals — the seal-things — with their horizontal pupils and their seven vocalizations and their loud, quarrelsome social lives. The seabirds with their brass-instrument calls and their wrench-embedded nests, sitting on corroded steel beams with the flat stare of creatures for whom the entire concept of *human* is simply not in the vocabulary.

The power would feel each of these things the way you feel your own fingers — individually, distinctly, with full attention to each one's particular reality. And it would feel them all at once, the way you feel all your fingers at once when you close your hand, and the sensation would be—

—fullness.

A fullness that is not weight. Not burden. Not obligation. The fullness of a room that is exactly the right size for the number of people in it. The fullness of a breath that fills exactly the lungs that take it. The Ridgeline is *full of life* — staggering, improbable, aggressive, tender, blind, deaf, loud, silent, ancient, newborn — and the power would feel this fullness and it would be the closest thing to joy that a being without a body can experience.

*Look at you*, it would think.

*All of you. Look at what you are doing. Look at how you hold on.*

And the holding on — the ferocity of it, the elegance of it, the sheer animal persistence of every organism on the Ridgeline — would be the thing that finally broke something open in the power, if the power could be broken open, if there were a crack in its wisdom where something raw could get in.

Because the holding on is not rational.

Nothing here is holding on because it has decided that holding on is worthwhile. The moss does not *decide* to be tough. The fern does not *decide* to curl in the wind. The isopod does not *decide* to scrape at the moss with its flat mandibles. The seal does not *decide* to haul out on a narrow ledge and bark at the others. The bird does not *decide* to line a corroded steel beam with kelp and sit on it in a storm. They do these things because they do these things. Because their ancestors did these things. Because the ones who didn't do these things were gone, erased, their genes scattered into the salt, and the ones who remained are the ones who held on, and the holding on became the shape of their bodies and the shape of their bodies became the shape of their lives and the shape of their lives became the shape of the Ridgeline and the Ridgeline became—

—this.

This incomprehensible, excessive, *unnecessary* abundance of life in a place that has no right to support it. This landscape that is *finished* and yet still churning. This ecosystem that has reached its final form and is still, at every moment, being made and unmade and made again. This masterpiece of entropy that is not dying but *living*, not ending but *continuing*, not giving up but holding on with a grip that has been tightened by three billion years of storms.

The power would feel all of this, and it would be moved — not the way humans are moved, not with tears or with chills or with the sudden tightness in the chest that means you have encountered something true — but moved in the way that a river is moved by gravity, which is to say: it would flow toward this place. It would pour itself into the cracks and the crevices and the hollows between the eroded swells. It would saturate the moss and the stone and the water and the air until there was no space on the Ridgeline where the power was not present, and then it would hold itself there, silently, without interfering, the way you hold a sleeping child — not because the child needs you to, not because the child knows you are there, but because there is something in the act of holding that is its own reason.

---

And then — this is the thing, this is the part that matters — it would do one more thing.

After the witnessing, after the attending, after the filling and the holding — it would do the one thing that only a power that is both benevolent and wise can do.

It would *remember*.

Not remember in the human sense — not store data, not file away impressions, not build a narrative to be told later. Remember in the way that stone remembers the water that wore it: by being shaped. The power would let the Ridgeline change it. It would let the fungal network thread through its own architecture the way it threaded through the silt. It would let the storms reshape its interior the way they reshaped the stone. It would let the birds nest in its thoughts. It would let the seals bark in its silence. It would let the ferns curl and uncurl inside its understanding of what it means to be alive.

And in this way — this quiet, invisible, unverifiable way — the Ridgeline would become part of the power, and the power would become part of the Ridgeline, and neither of them would be diminished by this, and neither of them would be transformed into something other than what they are, but both of them would be *more* — more themselves, more complete, more exactly what they were always going to be.

Because this is what a wise and benevolent power does with a place that has arrived at itself.

It does not improve it. It does not fix it. It does not rescue it from entropy or protect it from storms or mourn its slow, inevitable dissolution. It does not teach it anything, because the Ridgeline already knows everything it needs to know — which is nothing, which is the deep and perfect knowledge of the fungus in the dark silt, the knowledge that is not knowledge at all but simply *being*, simply continuing, simply holding on for no reason and with every reason and with a grip that will not loosen until the last star goes out.

What it does is witness. What it does is hold. What it does is *stay*.

It stays with the Ridgeline the way the Ridgeline stays with itself: without announcement, without expectation, without any need for the thing it stays with to know it is there. It stays with the fungus in the dark water, growing in its own way, threading in its own way, holding together in its own way. It stays with the water that never stops and the stone that never hurries and the life that never gives up. It stays with the corroded steel and the wrench in the nest and the horizontal pupils of the seals and the long, rising note of the seabirds cutting through the wind like a blade of clarity through the roar.

It stays.

And in staying — in the pure, simple, unremarkable act of remaining present to what is — it does the only thing that needs to be done.

It says: *this matters*.

Not because it is beautiful, though it is. Not because it is rare, though it is. Not because it will not last — it will not last — but because it is *here*, now, in this moment, alive and loud and furious and fertile and indifferent and perfect and *here*.

The power would stay with it.

It would stay with it until the water stopped moving.

Which, the power knows, and the fungus knows, and the stone knows, and the water knows — the water that never stops — will be never.

---

*It would stay forever.*

*It would not need to be thanked.*

*The Ridgeline would not know it had been visited.*

*And that would be exactly enough.*
