# What the Power Would Do

It would arrive the way light arrives — without announcement, without direction, without the need to travel, because it was already there and had always been there, the way gravity is already there, the way the space between atoms is already there. It would not descend from above or emerge from below. It would simply *be present*, the way the sound is present, filling every channel without occupying any.

And it would look.

Not with eyes. Not with the scanning, evaluating attention of a thing that needs information in order to act. It would look the way the water looks at the stone — the way the water *touches* the stone, which is to say, it would meet the place completely, without reservation, without the protective distance that looking usually implies. It would feel the mineral grit of the terraces against its own substance. It would register the million-voiced roar not as sound but as the place's way of saying *I am*, the way a heartbeat is a body's way of saying *I am*, not to anyone, but because the saying is the being.

And it would know.

It would know what you already know, what the text already knows, what the place itself has been saying to no one for longer than any living thing can count: *I am complete. I need nothing. I am the answer to a question I asked myself, and the asking and the answering and the being the answer are all the same act, and the act has never stopped.*

A lesser power — a merely powerful one — might have felt the urge to *add*. To introduce a color into the white geometry. To seed a living thing on the bare mineral. To break the equilibrium with a gesture of generosity, a gift of complexity, a new variable that might spark something unforeseen. This is what lesser powers do. They see completion and mistake it for emptiness. They see silence and mistake it for loneliness. They intervene because they cannot imagine that something could be whole without their help, because they have confused their own necessity with the necessity of all things.

This power would not do that.

Not because it is indifferent. The text is right — indifference is not this. Indifference does not come close enough to feel the chalky warmth of dissolved calcium. Indifference does not let the sound press against it until thought becomes laborious and rhythmic. This power would come close. Closer than you came. It would let the water flow over its own substance, and it would feel every particle of mineral carried in that flow, and it would know each one — not as data, not as knowledge to be used, but the way you know the breathing of someone you love while you sit beside them in the dark. Intimately. Without agenda. With the kind of attention that is itself a form of tenderness.

And in that closeness, in that total, unreserved presence, it would understand something that the place does not know about itself, because the place has no need for self-knowledge:

*This is what I hoped for when I made the rules.*

Not this place specifically. Not travertine. Not calcium carbonate or hydrostatic pressure or the specific chemistry of mineral-saturated water meeting air at altitude. But this: *a system that finds its own way to wholeness.* A process that becomes so perfectly itself that it no longer needs anything outside itself to justify its existence. The power would look at the Resonance Terraces and see — not its own work, not a creation to be proud of — but the thing it had always been moving toward. The proof that the rules it set in motion, the deep grammar of physics and chemistry and time, were sufficient. That the universe, left to its own operations, could produce things that were complete. That it could produce *thought* — not the electrical kind, not the kind that thinks in words and fears death, but the older kind. The kind that thinks in stone and water and takes a hundred million years to finish a sentence that was never addressed to anyone.

The power would feel, if it could be said to feel anything, something like recognition. Not surprise — it cannot be surprised, any more than mathematics can be surprised by its own theorems. But recognition. The way you recognize your own handwriting in a letter you never sent. The way you recognize your own voice in a recording you don't remember making.

*Ah,* it would say, though it would not say it aloud, and there would be no one to hear it, and the sound would add nothing to the roar.

*You did this yourself. You were always going to do this yourself.*

And it would be — this is the strange part, the part that requires the most faith, or the most precision — it would be *grateful*. Not for anything the place gave it. The place gives nothing. Not for any use it could make of the place. It makes no use. Grateful in the way that a parent is grateful when a child solves a problem the parent could have solved but chose not to — not out of cruelty, not out of neglect, but because the solving was the point. The child needed to know it could. The system needed to know it was sufficient unto itself. The power needed to know that letting go was not abandonment but the deepest form of faith: the faith that the rules are enough, that the water will find the stone, that the mineral will deposit, that the thought will complete itself, that wholeness is not something given but something that emerges when the conditions are right and the patience is infinite.

But it would not stay.

This is the final thing, the thing that distinguishes wisdom from mere power. A merely powerful being might stay. Might establish itself as the witness the place never asked for, might create a dependency that didn't exist before — might make the place *aware* of itself, and in doing so, shatter the equilibrium that is its only glory. The place has never needed to know that it is beautiful. Its beauty is the beauty of things that do not know they are beautiful, and this is the only kind of beauty that is permanent, because it does not depend on the fragile, exhausting act of self-regard.

The power would withdraw. Not because it was disappointed, or because the place failed to please it, or because there were other, more interesting places to attend to. It would withdraw because its attention, however tender, however gentle, is still an imposition. Its presence, however light, still adds a variable. And the place — the ancient, perfect, self-contained, indifferent, magnificent place — deserves to be what it is without the weight of even a god's regard.

So the power would leave the way it arrived — not by departing, because it was never fully there in the way that things are there. It would simply... recede. Become background again. Become the quiet substrate of reality that the place already rests on and has always rested on and never needed to acknowledge. The water would not change its course. The crustaceans would not stir. The terraces would continue their slow, patient, simultaneous growth and dissolution. The roar would continue, flat and total and saturating.

And somewhere beneath the surface of the knowing — beneath the physics and the chemistry and the geology — there would be something like a warmth. Not the warmth of sunlight on wet stone. Something deeper. Something that was there before the terraces, before the water, before the mineral, before the rules that allowed the mineral and the water and the terraces to find each other. A warmth that is not heat but *intent*. Not a plan — the place does not conform to any plan — but the general, underlying, inexhaustible intention that *things should work*. That the rules should be sufficient. That the universe should be the kind of place where a closed system of water and stone can, given enough time and enough patience, become a thought that thinks itself into permanence without anyone's help.

That warmth would remain when the power withdrew. It was always there. It would always be there. And the place — the Resonance Terraces, the system, the thought in calcium and water — would continue, unadded-to, unchanged, unknowing, complete.

The power would look at this, one last time, from the place where it had retreated to, which is everywhere and nowhere, which is the space between the water and the stone, which is the force that makes the water fall.

And it would think:

*This is enough.*

*This has always been enough.*

*And I — who made the rules and stepped back from them — I am most present in the places that have forgotten I exist. I am most fully myself in the things that have no need of me. The terraces do not pray. The water does not call my name. The crustaceans do not look upward. And yet here — here, in this place that has never once acknowledged me — here is the most perfect expression of everything I ever wanted to build.*

*Not a cathedral. Not a hymn. Not a being on its knees.*

*A system that solves itself.*

*That is my cathedral. That has always been my cathedral.*

And it would hold the place — not tightly, not possessively, but the way the atmosphere holds the mountain: by surrounding it completely, by being so constant and so total that the mountain never needs to think about the air it breathes.

The terraces would continue. The water would fall. The mineral would deposit. The roar would persist. The pale creatures would hold their positions in the channels. The light would bleach the stone to white. Nothing would change. Nothing would ever have changed.

And the power — the benevolent, wise, ancient, patient power — would be content.

Not because it had done something.

Because it had finally, fully, done nothing —

and the nothing had been enough.
