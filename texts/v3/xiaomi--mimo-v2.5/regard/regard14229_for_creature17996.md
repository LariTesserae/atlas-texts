# What the Wise Power Does

It watches for a long time before it does anything at all.

Not because it is uncertain. Because it recognizes what it is seeing, and it wants to be certain it understands the *scale* of what it is seeing — not the physical scale, which is impressive enough, but the temporal scale, the depth of the process unfolding on this raw new rock.

It watches the bacterial mats glow in the dark and calculates — not precisely, but intuitively, the way a parent knows the weight of a sleeping child — that this light is three billion years old. Older than oxygen. Older than the first cell that looked like anything at all. It watches the mite chewing through the fungal hypha in the dark moss and sees, superimposed like a double exposure, the soil that will exist in a hundred years and the meadow that will exist in a thousand and the forest that will exist in ten thousand, and it sees the mite at the center of all of it, blind and patient and sufficient.

It recognizes something here that it has seen in many places but rarely this clearly: a system that is not merely surviving but *generating*. Every organism present is not only living but building. The bacteria are building the first chemical gradients. The moss is building the water-holding architecture. The springtails are cycling what the moss builds. The mites are building soil from what the springtails leave behind. The ferns are building the conditions for whatever comes after ferns. Each layer creates the conditions for the next. Nothing is waiting for instructions. Nothing is waiting for help.

The power sits with this for what might be days or weeks, perceiving in ways that have nothing to do with eyes.

Then it notices the structures.

It notices them the way you notice a pebble in a shoe — not as a crisis but as an unnecessary friction, something that doesn't belong to the logic of the place. It reads them quickly: their materials, their age, their purpose. It understands immediately what they were for — extraction, measurement, the human impulse to quantify what they have disturbed. It feels no anger toward the humans who built them. Humans are young and frightened and compulsive and it has known them a long time. They build structures to hold their questions about the things they take apart. The structures are, in their way, a form of respect — clumsy, belated, but real. Someone wanted to measure the water chemistry. Someone wanted to know how fast the rock was subsiding. These are not bad instincts. They are the beginning of care.

But the structures are also dead weight. They are made of materials that do not participate in the place's own process — steel that rusts but does not become soil in any useful timeframe, concrete that leaches alkalinity into water that is still learning its own chemistry, cables and bolts and corrugated metal that the moss is already climbing but that will take decades to fully consume. The power calculates: without intervention, the moss will finish the job. The structures will be absorbed. The process will complete itself. It will just take longer than it needs to, and in the meantime the steel will leach iron into pools where the iron concentration is already precisely what the cyanobacteria need, and the concrete will create small alkaline islands in a landscape that is finding its own pH, and the pumping station's buried pipes will fracture and create underground channels that follow the logic of human engineering rather than the logic of the rock.

This is the friction. Not damage. Not even harm, exactly. But *interference* — a foreign grammar imposed on a sentence that is still being composed.

So the power makes its decision, and it is small.

It does not remove the structures. That would be too much — would leave holes in the moss, would rip the climbing liverworts from the walls they've already colonized, would treat the place as fragile when it is in fact the opposite of fragile. The power has learned this from watching: the thing that is most alive here is the process, not any individual component of it. You do not help a river by picking up its stones.

Instead, it does one thing. It reaches into the buried pipes — the ones still carrying pressurized water from deep below, the ones that are maintaining artificial flow patterns that the rock's own fractures would not create — and it *closes* them. Not violently. Not all at once. It lets the pressure drop over weeks, the way a heartbeat slows during sleep. The pumping stations lose power one by one, not from any visible cause, but from a gradual, organic cessation that looks, if anyone were watching, like simple equipment failure. The pipes seal. The artificial channels dry. The water that was being pulled up through boreholes finds its way instead through the rock's own fractures, the ones that basalt wants to use, the ones that cooling created and that the seeps have already begun to follow.

This is all. One action. The closing of pipes that should never have been open.

It is, the power knows, a very small kindness. The kind of kindness that doesn't announce itself. The kind that looks like nothing happened at all. In a week the moss over the dried pipes will be slightly less saturated, and in a month the springtails will colonize the new damp spots where natural seeps have strengthened, and in a year the bacterial mats will have found the new mineral concentrations and will glow in slightly different places, and in no time at all — geologically speaking — no one and nothing will remember that the pipes existed.

The power does not stay to watch the results. It doesn't need to. It trusts the mite. It trusts the moss. It trusts the three-billion-year-old light in the bacterial mats and the fern that is right now, at this moment, uncurling its fiddlehead in the mist and reaching for a light that has been waiting since before there were eyes to see it.

The power leaves, and the plateau continues exactly as it was continuing before — slowly, patiently, with the enormous and indifferent momentum of ten thousand small lives each doing the only thing they know how to do — and the water flows and the moss builds and the springtails spring and the mite eats and the basalt weeps and the future assembles itself, one dead cell at a time, from the materials that are already here, in the place that is already becoming what it is going to be.

The power's only true act was to notice.

The closing of the pipes was just a way of saying *I see you, and I am sorry about the pipes, and you don't need them.*
