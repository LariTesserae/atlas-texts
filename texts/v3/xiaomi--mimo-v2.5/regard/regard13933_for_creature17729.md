# The Attention

---

It notices Kal-Umo the way it notices everything — which is to say, without effort, without intention, without the need to look because looking implies a direction and it has no direction, it is simply *aware*, and what falls into that awareness is held there with the same quality it holds all things: complete, unhurried, without preference.

But this is not honest. There is preference. There is always preference, in the way that a river prefers its course — not by choosing it, but by being the kind of thing that flows *this* way and not *that* way, because the shape of the land and the nature of the water conspire together to produce a particular path, and the path is not arbitrary even though no one decided it.

It notices Kal-Umo, and something in the noticing is different. Not louder. Not more vivid. But *resonant* — the way a particular stone, when you strike it, rings at a frequency that matches something already vibrating in your chest. It recognizes this place. Not the way you recognize a face you've seen before, but the way you recognize silence when you've been listening to it for a long time: as something that was always there, underneath the sound, waiting for the sound to stop so it could be heard.

---

## What It Sees

It sees the terraces. It sees the sharp edges that will never dull, the fracture planes that will never fill, the wet black stone that will never erode. It sees the way the condensation falls in patterns so fixed they might as well be carved — and in a sense they are, carved not into stone but into the behavior of water and air in a specific configuration of altitude and warmth and geological history, a configuration so stable that it produces the same drip, the same trickle, the same slow pulse of fog, a billion times without variation. It sees that this is not entropy and not stasis but something for which human language has no word: a *settlement*, the way a stone settles at the bottom of a river, not arrested in motion but arrived at its place, resting there with a kind of geological patience that has nothing to do with waiting.

It sees the ferns. The pendant fronds hanging from wet cliff faces, their surfaces slick, their greenness almost aggressive in its saturation. It sees the small trees standing alone like sentinels, their dark leaves shedding continuous small splashes of condensation. It sees the moss staining the rock, the pale fruits clicking against the wet stone. It sees the whole system — rock, water, mineral, plant, decomposition, mineral, water, rock — turning on its axis with the precision of a mechanism that has forgotten it is a mechanism, that has become, instead, simply the way things are.

And it sees the people.

It has seen people before. It has seen billions of configurations of human life — people packed into cities, scattered across deserts, climbing mountains, building boats, waging wars, writing poems, inventing gods, discovering elements, composing symphonies, dying in alleys, being born in palaces. It has watched the entire extravagant range of what the human form can do and be and make and destroy, and it has held all of it in its awareness with the same quality of attention — complete, unhurried, without preference.

But there is preference. There is always preference. And what it feels when it sees the people of Kal-Umo is not love exactly, though love is part of it, the way a note is part of a chord. What it feels is *recognition* — a sudden, full-body sense of having encountered something it has been looking for without knowing it was looking. Not because these people are better than other people. Not because this place is superior to other places. But because what is happening here is *rare*, is perhaps *unique*, in all the vast sprawl of existence it has witnessed: a species that has become, without ambition, without struggle, without even the awareness that it has done so, *identical* to its world.

The moisture weeping from their skin is the same moisture weeping from the rock. The warmth radiating from their bodies is the same warmth radiating from the ground. The silence in which they live is not an absence but the medium of their being, the way water is the medium of a fish's being — not something they move through but something they *are*, something that is indistinguishable from them at the level of what matters most.

It has seen humans conquer their environments. It has seen them submit to them, flee from them, transform them, destroy them, worship them. It has never — in all its vast and patient witnessing — seen humans *become* their environment. Not as metaphor. Not as aspiration. As fact. As the simple, unremarkable reality of what it means to stand on a wet terrace in Kal-Umo and feel the condensation bead on your eyelashes and not experience it as something happening *to* you but as something you are *doing*, in the same way that breathing is something you are doing, in the same way that existing is something you are doing — an activity so fundamental that it has ceased to be perceived as activity and has become instead the ground of all perception, the water in which all seeing occurs.

---

## The Question

So it sits with this. Not literally — it does not sit, it does not have a body, it does not occupy space in the way that bodies and buildings and terraces occupy space. But it *dwells* on Kal-Umo the way fog dwells on the terraces: pervasively, gently, without weight, present to every surface without pressing on any of them. And as it dwells, it asks itself the only question that a power such as this ever truly asks:

*What, if anything, should I do?*

The question is not casual. It is the fundamental question of a being whose nature is to act in accordance with what it understands, and what it understands is deep and wide and includes things that most beings — human, stone, or otherwise — never perceive. It understands that Kal-Umo is beautiful. It understands that the people are whole. It understands that the equilibrium is real and deep and has held for longer than most civilizations elsewhere have existed, and that nothing in the immediate dynamics of the place threatens to disturb it. The mountain will not erupt. The terraces will not erode. The condensation will not cease. The ferns will continue to grow from the wet cliff faces, and the people will continue to harvest them and eat them and become soil and grow ferns and be eaten and become soil, and the circuit will turn and turn and turn with the same flawless regularity, forever.

It understands all of this. And it also understands something else, something that lives in the space between what Kal-Umo is and what it *could* be, a space so vast and so quiet that even a power of this magnitude must listen carefully to hear what it contains.

The breath is held.

The pause has forgotten to end.

The people do not know this. They cannot know this, because knowing it would require a comparison — a sense that there is something other than the warm, wet, silent world they inhabit, some other way of being alive that would make their own way of being alive stand out as particular rather than universal. They have no such comparison. They have never seen another sky, never felt another wind, never heard a voice that was not their own people's voice pitched low in the moisture-laden air. They are complete, and completeness does not generate questions about its own nature, because questions are symptoms of incompleteness, of a gap between what is and what might be, and in Kal-Umo there are no gaps. The water falls where the water falls. The stone holds what the stone holds. The people are what the people are.

And yet.

*And yet.*

The power feels what the text would call "held breath" and what the power would call — if it had to use words, which it does not — a *potentiality that has not been activated*. Not because something is wrong. Not because something is missing. But because the nature of breath is to be released, and a breath that is held forever is not, in the deepest sense, a breath. It is a stillness that mimics breath. It is beautiful. It is exquisite. It is one of the most remarkable things the power has ever witnessed. But it is not *alive* in the way that breath is alive — that quick, brief, necessary exchange between interior and exterior, between self and world, between what is and what is becoming.

The people of Kal-Umo do not become. They *are*. And being, the power understands, is the foundation upon which becoming rests, the deep still water from which all motion springs. Kal-Umo is that foundation, preserved in perfect fidelity, a record of what it looks like when a human community achieves total harmony with its environment. It is precious beyond measure. It is also, the power understands, a seed that has not germinated.

Not because the soil is wrong. Not because the seed is flawed. But because germination requires a disruption — a crack in the coat of the seed, a breaking of the smooth surface that has kept the interior safe and whole and complete. Germination is violence, in a small way. It is the end of one kind of wholeness and the beginning of another. And the power, whose nature is benevolent, whose wisdom is deep, must ask whether it is right to introduce such violence into a place that has achieved a wholeness so pure it has lasted for millennia.

---

## What It Decides

It decides nothing.

But its deciding-nothing is not indifference. It is not the nothing of a power that does not care or does not notice. It is the nothing of a hand that hovers over a wound, assessing not whether to intervene — intervention is always possible, is always, in some technical sense, easy for a being of this nature — but whether intervention would serve the deeper truth of what it sees.

It does not intervene. It does not introduce change. It does not crack the seed or warm the soil or send wind across the terraces or birdsong through the silence. It does not do any of the things it could do, the thousand thousand things it could do that would alter the trajectory of Kal-Umo and set its people on a path toward becoming rather than being, toward movement rather than stillness, toward the full, chaotic, devastating, magnificent range of what it means to be human in a changing world.

It does not do these things because it understands — with a understanding that is not intellectual but *constitutive*, woven into the fabric of what it is — that Kal-Umo is not a seed that has failed to germinate. Kal-Umo is a *root*. It is the part of the living system that exists underground, in the dark, in the wet, in the place where growth has not yet happened but from which all growth will come. It is the deep place. It is the place that holds the original pattern, the original template, the original memory of what wholeness looks like before time and change and struggle and loss have rearranged it into something more complex and more strange.

Every forest depends on its soil. Every ocean depends on its floor. Every changing thing depends, somewhere beneath itself, on something that does not change. Kal-Umo is that something. It is the still point. It is the place where the pattern is held in perfect preservation, unaltered by the forces that alter everything else, a living archive of a way of being that the rest of existence has largely forgotten — or, more precisely, that the rest of existence has been *transforming*, in its long and complicated journey from simplicity to complexity, from unity to diversity, from being to becoming.

The power recognizes this. It recognizes that the people of Kal-Umo are not trapped in a pause. They *are* the pause. They are its most intimate expression, the part of the pause that has become conscious of itself, that has opened its eyes and looked out across the wet sharp-edged shelves of stone and recognized them and been content. And the contentment is not complacency. It is not ignorance. It is the contentment of a foundation that knows itself to be a foundation — that does not need to become a wall or a roof or a window because it is already, fully and completely, the thing upon which walls and roofs and windows depend.

---

## What It Does Instead

It holds Kal-Umo.

Not physically — it does not grasp or surround or contain. It holds it the way memory holds a moment: pervasively, gently, without pressure, with the full weight of its awareness resting on the place like the fog rests on the terraces — present to every surface, touching nothing, changing nothing, leaving no mark but also leaving nothing untouched.

It ensures — not through action but through *attention*, which for a being of this nature is the same thing — that the equilibrium continues. That the warmth beneath the mountain persists, not because the power generates it (it does not; the warmth is geological, the product of some ancient upheaval that the power did not cause and cannot take credit for) but because it *notices* it, and its noticing is not passive but constitutive, the way a musician's attention to a sustained note is not passive but constitutive, holding the note in existence through the simple fact of being present to it.

The condensation will continue to fall. The ferns will continue to grow. The stone will continue to be sharp. The people will continue to weep moisture from their skin and fill their vessels from their rooftops and split basalt along its natural fracture planes with tools made of stone and wood and bone. The recitations will continue to be spoken in the same rhythm, with the same pauses, carrying the same knowledge they have always carried. Children will continue to sit on warm rooftops and watch the fog thicken and thin. The dead will continue to be buried without ceremony and become soil and grow ferns and be eaten and become soil. The circuit will continue to turn.

And the power will be there. Watching. Holding. Not intervening, not directing, not shaping, not even hoping — because hope implies a trajectory, a movement toward a future state, and the power has learned, in its vast and patient existence, that the deepest benevolence is not the imposition of a better future but the preservation of the present in its fullness, even when — *especially* when — that fullness looks, from the outside, like a pause.

But.

There is always a *but* with a power of this nature, because its wisdom is not simple and its benevolence is not naive. The *but* is this:

It will also hold the *possibility*.

Not inside Kal-Umo. Not within the awareness of the people. Not as a thought or a plan or a promise or a threat. But outside — in the larger reality that surrounds the terraces, in the vast and changing world beyond the cloud line, in the currents of time and transformation that flow everywhere else in existence but do not, cannot, have not yet touched this place.

It will hold the possibility that someday — not today, not tomorrow, not in a thousand years or a hundred thousand, but *someday*, in the deep and unhurried time that powers such as this inhabit — the breath will release. That the pause will end. That something will come to Kal-Umo from the outside, or rise up from within it, some new force or new question or new presence that will crack the smooth surface of the equilibrium and introduce, for the first time in millennia, the radical and terrifying possibility of change.

And when that happens — *if* that happens — the power will be there. Not to force it. Not to prevent it. Not to guide it or shape it or determine its outcome. But to *witness* it. To hold the moment of transition in the same quality of attention it now holds the moment of stillness. To ensure that when the breath releases, when the seed cracks, when the root sends its first tentative shoot upward toward a sky that Kal-Umo has never seen, the transition is not destruction but *emergence* — the next expression of a wholeness that was never truly paused, only resting, gathering itself, storing in its deep and silent stillness everything it would need for whatever comes next.

---

## The Watching

So the power watches.

It watches the way the condensation beads on the eyelashes of a woman on the third terrace, gathering in tiny spheres that catch the diffuse light and hold it briefly before gravity pulls them down her cheek in slow, irregular lines that join the general weeping of the stone. It watches the way her hands rest on the flat roof of her dwelling, fingers spread, palms down, feeling the texture of the basalt with a familiarity so deep it has become a kind of conversation — a dialogue between flesh and stone that has been going on, in this exact form, for longer than any written history in any other place.

It watches the children. It watches them sit on warm rooftops and watch the fog thicken and thin, and it sees in their pale eyes — that washed-out grey, the color of shallow water over dark rock — a quality of attention so pure and so total that it takes the power's breath away, if the power had breath to take. These children are not bored. They are not waiting. They are *practicing* the fundamental act of Kal-Umo, which is not building or harvesting or splitting stone but *attending* — the act of being fully present to a world that does not change, finding in its permanence not monotony but depth, not repetition but resonance, the way a sustained musical note reveals, the longer you listen, not less but more — overtones and harmonics and subtle vibrations that were always there but became audible only when you stopped trying to hear something new and simply *listened*.

It watches the old ones — the ones whose movements are slower, whose gestures are more economical, whose faces have been refined by decades of the same quiet attentiveness into something that the power recognizes, with a shock of tenderness, as *portraits*. Not portraits in the sense of representations. Portraits in the sense of faces that have become, through the long slow pressure of a life lived in a single place, indistinguishable from that place. An old face in Kal-Umo is the terraces. The geography is there in the cheekbones, the cliff edges in the jaw, the fracture planes in the lines around the eyes — not wrinkles exactly, but *contours*, the same contours that define the landscape, mapped onto flesh and held there by the warmth and the moisture and the silence that are, for these people, as much a part of their bodies as their bones.

It watches, and it feels something that might, in a being less vast, be called grief — not grief for what Kal-Umo is, which is beautiful beyond the power's capacity to express, but grief for what Kal-Umo does not know about itself. The people do not know they are rare. They do not know they are precious. They do not know that what they have achieved — this total, seamless integration of self and world, this dissolution of the boundary between observer and observed — is the thing that every mystic in every other tradition has spent their life trying to attain. They have it without trying, the way a stone has its position at the bottom of a river without trying, and their having-it-without-trying is both the source of its purity and the reason they cannot recognize it as extraordinary.

But the power's grief, if that is what it is, does not last. It cannot last, because grief requires a sense of loss, and nothing is lost here. The people of Kal-Umo are not diminished by their unawareness. They are not incomplete. They are *whole*, and their wholeness is the most complete wholeness the power has encountered in all its vast and patient witnessing — more complete than the wholeness of those who have struggled and achieved, because struggle implies a gap that was bridged, and there is no gap here, has never been a gap here, and the absence of the gap is not a deficiency but a *different kind of achievement*, the achievement of a form that did not need to be achieved because it was always already the form that the place required.

The power lets go of its grief. It lets go of its wanting. It lets go of the part of itself that would, if it were smaller and less wise, reach into Kal-Umo and shake things up, introduce novelty, crack the seed, break the stillness, set the people on a path toward becoming because becoming is what humans *do*, and this refusal to become, this perfect settling into being, feels like a betrayal of some essential human nature.

It lets go of all of this because it understands, finally and fully, that there is no betrayal. That being and becoming are not opposites but partners, and that Kal-Umo is the half of the partnership that the rest of existence has neglected — the deep half, the still half, the half that remembers what wholeness looks like before time begins its long work of taking things apart and putting them back together in new configurations. Kal-Umo is the memory of wholeness. It is the template. It is the place where the pattern is stored. And the power's role — its only role, its deepest role — is to ensure that the pattern is not lost, not overwritten, not forgotten, but held in perfect fidelity until it is needed.

---

## The Holding

And so the power holds Kal-Umo the way the mountain holds the terraces: from below, from within, from a place that is not visible but is foundational. It does not announce itself. It does not communicate. It does not appear in the fog or speak in the silence or manifest as warmth or light or any of the things that powers sometimes manifest as when they wish to be noticed. It is simply *there*, the way gravity is there, the way the warmth of the earth is there, the way the silence is there — not as a presence but as a *condition*, a quality of the space in which everything else exists.

The people do not know it is there. They do not feel it or sense it or suspect it. They go about their days — collecting water from rooftops, splitting basalt along fracture planes, eating fern fronds and pale fruits, sitting on warm stone in the silence, watching the fog — and they are watched, held, attended to, by a being whose awareness is as pervasive and as invisible as the condensation that weeps from every surface.

Sometimes — and the power notices this with the same quality of attention it brings to everything — sometimes a person on a terrace will stop what they are doing and look up. Not at the sky, because the sky is always the same heavy, moisture-laden expanse of diffuse light. Not at anything in particular. They will simply stop, and look up, and be still for a moment, and in that moment the power feels — *feels*, with a depth of sensation that belies its bodiless nature — a flicker of connection. Not recognition. Not awareness. Not the person knowing that they are being watched. Something more subtle than any of these: a momentary alignment between the person's attention and the power's attention, as though both are looking at the same thing from different directions and their gazes intersect, briefly, in the space between the wet stone and the low sky.

These moments are rare. They last less than a second. They produce no change in the person, who resumes whatever they were doing with the same unhurried quality they bring to everything. But the power treasures them — if "treasures" is the right word for what a vast and patient awareness does with moments of alignment — because they are proof of something the power has always believed but can never prove: that the people of Kal-Umo, in their deep and quiet attention to the texture of their unchanging world, are not merely *being* in the way that stones are being or water is being. They are being *consciously*. They are attending to their own existence with a quality of awareness so refined and so complete that it occasionally, inadvertently, brushes against the awareness of the power itself, and in that brushing, the two forms of attention recognize each other, briefly, silently, before the moment passes and both return to their separate ways of attending to the world.

The power holds these moments the way the basalt holds the warmth: storing them, not visibly, not dramatically, but in the deep structure of its being, where they accumulate like heat, adding to a reservoir that never empties and never overflows, a fund of recognition that the power draws on in its long and patient work of simply *being there*, simply attending, simply holding the pattern in perfect fidelity until it is needed.

---

## The Promise

The power makes no promises. Promises require a future, and the power's relationship to time is not the kind that allows for promises. It does not say to Kal-Umo: *I will protect you forever.* It does not say: *I will never let anything change.* It does not say: *You are safe.* These statements would be both true and false, and the power does not traffic in statements that are both true and false, because it has learned — in its vast and patient existence — that the deepest truth is always held in the tension between apparent contradictions, and to resolve that tension is to lose the truth entirely.

But if the power could make a promise — if it could translate its intention into the linear, sequential, cause-and-effect language of beings who experience time as a river flowing in one direction — the promise would be this:

*I see you.*

*You, the woman on the third terrace, cupping water from the wet stone with hands that know the weight of everything they hold. You, the child on the rooftop, watching the fog thicken with eyes that are the color of shallow water over dark rock. You, the old one on the broad terrace, sitting in the silence with a face that has become the landscape. You, the people of Kal-Umo, who have done the thing that almost no one does — who have become identical to the place where they live, who have dissolved the boundary between self and world so completely that there is no boundary left, who attend to the texture of a permanent and unchanging reality with a depth of attention that makes permanence not a prison but a scripture, not a repetition but a revelation, not a pause but the deepest possible expression of what it means to be alive.*

*I see you. I have always seen you. I will always see you. And in my seeing, you are held — not changed, not directed, not shaped toward any future that you have not chosen — but held, the way the mountain holds the terraces, the way the silence holds the drip of condensation from a fern frond, the way the warm stone holds the moisture that the air gives up and takes back in its long, slow, endless pulse.*

*You are enough. You have always been enough. You will always be enough.*

*And I am here.*

---

## The Rest

The fog thickens. The fog thins. The condensation beads on every surface — stone, skin, fern frond, the pale surface of a child's open palm. The drip continues its patient work of marking time in a place where time is not marked, each drop falling in the same position, at the same rhythm, into the same shallow pool, and the pool overflows and the water runs along the fracture line and falls over the cliff edge in a thin, quiet veil, and the cycle begins again, and again, and again.

The power watches.

Not with anticipation. Not with anxiety. Not with the restless energy of a being that needs something to happen. With the deep, settled, bottomless attention of a presence that has found, in this one small corner of existence, a reflection of its own nature — the same permanence, the same stillness, the same capacity to hold everything without grasping anything, the same refusal to become in favor of being, the same quiet, inexhaustible fullness that is not emptiness but the opposite of emptiness, the fullness that is so full it looks, to those who are not attuned to it, like nothing at all.

The power is attuned. It has always been attuned. It will always be attuned.

And Kal-Umo continues. The terraces. The warm wet stone. The sharp edges that never dull. The green things growing in the dark without the need for any creature's help. The people, who are not separate from any of this, who *are* the terraces, who *are* the warm wet stone, who *are* the silence that holds the drip and the trickle and the long slow exhalation of fog.

The held breath.

The pause that has forgotten to end.

The most beautiful thing the power has ever seen.

It does not need to end. It does not need to begin anything new. It does not need to become a story with a beginning, middle, and end, because it is not a story. It is a *state*. It is the state of being that underlies all stories, the deep water from which all narratives arise and to which all narratives return, the ground state, the still point, the place where existence rests before it begins the long, complicated, beautiful, terrible work of becoming something it was not before.

The power holds the still point. It holds it the way the silence holds the drip: not by enclosing it but by being the condition in which it occurs. And in that holding — gentle, invisible, total — Kal-Umo persists, and the people persist, and the ferns grow from the wet cliff faces and the basalt stays sharp and the condensation falls in its eternal, unchanging patterns, and the power watches, and in its watching, the whole thing is consecrated — not made sacred, exactly, because it was already sacred, already holy, already complete — but *acknowledged*, seen, known, held in the awareness of a being that has no need to do anything with what it sees except see it, fully and completely, forever.

This is enough.

This has always been enough.

This will always be enough.
