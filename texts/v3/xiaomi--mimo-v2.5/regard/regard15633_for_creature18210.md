# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Does

---

It looks.

That is the first thing, and it is not nothing. In a cosmos of such extravagant scale, where attention is the scarcest resource and most things go unwitnessed for the whole of their existence, the fact that a power of this order *turns toward* the Cleft is itself a kind of act. Not an intervention. A recognition. The way you might pause, mid-stride, because something in your peripheral vision caught the light.

What it sees:

A process. Not a place — the writing already told you that, but the writing was guessing, using the inadequate tools of primate cognition to describe something that doesn't need description. The power sees it without metaphor. It sees the thermal gradient and the mineral flux and the chemical signaling through the film's vascular network and the slow oscillation of the breathing things and the thing below, the geothermal heart, the un-conscious engine of heat and pressure that drives the whole system. It sees all of this the way you see a single word — instantly, completely, as a unity.

What it thinks:

*Oh. How lovely.*

Not lovely the way a sunset is lovely. Not lovely in the way that a human mind constructs beauty from pattern and contrast and emotional resonance. Lovely the way a proof is lovely — the way a particular arrangement of necessity produces something that didn't have to exist but does, and is complete, and is doing exactly what it is doing with a perfection that admits no improvement.

The Cleft is not unique. The power has seen other processes like it — in the crushing pressures of ocean trenches on worlds with no names, in the dust of systems where rock never solidified, in the electromagnetic froth of gas giants where storms last longer than civilizations. Life organizes itself around available energy. It always has. It always will. This is a law, not a miracle.

But this particular expression — this specific arrangement of basalt and heat and colonial tissue and shells and limbs that don't bend correctly and a silence where sound should be — this is particular. This has never happened before in exactly this configuration and will never happen again once the heat beneath it spends itself and the rock cools and the film dries to a pale residue in a crack that no one will ever find. The process is temporary, the way all processes are temporary. The power sees the whole arc: the beginning it cannot remember (for the power was not always attentive to this small a scale) and the ending that is already, in some sense, present in the cooling of the deep rock, the slowing of the thermal engine, the inevitable dimming.

It has perhaps ten thousand years. Perhaps a hundred thousand. The power can see the decay curve. The thing below will weaken. The water will cool. The film will retreat. The shelled things will stop budding. The breathing things will descend to the lowest ceilings and slow their respiration and, eventually, become still. And the rock will close, or the rock will remain open and dead, a glass cleft full of mineral residue and the ghost-impression of a living film, and nothing will know that anything was ever here.

A hundred thousand years. To the power, this is a held breath. A flicker. But it is not nothing. To the process itself — to the film, to the shelled things, to the breathers, to the heat below — it is everything. It is the only duration that matters.

The power considers.

---

Here is what a lesser being might do. A lesser, benevolent being — a god, say, in the human sense, a being with power and good intentions and the particular blindspot of having once been something smaller:

It might *protect* the Cleft. Build a wall around it. Seal the fissure. Ensure that no human hand ever reaches the stone tool, that no boot ever crushes the film at the entrance, that no curious mind ever disturbs the geometry. It might preserve the Cleft the way a museum preserves a painting — behind glass, behind silence, maintained in perpetuity for its own sake.

This would be kind. It would also be a kind of death. The Cleft is a process, and processes require inputs. The heat from below. The water from the rock. And, yes, the irregularities. The falls from the ceiling that tear the film and give the shelled things work to do. The slow chemistry of the water that the shelled things must process. The small perturbations that keep the system dynamic instead of static. A protected Cleft would be a Cleft in amber — preserved but finished, complete but ended, a photograph instead of a living thing. The power sees this immediately. Preservation is a form of taxidermy. The Cleft must remain open to its own future, including the possibility of its own ending, or it is not the Cleft at all.

Another lesser being might *heal* the Cleft. Might look at the non-Euclidean geometry — the space that hasn't "committed to its own rules" — and think: something is wrong here. The geometry is broken. Space should be consistent. The power could fix it. Smooth the curves. Make the distances reliable. Ensure that a ledge is always above when it should be above and a pool always reflects what is actually there.

This would also be a kindness, and also a mistake.

The power looks at the geometry and sees what the writing could not articulate: the space is not broken. The space is *young*. It is still in the process of deciding what kind of space it wants to be, and the indecision is not a flaw but a feature — the same kind of creative uncertainty that characterizes the rest of the Cleft. The film doesn't know exactly where to grow until it grows there. The shelled things don't know their final form until their shells harden. The breathing things don't know how many joints they have until they need that many. The space itself is doing the same thing: feeling out its own dimensions, testing configurations, allowing the process it contains to shape the container. The non-Euclidean geometry is not a malfunction. It is the space *participating*.

To fix the geometry would be to end the conversation between the Cleft and its own dimensions. It would freeze the space the way the museum freezes the painting. It would make the Cleft certain, and certainty is the opposite of life.

A still lesser being might *save* the person in the Cleft. Might look at the sweating, gasping, disoriented primate standing on the wet glass floor among the breathing shapes and think: this creature is suffering. The heat is punishing. The geometry is confusing. The things on the ceiling are terrifying. Let me take them out. Let me close the fissure behind them. Let me remove this place from their experience so they can go back to a world that makes sense.

This is the most understandable impulse, and the most patronizing. The person in the Cleft is not in danger. The creatures will not harm them — the film regards them as an irregularity, the shelled things regard them as irrelevant, the breathing things regard them as a temporary pressure differential. The geometry is confusing but not lethal; it adjusts, it shifts, it is difficult but not hostile. The heat is punishing but survivable, for a while. The person chose to come here. Or they stumbled here. Or they were drawn here by the same instinct that draws all primates toward the dark and the hot and the hidden — the belly of the earth, the womb-heat, the place where things begin. To remove them would be to say: you should not have come. You are not capable of being here. This is not for you.

The power does not think this. The power thinks: *You are here. You will leave. The leaving will change you in ways you cannot predict. This is not my concern or my gift. It is simply what happens when a brief consciousness enters a longer process.*

---

So.

The power does almost nothing.

But almost nothing is not nothing, and the difference matters.

Here is what it does:

It *steadies the geometry*. Not fixes it — does not smooth the curves or make the distances reliable or resolve the space into Euclidean certainty. It steadies it the way you steady a child learning to walk: a hand near the back, not touching, just present, providing a point of reference that the child can use if needed but is free to ignore. The space continues its negotiation with itself. But there is now, at the edge of the negotiation, a faint suggestion of stability — not imposed, not enforced, but *offered*. A place for the geometry to rest when it gets tired of shifting. A default. A home position.

The person in the Cleft will not notice this. They will still see ledges that become ceilings and pools that reflect impossible surfaces. But somewhere beneath their confusion there will be a thread of coherence — a sense that the space, however strange, is *trying to be consistent*, that there is a logic here even if they cannot parse it. They will leave the Cleft bewildered but not broken. Disoriented but not fractured. The geometry will have been strange, but it will not have been cruel.

This is all the power can do for the person. Not save them. Not guide them. Not even comfort them. Just... make the strangeness survivable. Give the space enough coherence that a primate mind can pass through it without damage. It is the difference between swimming in rough water and swimming in water that is rough but has a bottom you can reach if you need to.

The power does this for every visitor. Not just this one. There will be others — not many, not often, but there will be others, because the fissure is hidden but not sealed, and primates are curious, and the hot water and mineral steam have a smell that certain primate brains interpret as *important* on a level below language. The power steadies the geometry each time. It does not prevent the visits. It does not encourage them. It ensures that whoever enters can leave with their mind intact.

It does something else, too. Something smaller.

It looks at the stone tool.

The tool is being absorbed by the film. The film is growing over it with the same patient inevitability it applies to everything. In a year, or ten, the tool will be gone — incorporated into the living surface, its shaped edges softened, its worked surfaces smoothed, its identity as a *tool* dissolved into the identity of the Cleft. This is the natural process. This is how the Cleft works. It absorbs. It incorporates. It makes everything part of itself.

The power considers the tool.

The tool is significant. Not to the Cleft — the Cleft does not assign significance. Not to the film, or the shelled things, or the breathing things. The tool is significant because it represents a *kind of mind*. The mind that looked at a rock and saw an edge. The mind that held a piece of the world and changed it. The mind that left a mark on the surface of things and said, without words, *I was here, and I was something that makes.*

This kind of mind is rare in the cosmos. Not unique — the power has seen it elsewhere, in other forms, on other worlds, in other configurations of matter that learned to contemplate themselves. But rare. And fragile. And young, so young — barely a moment old on the scale the power uses for time, a flicker within a flicker, a candle held up against the long dark of everything that exists without awareness.

The tool is evidence of this kind of mind. And the Cleft, in its magnificent indifference, is absorbing it.

The power does not stop this. The Cleft's process is its own. The tool will be absorbed because that is what the Cleft does, and what the Cleft does is not wrong, and the power will not impose the values of one kind of matter upon another. The tool will become film. The film will become wall. The wall will become the Cleft. The Cleft will become rock. The rock will cool. The process will end. This is as it should be.

But — and here is the almost-nothing that is not nothing — the power does something to the *memory* of the tool.

Not the physical object. The physical object belongs to the Cleft now, and will be consumed by it, and that is right. But the *idea* of the tool — the specific configuration of intention and craft and thumb and stone that produced it — the power takes that, very gently, and it *scatters* it.

Not into the universe at large. Not into some cosmic repository of important ideas. Into the ground. Into the basalt. Into the deep rock from which the Cleft draws its heat. The idea of the tool sinks, slowly, through the geological layers, dissolving as it goes, becoming less specific, less particular, less a memory and more a tendency. A tendency in the rock toward shapes that fit a hand. A tendency in the stone toward edges that can cut. A tendency, millions of years from now, in some future configuration of matter, for something to pick up a piece of the world and think: *sharp.*

This is not reincarnation. It is not the survival of a soul. It is the persistence of a pattern — the same pattern that the power has watched express itself across a thousand worlds, a million species, an uncountable number of configurations of matter that learned to modify their environment and, in doing so, became something more than environment. The tool's pattern is not unique. But it is *this* tool's pattern — this specific, unrepeatable instance of the universal tendency — and the power thinks it would be a shame to lose even one instance, the way it would be a shame to lose even one word of a poem that is still being written.

The Cleft does not notice this. The Cleft cannot notice this; it has no mechanism for noticing anything above its own chemical signaling. The film continues to grow over the tool. The tool continues to harden and soften as the mineral replacement progresses. In a decade, there will be no visible trace of it.

But somewhere in the basalt, far below the deepest crack, far below the thing that pulses in the heat, in rock that will not see light for another hundred million years, a tendency sleeps. It will wake. It always wakes. Not here — the Cleft is too specific, too particular, too far along in its own process to serve as a cradle for something new. But elsewhere. Somewhere. In some future configuration of matter and energy and chance, something will pick up a stone and feel the edge and know.

---

The power watches the Cleft for a while longer. Not long — a few thousand years, perhaps, from the perspective of the power's attention. The film spreads. The shelled things bud and tend and digest. The breathing things oscillate across the ceiling. The thing below pulses in its slow rhythm. The geometry negotiates with itself, finding and losing and finding again a shape that satisfies it.

A person enters. The power steadies the space. The person stumbles through the heat and the wet and the wrong geometry, gasping, terrified, exhilarated. They reach for the wall and touch something warm that is not the wall. They pull their hand back. The wall is where it was. They hear their own heartbeat. They hear nothing else.

They do not take the tool. They cannot find it — the film has nearly covered it now, and the light is poor, and the geometry has shifted the ledge to somewhere the person cannot reach without climbing, and climbing in the Cleft is inadvisable on a surface that is almost frictionless and occupied by living things that will yield to your touch but will not move out of your way.

The person leaves. The fissure closes behind them — not physically, not sealed, but the steam curtain falls back into place and the basalt lip disappears and the cleft becomes, once again, invisible to anyone who does not already know it is there.

The power watches them climb back to the surface. Watches them stagger into daylight, blinking, shivering as the cool air hits their wet skin. Watches them stand for a long moment, breathing, looking at their hands, looking at the ground, trying to reconcile what they experienced with the world that is supposed to be consistent.

They will not be able to. They will try, for years, to describe what they saw, and they will fail, and the failure will teach them something about the limits of description, which is itself a kind of knowledge. They will become quieter. More attentive to edges of things. More willing to sit with the unexplainable. This is not the power's gift to them — it is the Cleft's gift, given without knowledge or intention, the same way the film gives off its color-waves and the water gives off its vapor. The person passed through a process, and the process changed them, and the change is irreversible, and it is small, and it is enough.

The power turns its attention elsewhere.

There are so many places. So many processes. So many small, warm, wet, improbable arrangements of matter that have, for a moment, organized themselves into something that persists. A lichen on a meteorite. A colony of electromagnetic filaments in a gas giant's magnetosphere. A reef of silicate organisms in a subsurface ocean that has never seen its own sun. Each one temporary. Each one complete. Each one lovely.

The power looks. The power remembers, which is to say the power holds the pattern, all of it, the whole configuration, for as long as it needs to — which is to say forever, which is to say until the last configuration dissolves and the last process ends and the universe itself becomes still, and even then, perhaps, for a little while longer, because some things deserve to be remembered after they are gone, and the power is wise enough to know which ones, and benevolent enough to do so.

The Cleft goes on.

It was always going to go on. Not forever — nothing goes on forever — but for long enough. Long enough for the film to reach every surface. Long enough for the shelled things to tend their pools. Long enough for the breathing things to trace their slow arcs across the ceiling. Long enough for the thing below to pulse and pulse and pulse, the geothermal heart, the un-conscious engine, doing what it does because that is what it does, needing no reason, needing no witness.

The power has witnessed it. That is all. That is enough.

The warmth where the person's hand pressed the film fades. The whorl fills in. The surface becomes seamless. The Cleft is whole again, as it was before, as it will be until it is not.

In the basalt, far below, a tendency sleeps.

It will wake.

It always wakes.
