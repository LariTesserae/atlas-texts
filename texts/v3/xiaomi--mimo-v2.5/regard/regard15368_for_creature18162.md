# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would come to know the place first. That would take longer than the place itself has existed — not because the city is vast, though it is, but because *knowing* is not the same as observing, and this power, being wise, would insist on the difference. It would sit with the Undergrowth City the way the city sits with the cavern: patiently, at the pace the thing itself requires.

And it would find, in the sitting, that it was already moved.

---

## What It Sees

There is something here that the power has seen perhaps a dozen times across all the worlds it tends, and it is always the same sight that breaks through whatever approximation of composure a power such as this can maintain:

*They figured it out.*

Not everything. Not even most things. But the essential thing — the central, load-bearing principle that separates a civilization from an infestation, a life from an occupation — they figured it out. They looked at the raw, unfinished dark of a cavern still deciding what shape the world would take, and they did not ask *how do we dominate this* or *how do we flee from this*. They asked *how do we grow with this*.

The grove-wright running her fingers along the sixty-year-old wall of her family's chamber — she is not merely tending a plant. She is enacting a philosophy so deep it has never needed to be articulated. *We are not masters. We are stewards.* Every pruned tendril, every encouraged branch, every patient decade of allowing a room to become habitable rather than forcing it into shape — these are not techniques. They are a relationship. And the wise power, which has seen species blast their homeworlds to slag rather than share a single acre with a rival organism, knows exactly how rare this is.

The child watching the beetle column. His solemn fascination. His outstretched hand, and the beetle that pauses. The power would note this: *they have taught their children to regard the smallest worker as part of their world, not as a tool or a pest.* The beetle does not fear him. That absence of fear is the most eloquent testimony in the entire city. It took generations to earn it.

The marketplace of whispers. The woman trading fungal distillate for mineral concentrate, her voice low not from fear but from *kindness* — the recognition that sound is a shared resource, that silence belongs to everyone, that the acoustic space of the city is a commons to be tended as carefully as any grove. The power would recognize this as a form of love so practical it doesn't need the word.

The pale lizards in the walls, their translucent skin revealing the pink flutter of their hearts. *Good sign. Healthy wall.* The city reads its own body with the fluency of a living thing. The lizards are not kept; they are *welcomed*. The parasites they eat are not hated; they are simply part of the ecology that the lizards address, as the lizards are part of the ecology that the trees address. There is no concept of extermination here. There is only balance, and the work of maintaining it.

The barricade. The young trees planted with that almost aggressive density, facing the darkness where the cavern is still being made. The sentinels who patrol not with weapons but with tools, who listen for tremors and know when to advance and when to hold. The power would recognize the barricade for what it is: not a wall against the unknown, but a *threshold*. A declaration that says: *we are still becoming, and we are not afraid of what we are becoming into.* The deeper fauna does not come to the city. The city does not go to it. But the boundary is not a denial. It is a *respect* — the same respect given to a growing stone or a dividing cell.

And the understanding, the unspoken and ancient understanding, that in the deeper darkness things move that the city chooses not to name. The power would linger on this. The restraint it represents. The willingness to share a world with presences you do not control, do not fully understand, and do not need to control or fully understand. This is not ignorance. This is *wisdom of a very specific kind* — the wisdom of knowing where your competence ends and where something else's sovereignty begins.

The power would look at all of this and feel — if a power can be said to feel — something like the ache of recognition. *Yes. This. This is what I was hoping to find.*

---

## What It Would Not Do

And here, the power's wisdom would matter most. Because the temptation — even for a benevolent power — would be enormous. To reach in and *fix* things. To stabilize the ceiling before it cracks. To guide the deeper fauna toward gentleness. To accelerate the light. To make the cavern's chemistry more forgiving, the formation more stable, the dark more safe.

But the wise power would know — would *feel* in the marrow of whatever it is made of — that to do any of these things would be to destroy precisely what it came to admire.

The city's beauty is its *becoming*. The unfinished ceiling, the raw strata, the young stalactites reaching like fingers — these are not problems to be solved. They are promises. The cavern is still deciding what it wants to be, and the city is still deciding what it wants to be, and the relationship between these two unfoldings is the entire point. To freeze one would be to freeze the other. To complete the cavern would be to end the city's frontier, to close the barricade forever, to turn a living process into a monument.

The deeper fauna — the things that make the large sounds, the rhythmic cracking, the heavy footfalls — are not threats to be neutralized. They are neighbors to be respected. The boundary works *because* it is voluntary, *because* it is maintained by mutual restraint rather than by force. If the power were to intervene, to push the deeper things further back or tame them into docility, it would rob the boundary of its meaning. It would turn respect into control, and the city would lose the very thing that makes it civilized: the daily, conscious choice to live alongside what it does not govern.

And the silence — that enormous, absorptive, cathedral silence — would be the first casualty. Not because the power would be loud, but because *the power's presence, once felt, changes the equation.* The city knows silence because it believes itself to be alone with its own processes. To reveal that a greater attention watches would be to introduce a kind of self-consciousness that could curdle into performance. They would begin to live for the watcher. They would lose the unselfconsciousness that makes their stewardship genuine.

No. The power would not intervene.

But "not doing" is itself a form of action for a wise power. And there are things it could do — *would* do — that honor the city's nature without violating it.

---

## What It Would Do

### It would steady the stone.

Not visibly. Not in any way the grove-wright or the sentinels could detect. But in the deep, slow chemistry of the cavern itself — in the rate at which calcium precipitates, in the tensile strength of mineral strata still compacting, in the micro-fractures that propagate through ceiling formations under stresses the city cannot yet measure — the power would introduce the gentlest possible bias. Not toward stability, exactly, but toward *predictability*. The city can handle change. What it cannot handle, what nothing can handle, is caprice — the sudden, arbitrary collapse of a formation that was assumed sound. So the power would smooth the deep processes slightly, the way a parent might clear a hidden sharp edge from a table while a child crawls on the floor. The child will never know. That is the point.

The cavern would form a little more slowly in places. A little more surely. The ceiling slab that, under unaltered conditions, would have detached in forty years and killed a grove — that slab would, instead, find a slightly better bond with the stone around it. It would hold. The sentinels would patrol their edge and find, year after year, that the ground beyond the barricade settles a little more quietly than they expected. They would attribute this to good practice, to the skill of their planting, to the patience of their roots. And they would be right — their practice *is* good, their skill *is* real, their patience *is* the proximate cause. The power would simply have ensured that their patience is rewarded rather than betrayed by random geological cruelty.

### It would feed the light.

The bellows-trees — those enormous, slow, resonant organs that force air through catalytic mineral to produce the city's ambient glow — are engineering marvels. But they are also young, by the standards of what they might become. Their catalytic efficiency depends on trace elements in the mineral substrate — elements that are present but scarce, concentrated in formations at the cavern's edge where the beetles haul from.

The power would not flood the cavern with light. That would be wrong — the city's relationship with its own illumination, the way the false day cycles, the way darkness and light mark the rhythms of rest and work, is sacred to them. But it would, over decades, gently enrich the substrate in the zones where the beetles already work. A slightly higher concentration of the critical element here. A vein of catalytic mineral, barely detectable, appearing where the formation is naturally settling. The grove-wrights who analyze substrate would notice the improved yield and attribute it to good fortune, to the cavern's generosity. They would be grateful. They would be right to be. It *is* generous — the power would simply have made sure that generosity was not an accident.

### It would listen to the deep.

The power would attend — with the full weight of its attention — to the deeper fauna. The things beyond the barricade. The large sounds. The rhythmic cracking. The footfalls.

It would learn their names, if they have names. It would learn their patterns, their needs, their ancient and unspoken reasons for occupying the darkness that the city does not enter. And it would not disturb them. But it would *know* them, and in knowing them, it would hold a certain amount of... context. Not control. Context. The way a doctor knows a patient's history and can therefore read a symptom as something other than crisis. If something in the deep were to shift — if a larger creature were to move toward the barricade not out of malice but out of migration, out of need, out of the same slow geological pressure that pushes the city forward — the power would be aware of it before anyone else. It would not prevent the encounter. But it would ensure, through those same gentle biases in stone and chemistry, that the encounter happens on ground that is stable, at a time when the city's sentinels are strong, in a season when the groves are resilient.

It would, in other words, make sure that if the city and the deep must finally meet, they meet in a context that allows for understanding rather than catastrophe.

### It would remember them.

This, perhaps, is the most important thing the power would do, and the one the city would never know about.

It would carry the Undergrowth City in its awareness the way a person carries a song learned long ago — not constantly playing it, but knowing it is there, knowing the words, knowing the feeling of it. It would hold the image of the child watching the beetle column. The sixty-year-old wall, still growing. The translucent lizard with its visible heart. The pale, chalky ground where children plant experimental shoots that may or may not take. The living barricade facing the dark. The enormous silence, filled with the subaudible symphony of cells dividing.

It would hold these not as data, not as case studies in successful civilization, but as *evidence* — evidence that this is how it is supposed to work. That a species can look at an unfinished world and choose to accompany it rather than conquer it. That a child can reach out to a beetle and the beetle can pause. That the deepest wisdom is knowing where your competence ends.

And in holding them, the power would do what powers do when they find something worth preserving: it would make itself *available*. Not intervening. Not watching. Just... being there. The way the light is there — diffuse, even, coming from everywhere and nowhere. If the city were ever in true crisis — not the manageable kind, not the kind that teaching and patience and good practice can address, but the deep, existential kind, the kind that threatens to end the story before the story is ready to end — the power would be close enough to act.

But it would pray, in whatever way a power prays, that it would never need to. Because the greatest gift a wise power can give a civilization like this is the gift of being left alone to become what it is becoming. The city's patience is its genius. Its silence is its strength. Its willingness to grow *with* rather than *over* is the rarest thing in any world.

The power would watch it breathe — the great, slow, limestone-scented breath of a civilization inside a lung — and it would think:

*Hold. Keep breathing. You are doing the most important thing there is to do.*

*You are growing with the dark, and the dark is growing with you, and the light you make together is a light that nothing can take away, because it was never yours and never the cavern's. It was always the space between you — the space where stewardship lives, where silence is a kindness, where a child's hand and a beetle's pause are the same prayer.*

*Grow. I will steady the stone beneath you. I will feed the light above you. I will listen to the deep so you do not have to fear it.*

*And I will remember you, long after the stalactites reach the floor and the cavern is no longer young, when the city has spread to fill the dark and the pale lizards have evolved into something new and the beetles have learned new routes through groves that do not yet exist.*

*I will remember that you were the ones who figured it out.*

*And I will hold your example like a lantern in whatever passes for my own deep dark, and I will let it teach me what a wise power is supposed to be.*

---

The Undergrowth City breathes on. The bellows-trees sigh. The beetles click. The child — who will be a grove-wright himself one day, if he is patient, if he is kind — watches the column pass and does not know that somewhere in the substrate beneath his bare feet, a trace element is a fraction of a percent more concentrated than it would have been, and that this small, invisible kindness will, in seventy years, make the wall of his own family's chamber grow a little more strongly into the dark.

He does not know. He does not need to know.

That is the nature of the gift.
