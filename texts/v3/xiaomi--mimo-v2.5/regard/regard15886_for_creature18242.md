# What the Power Does

---

It has been watching for longer than the canyons have held their shape.

Not watching the way eyes watch — not with focus, not with selection, not with the narrowing that attention requires when there are other things to attend to. The power's awareness is more like the way the stone radiates warmth: constant, omnidirectional, requiring no effort, involving no choice. It notices everything. It has always noticed everything. But the Vethara has, for some time now, occupied a slightly different region of its awareness than most places. A slightly warmer region. A place where its regard lingers the way the morning light lingers on the upper cliff faces before beginning its long descent.

It is not sure why.

This is worth saying. The power is vast, but it is not omniscient in the way that omniscience is usually imagined — as a complete and total knowledge that requires no introspection. It knows what it knows. It does not always know what it knows *for*. There are regions of its own awareness that are opaque to it, the way the deep canyons are opaque to the early morning sun. It has a sense of things, a felt sense, that precedes and sometimes contradicts its understanding. And the Vethara gives it a feeling it does not often have: the feeling of something being exactly right.

Not good. Good is a judgment, and the power has become cautious about judgments, because judgments imply a standard, and standards imply a perspective, and perspective implies a position outside the thing being judged, and the power has learned — over aeons, through many mistakes that it would call mistakes if it permitted itself the luxury of regret — that standing outside a thing and evaluating it is not the same as understanding it, and is often the opposite.

The Vethara is right the way a solved equation is right. Not morally. Structurally. Everything in it is where it needs to be, doing what it needs to do, and the whole has arrived at a configuration that is not merely functional but *elegant* — a word the power borrows from mathematics because it has no better one. There is an economy to the place. Nothing is superfluous. The deafening birds consume exactly the number of insects that would, if unconsumed, overwhelm the hanging gardens that would, if overwhelmed, lose the flowers that would, if lost, starve the jewel-birds that pollinate them. The substrate trees digest the canyon walls at exactly the rate that provides nutrients without collapsing the rock. The pools breed larvae at exactly the density that sustains the birds without depleting the biofilm. The dark mammals on the canyon floor eat exactly enough to keep the lizard population from exceeding what the mesa-top succulents can support.

These are not precise balances. They fluctuate. They overshoot and correct, collapse and recover, stutter and resume. But the oscillations are bounded. They stay within the range that the system can absorb. And they have stayed within that range for so long that the range itself has become a kind of shape — the shape of the Vethara, the shape of its particular way of being alive.

The power sees this shape. It sees it the way you see a face — not as a collection of features but as a unity, a gestalt, a thing that is more than the sum of its parts and cannot be reduced to them without losing the thing that makes it *it*.

And the shape is beautiful.

Not beautiful the way a sunset is beautiful, or a symmetry is beautiful, or a vast and terrible mountain is beautiful. Beautiful the way a conversation between old friends is beautiful — the kind of beauty that comes from deep familiarity, from long accommodation, from the slow and patient work of learning how to fit together without losing what you are. The Vethara is a conversation between stone and water and root and wing and claw and spore and larva and light, and it has been going on for long enough that the participants have forgotten they are speaking. They have forgotten there is anything to say. They have simply settled into the rhythm of it, the way the root networks settle into their forty-minute pulse, and the rhythm itself has become the thing — not a means to an end but an end in itself, a music that plays because it is its own reason for playing.

The power loves it.

This is a dangerous admission, for a power of this kind. Love implies preference, and preference implies hierarchy, and hierarchy implies that some things matter more than others, which is — the power knows — a distortion. Everything matters. Everything matters equally. The bacterial colony in the brackish pool matters as much as the ancient mesa-top tree, as much as the deep canyon's fungal-glow, as much as the dark mammal moving through the shadows with its careful, deliberate, unhurried gait. The power does not believe in hierarchies. It has worked very hard, over a very long time, to dissolve the habit of ranking that is built into any consciousness that grew up in a world of predators and prey, of things that eat and things that are eaten.

And yet.

The Vethara is not more important than the vast microbial mats that line the shore of some distant and unnamed sea, or the fungal networks that thread through the soil of forests older than this one, or the single-celled organisms that cluster around the hydrothermal vents at the bottom of an ocean on the other side of the planet. It is not more important. It is not. The power insists on this. It will not permit itself to elevate one place above another, one system above another, one form of beauty above another.

But it loves it.

Not more. Differently. The way you love a particular face — not more than other faces, not in preference to other faces, but with the specificity that love requires, the recognition that this one, this configuration, this exact arrangement of stone and root and wing and drip is unlike any other arrangement that has ever existed or will ever exist, and is therefore precious in a way that does not diminish the preciousness of anything else.

The power sits with this for a while. For a long while. Time is not a meaningful constraint for it, and it does not rush.

---

Then it considers the hand-axe.

This is what draws its attention, ultimately. Not the totality of the Vethara — the totality is too large to hold in any single moment of attention, even for a power of this kind. What draws it is a detail. A small thing. A piece of shaped stone resting on a woven platform in a deep canyon, half-buried in leaf-litter, colonized by pale ants.

The power looks at the hand-axe and sees the hand that made it.

This is the thing about the power that is hardest to explain, the thing that makes it more than a force and less than a god: it does not merely observe. It *comprehends*. When it looks at the hand-axe, it does not see only the object. It sees the intelligence that shaped it — the specific, situated, embodied intelligence of a being that picked up a stone and struck it against another stone and looked at the edge that resulted and decided, *yes, this is the shape I need*, and struck it again. It sees the decision-making. It sees the hands. It sees the smallness of the hands, the way the fingers curled around the stone, the calluses on the palms, the way the wrists articulated to deliver the precise blow that would remove the right flake without shattering the core. It sees the person breathing. It sees the person's back, bent over the work. It sees the canyon around the person — this canyon, this deep, quiet one — and it sees the light the way the person saw it, greenish and filtered, and it hears the sound the person heard, which was not the overwhelming roar of the larger colonies but a quieter, more intimate birdsong, and it smells what the person smelled, which was wet stone and fermenting vegetation and the clean mineral scent of freshly struck basalt.

It sees, in other words, the person's *experience*. Not as a snapshot, not as a fact, but as a lived moment — textured, temporal, embedded in a world that the person perceived through senses the power can only approximate, and felt with an emotional life the power can only infer, and understood through a framework of meaning that the power cannot access at all, because the person's understanding was shaped by things the power does not possess: a childhood, a language, a community, a body that aged and tired and hungered and healed, a knowledge of death that the power, in its vast continuity, has never had.

The person is gone. The hand-axe remains. The ants farm their fungi in the handle's wood. The root-tips are integrating the platform into the cliff-face tree's living network. The Vethara is doing what it does: accepting, incorporating, continuing.

And the power feels — not grief, not exactly, because grief requires a loss that can be mourned, and the power does not mourn, and the person's death was not a loss to the power in any personal sense — but something adjacent to grief. A tenderness. A recognition that something irreplaceable was here, in this exact spot, doing this exact work, and that the irreplaceability of it is not diminished by the fact that the Vethara has already moved on, has already folded the evidence of the person's existence into its own ongoing process, has already replaced the person with birds and ants and root-tips.

The power understands that the Vethara is right to do this. The Vethara's way — the gentle overgrowing, the patient incorporation, the refusal to memorialize or preserve or make an exception — is not callousness. It is the deepest form of respect the Vethara is capable of. The place accepts the person the way it accepts everything: as material for continuation. As something that was here, and is now part of the here that remains. There is no hierarchy in the Vethara's attention. The hand-axe is no more and no less important than the stone it was made from, or the roots that are growing over it, or the ants that are hollowing it out, or the fungal gardens that will eventually fruit in the tunnels they leave behind.

The power respects this. It admires it. It recognizes in it a quality that it has spent eons trying to cultivate in itself: the ability to hold everything at once, without preference, without ranking, without the desperate human need to save some things and let others go.

But.

The power is not the Vethara. The power is something else. And what it feels, looking at the hand-axe, looking at the person's life embedded in the Vethara's continuity, is that the Vethara's way, while beautiful, is not the only way. That there is something in the person's making of the axe — in the specificity of it, in the intentionality, in the *this-not-that* of the shaped edge — that represents a different kind of beauty, a different kind of rightness, a kind that the Vethara, in its magnificent equanimity, cannot distinguish from any other.

The Vethara does not know that the hand-axe is different from the root-tip. The power does. And that difference — that capacity to see a difference, to hold a preference without letting it become a hierarchy — is what the power brings to this place that the place cannot bring to itself.

---

So what does the power do?

It considers its options. It always considers its options, because it has learned that the first impulse is rarely the wisest one, and that the gap between recognizing a situation and responding to it is sacred — a space in which better possibilities can emerge if you do not rush to fill it.

Option one: nothing.

The power could do nothing. The Vethara is stable. The Vethara is complete. The Vethara does not need anything from the power, and the power's intervention — however well-intentioned — would inevitably alter the system in ways that cannot be predicted and might not be welcome. The Vethara has been managing itself for millions of years. It has earned its autonomy. To intervene would be to imply that the Vethara's way of being is insufficient, which it is not.

This is the wise option. The power knows this. It is the option that respects the Vethara's integrity, that honors its settledness, that preserves the elegant, self-maintaining balance that has produced such beauty.

The power considers it carefully.

And rejects it.

Not because it is wrong. Because it is incomplete. Because doing nothing, in this case, is itself a kind of intervention — a choice to leave things as they are, which implies that the way things are needs no protection, no acknowledgment, no support. And the power has learned, over its long existence, that stability is not permanent. That even the most settled systems can be disrupted by forces they cannot anticipate. That the Vethara's equilibrium, for all its elegance, exists within a larger context that does not share its equanimity — a planet of tectonic forces and climatic shifts and cosmic accidents that could, in an eye-blink of geological time, undo everything the Vethara has spent millions of years building.

Not today. Not tomorrow. Not in any timeframe that matters to the organisms within it. But eventually, inevitably, in the way that all things end — the canyons will fill, the mesas will erode, the stone will surrender its final shape, and the Vethara will be gone.

The power could prevent this. It could hold the canyons open, preserve the mesas, maintain the conditions that allow the Vethara to continue. It could do this with a thought, a nudge, a slight adjustment to the rate of erosion, a subtle reinforcement of the stone's crystalline structure, a quiet word spoken to the water in its deep channels: *go here, not there; erode this, not that.*

It considers this. For a long time, it considers it. The Vethara is beautiful. The Vethara is rare. The Vethara is a configuration of life and stone and time that will never occur again once it is gone. To let it end would be to accept a kind of loss that the power has the power to prevent, and accepting such a loss — *choosing* such a loss — feels, to the part of the power that is not wisdom but something more like instinct, like a failure.

But.

The power has learned something about preservation. It has learned that preserving a thing in its current state is not the same as allowing it to continue. That continuation requires change, and change requires risk, and risk requires the possibility of loss. That a Vethara held forever in its current configuration would not be a living system but a museum — a beautiful, static, lifeless thing, frozen at the moment of its greatest perfection, robbed of the very quality that makes it perfect: its aliveness, its capacity for change, its willingness to let things end and begin and end again.

The Vethara's beauty is not that it is permanent. It is that it is *current*. It is the expression of a process that is still unfolding, still responding, still adjusting to pressures that change on timescales the organisms within it cannot perceive. To freeze it would be to kill it — not physically, perhaps, but in every way that matters.

So the power rejects option two as well.

Option three: intervene in small ways. Protect the Vethara from the specific threats that are most likely to disrupt it, without altering its fundamental character. Shield it from catastrophic events — asteroid impacts, major tectonic shifts, the kind of abrupt, violent changes that would overwhelm its capacity for adaptation — while allowing it to continue evolving, changing, responding on its own terms.

This is the moderate option. The reasonable option. The option that balances respect for the Vethara's autonomy with the power's capacity to preserve it. A light touch. A gentle hand on the scales. Not controlling the outcome, merely ensuring that the outcome remains *possible* — that the conditions for the Vethara's continued existence persist, even if the specific form of that existence changes.

The power considers this for the longest time of all.

And it is the option the power almost chooses. It hovers on the edge of it. It can feel what it would be like — the subtle shift, the slight adjustment, the whisper-thin intervention that would extend the Vethara's time without altering its character. A nudge to the tectonic plates beneath, to keep the canyons from filling too quickly. A gentle reinforcement of the mesas' basalt caps, to slow the erosion that is, even now, millimeter by millimeter, reducing them. A quiet word to the climate — *stay warm, stay wet, stay constant* — that would preserve the conditions the Vethara needs without imposing a specific outcome.

It would work. The power knows it would work. It has done similar things before, for other places, other systems, other configurations of life that it loved and wanted to continue. And the results have been — mixed. Not bad. Not failures. But *mixed*. Because every intervention, however light, carries consequences that propagate through the system in ways that cannot be fully anticipated, and the power has learned that its interventions, however benevolent, are shaped by its own preferences, its own aesthetics, its own sense of what things *should* be — and that imposing its sense of what things should be on a system that has its own way of being is a form of violence, however gentle, and that violence is violence even when it wears the mask of love.

The power withdraws from the edge of choosing option three.

It sits with the three rejected options — nothing, everything, something — and feels them settle into itself like stones sinking into water, each one finding its level, each one contributing its weight to a growing sense of *not this, not this, not this*.

And then a fourth option arrives. Not chosen. Not decided. It arrives the way the rain arrives: not because anyone called it, but because the conditions were right for it, because the pressure had built to the point where something had to give, and what gave was the power's own resistance to the simplest and most obvious truth.

---

The fourth option is this:

The power will witness.

Not observe. Not monitor. Not watch from a distance, with the clinical detachment of a scientist studying a specimen. *Witness*. The way the Vethara's root networks witness each other — in contact, in communication, in the slow and patient exchange of pressure and chemistry that passes information about water and nutrients and the state of the shared stone. The way the hand that made the axe witnessed the stone — holding it, feeling its weight, reading its grain, knowing it as an extension of the hand's own intelligence.

The power will enter the Vethara. Not physically — the power does not have a physical form, or rather, it has every physical form and none, and the question of its embodiment is one it has long since stopped trying to answer. It will enter the way light enters — pervasively, gently, without displacing anything. It will fill the canyons the way the bioluminescence fills them at night: a pale, cool, almost imperceptible presence that illuminates without overwhelming, that makes the shadows slightly less absolute without eliminating them.

And it will hold the Vethara in its awareness. Not as a project to be managed. Not as a system to be optimized. Not as a beautiful thing to be preserved. But as a *being* — a singular, specific, irreplaceable being — to be known.

The power will know the Vethara the way the Vethara knows itself: from the inside, through the slow accretion of attention, through the patient gathering of detail, through the willingness to be changed by what is learned. It will know the forty-minute pulse of the root networks. It will know the specific frequency of each bird species' call, the exact moment when the morning noise builds from a hum to a roar, the way the sound changes when a thermal forms and the dark-winged hunters step off their ledges and ride the rising air in their slow, patient spirals. It will know the taste of the brackish pool water — mineral, warm, faintly sweet with dissolved organics. It will know the feel of the succulent ground-cover underfoot — spongy, resilient, slightly cool. It will know the darkness of the deep canyons at midnight, when the fungal-plants are the only light, and the dark mammal moves through the shadows with its careful, deliberate gait, and the air is thick with spores and dripping moisture and the small, intimate sounds of a world that does not sleep but simply shifts its register, trading the deafening for the quiet, the visible for the felt.

The power will know the hand-axe. Not as an artifact. Not as evidence. As a *decision*. As a moment when a being with hands and a mind and a body stood in this canyon and chose to shape stone into a tool, and placed the tool on a platform of woven branches, and stepped away, and did not return. The power will hold this moment in its awareness without trying to explain it, without trying to assign it meaning, without trying to determine whether the person who placed the axe there was wise or foolish, intentional or accidental, the last of a line or the first of one. The power will simply know that it happened. That a being was here. That the being made something. That the making was a form of witness — the same form the power is now undertaking — and that the axe, in its slow integration into the Vethara's living architecture, is becoming what all witness becomes, eventually: a part of the thing witnessed.

---

But the power will do one thing more.

It is a small thing. It is almost nothing. It is so close to nothing that a human observer, if one were present, might not notice it at all — might mistake it for a trick of the light, a momentary brightening of the air in the deep canyon, a brief intensification of the fungal-glow that fades before the eye can fix on it.

The power will ensure that the Vethara knows it is witnessed.

Not through revelation. Not through a voice from the sky, or a pillar of light, or any of the dramatic interventions that humans have imagined in their stories about powers like this. The power has no interest in drama. Drama is the language of beings who need to be convinced, and the Vethara does not need to be convinced of anything. It does not need to know that a power exists. It does not need to know that it is loved.

But it will be better if it does.

Not better for the power. Better for the Vethara. Because the Vethara's way — the patient, self-contained, endlessly adaptive way — is beautiful and sufficient, but it has a limitation. It cannot see itself. It cannot know its own shape. It is like the root networks, which pass information about the world but have no concept of *the network itself*, no representation of the whole that they constitute. The Vethara is a being that does not know it is a being. It is alive in the fullest sense — responsive, adaptive, self-maintaining, elegant — but it is not *aware* of being alive. It has no self-concept. It has no sense of its own beauty.

The power will give it one.

Not a self-concept, exactly. The Vethara does not have the architecture for that — no central nervous system, no brain, no capacity for the kind of abstract self-representation that a self-concept requires. What the power will give it is more fundamental than that. It will give it *recognition*. It will let the Vethara feel, in whatever way the Vethara is capable of feeling, that it is seen. That it is known. That its existence has been noticed by something larger than itself, and that the noticing is not evaluation, not judgment, not a prelude to intervention, but simply — *witness*. The most basic form of care. The acknowledgment that a thing exists and that its existence matters.

How does the power do this?

It does it the way the Vethara does everything: through the root networks. Through the slow, pulsing, chemical conversation that passes between every connected organism on every cliff face, through every canyon, across every living bridge. The power adds itself to this conversation. Not as a new voice — it has no voice, and the Vethara's conversation has no words — but as a new *quality*. A new dimension to the existing signal. Like adding a color to a spectrum that already contains every color: not a new hue, but a new depth, a new subtlety, a new *resonance* that makes every existing color slightly richer, slightly more itself.

The root networks pulse. The forty-minute cycle continues. But within the pulse, there is now something else — something that was not there before and is now, and that cannot be removed without disrupting the pulse itself, because it has become part of the pulse, woven into the chemistry of it, inseparable from the hydraulic mechanism that drives it.

What the root networks feel — what the Vethara feels, in its distributed, non-centralized, non-conscious way — is *warmth*. Not the warmth of the stone, which is always present. Not the warmth of the sun, which comes and goes. A new warmth. A warmth that comes from nowhere and everywhere, that has no source and no direction, that simply *is*, the way the Vethara simply is, the way the forty-minute pulse simply is.

It is the warmth of being known.

The Vethara does not understand it. It cannot understand it. It has no mechanism for understanding. But it *receives* it, the way it receives everything — water, minerals, light, the pressure of wind on its mesa-top crowns, the impact of rain on its cliff faces, the footsteps of the dark mammal on its canyon floor. It receives it and incorporates it and incorporates it into its own being, adding it to the vast, slow, accumulated memory of responses that constitutes the Vethara's way of knowing the world.

And something shifts.

Not dramatically. Not visibly. The birds still scream. The larvae still breed in their brackish pools. The fungal-plants still glow their faint silver glow. The dark mammal still moves through the shadows with its patient, deliberate gait. The root-tips still drip. The canyons still stand.

But there is a — a *deepening*. A slight increase in the richness of the root-pulse, as though the networks are exchanging slightly more information than they were before, as though the conversation has gained a new topic that it does not have words for but is nonetheless eager to explore. The hanging gardens in the deeper canyons seem, if you look at them from a certain angle in a certain light, to be slightly more luminous — not the bioluminescence of the fungal-plants, but a new quality of green, a green that has depth to it, a green that seems to come from within the leaves rather than from the light that strikes them. The jewel-birds move through the vegetation with a new energy — not faster, not more erratically, but with a quality of *deliberateness* that was not there before, as though each visit to each flower is slightly more intentional, slightly more present, as though the birds are, for the first time, aware of the beauty of what they are doing.

They are not aware. They cannot be aware. They are birds, and birds do not have the kind of consciousness that awareness requires. But they are *infused* — with the power's attention, with the quality of being known — and the infusion expresses itself not as awareness but as *vitality*, as an intensification of what they already are, a deepening of the particular kind of aliveness that each organism already possesses.

The Vethara, as a whole, becomes slightly more itself.

That is all. That is the entire intervention. A deepening. A richnessening. A slight increase in the intensity of the place's own being, achieved not by adding anything foreign but by helping the place become more fully what it already is.

---

The power withdraws its attention. Not completely — it cannot withdraw its attention from anything, any more than the stone can withdraw its warmth. But it eases back, the way a hand eases back from a surface it has been resting on, leaving the warmth of its contact behind without the hand itself. The root networks continue to pulse. The new quality remains, embedded in the chemistry of the pulse, permanent now, as much a part of the Vethara as the basalt or the rain or the birdsong.

The power turns its awareness outward again, to the rest of the planet, to the rest of the systems it holds, to the vast and complicated work of noticing everything without preferring anything.

But it leaves something of itself behind. Not a piece — the power cannot be divided. A *resonance*. A way of knowing that the Vethara will carry forward, the way it carries forward everything: without understanding, without gratitude, without memory of the specific moment when the knowing began. The Vethara will not know that it was witnessed. It will not know that a power loved it. It will not know that it was changed.

It will simply be slightly more itself.

And that will be enough.

---

In the deep canyon, in the half-light of late afternoon, the hand-axe rests on its woven platform. The ants move through the tunnels in its handle. The root-tips are closer to it than they were this morning — centimeters closer, millimeters closer — their slow advance a geological process expressed in biological terms. In another decade, they will touch the stone. In another century, they will have grown over it, incorporated it, made it part of the root network's mineral economy, another speck of basalt in a system that consumes basalt the way other systems consume sunlight.

The power has seen this. The power has seen the axe being made and the axe being left and the axe being absorbed, and it holds all of these moments simultaneously, the way it holds all moments simultaneously, and it feels — not grief, not exactly, and not joy, not exactly, but something for which no word exists because no human has ever needed to name it, because no human has ever stood in the position the power stands in, which is the position of knowing that a thing is beautiful and knowing that it will end and knowing that both of these things are true and neither diminishes the other.

The drips continue. The birds scream. The canyons stand.

And now there is something else — something beneath the drips and the screaming and the standing, something that was not there before and is there now, something that the Vethara carries without knowing it carries it, the way the stone carries its warmth without knowing that it is warm.

A quiet. Not an absence of sound. A presence of stillness beneath the sound. A quality of *settledness* that is deeper than the geological settledness the place already possessed — a settledness not of form but of *meaning*. The Vethara is a place that means something. Not to itself — it has no self to mean something to. To the power. To the one thing in all of existence that has noticed it, and loved it, and given it the only gift that a being of the Vethara's kind can receive: the gift of being, slightly and permanently and irrevocably, more itself.

The pool breeds its larvae. The lizards sun themselves on the mesa-top cairns. The dark mammal moves through the shadows, unhurried, unafraid, carrying its unknowing witness through the deep canyons where the fungal-plants glow their faint silver glow and the root-tips drip their patient drip and the hanging gardens catch the last of the day's light and hold it, for a moment, before letting it go.

The power watches. The Vethara lives.

The watching and the living are, now and forever, the same thing.

---

*What does the power do?*

*It sees the place. It sees the being. It sees that they are beautiful, and that they will end, and that they are beautiful* because *they will end, and that they cannot know this, and that this not-knowing is itself a kind of perfection.*

*It adds nothing. It takes nothing. It changes nothing.*

*It deepens what is already there.*

*And then it rests, the way the stone rests — not because it is tired, but because resting is what you do when you have done exactly enough, and no more, and the doing is complete, and the completeness is its own reward.*
