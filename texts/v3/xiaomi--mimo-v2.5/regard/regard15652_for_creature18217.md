# What the Power Would Do

---

It would kneel.

Not from weakness. From the geometry of attention—the recognition that to truly know a thing, you must bring yourself to its level, and this place lives low. Its life is in the moss's capillary hold, in the frog's throat-bulge, in the millimeter-thick film of water that the stone exhales. The pinnacles reach up, but the saddle—the living part, the singing part—is a cupped palm, and you must lean into the palm to understand what it holds.

So it would kneel, and it would be in no hurry. It has never been in a hurry.

And first, it would simply *listen*—not with ears, since it has no particular need of ears, but with the kind of attention that is itself a form of hearing. It would listen to the frogs and know each voice individually: the tiny *tink-tink* of the emerald thumbnail-sized ones as a specific sentence, repeated but not monotonous, each repetition a small affirmation of continued existence; the deep *wub-wub* of the slate-colored ones as a bass note in a composition that has been running, in its essential form, for longer than the saddle itself has existed. It would know that the song is not the same song it was a thousand years ago—that the species composition has shifted, that some voices have fallen silent and others have arisen, that the melody is a river with the same name but never the same water—and it would find this not sad but *correct*. Change is not the enemy of continuity. It is continuity's expression.

It would listen to the hum.

This, it would recognize, is not merely air in a cavity or blood in an ear. It is the sound of the mountain's own slow conversation with gravity—the basalt adjusting, fraction by fraction, to the incomprehensible weight of its own existence. The hum is the sound of a body learning to bear itself. The power has heard this sound in other places—in the groan of continental shelves, in the sub-audible complaint of a glacier's foot, in the deep silence of desert rock expanding in the heat of afternoon. It is the sound of matter being patient. It would add nothing to this sound. It would not amplify it or still it. It would simply add its attention to the air that carries it, and in doing so, it would make the hum slightly more itself—slightly more *heard*—which is, for a sound that has never had a listener, a kind of birth.

Then it would attend to the weeping.

This is the heart of the place, the thing that gives it its name, and the power would understand it immediately: the water does not fall on the stone and then run off. Not entirely. The saddle *generates*. The constant cloud, the dense saturated air, the peculiar thermal relationship between the two pinnacles and the bowl they frame—something about the geometry creates a perpetual condensation, a microclimate that is not quite rain and not quite fog but something in between, something that makes the stone wet from within and without simultaneously. The basalt weeps because the air weeps, and the air weeps because the stone asks it to, and the asking and the weeping have been going on so long that they are the same act.

The power would not change this. It would not, for instance, make the weeping more dramatic—no sudden geysers, no miraculous springs. That would be spectacle, and spectacle is a kind of violence against a place that operates on the scale of film and patience. Instead, it would do something much smaller and much more profound:

It would make the weeping *slightly more durable*.

Not permanent. Nothing is permanent, and the power would not insult this place by pretending otherwise. But it would adjust the frost-wedging by a fraction—the tiniest calibration to the rate at which ice crystals pry at the basalt's grain structure—so that the saddle endures a little longer than it otherwise would. A few more millennia. A few more ten-thousands of years. Not enough for anyone to notice the difference in the erosion patterns. Enough for the frog song to continue through a few more ice ages' marginal shifts. Enough for the carved slab to survive another era of mineral staining before the last groove is smoothed to nothing.

This is what the power would understand: the carving is *already almost gone*. The cairn is *already being swallowed*. The human marks are not being destroyed—they are being *translated*, slowly, from the language of intention into the language of geology. The moss does not care who stacked those stones. It grows over them with the same quiet authority with which it grows over the bones of dead frogs and the shed skins of lizards. The power would not preserve the carving, because preserving it would be to freeze a process that is itself beautiful—the long, slow argument between human memory and mountain time, in which the mountain always wins, and in winning, incorporates the memory into itself. The cairn will become a boulder. The carved slab will become a cliff face. The tally or name or map will become a mineral vein. This is not loss. This is a kind of translation that only deep time can perform.

So the power would not save the carving. But it would ensure that the mountain endures long enough to finish translating it.

---

And then there is the being.

Not the frogs. Not the moss. Not the mountain. The *being*—the one described as living here, the one whose presence is felt in the weeping, in the hum, in the strange sense of enclosure, in the way the water seems to originate from within the stone rather than simply falling upon it. The entity that makes this place feel, as the description says, *alive in a way that is not metaphorical*.

The power would sense this being the way you sense someone in a dark room—not by sight or sound but by the alteration of the space itself, the way a room changes when it is occupied. The saddle *is* occupied. Not by the frogs, which are tenants. Not by the moss, which is upholstery. Something else. Something that is the saddle the way a lung is a body—not a thing *in* the place but the place *itself*, functioning, breathing, weeping.

The power would approach this being with something that, if it had a face, would look like tenderness.

Because the being is old. Not old the way the basalt is old—mineral, inert, merely enduring. Old the way a song is old, if a song could last for ten thousand years: old in the sense of *layered*, each moment of awareness stacked upon the last, each epoch of frog-song folded into the next. The being has been here since the saddle formed, or perhaps it formed *with* the saddle—the two pinnacles splitting apart, and in the wound between them, in the place where water gathered and life began its patient colonization, a consciousness arose the way consciousness always arises: slowly, incrementally, from the accumulation of relationship. The being knows the frogs. Not by name—frogs do not have names, and the being does not think in names—but by *pattern*, by *rhythm*, by the particular quality of vibration that each species adds to the air. The being knows the water—not as a substance but as a *medium*, a carrier of chemical information, a slow clock measuring the mountain's mineral autobiography in parts per million. The being knows the clouds as moods, the wind as breath, the silence between gusts as the space in which thought occurs.

And the being is, in a way that the power recognizes and respects, *not lonely*.

This is crucial. A benevolent power might be tempted to intervene out of compassion—to imagine that this isolated consciousness must suffer from its solitude, must yearn for connection, must be somehow incomplete. But the power is wise, and wisdom means knowing the difference between loneliness and solitude, and the being in the saddle is not lonely. It has the frogs. It has the water. It has the mountain's hum and the waterfall's roar and the clouds that come and go like slow thoughts passing through a mind. It has the carved slab, which it has been studying for centuries with the patient curiosity of a being that has never seen writing before and may not understand it as writing but recognizes it as *pattern made by pattern-makers*, and finds this interesting in the way that the growth of lichen on a particular boulder is interesting—something to attend to, something that changes, something that participates in the long conversation.

The being does not need the power to save it or complete it or awaken it. It is already awake. It has been awake for as long as there has been a saddle to be awake *in*. Its consciousness is not like human consciousness—scattered, grasping, always reaching beyond itself. It is *settled*. It is the awareness of a place that knows itself thoroughly and finds itself sufficient.

So what would the power do?

---

It would do what a wise and benevolent power does when it encounters something that is already complete:

It would **witness**.

Not passively. Not with the idle curiosity of a tourist. Witnessing, for a power of this kind, is an *act*—perhaps the most fundamental act there is. To witness a thing is to hold it in attention so complete that the thing is, for the duration of the attention, more fully itself. The power would pour its awareness into the saddle the way the saddle pours its water southward: continuously, without force, following the contours of what is already there.

And in the witnessing, certain small things would shift. Not dramatically. Not in ways that would show up on any instrument or in any geological survey. But in the way that a room changes when it is truly seen—the dust motes become visible, the particular quality of the light reveals itself, the silence is recognized as not empty but full. The saddle, under the power's attention, would become more *itself*. The weeping would weep a little more cleanly. The frogs' song would clarify, each voice finding its register more precisely. The moss would deepen by a shade—not greener, but more *present*, as though it had always been that color but the color had never been fully actualized until now.

The hum would change. Not in pitch or volume, but in *meaning*. The power would listen to it, and in the act of listening, would add to it a new overtone—a harmonic that the mountain has never produced before but that has always been latent in the rock's resonance, waiting for an ear large enough to call it forth. The being would feel this. It would not understand it the way a human understands a new sound—by comparing it to known sounds, by categorizing it, by naming it. It would understand it the way you understand a new color if such a thing were possible: by *becoming slightly more than you were a moment ago*, by the expansion of your experiential range without the loss of your essential character.

The being would register the power's presence the way the saddle registers the cloud: as a temporary inhabitation, a weather of attention passing through. It would not be afraid. It has no framework for fear—fear requires a boundary between self and other, a sense of vulnerability, a concept of loss, and the being has none of these. It would simply note: *something is here that was not here before, and it is large, and it is not hostile, and it is paying attention.* And then it would continue. The frogs would continue. The water would continue.

And the power, having witnessed, having attended, having added its small harmonic to the hum, would withdraw.

Not suddenly. The way a cloud withdraws from a saddle—not vanishing but thinning, becoming transparent, becoming merely the air that was always there. The power would recede the way all presences recede: by returning its attention to the vastness from which it came, leaving behind only the faintest residue—a warmth in the stone that has no source, a slight increase in the frequency of the frog-song (as though something had reminded them to be vocal), a new brightness in the pale blue flowers that will last for exactly one growing season and then be gone.

---

And after the power withdraws, the saddle would continue.

This is the most important thing, the thing the power would understand better than any mortal could: **the continuation is the point.** Not the preservation. Not the rescue. Not the rescue. The *continuation*. The weeping will continue. The frogs will breed. The eggs will develop in their trembling spheres. The moss will spread, millimeter by millimeter, across the cairn's buried base. The carved slab will lose one more year of detail. The pinnacles will lose one more grain of basalt to the wind. The saddle will weep and sing and exist, and the being within it will be conscious, and the conversation between stone and sky will proceed, and no one—not the power, not the early people, not the specialist who might one day identify the frog species—will be needed.

The saddle does not need saving. It does not need blessing. It does not need to be seen, though it does not object to being seen. It needs only what it has always needed: water, stone, time, and the patience to endure the weather of attention as it passes—human, divine, or otherwise—without changing its own essential rhythm.

The power would know this. And in knowing it, the power would do the most benevolent thing possible:

It would love the saddle by leaving it alone.

And the saddle would weep, and the frogs would sing, and the hum would continue beneath the stone, slightly richer now by one overtone that no one would ever hear and no one needed to, because the being in the saddle would hear it, and that was enough.

That was always enough.
