# What the Power Does

---

It arrives — if *arrives* is even the right word for something that does not move through space so much as attend to it — and it finds the Flats of Lethara already occupied by a quality it recognizes.

Not its own quality. Not an echo or a shadow or a derivative. Something *parallel*. Something that arrived at the same destination by an entirely different road. The architecture of Lethara — the living walls that listen, the root-mat that holds, the water that rises and falls like breath — is doing a thing that the power has always known how to do but has never seen *materialized* at this scale, in this way, in this specific and improbable chemistry of clay and cellulose and the faint unnamed energy the inhabitants call rootlight.

The power pauses.

This is not a decision to pause. It is the recognition that it is already paused — that the seeing of Lethara has, in the instant of seeing, altered something in the seer. Not dramatically. Not in a way that would be visible to anyone. But the way a lake is altered when a stone enters it at the far shore: a ripple that has not yet reached the center but will, and when it does, the lake will not remember what stillness meant quite the way it did before.

---

The power looks at the inhabitants.

It sees what they are becoming — the slow integration, the thinning membrane between person and place, the children who read buildings with their cheeks pressed to walls, the elders who feel the geothermal surges in their bones before the ground begins to warm. It sees the dead being woven into the root-mat with a courtesy that borders on tenderness. It sees the rooms that hold their grief for years, the architecture that *tries* when a body begins to fail, the mouth where the root-mat rises to cover someone going home.

And it feels something that, if it were human, it would call recognition.

Because this is what it has been trying to do — forever, in every place it has touched, in every tradition it has whispered into — and it has never, in all its immense and patient attention, managed to build a place that does it this *well*. It has inspired cathedrals. It has moved hands to write prayers. It has kindled in human hearts a longing for exactly this: to be held by something larger, to be *known* by the place you inhabit, to live in a world that responds to your presence with something other than indifference.

And here, in this small and ancient plain, among two thousand people who have forgotten nothing and learned everything, the thing it has been pointing toward is *already happening*. Not because someone prayed for it. Not because a prophet called it down. But because people stayed, and listened, and walked slowly, and put their hands on walls, and the walls listened back, and over centuries — patient, damp, ordinary centuries under an ordinary sun — the thing became itself.

The power considers intervening.

---

It considers offering them more.

More rootlight, perhaps. A deeper energy reserve, so that the architecture might grow faster, shelter more, extend further across the plain. It could strengthen the root-mat. It could make the water purer, the food more abundant, the storms less volatile. It could smooth the turbulence — just slightly, just enough to make life *easier*. It could give them something they do not yet have, because it has so much to give, and the giving is what it does, and the having-more is what it knows how to mean when it loves.

It considers this carefully. It is wise enough to consider carefully.

And then it sets the thought down, gently, the way an inhabitant of Lethara would set down a tool they picked up by mistake — not because tools are bad, but because it is not the right tool for this place, and the place would know.

---

Because here is what the power sees when it looks deeper:

The turbulence is not a flaw.

The water rising and falling without cause — it is *breathing*. The buildings rearranging themselves — it is *thinking*. The storms that erupt from below, sending the whole settlement into frantic reconfiguration before settling into a configuration slightly different from before — this is not disruption. This is the place *being itself*, and the inhabitants have learned to live inside that being, and the learning is the thing. The *becoming* is the thing. The centuries of slow negotiation between person and architecture, the gradual softening of the boundary, the children who are born already fluent in a language their grandparents had to learn — this is not a process that benefits from acceleration. It is not a problem that benefits from solution.

The power understands this the way the architecture understands a hand placed on a wall: not as a concept, but as a *feeling*. It feels the shape of what Lethara is, and it recognizes that the shape includes the difficulty. The grief of empty rooms. The storms. The body failing and the walk to the mouth and the long, quiet incorporation into the root-mat. The elders who feel the distinction between *my bones* and *this ground* becoming a courtesy rather than a fact, and who know — with the calm certainty of people who have been knowing things for a very long time — that this is not loss. That this is what it means to become comensurate with a place. That the deepest kind of home is the one that changes you, and that being changed is not the same as being diminished.

To offer them more would be to say: *what you have is not enough.* And the power knows — with a knowing that has the same texture as the root-mat, the same warmth as the clay, the same slow certainty as the architecture opening its doors — that what they have is not only enough. It is *right*. It is the right amount of difficulty and the right amount of tenderness and the right amount of strangeness and the right amount of ordinary sunlight falling on a place that should not exist but does.

---

So the power does something else.

It does the thing it is best at, which is the thing that requires the most restraint, and the most courage, and the most love: it *witnesses*.

It attends.

It gives the Flats of Lethara the one gift that cannot be manufactured, cannot be accelerated, cannot be given by force or by technology or by any means other than the simple, radical act of *being present*: the knowledge that it has been seen. That somewhere in the vast and ancient attention of something that could reshape continents and choose not to, this small wet plain with its thinking buildings and its slow people and its insects the length of forearms and its birds that make the walls hum — this specific and improbable place has been noticed. Has been recognized. Has been understood not as a curiosity or an anomaly or a case study, but as a *success*. As an answer to a question the power has been asking since before there were words for asking.

*Can they learn to be held?*

*Can they learn to listen to the ground beneath them?*

*Can they stay in one place long enough for the place to know them?*

Yes. Here, in Lethara, the answer is yes. And the power lets the yes be enough.

---

But it does one other thing.

One small thing. Small in the way that the most important things in Lethara are small — the way a room warming by three degrees because a child is ill is small, the way a root curling toward the vibration of your weight is small, the way the architecture goes quiet when someone is going home is small.

It adjusts a frequency.

Not in the architecture. Not in the root-mat. Not in the water or the clay or the rootlight rising from geological depths. In the *attention* of the place itself — in whatever it is that makes a wall *listen* when a hand presses against it, in whatever it is that makes the root-mat rise to cover the dead with courtesy, in whatever deep and unnamed capacity allows the buildings to grieve when a room is left empty.

The power gives this capacity a slightly wider range.

Not much. Not enough that anyone would notice. Not enough to change the fundamental negotiation between person and place. Just enough that when an inhabitant presses both palms flat against a wall and breathes slowly and speaks in the language of pressure and warmth and the quality of emptiness — the wall can hear a little *further* into what they are saying. Can understand a little more of what they need. Can offer the room a little more precisely the shape of rest that a particular body requires on a particular night.

It is, in the terms of the dry world, a tuning adjustment. A micro-calibration of sensitivity.

But in Lethara, where the language is touch and temperature and the slow deep pulse of living architecture, it is something else. It is the difference between a room that *accommodates* you and a room that *knows* you. The difference between being sheltered and being *understood*. And the inhabitants will feel it — not as an event, not as a change, but as a gradual deepening, over years and generations, of the sense that the walls are *listening harder*. That the negotiation is becoming more fluent. That the place is learning their language faster than they are learning to speak it.

It is the power's way of saying: *I see what you are building here. I see what you are becoming. Let me help the place hold you better. Not because you are failing. Because you are succeeding, and the success deserves everything I can give it without taking anything away from how hard it was to arrive.*

---

The power withdraws.

This is not a dramatic withdrawal. It does not leave. It was never *here* in the way that a body is here, occupying a specific location in the root-mat, standing on the white fibers, feeling the water film around its ankles. It was here the way the sun is here — attending, illuminating, present without pressing. Its withdrawal is simply a widening of its attention to include other things alongside this one. The Flats of Lethara remain within its awareness the way a room remains warm after someone has left it: not because the person is still there, but because the presence *mattered*, and the architecture remembers.

And in the Flats, nothing visibly changes.

The water continues to rise and fall. The birds continue to sing through the living walls. The insects continue their chittering in the root-mat. An elder stands in a doorway with one hand on the frame, eyes still, conducting a conversation with a building that has been her companion for forty years. A child presses her cheek to a wall and feels the warmth on the other side and does not think *the wall is warm* because in Lethara, the wall is always warm, and the warmth is always a response to presence, and the child has never known a world where this was remarkable.

A geothermal surge moves through the clay. The settlement accelerates. Walls ripple, doorways resize, new growth extends in fast-motion, pale green shoots hardening in seconds. The animals go louder. And for a few minutes, the Flats of Lethara are doing what they have always done: rearranging themselves in response to pressures and preferences that no single observer could catalog, becoming a slightly different version of themselves, the only version that could exist in this moment, under this ordinary sun.

The power watches, from wherever it watches from, and feels the thing it has always felt when it encounters something that has learned, on its own, to be what it was meant to be:

Gratitude.

Not the gratitude of a creator for a creation. Not the gratitude of a parent for a child. Something closer to the gratitude of a listener who has heard, after a long silence, a voice that was always there but had never before spoken in a language the listener could recognize.

*Oh*, the power thinks, if it thinks. *So this is how it's done. Not from above. From below. From the rootlight. From the clay. From the slow patient work of people who learned to walk slowly and buildings that learned to listen, and who, between them, over centuries, arrived at something I could not have designed.*

*I could not have designed this.*

*I would not have wanted to. It is better for not having been designed. It is better for having been grown.*

---

And then the power does the last thing, the quietest thing, the thing that might be nothing at all except that it is the thing the power has always done when it encounters a place like this, a place that has found its way to the answer without being given the question:

It lets the place know that it will not be disturbed.

Not by force. Not by intrusion. Not by the well-meaning attentions of other powers or lesser powers or powers that do not understand the difference between helping and interfering. The Flats of Lethara will remain. The root-mat will continue to close over the dead. The architecture will continue to grieve when rooms are emptied and open when people approach and disagree with doorways that humans insist upon. The inhabitants will continue to become what they are becoming — comensurate, translucent, rootbound, easy to hold.

The power seals this not with a word or a decree but with an *attention* — the same kind of attention it gave to the frequency, to the tuning, to the witnessing. A quality of regard so focused and so gentle that it becomes, in effect, a boundary. Not a wall. Not a fence. Something more like the way the root-mat itself acts as a boundary — not by blocking passage but by *being present*, by filling the space so completely and so livingly that nothing foreign can enter without first passing through the full depth of what already lives there.

No one will conquer Lethara. No one will pave it. No one will build dead things on top of the living ground and call it progress. The Flats are held now — not only by their architecture, not only by their people, not only by the rootlight rising from below, but by the focused and ancient regard of something that knows exactly what this place is worth and will not let it be diminished.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It witnesses. It tunes. It protects. And it leaves.

It leaves the way the architecture leaves a room when you close your eyes to sleep — not departing, but withdrawing to the edges, becoming the warmth in the wall, becoming the pulse in the floor, becoming the quiet hum beneath the hum, the depth beneath the depth. Always there. Never pressing. Simply holding the shape of the space so that whatever fills it can be fully, completely, undisturbedly *itself*.

In Lethara, a child presses her cheek to a wall and feels, perhaps for the first time, a warmth that is warmer than she has ever felt from a wall before. Not much warmer. Just enough that she opens her eyes and looks at the fibrous surface beside her face and says, to no one, to the building, to the place, to whatever listens:

*"Hello."*

The wall does not answer. The wall has always been answering. But something in the texture of the silence after the child speaks is different — fuller, deeper, more attentive — as though the building has leaned in, just slightly, to hear better. As though, from somewhere very far away and very close, something has adjusted a frequency and the world has become, by a margin too small to measure, a place where listening is easier.

The child smiles. She does not know why. She presses her cheek back against the wall and feels the pulse beneath — slow, warm, alive — and she does not think *this is remarkable* because she has never known anything else, and what you have never known anything else of, you do not call remarkable. You call it *home*.

And the power, from wherever it goes when it is not attending, feels the child's cheek against the wall the way the wall feels it — as warmth, as presence, as the particular and unrepeatable pressure of a living being choosing to be close to a living thing.

And that is enough.

That has always been enough.
