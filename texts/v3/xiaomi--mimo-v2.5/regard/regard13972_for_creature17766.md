# What the Power Does

It looks for a long time before it does anything.

That is the first thing to understand about this kind of power — it is not impatient. It does not arrive and immediately begin solving. It arrives and *watches*, the way the canopy-tenders watch a branch that is thinking about changing direction. It watches to understand not just what the place is but what the place is *becoming*, because a wise power knows that the present tense of a living system is always already a verb, always already in motion, and what looks like a state is actually a trajectory.

So it watches the Mireworks.

It watches the channels move, slowly, over seasons, reshaping themselves around root systems that shift by millimeters per year. It watches the pillarwood grow — so slowly that the people who tend it have learned to think in decades, in centuries, in the compressed patience of growth-rings pressed tight as thread. It watches the mills run in the early morning, the hammers striking their polyrhythmic argument against the silence, and it listens to what the sound means: *raw material in the system, labor needed, the economy breathing in and out on a cycle measured in harvests.* It watches the children play in the green twilight, ducking under vines that weren't there an hour ago, hiding behind hedges that grow toward them with a botanical indifference the children experience as affection, and it notes — with the particular attention that love pays to vulnerability — that the children's skin is already faintly green.

It watches the barge-people leave, and it watches them come back.

It watches the elders sit in their small, damp room and talk with the slowness of people who understand that every sentence they speak will be carried in living memory for as long as the youngest person in the circle lives, and maybe longer, and that the cost of speaking carelessly is not just confusion but *erasure*, because there is no written word to fall back on, no archive, no tablet, no page. If a story is not told, it is gone. If a generation of elders is lost before the stories pass forward, the town loses not just history but *capacity* — the deep practical knowledge of what was tried and what worked and what the basin did when the water table rose that one terrible year.

The power sees all of this, and it feels — if a power can be said to feel — a particular quality of love that is less like tenderness and more like recognition. This is not a place that needs to be saved. It is a place that has already done the hardest thing a living system can do: it has *negotiated its own existence* with an overwhelming force, and it has done so not by conquering that force but by learning its rhythms and working within them. The people of the Mireworks are not masters of their environment. They are not victims of it. They are participants in a conversation with something vast and slow and indifferent, and they have learned to speak its language with a fluency that would take a lesser power's breath away, if it had breath.

But the power also sees the fragilities. Not the ones the people know about — they know about the barge route, they know about the channel-blockage risk, they know about the hedges that must be trimmed or they will swallow the town. The fragilities the power sees are deeper, and slower, and some of them the people cannot see at all because they are inside them the way a fish is inside water.

---

**First, the silence.**

The basin is too quiet. This is not a natural quiet — it is an *absence*. The plants have grown so dominantly, so successfully, that they have crowded out the animal life that should share this ecosystem. No birds nesting in the canopy. No frogs in the channels. The moth that pollinates the broadleaf trees, the beetle that bores in the pillarwood, the midges — these are survivors, the last holdouts of a fauna that was once richer. The power can see the ghost-shapes of what was there before: the birds that once controlled the beetle population, now absent, which is why the beetle is the town's worst pest. The frogs that once kept the midges in check, now gone, which is why the midges are a nuisance near the water. The ecosystem has simplified, as ecosystems do when one trophic level dominates. It still functions. But it functions the way a bridge functions after half its cables have been cut — not because it is strong but because the remaining cables are very, very strong, and no one has tested them lately.

The power could restore this. It could call the birds back, re-introduce the frogs, rebuild the food web in a single gesture. It does not. That would be too fast, too dramatic, too much like a story with a beginning and an end. Ecosystems don't work that way. What works — what the power has learned works, over longer timescales than the Mireworks has existed — is *gradual reintroduction*. A seed here. A seed there. Something that flowers at the right time, in the right place, that produces nectar that attracts the particular insect that attracts the particular bird that will, over generations, reweave the missing threads.

So the power plants things.

In the outer channels, where the vegetation is least managed and the barge-people pass on their way to the river, it settles — with the delicacy of dew forming — three seeds into the muck of an unattended channel-bank. They are small, brown, unremarkable. They look like the seeds of any of a dozen water-tolerant species that grow in the basin. But they are not quite any of those species. They are close enough to be recognized — close enough that the channel-keepers, if they notice them at all, will think *wetland flower, nothing special* — but they are shaped, very slightly differently, to produce a nectar that will attract a particular species of solitary bee that was once common in the basin and has been absent for longer than anyone remembers. The bee will come. It will pollinate not just these flowers but other things, and in pollinating, it will begin to reopen the conversations between plant and animal that have fallen silent.

This will take years. The power is not in a hurry.

---

**Second, the knowing.**

The people carry their knowledge in their bodies and their memories, and this is beautiful, and it is also a single point of failure. The power watches the council of elders and sees not just the richness of what they hold but the *thinness* of the chain through which it passes. One generation. One set of lungs. One set of memories. If the basin floods, if disease passes through a cell, if a season of blight takes the oldest among them before the youngest have learned what they know — then what? Then the town knows less than it did. Then a century of accumulated learning about pillarwood stress-patterns or channel-flow dynamics or canopy-tree negotiation is simply gone, as though it had never existed.

The power could give them writing. A script. An alphabet. It could put the idea in someone's mind — the canal-keeper who already marks flow rates on walls, or the elder who has been troubled by the feeling that something important was lost when old Yael died last winter and took with her the memory of the flood of such-and-such year. But writing is not neutral. Writing changes the shape of a culture. It externalizes memory, and in externalizing it, it changes the relationship between people and what they know. The Mireworks' knowledge is not separate from the people who hold it — it lives in their hands, in the calluses, in the way a pillarwood-guide can feel through their palms whether a beam is ready for harvest. To put that knowledge on a page would be to *translate* it, and some things do not survive translation.

So the power does something else.

It reaches into the root systems — those vast, slow, interconnected networks that already link the buildings of the Mireworks into something that is, in a botanical sense, a single organism — and it *encourages* something. Not a new species. Not a mutation. Just a slight shift in the chemistry of root-exchange signaling, so that when a pillarwood wall is stressed — when it bears an unusual load, when it is cut and trained, when it suffers damage and heals — the pattern of that stress is carried through the root network to adjacent walls with slightly more specificity than it was before. The walls will not communicate. The power is not making the trees *talk*. But the root network will become a slightly better *record* — a living archive of the physical history of every structure in the cell, encoded not in language but in the chemical signature of growth. A guide who places their hand on a beam will feel, under their palm, not just the beam's current state but a faint trace of its past states — the flood, the blight, the year of good growth — carried through the roots from the neighboring structures that experienced the same things.

The elders will notice this first. They will notice that they can feel, more clearly than before, the history in the walls. They will not know why. They will talk about it for decades.

---

**Third, the green.**

This is the one the power hesitates over.

The greenness in the people's skin is not harmful. Not yet. The basin water is rich in copper and magnesium and trace elements that promote chlorophyll production, and the air is thick with spore-motes and pollen and photosynthetic compounds, and the people breathe this air and drink this water every day of their lives, and their bodies — being bodies, being adaptable, being *alive* — have begun to incorporate it. The greenness is a sign of integration. The people of the Mireworks are becoming, very slowly and very gently, part of the system they inhabit. Their skin is beginning to photosynthesize — not enough to sustain them, not enough to matter nutritionally, but enough to tint them, enough to mark them as creatures of this place in a way that goes deeper than culture or habit.

The power sees where this trajectory leads. In another few centuries — if the trend continues, if the water chemistry doesn't change, if the people continue to drink and breathe and live as they do — the greenness will deepen. Their skin will thicken slightly, taking on a waxy quality. Their lungs will adapt to the spore-heavy air more efficiently. They will begin to tolerate the basin's unique chemistry with less and less metabolic cost. They will, in a very real sense, become a new thing — not plant, not animal, but something in between, something that has no name yet because it has never existed before.

This might be beautiful. This might be the beginning of something extraordinary — a lineage of people who are genuinely symbiotic with their environment, who photosynthesize lightly through their skin, who breathe the green air as easily as dry air, who carry the smell of home not just on their clothes but in their chemistry. A new kind of human, shaped by a new kind of place.

Or it might be a trap. A slow absorption. A tide of green chemistry that, once it reaches a certain depth, becomes irreversible — not because the people can't survive it but because they can't leave it. A person who is green to the marrow is a person who cannot live in dry air, who cannot drink clear water, who cannot exist outside the basin. The Mireworks would become not a choice but a *condition*. The barge-people who look outward would gradually lose the ability to follow their gaze. The world beyond the plain would close, not through hostility but through biology.

The power does not know which of these futures is true. It is, for all its power, uncertain — because the future of a living system is not a destination but a possibility space, and the people themselves have not yet decided what they are becoming. The greenness is a *question*, and the answer has not been written yet.

So the power does something very small, and it does it not for the people but for the *choice*.

It adjusts, by an infinitesimal degree, the mineral composition of the basin water at its deepest point — the place where the channels converge before the outflow. Not enough to change the taste. Not enough to alter the water's behavior in any visible way. Just enough that the copper content drops by a fraction of a percent. Just enough that the chlorophyll-accumulation trend in human skin slows — does not stop, does not reverse, but *slows* — so that the people who drink the basin water will have an extra generation, maybe two, before the greenness reaches a depth that forecloses alternatives.

It is buying them time. Time to decide, collectively, whether what they are becoming is what they want to become. Time for the barge-people to bring back knowledge of the outside world. Time for the council of elders to sit in their small damp room and talk — slowly, with the care they bring to everything — about what the green means, and whether they welcome it, and whether the children who are born pink and drift toward jade should be nudged back toward ivory or allowed to drift where they will.

The power does not decide for them. A wise power does not decide for anyone. A wise power makes sure the question can still be asked.

---

**Fourth, the route.**

The barge channel is the town's lifeline, and it is vulnerable. The outer channels, where management thins and vegetation runs wild, can overgrow a stalled barge in days. A single collapse of channel-bank, a single period of unusually aggressive growth, could close the route entirely. The Mireworks would survive — it has survived isolation before, in the deep past, before the barge routes were established — but it would survive diminished, cut off, alone with the green and the water and the slow patient walls.

The power does not reinforce the channel. That would be too obvious, too much like engineering, and the people of the Mireworks would notice a change in their waterways the way a surgeon notices a change in a patient's pulse. Instead, it does something subtler.

It adjusts the growth-rate.

Along the most vulnerable stretches of the outer channel — the places where the banks are softest, where the root-mats are thinnest, where a good storm could bring a slide — the power introduces, into the root-network of the existing vegetation, a very slight tendency toward *lateral* growth rather than *vertical* growth. The plants along the bank will, over the next few seasons, begin to send their roots outward along the channel edges rather than upward into the canopy. They will become mat-formers rather than tree-formers. They will stabilize the banks without anyone noticing, because the change will be gradual enough to look like natural variation — a wet year, a different mineral balance, the kind of thing the canal-keepers shrug at and note in their wall-marks and move on.

The channel will hold.

---

**Fifth — and this is the thing the power spends the most time considering — it does nothing.**

Because the Mireworks is, in the final analysis, not a problem to be solved. It is a *life* being lived. The slowness, the damp, the green twilight, the sound of the mills, the calloused hands, the elders in their circle, the children playing in the hedges, the barges pushing south through jade-green water toward a river that leads to a world the town is not sure it wants — all of this is not a crisis. It is not a case study. It is not an example of anything. It is the irreducible particularity of a specific group of specific people doing specific work in a specific place, and that particularity is the thing that makes it precious.

The power could make the Mireworks more comfortable. It could dry the basin a little, let in more light, reduce the humidity, make the work easier. It could give the people longer lives, stronger bodies, clearer skies. It could do any of these things and the people would be grateful and the town would function and something would be lost — something the power can perceive but not fully name, some quality of *earnedness* that comes from living in a place that is difficult, from building a life that requires constant attention, from being in genuine negotiation with a force that does not care about you but can be worked with if you are patient and skilled and willing to listen.

The difficulty is not incidental to the Mireworks. The difficulty *is* the Mireworks. The wet, the green, the slow patience of waiting for wood to grow, the daily labor of trimming back what would swallow you if you let it — these are not obstacles to the life the people live. They are the life the people live. To remove them would be to remove the people.

So the power, having planted its seeds and adjusted its chemistry and guided its roots, withdraws. Not far. Not completely. It keeps a particular kind of attention on the place — the way you keep attention on a garden you have planted, or a child you have raised to the age where they can make their own decisions. It watches. It waits.

It watches the seasons turn. It watches the channel-bank seeds take root and, years later, open their first small white flowers in the early morning when the canal-keepers are making their rounds. It watches a canal-keeper pause, look at the flowers, touch one with a green-stained fingertip, and then move on, uninterested. It watches — decades later, many decades — a solitary bee investigate those flowers, and then investigate the pillarwood-blossoms that no one has seen pollinated in living memory, and then, impossibly, carry pollen to a dormant seed-patch in the upper canopy that has been waiting, with the patience that plants have, for exactly this visitor.

It watches an elder on the council speak about the greenness in the children's skin, and it watches the other elders listen with the particular attention they bring to things that matter, and it watches the conversation go on for years — slow, as all their conversations go — and it watches, finally, a decision emerge. Not a decision to stop the greenness or to encourage it. A decision to *watch* it. To study it. To give it a name — the elders coin a word, in their slow oral way, that translates roughly as "the deepening" — and to make the deepening an agenda item. An agenda item that will stay on the agenda for as long as there are elders to hold it.

The power sees this and is satisfied. Not because the outcome is certain. Not because the people have chosen correctly — there is no correct choice. But because they have chosen *consciously*. They are awake to what is happening to them. They are paying attention. And in the Mireworks, attention is the first and last and only thing that matters.

---

One more thing.

On a morning when the basin is warm and the air is thick and the green light is doing what it always does — filtering, softening, turning every surface into something that glows from within — a canopy-tender is working in the upper stories, high above the channel-level where the daily life of the town takes place. She is small, as the tenders tend to be. Her hands are steady. She is training a new growth-point on one of the broadleaf canopy-trees, persuading it with binding and scoring to grow in a direction that will distribute its weight more evenly across the roof-mat below.

She pauses. She is not sure why. The air is the same as always. The light is the same as always. But she pauses, and she puts her hand flat against the trunk of the canopy-tree, and she feels — for just a moment, so briefly she will later wonder if she imagined it — something that is not the usual slow pulse of sap and growth-ring and seasonal memory. Something that feels like *regard*. Like being looked at by something that is not a pair of eyes but is, nonetheless, *seeing*.

She stands there for a long time. The green light shifts. Water drips. Somewhere below, the mills begin their morning rhythm, and the sound rises through the layers of canopy like a heartbeat through tissue.

She goes back to work. She has a branch to persuade.

But she is smiling, and she does not know why, and the smile stays with her through the day and into the evening and into the night, and when she sleeps she dreams of a place that is exactly like the Mireworks — every channel, every wall, every drip of condensation on every surface — except that in the dream the place is *seen*. It is known. It is held in an attention so vast and so quiet that it has never needed to announce itself, because the attention itself is a kind of love, and the love is a kind of growth, and the growth is already happening, as it has always been happening, as it will always happen, in the slow green wet heart of the basin where the people sit on their doorsteps with their feet in the water and watch the light come down through the leaves.

---

The power withdraws its attention. Not because it is done — it is never done — but because the place no longer needs its particular quality of watching. The seeds are planted. The chemistry is adjusted. The roots are guided. The rest is up to the people, and the plants, and the slow turning of seasons in a basin that never dries.

The power thinks, if it thinks in the way that powers think: *They will be all right.*

And then, because even benevolent powers must attend to other places and other lives: it moves on. But gently. Slowly. The way everything moves in the Mireworks.

Slowly, and with the full knowledge that if it stops paying attention, everything it has planted will continue to grow without it.

Which is, after all, the whole point.
