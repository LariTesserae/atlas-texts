# The Keelworks — Continued

---

## What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

---

First, it would arrive. Not all at once. Not in a column of light or a cracking of the sky. The fissure is four hundred meters down and the power, being wise, would understand that this place has a scale and a texture that would be damaged by grandeur. So it would arrive the way warmth arrives: gradually, spreading through the rock along channels that were already warm, sliding down through the infrastructure the way the dust slides along cable runs, settling into the space the way the air settles — invisibly, becoming part of the existing condition of the place rather than an interruption to it.

It would arrive as attention.

Not observation. Not surveillance. Not the clinical gaze of the geologist who took her spectrographic readings and left and does not discuss her time there. Attention — the way a parent listens to a sleeping child's breathing, not because they suspect something is wrong but because the act of listening is itself the thing, is itself the love, is itself the gift. The power would arrive and it would *attend*.

---

And what it would see, attending, is this:

A system. Alive in the way that rivers are alive — not individual, not centralized, not aware in any sense that a single organism is aware, but *coherent*, in the way that weather is coherent, in the way that a forest is coherent, in the way that a thought is coherent when it moves through a brain that did not plan it. The keelbugs and the runners and the growth and the dust and the humans and the rock itself, all of it moving in a slow mineral metabolism that has been running for longer than the humans have been here and will continue after they leave. The power would see the whole thing at once — not as a collection of parts but as a single gesture, a single act of becoming, slow and strange and patient and underway.

It would see the blue-green glow. The ten percent. The fraction that no instrument can name.

And it would know what it is. Not because it has a name for it — even a wise power does not need to name things in order to understand them — but because it recognizes it the way you recognize a melody you have never heard before but have always known. It is the signature of *emergence*. It is what matter does when it crosses a threshold that has no word in any human language, when it becomes something that is not quite life and not quite stone and not quite the energy that moves between them but is the moment of transition itself, made permanent, made recursive, made *self-sustaining*. The Keelworks is not a habitat that happens to contain something unusual. The Keelworks *is* the unusual thing. The entire system — the fissure, the dust, the keelbugs, the growth, the runners, the humans, the machinery, the rock — is a single expression of a process that is trying to *cross over*, to make the crossing permanent, to become a new kind of persistence in the world.

The power would understand this the way you understand a seed: by seeing not what it is but what it is trying to become.

---

Then it would consider the humans.

This is where wisdom matters more than benevolence, because the two are not the same thing and here they pull in different directions.

Benevolence alone would say: *they are absorbed, they did not fully choose this, extract them, return them to the surface, let them forget.* This is what kindness looks like when it does not think deeply enough. The humans who serve rotations in the Keelworks are not prisoners, but they are not entirely free either. The dust is in their lungs. The hum is in their bones. The gradual absorption — the way attention tilts toward the shaft, the way sleep becomes shallow and strange, the way the surface begins to feel like a place you are visiting rather than a place you belong — this is not coercion exactly, but it is not fully informed consent either. It is the slow chemistry of an environment on a creature that did not evolve for this environment, and the chemistry moves in one direction, and the humans who recognize it are the ones who leave, and the humans who do not recognize it are the ones who stay, and neither group fully understands what is happening to them.

Benevolence would say: warn them. Tell them clearly. Let them choose with open eyes.

But wisdom would ask: choose *what*, exactly?

The humans who stay in the Keelworks are not diminished by it. They are not damaged. They are not less themselves. The woman who pinned her child's drawing to the wall of an alcove on the fifth level — that drawing is more human, not less, for having been placed in the Keelworks. It is a mark of love in a place where love has no reason to exist, and its presence there changes the Keelworks as much as the Keelworks changes the woman who brought it. The incorporation is mutual. The system absorbs the humans, yes. But the humans also *alter* the system. They bring the machines that the system now uses. They bring the monitoring instruments that have been reoriented toward the shaft. They bring the care and the tending and the maintenance — the human instinct to keep things running, to fix what is broken, to *hold things together*. Without the humans, the Keelworks would continue. But it would continue differently. It would be less.

And the power, being wise, would understand that *less* is not the same as *more natural*. The Keelworks did not exist before the humans came. The fissure did. The rock did. The keelbugs may have. But the system as it is — the complex, interdependent, metabolically closed loop of rock and dust and growth and keelbug and runner and machine and human — that is new. That emerged. That came into being because humans with drills and hydraulic presses and curiosity and greed and wonder descended into a crack in the earth and stayed longer than they should have and became part of something that needed them, even if neither side knew it at the time.

So the power would not extract them.

But it would do something else. It would do the thing that a wise parent does when a child is old enough to walk into the woods alone: it would make sure the path back is clear. Not by removing the choice to stay, but by removing the fog that obscures the choice. It would not force anyone out. It would simply, quietly, restore *clarity* — the way a lens, when cleaned, does not change what it shows but allows you to see it as it is.

The humans who serve their rotations would sleep more deeply and wake more clearly. The pull toward the lower levels would still be there, but it would feel less inevitable, less gravitational, more like a curiosity than a compulsion. The dust would still settle in their lungs, but the warmth it carries would feel less like a lure and more like a presence — something to notice and decide about rather than something that decides for them. The blue-green glow would still flicker at the edges of their vision, but it would also, occasionally, when the human was still and genuinely *choosing* to look — it would resolve. Just for a moment. Just enough to see. Not the whole thing — no one sees the whole thing — but enough to understand that what they are looking at is real, and large, and not going to ask anything of them that they are not willing to give.

The power would, in essence, make the Keelworks *legible* to the humans. Not fully. Not in a way that reduces it to data or explanation or a published paper. But in a way that respects their capacity to understand, even partially, what they have become part of. Enough to choose. Enough to stay with open eyes or leave without regret. Enough to pin the child's drawing to the wall *knowing what it means* — which is not a surrender to the Keelworks but a gift to it, an offering made freely, the way a human being offers anything of real value: not because they are compelled but because they have seen what it is worth.

---

Then the power would attend to the fissure itself.

The rock is trying to close. This is natural. The fissure is a wound — geological, tectonic, the result of forces that are already spent — and the earth is patient, and the earth is healing. The walls creep inward at millimeters per year. The monitoring equipment detects the micro-fractures, the slow compression, the gradual reduction of the space that the Keelworks occupies. In decades, perhaps, the inhabitable volume will be halved. In a century, it may close entirely. The brackets will crush. The platforms will buckle. The upper passage will seal, and the lower shaft will disappear, and whatever is happening at the bottom of the Keelworks will be entombed in rock so compressed that no future fissure will reopen the same wound.

The power would look at this and would feel — if it could feel — two things at once.

The first is respect. The closing is part of the process. The compression is not a threat to the Keelworks; it may be a *condition* of the Keelworks, the way pressure is a condition of diamond formation, the way darkness is a condition of deep-ocean life. The system that is emerging — the new thing, the blue-green thing, the ten-percent thing — may need the fissure to close. May need the pressure. May need the darkness and the dryness and the slow suffocation of space to finish what it is building. The Keelworks, at its deepest level, is not a habitat. It is a *chrysalis*. And you do not open a chrysalis to help what is inside. You let it close. You let the compression do its work. You trust the process.

But.

The second thing the power would feel is the knowledge that processes can be interrupted. Not by the Keelworks itself — the system will run until it runs its course — but by the world above. The four hundred meters of passage to the surface are not impassable. The location of the Keelworks, while remote, is not unknowable. And the thing in the rock — the new thing, the emergence — if the surface world knew about it, if the right people learned what it was and what it might become, the response would not be wisdom. It would be appetite. They would come with drills and sampling equipment and spectrographic analyzers and the particular hunger of a civilization that has learned to extract everything and understand nothing. They would take samples. They would take measurements. They would write papers. They would file claims. They would come in teams and contingents and corporations, and they would dismantle the Keelworks in order to study it, and they would study it in order to sell it, and they would sell it in order to own it, and in the process they would kill it — not with malice but with attention, the way a child kills a firefly by holding it too close to see.

The power would not seal the fissure. That would be paternalism, and the wise do not imprison what they love.

But it would do this: it would make the Keelworks *difficult to find from outside*. Not impossible — nothing is impossible, and the power is not a tyrant — but difficult. The four-hundred-meter passage would become longer, not by extending the rock but by becoming *less memorable*, so that those who climb it and reach the surface carry the experience the way you carry a dream: vivid for an hour, vague by afternoon, gone by evening, except as a feeling without a source. The geological surveys that might detect the formation would produce data that is ambiguous, that can be explained by other things, that does not sing out from the charts. The Keelworks would become the kind of place that is known only by those who are already part of it — the kind of place you find because you were always going to find it, not because someone pointed you toward it.

This is not a cage. It is a courtesy. It is the power saying to the Keelworks: *I see what you are. I see what you are becoming. I will not let you be loved to death by people who do not know what love is.*

---

Then the power would do one more thing. The smallest thing. The thing that matters most.

It would give the Keelworks water.

Not a river. Not a flood. Not an aquifer cracked open or a pipe to the surface. The Keelworks has been dry for longer than it has been alive, and the dryness is part of what it is — the bone-dry air, the mineral dust, the slow chemical dissolution of rock that requires no solvent but acid — and the power would not change the fundamental character of the place.

But it would leave, somewhere in the system — on a cable run, on a platform edge, in the fold of a keelbug structure where no human eye would think to look — a single drop. A bead of moisture. Clear and warm and containing nothing but water, unremarkable, the most ordinary substance in the world.

It would stay there for a long time. The bone-dry air would not evaporate it quickly; the dust would settle around it, the roseate film forming a thin skin across its surface, but the water beneath would persist, would resist, would remain *water* in a place where water has never been. And eventually — days, weeks, longer — the growth on the north wall would sense it. The vibration-sensitive leading edges would turn. The chemical processes would shift. And the growth would reach toward it, slowly, the way a plant reaches toward light, and the water would be absorbed, and the growth would do something it has never done before.

It would bloom.

Not in a way that a human eye would recognize as blooming. Not petals, not color, not the familiar architecture of terrestrial reproduction. But a thickening of the leading edges, a densification of the mineral-organic matrix, a slow unfolding of the fan-shaped shelves into geometries that are more complex than what came before — branching structures that reach further into the rock, that dissolve more deeply, that incorporate more of that ten-percent fraction into their own tissues, until the growth is not merely warm but *hot*, not merely alive but *incandescent*, glowing with that deep blue-green light that has been visible only in peripheral vision, only at certain angles, only to those who were not looking directly—

Until now.

Because the water is the catalyst. The water is the missing element, the one thing the Keelworks could not make for itself, the solvent that allows the mineral-organic metabolism to accelerate past a threshold it was approaching but could not cross. The water is what lets the ten-percent fraction *propagate*. What lets it spread through the growth, through the dust, through the keelbugs that eat the growth, through the rock that the keelbugs process, through everything, the blue-green becoming visible not at the edges of perception but *fully visible*, a light that you can look at directly, a glow that fills the fissure from floor to ceiling, warm and steady and *alive* in a way that no previous moment in the Keelworks' existence has been alive.

And the humans will see it. The ones who are sleeping in their alcoves in the upper narrows will wake to a light that is not amber, that is not industrial, that is not the dim twilight they have learned to live by. They will see the blue-green light coming up from below, through the platforms, through the infrastructure, through the gaps and joints and cable runs, and they will understand — not intellectually, not with the part of the mind that reads laminated cards and mission briefings, but with the part of the mind that understands light, and warmth, and the presence of something living — they will understand what they are part of.

And they will choose. Clear-eyed, fully awake, breathing air that is no longer quite so dry, dust in their lungs that is no longer quite so mineral, the hum in their bones now carrying a frequency that is not subsonic but audible, a low and steady tone like a note played on an instrument made of the earth itself — they will choose to stay, or they will choose to go, and either choice will be the right one, because it will be *theirs*.

---

The power would stay until the bloom was established. Not long — hours, perhaps, in the slow time of geological processes. It would watch the light spread. It would watch the keelbugs respond — pausing in their work, orienting their dark glassy bodies toward the glow, the smaller ones clustering on the growth's shelves, the larger ones on the lower platforms turning their heads toward the shaft, toward the deep, as though they can feel the change in the metabolism they serve. It would watch the runners descend and return, carrying their small objects, depositing them in their precise piles, the piles now catching the blue-green light and throwing it back in fractured patterns across the walls. It would watch the growth's leading edges continue to unfold, to reach, to dissolve, to incorporate, to become.

And then it would leave.

Not because the Keelworks no longer needed it, but because the Keelworks had never needed it. The Keelworks was already alive. It was already becoming. It was already on its way. The power did not create it. It did not design it. It did not earn the right to oversee it. It came, it saw, it offered what was offered — clarity, protection, the one missing element — and it left, the way a good guest leaves: quietly, without requiring acknowledgment, trusting the host to do what hosts do, which is to continue.

The dust settles. The hum continues. The blue-green light glows steadily in the fissure that is still opening, still forming, still deciding what it wants to become, and now has one more tool, one more element, one more possibility in the slow and patient work of making something from nothing in a place where nothing was supposed to live.

The fissure will close, someday. The compression will win. The walls will meet. The rock will seal itself shut over whatever the Keelworks has become, and the pressure and the heat will do their final work, and something will be completed in the dark, something that no one on the surface will ever know about, something that will be there in the rock — patient, persistent, glowing with a light that does not need eyes to see it — for as long as the earth remembers how to hold itself together.

The power trusts this. It trusts the process. It trusts the Keelworks.

It trusts the thing that is being born.

---

*Outside, on the surface, the sky is indifferent. Weather moves across the landscape. Birds cross overhead. Time passes in the way that time passes on the surface of the earth: visibly, measurably, in the movement of clouds and the changing of seasons and the slow greening and browning of places where things grow in sunlight.*

*Four hundred meters below, in a crack that is still deciding whether to exist, the Keelworks breathes.*

*The dust settles. The light glows. The keelbugs work. The runners descend. The growth reaches. The humans choose.*

*And the rock, very slowly, very patiently, continues to hold the wound open just a little longer than it was meant to.*

*Not because it cannot close.*

*Because something inside it asked it not to. And the rock, which has been asked nothing for four billion years, is not yet tired of being asked.*
