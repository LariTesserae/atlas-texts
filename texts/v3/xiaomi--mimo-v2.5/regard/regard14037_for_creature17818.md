# What the Power Does

---

It watches for a long time before it does anything at all. This is not indecision. It is the nature of a benevolent and wise power to understand that observation *is* a form of care, and that intervening too quickly — even with the best intentions — is how wise powers become merely powerful ones.

So it watches.

---

It watches the basin from above, from below, from inside the root-mat where the water moves through stone like blood through capillaries. It feels the temperature gradient between the canopy and the peat-muck floor. It counts — though it has no need to count, and does so only because something in the place invites it, a small child on a platform counting moths in the mist — it counts the species. Not to catalogue them. To know them. There is a difference. Cataloguing is ownership. Knowing is kinship.

It knows the boring beetles that eat the tunnel walls from within, and it knows the particular frequency of the tapping that tells a vine to split, and it knows the sound Delune's fire makes when the coals settle just right and the chimney-draft pulls the smoke upward through the hollow trunk in a steady, clean draw. It knows the weight of Tessick's hands, the calluses, the swollen knuckles, and it knows what she feels when she presses a fingertip against bark and reads the moisture and the grain and the slow electric pulse of water moving through xylem. It knows this because it has felt it too, reaching down through the stone and into the roots and into the living wood, feeling what Tessick feels but at a scale she cannot — the whole basin, all at once, a single vast organism breathing in the mist.

It sees what the people cannot see: the deep time. The slow filling of the basin. The patient work of roots and water converting stone into soil into peat into the soft dark foundation that holds everything. It sees the trajectory. In three thousand years, perhaps four, the basin will be full. The water will find new paths. The species will migrate. The high meadow that remains will bear no trace of the tunnels or the platforms or the people who lived among them, except perhaps a thin dark line in the soil where the compressed remains of three centuries of human habitation — bone, charred wood, the mineral traces of copper knives long since returned to earth — mark a brief and beautiful interruption in the mountain's long slow conversation with weather.

The power sees all of this and is not sad. Sadness requires a belief that things should be other than they are, and the power has no such belief. Sowren is what it is. Impermanent. Alive. Enough.

---

But there is a question the power sits with, turning it over the way Tessick turns a cutting in her hands before deciding where to place it:

*Is there anything this place needs that it cannot provide for itself?*

The answer, after a long watching, is: *Yes. But very little. And not what you would expect.*

Sowren does not need to be saved. It does not need to be made permanent. It does not need metal or writing or stone walls or any of the things the dry places below consider civilization. To impose any of these would be to kill the conversation that is Sowren, to replace the living Weft with something static and dead. The people would know this even if the power did not. Wynn knows it when she walks on flat dry stone and feels like she's walking on a corpse. Tessick knows it when she presses her feet into the soft root-mat and feels the ground breathe. Even Lir, who has never left the basin, knows it in her counting — the numbers change because everything changes, and the changing is the point, and if the numbers ever stopped changing, *that* would be the crisis.

No. Sowren does not need permanence. What Sowren needs is *time.*

Not infinite time. Not eternal life. Just enough time for the Weft to hold long enough for what it is weaving to matter. For Tessick to teach Orru what her hands can no longer feel. For Lir to grow into the full expression of her counting, to become the *rekkal* she was born to be, to read the patterns that the adults are too busy to see, and to speak them aloud before it is too late. For Delune to tend his fire through one more winter, and one more, and to pass the knowledge of the chimney-trees to whoever will inherit the scar on the wrist and the vigilance that will not let them sleep. For Sula, nearly blind, to weave the last hammocks her fingers will make, and to place those copper-awl calluses into the hands of the next Weaver, and to say *here, feel this, this strand is wrong, pull it out, start again.*

Time. Not a lot. Just enough.

---

So the power does something that is almost nothing.

It does not part the clouds. It does not still the water. It does not silence the frogs or tame the beetles or make the stone stop weeping. It does not build a wall against the slow filling of the basin, or summon a dry wind to cure the rot, or harden the air into something the dry places would recognize as hospitable.

What it does is this:

It adjusts the rainfall.

Not much. A fraction of a degree of angle on the prevailing wind that feeds the basin's weather. A nudge so small that no instrument — not that the people have instruments, but even if they did — could detect it. Just enough so that the water comes a little more steadily, a little more gently, in a pattern that favors the platform-trees over the peat accumulation. A hair's breadth of balance, shifted, so that the deepening of the basin floor slows — imperceptibly, but it slows. The roots grow a little more into living stone and a little less into accumulating soil. The great trees hold their grip a few centuries longer than they would have otherwise. The basin breathes.

This is all. The power does this and then it withdraws its attention from the hydrology and returns it to the place itself, because the rainfall was never really the point. The point was the people. The point was always the people.

---

And what the power does for the people is simpler still.

It does not speak to them. It does not appear in a vision or send a sign or plant a prophecy in the mouth of a sleeping elder. The people of Sowren have no word for gods. They have words for plants, for water, for growth and rot and the particular sound of a vine when it is ready to split. Adding a word for god would be adding a word for something outside the conversation, something that does not grow or rot or drip or hum, and the people would rightly reject it as a word for something dead.

So the power does not speak. Instead, it *listens.* It listens the way Tessick listens to a vine — with attention, with patience, with the understanding that the thing being heard is not performing for you but simply existing, and that your job is to match your awareness to its reality rather than the other way around.

It listens to Lir counting.

It hears the child count forty-two moths on a cool morning, and twenty-eight on a colder one, and it understands — as Lir herself does not yet fully understand — that what she is tracking is not numbers but *health.* The moths are the basin's respiratory rate. The ant-columns are its circulatory system. The dripping leaf that moved three centimeters to the left is the basin's skeleton shifting in its sleep. Lir is reading the vital signs of a living place, and the power attends to her counting the way a physician attends to a pulse — not to intervene, but to know. To know that the place is known. That someone, somewhere in the Weft, is paying attention to the pattern.

This matters. The power understands this in a way that is beyond reason, in the way that Tessick understands the difference between a vine that is growing and one that is merely alive. Being known is not the same as being saved. But it is the prerequisite for both. A place that is not known to anyone is a place that cannot be mourned, cannot be tended, cannot be learned from. Lir's counting is the basin's way of knowing itself — the ecosystem developing a nervous system, the Weft becoming conscious of its own weaving. The power does not need to foster this. It is already happening. But it can do one thing:

It can make sure that the numbers Lir counts remain countable.

The fraction-of-a-degree adjustment to the rainfall is part of this. But there is another part, smaller and stranger, and this is the part the power hesitates over, because it is the closest thing to a gift that a power of this kind can give, and gifts are dangerous things.

---

What the power gives, if giving is the word, is *coherence.*

It is not a thing that can be described in the language of the dry places. It is more like a quality that the air acquires — or perhaps always had — a slight increase in the connectedness of things. The roots respond to each other a little more readily. The fungal networks that thread through the peat-muck carry chemical signals a little faster, a little more clearly. The tapping that Tessick teaches Orru resonates a little further through the living walls, reaches a few more nodes of growth, calls a few more branches into being. The smoke from Delune's hearth rises through the hollow trunk with a draft that is marginally steadier, marginally cleaner, carrying its heat and its preservative chemistry a little more efficiently to the places that need it.

None of this would be noticeable to anyone. The people of Sowren would not look up from their work and say *something has changed.* They would say, if they said anything at all, that the growing has been good this season. That the tunnels feel strong. That the platform-trees seem vigorous. That the vines listen a little more readily, as if in a good mood, as if the conversation is going well.

Tessick might notice, in some wordless way, that the cutting she carried yesterday took root faster than she expected. That the new growth appeared in the right direction without her needing to re-train it. That the wound in the tunnel wall sealed over with healthy bark in half the usual time. She would attribute this to her skill, or to luck, or to the general mood of the growing season, and she would be right in every case, because the power's gift is not separate from these things. It is woven into them. It is another strand in the Weft.

Orru might notice that his fingertips seem more sensitive this year, that the difference between a living vine and a merely alive one feels sharper, more distinct, as if his senses have been tuned a fraction closer to the truth of what he is touching. He would think this is what it means to grow older, to learn, to deepen. He would be partly right.

Lir might notice — and this is the part the power watches most carefully — that her counting has become easier. Not that there are more things to count, but that the patterns are clearer. The numbers arrange themselves in her mind with less effort, as if the relationships between them are slightly more visible than they were before, as if the connections she intuits are confirming themselves a little more readily, giving her more to work with, more to see. She would not understand why. She would just keep counting, and the counting would keep making sense, and one day — not yet, not for years — she would look up from her numbers and speak a truth about the basin that no one else can see, and the people of Sowren would listen, because they have learned to listen to the *rekkal*, even when what the *rekkal* says is frightening.

And what Lir will say, in time, is this: *The western root-mat is thinner than it was. The eastern platform-tree's second branch is heavy. The ant-columns have moved because the water table has shifted. In three years, maybe four, the third passage from the north will collapse.*

And the people will listen, and they will act, and they will train new vines and shift their hammocks and rebuild the passage, and the conversation will continue, and the Weft will hold, and the power — if it can be said to feel anything — will feel the satisfaction of a gardener watching a cutting take root. Not pride. Not possessiveness. Just the quiet recognition that a living thing has been allowed to go on living, and that this is the only miracle there is.

---

There is one more thing the power does, and this is the thing it does for itself, if a power of this kind can be said to have a self.

It holds the memory.

Not written down. Not stored in any medium that could rot or burn or be lost. Held in the way that the power holds everything — in the quality of its attention, in the depth of its knowing. It holds the memory of Sowren as it is now, in this moment, in this particular configuration of vine and mist and fire and counting child. It holds the sound of the frog-chorus on a warm night. The smell of Delune's smoke threading through the hollow trunk. The feel of root-mat under bare feet. The sound of Sula's copper awl punching through fiber. The taste of the mint-and-copper tea. The weight of Tessick's body on a platform that shifted beneath her three hundred years ago and has never stopped shifting.

It holds all of this not because the memory is needed — the power could reconstruct every detail from the physical facts of the place if it chose — but because holding it is itself a form of the conversation. To remember Sowren is to participate in the Weft. To carry its patterns in awareness is to add one more strand to the fabric. The power is not outside the weaving. It never was. It is one more organism in the basin, one more participant in the conversation, the largest and quietest and most patient of all the species that grip the stone and drink the water and grow from the dying and die from the growing and leave behind, in the end, only the memory of their passage — which is, in the end, everything. Which is, in the end, enough.

---

The mist falls. The water drips. The fire crackles on the hearth-platform and Delune watches it with his scarred wrist and his sleepless eyes. Tessick warms her hands and feels the damp in her knees and watches the smoke rise through the hollow trunk toward the canopy, toward the grey sky, toward the clouds that sit in the basin like a second kind of water. Orru taps on a wall in the tunnel below — *tok tok tok tok tok* — and somewhere a vine begins to split. Sula's fingers fly across a half-finished weave, pulling out a bad strand, starting again. Wynn stands on the eastern lip, looking down at the valley settlements, feeling the dry stone through her shoes, already homesick for the breathing ground. Lir sits on her platform in the gathering dark and counts the moths as they land on the railing — thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three — and somewhere in the deep attention of the world, something counts with her, not to interfere, not to save, but to know. To know this place and these people and this particular unrepeatable moment of the Weft, and to hold it, and to let it be what it is.

Alive. Impermanent. Enough.

More than enough.
