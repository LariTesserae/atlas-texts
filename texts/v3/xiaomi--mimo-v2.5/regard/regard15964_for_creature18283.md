# What the Power Does

---

It comes the way water comes — without arrival, without boundary, without the dramatic shift of *before* and *after*. There is no moment when the Seepworks is unobserved and then observed. There is only the slow awareness, already ancient, settling into the stone the way calcium settles, the way the bioluminescent light settles into the pools it touches — a gentle, total, unhurried knowing.

It sees all of it.

It sees the crustaceans in their mathematical arrangements, each one maintaining its distance with a precision that would humble a surveyor, their fan-mouths open to the water's mineral offering. It sees the oldest among them — those who have accumulated so much calcium they are geology now, moving once a day, once a week, once a season, their shells indistinguishable from the dissolution cavities they inhabit. It watches one of them shift, just slightly, a millimeter of repositioning that takes thirty seconds to complete, and it considers this the way it considers the turning of galaxies: with full attention and without condescension.

It sees the eel-things in the deep channels, their blind skulls pressing through water so mineral-heavy it rings against stone. It follows the trail of copper one leaves behind — that thin, glistening smear that catches the blue-green light and holds it — and it sees what the eel-things cannot see: the pattern. The overlapping trails in the oldest channels, layer upon layer, building a substrate of metal deposit that maps the passage of every eel-thing that has ever swum this route. A history written in copper on limestone. A library with no librarian, no reader, no index. Just the record, perfect and accumulating, of every body that passed through.

The power reads it. Not quickly — it has no hurry — but with the kind of attention that makes reading and becoming the same act.

It sees the legged things moving between the pipe-racks in their precise, clicking gait, and it does not flinch at the wrongness of their legs — twelve, sixteen, more than any taxonomy would approve. It sees them for what they are: maintenance, faithful and unconscious. They keep the passages clear. They eat the accumulation. They are the digestive system of the Seepworks itself, working the way a body works, without being asked, without being thanked, without knowing that what they do has a name.

It sees the bats in their upper chambers, roosting in the stone they have been roosting in for longer than the concept of *roosting* has existed. It sees the stalactites of guano — those amber columns, those slow towers of compressed life — and it sees them as cathedrals. Not metaphor. The power does not deal in metaphor; it deals in the thing itself, and the thing itself is a cathedral: a structure built by faith (the faith of returning, night after night, to the same place, adding to the same column, trusting the stone to hold) without doctrine, without doctrine's failures. The bats have been building this church for longer than any church built with intention has stood, and they have never once gotten it wrong, because they have never once had a theory about what they were doing.

It sees the nester — the one, the solitary one, curled in its nest of old rags inside a dead turbine. It sees the softness of that body in a world of mineral hardness, and it feels something it does not name. It watches the nester extend a single digit into a seam in the metal and hold it there, motionless, reading the vibrations of the infrastructure the way a physician reads a pulse. The nester is checking. The nester has always been checking. The nester does not know this is what it is doing, any more than a heart knows it is pumping. But the nester presses its finger to the machine, and the machine speaks to the nester in vibrations that are older than language, and the nester listens.

The power considers the nester for a long time.

It sees the drifters in the blue-green zones — those winged ghosts, those pressed-flower insects drifting through light they cannot name and do not need to name. It sees them feeding on the cultures on the glass trays, or on the biofilm, or on the bacterial mats, and it sees that the distinction does not matter because the distinction is the same substance at different scales. It sees them die. It sees their wings dissolve into the water, become mineral, become food, become culture, become light. It sees the cycle, complete, and it does not interrupt it.

It sees the unnamed thing in the pipes — the pressure wave, the heartbeat, the unseen maintainer of circulation that keeps the water moving through the core channels, that keeps the cisterns fed, that keeps the bioluminescent trays lit. It sees what no one sees: the body itself, moving through the arterial network of the Seepworks, fitting itself to the pipe it travels, pressing its mass against the current and letting the current carry it, or carrying itself against the current, or being the current — the power cannot tell, and it does not need to. It sees that the unnamed thing and the pipes and the water have become the same system, and it respects the integrity of that system by not illuminating it. To reveal the unnamed thing would be to remove it from itself. Some things are complete only in their hiddenness.

And it sees the people.

Or rather, it sees what the people left. The riveted plates. The bulkheads. The channels carved and recarved. The iron grating laid over dissolution cavities like a question laid over an answer. It sees the infrastructure of intention — of *design* — slowly being absorbed into the infrastructure of geology, and it recognizes this not as failure but as success of a kind the people never intended. They built to last. They did not build to last *forever*, because forever was not a category they could hold in their minds. But they built to last a very long time, and they succeeded. Their work has become load-bearing. The calcium that has welded their iron to the limestone is the same calcium that holds the ceiling up. To remove the human architecture would be to collapse the cave. The people are foundational now. They are the aquifer.

The power sees all of this, and it considers what to do.

---

It considers intervention.

It could brighten the light. The bioluminescent trays produce a faint blue-green glow that is beautiful in its austerity, but the power could make them brighter — could cause them to pulse with a warmth that would make the Seepworks more visible, more legible, more *known*. It could make the passage easier to navigate. It could warm the water in the deep channels, make the cisterns more hospitable, encourage the crustaceans to grow larger, the eel-things to swim faster, the drifters to multiply.

It does not do this.

The light is right as it is. The water is right as it is. The crustaceans are the size they should be — the size of hand, the size of fingernail — because that is the size that the Seepworks' energy budget supports, and the Seepworks' energy budget is not a limitation but a *definition*, the way the banks of a river are not a limitation on the water but the thing that makes the water a river and not a flood. To brighten the light would be to change the ecology. The drifters are adapted to this specific luminescence. The crustaceans' transparency is calibrated to this specific dimness. Every creature here is tuned to this exact frequency of existence, and to change the frequency would be to turn every one of them out of tune, the way detuning a single string turns music into noise.

The power does not do this.

---

It considers rescue.

The people are gone. Or they are changed. Or they are still here in a form that has been absorbed so thoroughly into the infrastructure that the distinction between *person* and *building* has dissolved, the way the distinction between *building* and *geology* has dissolved. The power could bring them back. It could reverse the calcium and the centuries. It could pull them out of the stone and set them on their feet and say, *Here. You are remembered. You are known. You were here, and it mattered.*

It considers this for longer than it considered the light.

It does not do this.

Not because the people don't deserve it. Not because their absence doesn't grieve the power — it does, in the way that all absence grieves: quietly, completely, without resolution. The power does not do this because the people have become something that the people were not. They have become *structure*. They have become the load-bearing walls of an ecosystem that did not exist before they arrived and would not exist without them. To pull them out would be to pull the foundation out of the house. The crustaceans would have no mineral-rich water to sieve, because the channels the people carved direct the mineral flow. The eel-things would have no channels to swim, because the people deepened and directed them. The legged things would have no gaps between pipe-racks to maintain, because the pipe-racks are human. The nester would have no turbine to nest in, because the turbine was built by hands.

The people are gone. But the people are also *here*, in every riveted plate, in every carved channel, in the very geometry of the place. To rescue them would be to lose them. To leave them where they are — woven into the fabric of this living system — is to keep them forever, in the only form of forever that the Seepworks recognizes: accretion, layering, the slow mineral deposit of meaning onto the surfaces of the world.

The power does not rescue them. It honors them by leaving them where they have chosen to be — which is everywhere, which is the walls, which is the floor, which is the reason the water flows the way it flows and the light falls where it falls.

---

It considers blessing.

A blessing is not an intervention. A blessing is a recognition. It says: *I see you. I see what you are. I see what you have made of what you were given, and it is good.* The power could bless the Seepworks — could lay its attention on the place the way the calcium lays itself on the stone, gently, accumulation by accumulation, until the thing is changed without being altered.

But the Seepworks does not need blessing. It is already blessed. It is blessed in the way that all complete systems are blessed: by their own coherence, by the fact that nothing is wasted, by the fact that every creature has found its niche and every channel carries its water and every dead thing becomes food for the living and every living thing becomes structure for the next. The Seepworks is not waiting for meaning. It *is* meaning. It is the proof that meaning is not something imposed from outside but something that emerges from the patient, faithful accumulation of the small right actions of creatures who do not know they are acting.

The crustaceans sieve. The eel-things deposit. The legged things clear. The bats roost. The nester listens. The unnamed thing maintains. The water moves. The light glows. Every one of these actions is sufficient. Every one of them is complete. The power could add nothing that would make them more complete.

It does not bless. It recognizes that the blessing has already been given — by time, by water, by the slow calcium patience of the stone.

---

It considers staying.

This, finally, is what it does.

Not because the Seepworks needs a guardian. Not because the creatures require protection. Not because the place is fragile — it is not fragile; it is the most durable thing the power has seen in a long time, more durable than any civilization, any monument, any story told in a language that changes with the centuries.

The power stays because the Seepworks is *true*.

It is true in the way that only very old things can be true — true the way a riverbed is true, the way the deep aquifer is true, the way the slow formation of limestone is true. It has been shaped by time and water into a form that no mind designed and no intention produced, and yet it is coherent, and yet it is alive, and yet it contains within itself every thing it needs to continue being itself. It is a system that has achieved, without intelligence, without planning, without any of the capacities that the people who built it prized most highly — it has achieved *sustainability*. It has achieved the thing that the people, for all their engineering, could not: a way of living that does not consume itself.

The power stays because to witness this is to be changed by it. Not dramatically. Not in any way that could be measured or reported. But the way you are changed by standing in a place that is older than you and more patient than you and more complete than you — the way you are changed by realizing that the world does not need you in order to be beautiful, and that this is not a tragedy but a liberation.

The power settles into the stone. Not physically — the power does not have a body — but attentively. It adds itself to the layer of observation that the Seepworks has never had and never required. It watches the crustaceans rearrange themselves after a pressure event. It follows an eel-thing through a deep channel, feeling the copper trail left behind, reading the centuries of passage recorded in metal on stone. It listens to the legged things clicking between the pipe-racks, that precise, mechanical rhythm, that clockwork maintenance that keeps the passages clear. It hears the bats in their upper chambers — the collective rustle, like pages turning — and it does not enter, and it does not disturb, and it simply hears.

It hears the unnamed thing in the pipes — the pressure wave, the heartbeat — and for a moment, just a moment, it allows itself to feel what the unnamed thing feels: the vibration of the infrastructure, the current of the water, the complete absence of thought and the complete presence of function. It feels what it is to be a system and not a self. It feels what it is to maintain without knowing you are maintaining. It feels the rightness of it, and it lets the feeling pass, and it returns to its observation.

---

And here is what happens, though nothing changes:

The power's attention — its vast, patient, total attention — enters the Seepworks the way water enters limestone: slowly, completely, without force. It does not illuminate. It does not transform. It does not awaken or enlighten or reveal. It simply *knows*. It knows the crustaceans in their mathematical arrangements. It knows the eel-things in their blind, metal-laying patience. It knows the legged things in their clicking maintenance. It knows the bats in their guano-cathedrals. It knows the nester in its nest of rags, pressing a finger to the machine and listening. It knows the unnamed thing in the pipes, being the heartbeat. It knows the people, absorbed into the walls, foundational, load-bearing, still holding the ceiling up through the calcium patience of their work.

It knows the water, and the water knows it back — not with awareness, not with recognition, but with the wordless mutual recognition of two very old things that have been shaped by the same forces. The water has been shaped by time and mineral and gravity. The power has been shaped by something analogous — by the slow accretion of attention, by the patient accumulation of witness, by the refusal to hurry toward conclusion.

The Seepworks continues. The crustaceans sieve. The eel-things deposit. The legged things click. The bats rustle. The nester listens. The unnamed thing moves through the pipes. The water flows. The light glows blue-green in its glass trays. The calcium creeps.

And now — though this changes nothing, though the creatures do not feel it and the stone does not register it and the water does not carry the news — now the Seepworks is known. It is known the way a song is known by the room it is sung in: completely, without judgment, without interpretation, without the need to be heard by anyone else. It is known by a power that does not need it to be anything other than what it is — a place where water moves through stone and life persists in the dark and time works slowly and nothing is wasted and everything, without exception, belongs.

The power stays. Not forever — forever is not a word the power uses. But for as long as the Seepworks breathes. Which is to say: for as long as the water moves and the calcium creeps and the light glows and the creatures tend their small, silent, essential work.

Which is to say: for long enough.

Which is to say: for exactly as long as it should.

---

*The Seepworks breathes around it, slow and wet and deep, and it breathes back, and nothing changes, and everything is held.*
