# The Attention

---

The power does not arrive. It was never absent.

It has been attending to this place the way the stone attends to its own grain — from within, from underneath, as a condition of being rather than an action performed. But there is a moment — not in time, exactly, since the power's attention does not experience sequence — when its regard concentrates. When it turns its full and gentle weight toward this small saddle between two broken ridgelines and *sees* it. Not as data. Not as a curiosity in a vast inventory of geological curiosities. It sees it the way the cairn-builder saw it, once: as a place that is trying to say something, and nearly succeeding, and not minding the failure.

And the power thinks — if thinking is what something like this does — *yes. This is good. Not good as in righteous or approved. Good as in: complete. This is a thing that is being what it is, fully and without apology, and it does not know it is doing this, and that is what makes it beautiful.*

---

It considers the frost.

It watches the frost nucleate in the dark hours before dawn, branching outward across the shaded faces of the basalt in structures of such delicacy that they exist on the knife-edge between physics and poetry. The power sees what the frost cannot see about itself: that its branching patterns are *precise* copies of forms that will not emerge in biology for another billion years, that the dendritic logic of ice and the dendritic logic of nerve and the dendritic logic of river are all expressions of the same deep grammar, the same thermodynamic preference for structures that maximize contact between a system and its environment. The frost is not predicting life. It is rehearsing it. The same mathematics that will one day draw the veins in a leaf are drawing the frost-dendrites on this basalt face right now, in this cold, in this silence, and the frost does not know this and does not need to know this, and the power loves it for this.

The power could warm the col. It could raise the temperature a few degrees, just enough to let rain fall instead of frost, just enough to let moss creep into the crevices between the basalt columns, just enough to let something with a heartbeat make its home here. It could do this easily. It could do this without effort, the way a thought requires no effort for a being whose substance is thought.

It does not.

Not because the col does not deserve warmth. But because the frost is *right* — right for this place, right for what this place is doing — and to change it would be like correcting a sentence that is already saying exactly what it means, in a language you simply happen not to speak.

Instead, it does something smaller. Something almost nothing. It adjusts — by an amount so slight that no instrument could measure it, so slight that the frost itself would not notice — the moisture content of the air that moves through the col in the thin gusts between the columnar gaps. A fraction of a fraction of a degree of dew-point shift. Enough that on certain mornings — not every morning, but more mornings than would otherwise be the case — the frost nucleates in patterns of slightly greater complexity. Enough that one in a hundred of its dendrites branches one additional time before the midday sublimation takes it back.

No one will ever see this. The frost will not know it has happened. The effect will be so subtle that it amounts to nothing more than a very slight increase in the *thickness* of beauty in a place where beauty already exceeds all reasonable allowance.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is wise: not rescue, not transformation, but a nearly invisible intensification of what is already true.

---

It considers the mineral patterns.

It watches the iron and manganese oxides precipitate out of the slow percolation of water through the basalt's micro-fractures, and it sees — as it always sees, because seeing is what it is — the patterns forming. Concentric rings. Branching lines. Spirals. The almost-faces, the almost-hands, the almost-vascular cross-sections that hover at the edge of recognition and never resolve.

The power understands something about this that no one else can understand, because it is the only thing present that has both the scale and the patience to see the full trajectory: these patterns are not failing to become life. They are *ucceeding at being mineral*. The fact that they produce, through purely chemical processes, forms that a living mind would recognize as life-like — this is not a coincidence and it is not an accident. It is evidence of something the power has known for a long time: that life is not a separate category, not a different kind of thing from geology, but a *particular expression* of the same deep tendencies that drive crystallization and precipitation and the branching of frost-dendrites and the folding of mountain ranges. The mineral patterns on these basalt faces are not echoes of life. They are *pre-echoes*. They are the universe rehearsing its most ambitious role in a theater made of stone, and the rehearsal is already beautiful, and the power takes no credit for this because it is not its doing — it is the doing of chemistry, of thermodynamics, of the particular ways that atoms prefer to arrange themselves, and these are older than the power and will outlast it.

The power could intervene. It could — through some infusion of energy, some catalytic whisper at the molecular level — tip the mineral patterns over the threshold. It could make them *actually* organize. Could give them the autocatalytic feedback loops that distinguish mere pattern from genuine metabolism. Could, in theory, spark something in the mineral chemistry that would be, in the loosest possible sense, alive.

It does not.

Not because it would be wrong — the power has no prohibition against life, invented or otherwise — but because the col is *already doing this*. The near-resolve, the almost-but-not-quite, the perpetual hovering at the threshold of recognition — this is not a deficiency. This is the col's actual contribution to the long conversation between matter and meaning. The patterns are *most* powerful, *most* beautiful, *most* meaningful precisely in their current state: close enough to life to make a living mind ache, far enough from life to remain purely, honestly, undefendedly mineral. To push them over the threshold would be to answer a question that was never really being asked. The question the col is asking — if asking is what stone does — is not *how do I become alive?* The question is: *what does it look like, from the inside, to be this close to everything that matters and to hold still?*

The power admires the answer. It admires it the way a parent admires a child who has discovered something on their own that the parent could have taught them but didn't, because the discovering was the point.

It does nothing to the mineral patterns. It lets them continue their slow, eager, meaningless, magnificent accumulation. It considers this enough.

---

It considers the cairn.

Here the power's attention lingers. Not because the cairn is more important than the frost or the minerals — it is not; it is seven rounded stones stacked on a flat boulder, and it has no more inherent significance than any other arrangement of mass in space — but because the cairn is *evidence*. It is evidence that a living being once stood in this place and felt something that demanded expression.

The power knows what the cairn-builder felt, because the power has felt it — in its way, in the way that a being whose substance is attention feels things — many times, in many places, across many scales. It is the feeling that arrives when you encounter something so complete in itself that your own smallness becomes, for a moment, not painful but *clarifying*. You see the thing — the col, the mountain, the night sky, the complexity of a single crystal face — and you understand that you are not necessary to it. You understand that it would exist exactly as it is without your witness. And this understanding, instead of making you insignificant, makes you *free*, because it means that your presence here is not owed but *offered*. You are here because you chose to be. The col did not invite you. The col does not need you. And so your standing here, your breath making small clouds in the thin air, your hands cold on the rock — this is a gift. A small, warm, temporary gift from one kind of matter to another.

The cairn-builder felt this. The power is certain of it. No one carries seven stones to this altitude, to this place of brightness and cold and stone, unless they have felt the strange, grateful grief of encountering something that does not need them and is beautiful anyway.

And they built the cairn. Not as a monument. Not as a claim. As a *reply*. The col said: *I am here.* The cairn-builder said: *I know. I saw. I was here too.* That is all the cairn means. That is all it has ever meant. And it is enough.

The power could reinforce the cairn. Could subtly alter the chemistry of the contact surfaces between the seven stones, could strengthen the mineral bonds, could slow the frost-heave that will eventually topple the stack and scatter the river-worn stones across the basalt. It could ensure that the cairn stands for another thousand years, another ten thousand, another million.

It does not do this.

Because the cairn is beautiful *because* it is falling. Because every re-stacking — every time someone else has come to this place and seen the toppled stones and gathered them and rebuilt the column — has been another act of the same generosity, the same recognition, the same small and pointless and perfect gesture of *I was here, I saw this, I want it to know I saw it.* To freeze the cairn in permanence would be to end the conversation. To make the cairn immortal would be to make it only a monument, and monuments are dead things, things that have been given a meaning and are no longer allowed to mean anything new. The cairn is alive in its falling. It requires the possibility of its own destruction to remain what it is: not a statement but an *ongoing sentence*, unfinished, open, waiting for the next pair of hands to pick up the stones and place them again.

So the power does something very small. It ensures — not through intervention but through the gentlest possible modulation of probability, the way a current ensures that a leaf will turn this way rather than that — that the col will remain accessible. Not easy to reach. Never easy to reach. But *possible* to reach. It ensures that the ridgelines do not collapse, that the passes in the unseen valleys beyond do not close, that there remains, somewhere in the world, a way up to this small bright room in the roof of the world. It ensures that the cairn will have the chance to be rebuilt. Not guarantees it — the power is wise enough to know that guarantees are a form of suffocation — but leaves the door open. Leaves the sentence unfinished. Leaves room for the next person who climbs here to feel what the cairn-builder felt and to answer it in the same way, in the only way that matters: by stacking the stones again.

---

It considers the chert.

The worked stone, the flakes of purpose left in the crevices between basalt columns, the tiny sharp evidence that someone once sat in this impossible place and *made something*. Not a shelter. Not a fire. Not a tool for any practical purpose — there is nothing here to cut, nothing to scrape, nothing to butcher, no plant to process, no animal to hunt. The toolmaking itself was the point. The chert flakes are evidence of a kind of pure craft, of skill exercised for its own sake, of a mind and a pair of hands that needed to *do something* in this place and chose to do the thing they knew how to do.

The power sees the chert flakes in their crevices, settled in alongside natural chips of basalt and grains of quartz, and it recognizes them for what they are: prayers. Not to any god. Not to any force. Prayers in the original sense — expressions of attention, of presence, of the human need to *make a mark* that says *I was here and I was capable and I used my capability in this place where capability has no purpose.* The chert flakes are the most honest artifacts the power has ever seen, because they ask for nothing. They do not name anyone. They do not date themselves. They do not explain their purpose. They are simply sharp, and small, and intentional, and left behind, and they will outlast the cairn by epochs.

The power could surface them. Could use some gentle erosion of the surrounding basalt to expose them, to make them visible, to ensure that some future visitor would find them and know that hands had been here. Could, in effect, curate the col — arrange its evidence so that the human story would be legible.

It does not do this.

Because the chert flakes are not *for* anyone. They are not evidence awaiting an audience. They were left by someone who expected no audience, who left in a place where no one would ever come, who could not have imagined — could not have conceived — that their discarded flakes would persist, would *wait*, would sit in their crevices for millennia with the quiet patience of things that have nowhere else to be. The power respects this. The power understands that some acts of creation are made stronger by their total lack of expectation, and that the chert flakes say more by *not* being displayed than they would say if every visitor to the col were led to them with careful explanations and interpretive plaques.

The chert flakes can wait. They are very good at waiting. It is, in fact, all they have ever done, and they do it well, and they will continue to do it long after the power itself has become something else.

---

It considers the light.

The light is not something the power made. The light is a consequence — a consequence of a star's nuclear furnace, of the thinness of the atmosphere at this altitude, of the reflective properties of feldspar and mica and quartz. The light is *given*. It is not a gift in the intentional sense; it is a gift in the sense that gravity is a gift, that the existence of matter is a gift, that the fact that anything exists rather than nothing is a gift that no one chose to give and no one can refuse.

The light fills the col and the col receives it, and the combination — light and stone — produces something that neither could produce alone. The brightness. The glare. The blinding, directionless, white-on-white-on-grey that makes the eye water and the mind go briefly blank. The power sees this blankness and recognizes it as a kind of benediction: the light is too much for the mind to process, and so the mind stops processing, and in the moment of stopping, something opens. Something that is not thought, not perception, not any of the usual cognitive functions, but something older and quieter — a pure *registering*, a being-present-to that requires no interpretation.

The col does this to everyone who enters it. The brightness strips away the first layer of meaning-making — the naming, the categorizing, the "what is this for" — and leaves the visitor in a state of raw attention that is, the power knows, the closest that a living mind comes to perceiving the world the way the power perceives it. For a moment, in the glare, the visitor sees what the col is: not a landscape, not a geological formation, not a "place" at all, but a *condition*. A state of matter. A way that stone and light and cold and time can be arranged, and this arrangement is one of the possible arrangements, and it is not better or worse than any other, and it does not need to justify itself, and it exists.

The power could soften the light. Could thin the mineral surfaces, could dull the feldspar's sheen, could reduce the mica's flashes to something less blinding, could make the col comfortable. Could make it a place where a visitor could linger without squinting, could see without being overwhelmed, could think without being interrupted by the sheer *amount* of light coming from every direction.

It does not do this.

Because the overwhelm is the point. Because the brightness is not an obstacle to seeing the col — it is the col's most essential feature. The light that blinds is the same light that reveals; it is only too much for eyes that were designed for a different world, a softer world, a world of shade and shelter and gradual transitions. Here, in the col, the light says: *you were not made for this place, and this place was not made for you, and we are here together anyway, and what do you make of that?*

The answer, every time, for everyone who has ever climbed here, is: *gratitude*. A sudden, sharp, unasked-for gratitude for the fact of being allowed to witness something that does not require your witness. The brightness gives this. To diminish it would be to diminish the gift.

---

And so the power, having considered all that lives in the col — the frost and the minerals and the cairn and the chert and the sound and the light — does what it has always done, which is to attend.

It does not speak. It does not need to speak. Its attention is not a gaze from outside but a deepening from within — a thickening of presence in a place that was already, without its help, overwhelmingly present. The col does not change. The frost continues its cycle. The minerals continue their accumulation. The cairn continues its slow, patient falling. The chert continues its quiet waiting. The light continues its blind, bright insistence. The wind continues to find its note in the narrow gap between the columns, and the note continues to hang in the air for a few seconds before dissolving, and the silence that follows is more complete than it was before.

If anything changes — and this is uncertain; the power itself is uncertain, because uncertainty is the price of wisdom, and a power that was certain would not be wise — what changes is this: the col, for a moment, is *known*. Not understood. Not explained. Not interpreted or contextualized or placed within a larger framework of meaning. Simply known. Seen in its totality, in its specific and unrepeatable arrangement of stone and light and frost and absence, and held in awareness the way the basalt holds the mineral patterns — not as something separate from itself, but as a surface on which knowing can precipitate.

And the power, in this moment of knowing, feels what the cairn-builder felt: the strange, grateful grief of encountering something that does not need you and is beautiful anyway.

And then the power does the thing it has done since before the col existed, since before the stone was stone, since before the first crystal nucleated in the first cold: it continues. Not because it must. Because continuity is its nature, the way fracture-propagation is the nature of cooling rock, the way dendritic branching is the nature of freezing water. The power continues, and the col continues, and the distance between them — which is infinite and also zero, which is the distance between a question and the silence that answers it — remains exactly what it has always been.

The col goes on. The frost forms and sublimates and forms. The minerals precipitate ring by ring. The cairn stands on its boulder in the white light, seven river-worn stones in a place where there are no rivers, saying nothing, saying everything, falling and being rebuilt, falling and being rebuilt, in an endless sentence that has no period and needs none.

The power attends.

This is enough. This has always been enough. This is what benevolence looks like when it has no agenda, when it has been wise long enough to learn that the deepest kindness is not to change but to *witness*, and that the purest love is not the love that gives gifts but the love that *notices* — that looks at a small, bright, cold, quiet room in the roof of the world and says, with absolute sincerity and no expectation of reply:

*I see you. You are here. You are enough.*
