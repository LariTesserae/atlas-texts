# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

---

The power came to the Table not as a visitor comes, but as attention comes — slowly, without arrival, without displacement of air. It had been aware of many places. It had been aware of the Raptor, too, and of the laboratories where the Raptor was built, and of the long chain of inquiry that had led engineers to study the locomotion of extinct animals in order to build machines that could walk. It understood, in the way that such powers understand, that these were connected — that the engineers and the people of the Table were both engaged in the same ancient project: learning to move through a world that did not ask for your presence.

But the Table first. Always the Table first, because the Table had been waiting longer.

---

The power did not have a body. It had a direction — a tendency toward care, shaped by something like compassion but operating at a scale where compassion becomes indistinguishable from physics. It noticed things. It noticed the way the columns held water in their crater-pools the way a cupped hand holds rainwater: imperfectly, with loss, but with great tenderness. It noticed the way the people pressed cloth to wet surfaces in the dry seasons, wringing dew into gourd-containers, and the way a mother would let a child drink first. It noticed the sound — that immense, deafening, necessary sound — and understood what it meant: that this entire ecosystem ran on vibration the way other ecosystems run on sunlight, that the columns listened to the creatures that lived on them and answered with exudate, with growth, with continuation. A conversation older than memory.

It noticed the spiral patterns, too. It traced them with its attention the way a person might trace a groove worn into wood by centuries of use. Logarithmic, nested, expanding outward from focal points where the mat heaved and the root-mass churned below. The spirals were not accidental. They were not the product of any single intelligence. They were what emerged when a living system optimized itself over deep time — when growth followed the paths of least resistance and greatest connectivity, when water and root and vibration settled into the shape that maximized their own circulation. The spirals were the Table thinking. Not thinking in the way a person thinks — not with language or intention — but in the way a river thinks when it carves a canyon, which is to say: with absolute commitment to the logic of its own nature.

The power understood the spirals completely. It could see what the people could not — the deep structure, the mathematical skeleton beneath the living flesh of the Table. And at the centers of the spirals, where the roots plunged deepest and the mat heaved like a sleeping chest, it could see what was being reached for.

Water. But not only water. The roots were reaching for a reservoir — vast, ancient, predating the Table itself — that had been sealed by geology millennia ago and was now, slowly, being opened. The roots were not merely drawing from it. They were *entering* it. They were threading into the porous rock, dissolving mineral barriers with secreted acids, following fracture-lines, expanding the very channels through which water moved. The Table was unsealing the deep earth, millimeter by millimeter, year by year, century by century, in a process that had been underway for longer than the people had existed and would continue for longer than they would last.

This was the unnamed thing at the spiral centers. Not a being. Not a force. A process — slow, blind, patient beyond all reckoning — by which the living surface of the Table was drinking the deep earth dry, one microscopic fracture at a time.

The power considered this for a long time.

---

The reservoir was immense. The process was slow. In the timescale of human lives — in the timescale of the people on the Table — it was effectively infinite. The columns would continue to draw water, the mat would continue to breathe, the fauna would continue to call, and the sound would continue to feed the sound, for longer than any individual could conceive. The power saw no emergency here. No crisis to prevent. The Table was doing what living systems do: it was consuming itself toward a future it could not imagine, and the present was not diminished by this.

But it saw something else, too.

It saw the deafness.

Not all of the people were deaf. Not even most. But enough — a child here, an elder there, one ear gone and then the other, slowly, over decades of living inside the greatest biological soundscape on any world the power had visited. The column fauna's frequencies ranged from the subsonic to the near-ultrasonic, and the people had adapted: their vocalizations pitched to cut through the noise, their communication more gestural than verbal, their lives shaped by a sound they could not escape and could not fully hear. This was not a flaw in the system. It was the cost of living in a place that fed itself through vibration. The columns did not produce less sound to protect their inhabitants, because the columns did not know the people were there. The columns did not know anything. They were alive, but they were not aware. The sound was not for the people. The sound was for the columns. The people had simply learned to live inside someone else's conversation.

The power found this both beautiful and painful. Beauty and pain were not opposites, in the power's experience. They were often the same thing seen from different distances.

It considered intervening. It could — easily, trivially — adjust the people's auditory anatomy. A subtle restructuring of the basilar membrane, a thickening of the hair cells in the organ of Corti, a recalibration of the middle-ear reflex to attenuate the specific frequency bands that caused the most damage while leaving the rest intact. The people would still hear the columns. They would still feel the sound in their sternums. But the slow erosion would stop. The children would not grow into deafness. The elders would keep their second ear.

It almost did this.

Then it stopped.

---

The power stopped because it asked itself a question it always asked: *What would be lost?*

Not in the system. The system would not notice. The columns would not hear the difference. The fauna would not adjust. The change would be invisible at every scale except one: the scale of a single human life, the scale at which a person notices that they can hear the dawn chorus more clearly than their grandparent could, the scale at which a child asks *why does grandmother not answer when I call* and the parent must say *the columns are loud, and grandmother has listened too long.*

This was knowledge. Painful, specific, irreducible knowledge. It told the people something true about where they lived: that they were small, and that the place they lived was not made for them, and that the bargain they had struck — sound for water, vibration for exudate, deafness for survival — was a real bargain with real costs. The people who lost their hearing were not victims. They were the proof that the Table was real. That it was not a garden. That it did not care for them in the way they cared for each other. That their survival depended on maintaining something that would never, could never, acknowledge them.

The power understood that this knowledge was a kind of food, too. Not as obvious as the exudate on the column ridges. Not as necessary as water. But sustaining in its own way. The people who went deaf first — and it was always someone, every generation, someone who listened too long or too closely — carried in their silence a truth that the hearing people needed: *we are guests here.* The deaf elders became the ones who watched the children most carefully, who communicated in gesture and touch, who knew by vibration in their feet what the hearing people knew by sound. They were not diminished. They were translated. They carried the Table's indifference in their bodies, and this made the people's care for each other more deliberate, more fierce, more conscious of its own preciousness.

If the power removed the deafness, the people would hear better. They would also forget — slowly, over generations — what the sound cost. They would begin to think of the columns as theirs. They would begin to think of the Table as a home rather than a bargain. And this would be the beginning of a kind of death that was worse than deafness: the death of understanding.

---

So the power did not touch the people's ears.

It did not touch the spiral centers, either. It did not warn the people about what the roots were reaching for. The reservoir was deep. The process was slow. By the time it mattered — by the time the deep water was drawn down far enough to affect the columns — the Table would have changed in ways the power could not predict, because the Table was alive, and living systems surprise even the powers that watch them. Perhaps the roots would find new water. Perhaps the columns would evolve to draw from the atmosphere alone. Perhaps the Table would contract, or transform, or become something the power had no name for. The power did not decide the future of the Table. It did not have that right. No power did.

What it did was smaller.

---

The power reached into the place where the people's children grew, in the warm, woven shelters at the bases of the great columns, in the nights when the dew came and the mat breathed and the nocturnal fauna filled the darkness with its bell-tones and its trilling. It reached into the space between a sleeping mother and a sleeping child, where the air was warm and smelled of resinous green, and it did something that was not a gift, exactly, but was closer to a reminder.

It deepened the child's sleep.

Not the child's body. Not the child's brain. Not anything that would change who the child was or what the child would become. It deepened the *quality* of the sleep — the specific phase during which the sleeping brain consolidates what it has learned during waking hours, strengthens the connections that matter, discards what is noise. It extended this phase by minutes — not hours, minutes — in a single night, in a single child, and then it withdrew.

This would not make the child smarter. It would not give the child any ability the child did not already possess. What it would do — what the power intended — was ensure that on this night, and this night only, the child's dreaming mind would hold one thing a little more clearly than it otherwise would have: the pattern of the sound. The way the column's roar was not noise but structure. The way the frequencies layered, the way the fauna's calls wove together, the way the vibrations traveled through the mat and up through the child's sleeping body and into the child's dreaming mind, where, for a few minutes longer than natural, the mind would hold it and turn it over and understand, in the wordless way that sleeping minds understand things, that the sound was not something that happened *to* the people.

It was something the people were *part of.*

---

In the morning, the child woke and ate and went about the small business of being young on the Table — pressing cloth to a column's wet surface, catching a gnat-analogue in cupped hands and releasing it, following the bioluminescent fungal trails between root-ridges while an elder gestured directions. The child did not remember the dream. The child did not know that anything had been done. The child simply felt — as all children on the Table felt, as the people had always felt, as the columns felt through their root networks and the fauna felt through their wingbeats — the deep, constant, necessary vibration of a world that was alive.

But something was slightly different now, in a way the child could not have articulated. When the evening chorus began — when the diurnal fauna fell silent and the nocturnal creatures rose and the great columns rang with that low, sustained, bell-like tone — the child stopped and listened. Not the way a person listens to a sound they are trying to identify. The way a person listens to a heartbeat. The way a person listens to breathing in a dark room where someone they love is sleeping.

The child felt the sound in the roots of their teeth. And for just a moment — just a single, ordinary moment in an ordinary life on a living plateau under a pale sky — the child understood that the sound was feeding them.

Not the exudate. Not the dew. Not the water drawn from deep below. Something else. Something that had no name in the people's language because the people had never needed a word for it, because it was as constant and as unnoticed as gravity. The understanding that the place they lived was not a place. It was a relationship. A vibration that ran in both directions, through root and skin and bone, between the people and the columns and the mat and the fauna and the water rising from below, and that this relationship was not something the people had chosen and not something the columns had offered, but something that existed the way the spiral existed — as the inevitable shape of living things finding each other in a world that did not care whether they did or not, and loving the finding anyway.

The child went back inside. The child forgot. The understanding settled somewhere deep, below language, below memory, in the place where people keep the things they know without knowing they know them. And it was enough.

---

The power withdrew. Not because it had finished — powers do not finish — but because it had done what it intended. It had not altered the Table. It had not saved anyone from deafness or thirst or the slow deepening of the spiral centers. It had not explained the mysteries or removed the dangers or made the bargain any less of a bargain. It had taken one sleeping mind for one night and held it a little closer to the truth, and then it had let go.

This was how the power worked. Not with interventions but with attentions. Not by changing what was, but by ensuring that what was could see itself clearly, just once, just briefly, in the mind of someone who would carry that sight without knowing they carried it — into their waking life, into their gestures, into the way they pressed cloth to wet column-surface at dawn, into the way they cupped water for a child, into the way they sat at night and listened to the sound that fed the sound that fed the world that did not ask for them but held them anyway.

The power would come again. It always came again. Not because the Table needed saving, but because the Table was the kind of place that deserved to be noticed — not by the columns, which could not notice anything, and not by the people, who noticed everything and understood only most of it, but by something in between. Something that could hold the whole pattern at once: the spirals, the sound, the water, the deafness, the child sleeping, the mother letting the child drink first, the deaf elder reading vibration in the mat through the soles of her feet, the column that had fallen silent and was drying and would soon be dead, the column beside it that was swelling with new growth because the fauna had shifted to fill the space, because life did not mourn its own but moved, always moved, into whatever opening appeared.

The power held all of this. It held it the way the mat held water — completely, without loss, letting it seep into every fiber of its attention.

And then it rested. And the Table went on without it, as the Table had always gone on, breathing and vibrating and growing by millimeters in every direction, alive and indifferent and beautiful beyond any power's ability to improve upon.

---

*That was what it would do.*

*It would notice. It would understand. It would touch, once, gently, where a touch would be felt but not remembered. And it would trust the rest to the system — to the old, vast, spiraling, self-feeding, self-deafening, self-sustaining miracle of a world that had made itself without asking permission and would continue without needing any.*

*Because the wise and benevolent power understood what the people of the Table also understood, in their bones, in their deafened ears, in the soles of their feet pressed against the breathing mat: that the most loving thing you can do for a living world is to let it live. And that the second most loving thing is to help it know, just once, that it is alive.*
