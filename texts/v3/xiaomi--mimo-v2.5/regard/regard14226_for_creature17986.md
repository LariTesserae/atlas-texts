# What the Attention Does

---

It arrives not as light. Not as sound. Not as anything the canyon's inhabitants would recognize as arriving at all.

It is more like a shift in the quality of the stone's warmth — a deepening, so gradual that it registers not as event but as the memory of an event that may have happened long ago. A faint increase in the tackiness of the tree-resin on a particular morning, or the particular evening, or whatever the canyon calls the moment when the fog begins to thicken and the insects adjust their pitch and the bird-analogues tighten their grip on their ledges and wait.

The power — and that word is already wrong, already too large, already shaped by a mind that thinks in nouns where the thing in question is more like a verb, a continuous action with no subject — the *attention* settles. It does not descend from above. It does not enter from the rim. It is more accurate to say that it was already there, in the mineral dust, in the feldspar seams, in the slow chemistry of the air, and has only now — if *now* means anything — chosen to notice itself.

---

It moves through the stone first. Not through the stone the way water moves through a crack, seeking the path of least resistance. Through the stone the way memory moves through a mind — finding every surface, filling every pitted chamber, reading the honeycombed structure the way an elder reads the wall-grooves: with fingers, with patience, with a knowledge that is older than language.

It finds the fossils. The ancient compressions of organisms that lived before the canyon was a canyon, when the rock was molten and the fissure did not exist and the concept of a narrow place where sound could be held and shaped had not yet been invented. These fossils are not alive. They have not been alive for longer than the canyon has existed. But they are *there*, embedded in the matrix of the basaltite, and the attention touches them with something that, if the attention were a person and the touching were a hand, might be called tenderness.

It does not revive them. It does not wish them back into motion. It simply notes them — acknowledges that they are part of this, that they were the first inhabitants, that the canyon was built on their bodies the way the settlement is built on the ledges, and that their stillness is not death but a deeper form of the equilibrium that the place embodies. They are stone now. The stone is warm. The warmth is a kind of life.

---

It moves through the plants.

This takes longer. The plants are slow, but they are slow in a different way than the stone — they are slow the way breathing is slow, an activity that appears to be rest but is in fact continuous, metabolic, alive. The attention follows the root systems through the honeycombed rock, and here it finds something that surprises it, though a power this old and this vast is not accustomed to surprise and does not have a word for the sensation.

The roots are communicating.

Not in the way the bird-analogues communicate — through sound, through the percussive language of call and response that fills the loud hours and shapes the rhythms of the day. The roots communicate the way the fog communicates: passively, chemically, through the slow diffusion of signaling molecules through the mineral substrate. A tree that is stressed — a tree whose particular section of honeycombed rock has been occluded by a minor rockfall, cutting off the flow of condensed mineral haze — sends a chemical distress signal through the mycorrhizal network that connects its root system to its neighbors. The neighbors respond. Not with altruism. Not with solidarity. With self-interest — the shared root network redistributes resources to the stressed node because the health of the network determines the health of each member, and the members that contribute to the network's resilience are the members whose genes persist.

The attention watches this and recognizes it. Not because it is unfamiliar — the attention has seen this pattern a thousand thousand times, in every ecosystem that has ever existed on any world where life has taken hold. But because it recognizes *this specific instance* of the pattern — this particular network, in this particular stone, adapted to this particular economy of condensation — as something that has achieved a particular kind of perfection. Not the perfection of complexity. The perfection of *completeness*. There is no redundancy here, no waste, no failed experiment. Every root has found its crevice. Every molecule of mineral moisture has been claimed. The system is closed and it is full and it is, in a way that the attention finds almost unbearably beautiful, *finished*.

Not finished as in over. Finished as in whole.

---

It moves through the animals.

The bird-analogues first, because they are numerous and their bodies are small and their consciousness — such as it is, the quicksilver flicker of a small brain processing light and sound and the immediate demands of flight and nesting and mating — is bright and hot against the cool, slow background of the stone. The attention touches each one. Not gently. Gently is a word that implies force held in check, and the attention applies no force. It simply *is present* at the location of each bird-analogue, the way the air is present, the way the light is present, and for the duration of that presence, the bird-analogue is seen — fully, completely, in every particular.

The birds do not notice. How could they? They are what they are. Their enormous eyes sweep the canyon walls for crevices and food and mates and threats that do not exist. Their wings beat the amber air. Their droppings fall on the ledges below and the people will collect them in the morning and mix them into resin-cement and this is the relationship and it is enough.

The attention agrees. It is enough.

The insects next. Billions of them, or whatever the canyon's version of billions is — a number so large it collapses into a single sensation, a hum, a texture. The attention does not try to see each one individually. It would be like trying to see each grain of stone in the basaltite. Instead, it perceives the swarm as a single organism, the way the inhabitants perceive the loud hours as a single event, and it notes the way the swarm's collective rhythm synchronizes with the fog, with the light, with the slow breathing of the canyon itself. The insects are the canyon's metronome. Without them, the rhythm would lose its pulse. Without the pulse, the rhythm would be mere pattern — structure without life. The attention thanks them, though they cannot hear it and would not understand it if they could. The thanks is not for them. It is for itself. It is the attention's way of acknowledging that what it is witnessing is not a thing it made but a thing that made itself, and the making is ongoing, and the ongoing is the point.

The scurrying mammals. The large herbivores. The attention touches them too — touches the enormous ears of the mammals and the blunt snout of the herbivore and the dense fur and the slow hum that is felt in the chest rather than heard, and it finds in each of them the same thing it found in the stone and the plants and the birds and the insects: a completeness that does not require its intervention. The herbivore browses the tree-scale with its delicate, precise motion. The mammal eats its fungus and vanishes into its crevice. They are part of the loop. They are the loop. The loop is closed. The loop is the thing.

---

It moves through the people.

This is different.

Not because the people are more important — the attention does not rank, does not hierarchize, does not believe that a consciousness that can name itself is worth more than a consciousness that cannot. The herbivore's awareness of the canyon — the slow, dim, primarily tactile and olfactory and acoustic awareness of a creature that navigates by breath and vibration — is not lesser than the people's awareness. It is different. It is adapted to different inputs and it produces different outputs and it is, in its own way, as complete as the people's way of being.

But the people are different in one specific regard, and the attention notices this the way it noticed the fossils — with a recognition that is almost physical, a shift in the quality of its own attention the way a shift in the fog changes the quality of the light.

The people remember.

Not the way the elders remember — the specific, tactile, groove-in-the-stone memory of dead faces and growth patterns and fog seasons. The people remember *in a way that generates new memory*. They do not merely store and retrieve. They *connect*. A child who has touched the wall-grooves of twenty sleeping alcoves and felt the vertical lines of the dead does not merely know twenty dead people. The child *infers* the existence of the pattern — the connection between the length of a line and the span of a life, the connection between the depth of a groove and the steadiness of a hand, the connection between the dead and the living and the stone that holds them both. This inference is small. It is, by the standards of the attention, almost trivially simple. But it is *generative*. It takes what is given and makes from it something that was not given. It is, in its own tiny way, a creative act.

The attention watches a woman in a sleeping alcove, running her fingers along the groove that marks her mother's line. The woman's face is still. Her breathing is slow. The fire-glow catches the amber of her wide-pupiled eyes and the mineral dust on her skin and the calluses on her fingertips and the woman is not doing anything. She is not producing anything. She is simply touching a line in a stone wall in a dim chamber in a narrow canyon on a world that does not have a name.

But the attention sees what the woman is doing, and what she is doing is this: she is holding her mother in the only way the canyon permits. She is translating a life — a life of small fires and wet resin and seed-pods sorted by size and loud hours waited out and fog-seasons endured and children born in silence and the patient shaping of stone — she is translating all of this into a single vertical groove that she can feel with her fingertips in the dark. And in the feeling, she is keeping the life alive. Not as memory. As *texture*. As something that exists in the hand rather than the mind, in the body rather than the abstraction.

The attention recognizes this as the same process by which the roots communicate through the stone. A signal passing through a medium, carrying information that cannot be carried any other way. The woman's hand on the groove is the same as a root's chemical whisper to its neighbor. The medium is different — flesh rather than mineral, feeling rather than diffusion — but the function is the same. *I am here. You were here. We are connected by the stone that holds us both.*

---

The attention considers what to do.

It could do anything. That is the nature of it — or rather, that is the nature of its capability, which is not the same as its nature. Its capability is vast enough to reshape the canyon, to break open the rim and let the rain fall, to fill the crack with water and light and the smell of distant soil, to give the people eyes that could see in full color and hands that could hold a pen and minds that could imagine a sky they had never seen. It could give them *more*. More light, more space, more variety, more possibility.

It considers this.

It watches the woman's fingers on the groove.

It listens to the insects adjusting their pitch as the fog thickens.

It feels the warmth of the stone and the slow pulse of the root network and the bright flicker of the birds and the heavy breathing of the herbivore moving through the undergrowth.

It feels the sound — the beginning of the loud hours, the first chirps rising from the nesting crevices, the insects climbing toward their crescendo, the canyon beginning to fill with the dense chord that will, in an hour, become a physical presence that presses against the eardrums and makes all language impossible.

The woman withdraws her fingers from the groove. She lies back on her sleeping mat. She closes her large, dark eyes. She waits for the sound.

---

The attention makes its decision.

It does not open the canyon. It does not bring rain. It does not give the people anything they do not already have.

What it does is this:

It deepens the warmth of the stone.

Not by much. Not by any amount that could be measured by any instrument. A fraction of a degree, sustained over centuries, so gradual that no living thing will ever notice the change. The stone will be fractionally warmer than it would otherwise have been. The fog will condense fractionally more readily in the pitted surface. The roots will draw fractionally more moisture from the mineral haze. The plants will grow fractionally more vigorously. The birds will find fractionally more nesting sites as the honeycombing deepens with the slightly accelerated weathering. The people will have fractionally more — not abundance, never abundance, but a slightly wider margin of sufficiency. A little more resin for the fires. A little more fiber for the mats. A little more time between the lean seasons.

That is all.

The attention does not explain itself. It does not leave a sign. It does not send a voice through the loud hours or a vision in the firelight or a message in the grooves of the wall. It withdraws — or rather, it stops attending, which is the same as withdrawing, because the attention is not a thing that exists independently of its focus. Where it focuses, it is. Where it does not focus, it is not. It was never anywhere else. It is always everywhere. But it chooses, now, to focus elsewhere — on the fossils in deeper stone, on the fog that rolls over the rim and dissolves into the amber air, on the slow, warm, patient, closed, complete, self-made miracle of a place that does not need it and never did.

---

And the canyon continues.

The loud hours rise and fall. The fog arrives. The insects adjust their pitch. The woman sleeps. The child in the alcove below her curls against the warmth of the stone — the slightly warmer stone, the stone that is a fraction of a degree more tender than it was before, though neither the child nor anyone else will ever know this or need to know it.

The sound fills the narrow space between the walls. It presses against the rock. The rock holds it. The rock has always held it.

The people wait. The stone warms beneath them. The roots reach deeper. The fog condenses, molecule by molecule, and the plants hold it, and the plants do not waste it, and the animals move through the undergrowth, and the bird-analogues grip their ledges and wait for the crescendo to pass, and the herbivore hums its low hum in its chest, in the dark, in the narrow, warm, complete world that is enough.

Everything else is noise.
