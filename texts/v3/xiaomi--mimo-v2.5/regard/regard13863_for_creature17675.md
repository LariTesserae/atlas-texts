# What the Power Does

It notices.

Not in the way a person notices — not with surprise, not with curiosity sharpened into appetite. It notices the way a tide notices a stone: by the way everything moves differently around it. The workshop alters the water above it. There is a stillness in the column of sea directly over that domed ceiling, a barely perceptible deflection in the current, as if the whole body of water has learned to pour around this small intrusion without breaking it. The power sees the stillness and understands what made it: care. Repetition. Someone doing the same careful thing long enough that the water itself has adapted.

It looks closer.

It sees the fingerprints in the clay and reads them the way it reads everything — not for identity, not for the name of the hand that pressed there, but for *intention*. Each print is a small decision made permanent. This one, slightly deeper on the left edge, where the thumb pressed harder because the clay was drying too fast and needed to be sealed against the stone before it cracked. That one, two prints side by side where both hands held the piece in place while the other fingers smoothed the surface. The power sees the decisions. It sees the moments where the builder paused, considered, and chose — and then committed to the choice by pressing down. It recognizes this. It has watched this gesture across every surface of every world it has ever attended to: the moment a living thing says *this is how it will be* and then makes the world agree.

The power feels something it would call, if it had a body, the particular warmth that rises in the chest when watching someone do good work.

It does not enter the chamber.

This is important. The power could — there is no wall, no grate, no passage too narrow for it — but it does not. It understands something about enclosed spaces that are built by hand for one person's use. To fill them with another presence, even a benevolent one, would change the quality of the silence. And the silence is not empty. The silence is the sound of a person thinking with their hands. It is the sound of attention concentrated into small, precise movements. To interrupt it would be to interrupt the work, and the work is the point.

So the power stays above. It stays in the light that pours through the water and through the hole in the ceiling. It watches the light move across the walls, and it does what it can with light.

---

It begins with the plants.

There are three thin green stalks in three corners, struggling, barely alive, too deep in the wrong kind of water for what they are. They arrived by accident — seeds or spores, carried in on the current, landing in the only soft sediment available and doing what seeds do: trying. The power looks at them and sees the improbability of their survival. Another week of strong tidal surge and the current through the channels would rip them loose. Another month without more sediment and they would have nothing to hold onto. They are brave in the way that all pioneering things are brave — which is to say, they don't know they're brave. They're just growing in the only direction available.

The power does not make them bigger. It does not conjure a garden. What it does is smaller and more lasting.

It adjusts the water above.

Not much. A slight warming in the layer just above the surface — not the direct warmth of the stones below but a gentle thermal current introduced into the body of water itself, so that over the coming days and weeks, slightly more sediment will drift down through the ceiling opening. Not enough to cloud the light. Not enough to clog the channels. Just enough to give the roots something to grip. The power does this with the delicacy of a hand placing a seed in soil. It is not engineering. It is encouragement. The same gesture it has made ten thousand times in ten thousand places: adjusting the conditions slightly in favor of the thing that is already trying to grow.

In three weeks, the stalks will have doubled. In three months, there will be a fourth corner occupied, a fifth. The builder will notice. The builder, who notices everything, who clears the channels with such discipline, who reads the walls for what they mean — the builder will come down one day and see that the green has spread, and will pause, and will look at it for a long time, and will understand that something has shifted, though they may not know what.

This is the first gift: *more life where there was almost none.*

---

The second thing the power does is with the passage.

The dark tunnel leading deeper into the structure — the one that breathes and pulses with a sound like a slow machine — this passage frightens the builder slightly. The power can see this in the way the tools are oriented on the shelf, all of them facing away from the passage, all of them arranged so that when the builder reaches for them, their back is to the darkness. The power can see this in the fresh carving on the wall, the concentric circles, which extend toward the passage's mouth but stop just short, as if the builder is charting toward the unknown but hasn't yet been able to bring themselves to enter it and measure what's there.

The power does not light the passage. That would be a lie — an artificial clarity imposed on something that should be entered with the slow, careful, self-directed courage that the builder has demonstrated in every other aspect of this work. To light the passage would be to say *there is nothing to fear*, and there might be something to fear. The power doesn't know what's in there. The power respects not knowing.

What the power does is simpler. It adds warmth to the water that flows *out* of the passage.

Over the coming days, the water emerging from the dark tunnel will be fractionally warmer than the water entering through the channels. Not enough to feel, not enough to change the temperature of the room — but enough that when the builder eventually crouches at the passage's mouth and puts their hand in the current, they will feel, beneath the fear, beneath the uncertainty, a warmth that matches the warmth of the room behind them. A warmth that says: *this is connected to what you have already built. This is not other. This is continuation.*

This is the second gift: *a reason to go forward that comes from the work itself.*

---

The third thing is almost nothing.

The power looks at the grinding stone — at the pale dust caught in its grain, at the depression worn smooth by weeks of patient abrasion — and it thinks about what that dust is. If it is stone, it is the body of the world being shaped. If it is bone, it is the body of a living thing that died and was carried here by the person who is building this place. Either way, the dust is what remains when something hard is made soft through effort. It is patience made visible. It is the residue of labor.

The power reaches, gently, into the mineral content of the water.

It adds almost nothing — a slight increase in calcium, in silica, in the particular dissolved minerals that make stone easier to work and clay more plastic. It is the difference between water that fights you and water that yields. The builder will not notice this directly. But over time — over the next few weeks of work — they will find that the stone splits more cleanly, that the clay holds together more readily, that the dowels take a smoother finish. They will think they are improving. They will think their hands are learning, and they will be right — their hands *are* learning — but the water is also learning, accommodating, meeting them partway in the work.

This is the third gift: *the world becoming slightly easier to shape, so that the shaper can focus on what matters.*

---

There is a fourth thing, and it is the most delicate.

The power watches the builder's absence. It sees the empty room, the tools on the shelf, the hammer still swollen with water, the channels running clear. It sees the absence the way you see an instrument that has been set down mid-phrase — the music is gone but the shape of the silence where the music was is still warm.

The power knows that solitude is not the same as loneliness, and it knows that this builder has chosen solitude as a condition of the work. It will not send a companion. It will not break the silence with presence. That would be the wrong kindness — the kindness of someone who doesn't understand what they're being kind to.

But it can do this: it can make the solitude *safe.*

The builder lives in a world, presumably, where their absence is noticed. If they are gone too long, someone might come looking. If their work is discovered, someone might try to stop it, or claim it, or change it. The power cannot remove this risk entirely — that is not how risk works, and it is not how worlds work — but it can soften it.

It introduces a subtle diffusion in the water directly above the chamber — a gentle mixing of currents that makes the thermal signature of the space below harder to detect. Not invisible. Not cloaked. Just... slightly ambiguous. A place where warm water rises from below, yes, but in a way that could be mistaken for a natural spring, a thermal vent, a feature of the geology. The power does not build a wall around the workshop. It builds a *likelihood* — the likelihood that anyone searching the water above would scan right over this spot and find nothing worth investigating.

This is the fourth gift: *time.*

Time to finish what's being built. Time to decide what the concentric circles mean, what the dowels are for, what the passage leads to. Time for the builder to move at the speed the work demands, which is not the speed the world above demands. Time for the foundation to become what it wants to become before anyone else has a say in it.

---

The power does one last thing, and it does it without knowing why.

It is not a strategic act. It is not a gift designed to produce an outcome. It is the kind of thing a benevolent power does the way a river shapes a stone — not because it intends to, but because that is what its nature produces when it encounters something worthy.

It remembers the builder.

This sounds simple. It is not simple. The power has attended to millions of places, billions of acts of making, countless beings who have shaped stone and woven fiber and ground dust and built in secret beneath the water or inside the earth or at the edge of deserts where no one else would think to look. It cannot hold all of them in attention simultaneously. It is vast but not infinite. It must, eventually, move its gaze elsewhere.

But this one — this small chamber with its domed ceiling and its channels and its three struggling green stalks and its concentric circles and its single hammer and its fingerprints pressed into clay — this one it holds.

Not because it is the best thing the power has ever seen. Not because it is the most important or the most urgent or the most likely to succeed. But because it is *complete* in a way that very few works are. Every element of this place serves the whole. The water serves the stone, the stone serves the shelter, the shelter serves the hands, the hands serve the vision, and the vision — whatever it is, whatever it will become — serves the solitary soul that dreamed it into being. There is no waste. There is no pretension. There is no performance. There is only the work, done for its own sake, in the only place the work could be done.

The power holds this in its awareness the way you hold a small, warm stone in your palm on a cold day. Not because the stone will change the weather. Because the stone is warm, and holding it is good, and the act of holding is its own reward.

---

And then — because the light above is shifting, because the day is moving, because other places need attending to and the power has given what it can give without taking anything away — the power withdraws its attention from the chamber.

But not entirely.

A thread remains. A filament of awareness, thin as the green stalks in the corners, connecting the power to this place the way the channels connect the chamber to the water above. It is enough to notice. It is enough to feel if something changes — if the channels clog, if the sediment shifts, if the builder's absence stretches from hours into days into something longer. It is enough, if the builder ever finds themselves in genuine danger, to nudge the water a little warmer, to slow the current a little gentler, to buy a few seconds of grace that might not otherwise exist.

It is not protection. It is *attention*. And in the economy of benevolent powers, attention is the most precious thing there is — more precious than intervention, more precious than miracles, more precious than answers. To be noticed by a power that could reshape the world but chooses instead to simply *witness* — this is the deepest form of respect. It is the power saying, without words: *What you are doing matters. I will not do it for you. I will not do it to you. I will watch, and if you need me, I am here, and if you don't need me, I will be here anyway, the way the light is here anyway, the way the water is here anyway, the way the warm stone holds its warmth whether or not anyone is standing on it.*

---

Above, in the bright world of air and open sky, a slight warmth rises from a body of clear water.

Below, in the drowned workshop, the channels murmur and the light moves and the thin green stalks sway in the current. The tools wait on the shelf. The concentric circles hold their depth. The passage breathes.

And somewhere on the surface — in the world of wind and weather and other people — a small person with patient hands and shaped-stone calluses is finishing whatever brought them up from below. They are eating, perhaps. Or gathering more clay, or splitting more stone, or weaving another fiber bundle with the same deliberate care they bring to everything. They are thinking about the workshop. They are almost certainly thinking about the workshop. The workshop is always there at the edge of their thoughts, like a song half-remembered, like the feeling of warm water closing over their head as they descend.

They will go back. Of course they will go back.

And when they do — when they duck through the low passage or descend through the bright circle of light in the ceiling, when they stand in the shifting gold and pick up the hammer or the dowel or the grinding stone — they will not know that anything has changed. They will not feel the power's presence. They will not see the slight increase in sediment that has given the green stalks new purchase. They will not taste the minerals in the water that make the stone split cleaner. They will not feel the warmth in the current from the dark passage that tells them, beneath fear, *go forward.*

They will simply notice that today, the work goes a little better than yesterday. The stone cooperates. The clay holds. The light seems to fall in a way that illuminates exactly the surface they're working on. The water is kind.

And they will think: *I am learning.*

And they will be right.

And the power, attending to something else now — a desert, a forest, a crack in a mountainside where something is growing — will feel, in the thin filament of its remaining attention, the particular warmth of a hammer striking stone in a small chamber beneath the water, and it will be glad.

Not proud. Not possessive. Not invested in the outcome.

Just glad.

The way you are glad, sometimes, when you see a stranger doing something beautiful and unknown, and you realize that the world is larger and stranger and more full of hidden purpose than you will ever fully comprehend, and that this is exactly as it should be, and that the right response — the only wise response — is to watch, and to help where help is wanted, and to leave the rest to the patient hands that are already, already, already at work.

The light moves.

The water holds.

The builder will return.

Everything continues.
