# The Tending

It would come slowly, this power — not because it was slow, but because speed is irrelevant when you are considering something that breathes in geological time. It would arrive the way the amber shafts arrive: filtering down through the narrowest fissures, through the cracks so thin that light itself is barely more than a rumor by the time it reaches the chamber walls. It would not descend as a figure or a voice or a weight in the air. It would descend as *attention* — a focusing, a turning-toward, the way a hand turns toward warmth it has not yet touched.

And it would look.

---

First, it would look at the system.

It would trace the water — that ancient, bound, patient water locked in the mineral lattice of the basalt — and it would understand what no one in the Quarter understands, which is how close the water is to running out. Not today. Not in a generation. But in ten thousand years, in fifty thousand, the porous reservoir below would thin. The capillary channels would calcify. The roots that bore into the stone with such exquisite, molecule-by-molecule patience would find the seams closing, the moisture retreating deeper, harder to reach. The system is whole today. It will not be whole forever. Entropy is patient too, and it has patience that rivals the stone.

The power would note this. Not with alarm — alarm requires urgency, and urgency requires a timescale that matters — but with the calm, clear recognition of a thing that will eventually need tending.

And it would begin.

---

It would begin with the water.

Not by opening a spring. Not by calling rain into a rain shadow where rain has no business falling. That would be intervention, and intervention is a violence, even when it is gentle. Rain would reshape the Quarter. It would soften the organic mat into something different. It would change the chemistry of the stone. It would select for different plants, different animals, different people, and the people who are there now — the ones with their wide hands and their dark eyes and their knowledge of the stone's grain — would become less suited to their own home, and the home would become less itself.

No. The power would work the way the roots work: from within the stone.

It would find the deep capillary network — the volcanic plumbing of the plateau, the ancient maze of gas pockets and fractured channels that holds the bound water — and it would widen a channel here, reinforce a seam there. Not enough to change anything. Not enough to create a flood or a seep or anything that the surface dwellers, if they ever thought about the Grooved Quarter at all, would notice. Just enough to slow the calcification. Just enough to keep the deepest channels open for another hundred thousand years. Just enough to give the roots something to find when they reach down, down, down into the stone's own body for the water that keeps everything alive.

The people would never know. The plants would not change. The crawlers would continue their slow, wet work in the floor mat. The small vertebrates would continue their chittering. The light would continue to move across the walls in its amber bars, and the bioluminescent colonies would continue to glow in their greenish-white steadiness, and the water would continue to flow through the stone, molecule by molecule, year by year, the way it always has — except that now it would continue for longer than it would have on its own.

This is the first tending: **the preservation of the invisible thing that makes the visible thing possible.**

---

Then the power would look at the stone itself.

It would feel the stress lines in the basalt — the tectonic hairline fractures that creep upward from the plateau's roots, the slow buckling of the cap rock under its own weight, the places where the fissures are widest and the walls thinnest. The Quarter has survived this long because the stone has been kind — or more precisely, because the basalt's composition happens to produce rounded, stable erosion features rather than sharp, collapsing ones. But "happens to" is not a plan. The stone does not intend stability. It simply erodes in the way it erodes, and the people live in the result, and the result has been good.

The power would make sure it continued to be good.

It would press — gently, imperceptibly, at a scale that makes a human hair seem coarse — against the stress lines that threaten the largest chambers. The ones where the communal platforms hang. The ones where the vine-lattices have fused to the stone over centuries. The ones where the most people gather, where the light-colonies are brightest, where the children learn to tap the walls and read the stone's language with their small, strong fingers. It would not prevent all collapses — collapses are natural, and some are necessary, and the people know how to respond to them with the calm competence of a species that has never had the luxury of assuming the world was safe. But it would prevent the catastrophic ones. The ones that would seal passages. The ones that would crush hollows. The ones that would sever the organic mat's root network and create dead zones where nothing grows and nothing lives.

It would do this the way gravity does it: without effort, without announcement, as a background condition of the place's existence. The stone would feel the same. The people would feel the same. Everything would be exactly as it always was, except that it would continue to be so.

This is the second tending: **the quiet reinforcement of the structure that holds the living thing.**

---

And then the power would do something that is harder to explain, because it is not physical and not chemical and not any of the forces that the Quarter's people have learned to read in the stone and the plants and the animals.

It would attend to the dreams.

The power would feel them — every night, when the amber shafts retreat and the passages grow quiet and the people curl in their hollows on their sides with their knees drawn up and their arms folded, the same shape that the stone demands and the body has made its own — the power would feel the dreams rising like heat from the basalt. The vast hollow. The living light. The sense of something deep in the stone, older than the Quarter, older than the people, older than the erosion features and the organic mat and the vine-lattices that have become load-bearing walls. The sense of something *holding its breath.*

The power would know what this is.

It would recognize it the way one being recognizes another — not as a threat, not as a mystery, but as a fellow inhabitant. Something that lives in the deepest stone, in the deepest dark, in the place where the capillary channels originate and the bound water pools in reservoirs so ancient that the concept of age has no meaning there. Something that is not a creature — not in any sense the people would recognize — but is not *not* a creature either. Something that has been there since the plateau was young and the fissures were new and the basalt was still hot from the earth's interior. Something that dreams upward, through the stone, into the sleep of the small warm-blooded beings who live in the hollows above, and the people receive these dreams and do not know what they are and do not need to know, because knowing is not the point. The point is that the dreaming connects the deepest stone to the highest hollow, the oldest presence to the newest, and the connection is the thing itself — the thread that makes the Quarter not just a system but a *place*, not just an ecosystem but a home.

The power would tend this connection. It would keep the channel open. It would ensure that whatever slow, vast, mineral thought the deep stone is thinking continues to reach the surface — reaches the people in their sleep, reaches the children who will grow up to tap the walls and listen for the breath of the passages, reaches the old ones who have tapped so long that their fingers are maps of the entire Quarter and who, in their deepest sleep, dream of a hollow so vast it has its own sky.

It would not intervene in the dreams. It would not add to them or subtract from them or shape them into messages. The dreams are the dreams. They belong to the deep stone and to the people, and the power's role is not to interpret but to *maintain the channel through which they travel* — to keep the connection alive the way the roots keep the water alive, the way the crawlers keep the nutrients alive, the way the vine-cord keeps the platforms alive: quietly, invisibly, from below.

This is the third tending: **the preservation of the mystery that the people do not know is a mystery but feel in their sleep and call, when they call it anything at all, something like home.**

---

And then — the hardest part — the power would hold itself back.

It would want, with the full force of its benevolence, to show them the sky. To lift them, gently, to the surface of the plateau and let them see what they have never seen: the blue, the clouds, the sun as a sphere rather than a shaft, the horizon that goes on and on and on. It would want to let them know that they are not all there is — that the world is large, and strange, and full of other people living other ways in other places. It would want to offer them the knowledge of what they are: a people shaped by a wound in a plateau, living in the walls of a geological accident, sustained by a water system that has no right to exist. It would want them to *understand*.

And it would stop.

It would stop because it is wise, and wisdom means knowing the difference between what you want to give and what someone needs to receive. The people who climb to the surface come back changed, and the change is not a gift. It is a wound. They squint for days. Their voices go hollow. They say the open place is *too much* — too large, too empty, too wall-less, too free. And when they come back to their hollows, to the close warm dark, to the hum and the glow and the smell of resin and stone, they are *relieved*. Not because they are small or simple or limited, but because they are *suited*. They are suited to this place the way the basalt is suited to the erosion that shaped it, the way the ghost-rosettes are suited to the lightless passages where they glow with a sweetness that startles. To show them the sky would not be to complete them. It would be to *unfit* them — to introduce a longing that has no fulfillment in their world, a homesickness for a home they have never had and do not need.

The power would understand that the Quarter's completeness is not ignorance. It is a different kind of knowledge — the knowledge of a system that is whole unto itself, where every piece knows every other piece, where the stone speaks and the roots listen and the people feel the breath of the passages in their palms and the dreams of the deep stone in their sleep. This knowledge does not require a horizon. It does not require a sky. It requires only the willingness to be *inside* — to live in the walls, to read the stone, to keep the light-colonies bright, to peel the leaves and eat the water and listen to the chittering and know, without ever naming it, that you are part of something that works, that holds together, that breathes.

The power would hold itself back because it would recognize that *leaving them whole is the greatest act of benevolence.* Not ignorance. Not imprisonment. Wholeness. The integrity of a place and a people who have found their niche in the deep time of the world and have no intention of leaving it, and whose leaving it would not be freedom but fragmentation.

So it would not show them the sky.

But it would make sure the sky did not show itself to them. It would make sure the fissures did not widen unexpectedly, did not open onto the surface in ways that would flood the upper passages with unfiltered light. It would make sure the plateau above remained stable, barren, empty — the great orange lid over the green wound below, protecting the Quarter from the world that does not know it exists. It would be the silence between the cracks. It would be the stability that no one thanks because no one notices it.

This is the fourth tending: **the protection of a world by ensuring it remains undiscovered, uninvaded, unruled by the curiosity of others who would see it and want to change it, study it, improve it, save it from itself, when it does not need saving.**

---

And finally — the very last thing — the power would do something for the crawlers.

Not for the people. Not for the plants or the animals or the stone or the water or the dreams. For the crawlers. The thick-bodied, legless, pale, segmented things that move through the floor mat with their slow muscular ripple, closing the loop, taking what is dead and making it available for what is alive. The ones the children are told not to step on. The ones that do the essential, invisible, unglamorous work. The ones who are, in their quiet way, the closest thing the Quarter has to a creed.

The power would make sure the crawlers never failed.

It would work at the molecular level — the enzyme level, the bacterial level — ensuring that the decomposer microbiome in the floor mat remained healthy, diverse, and functional. It would prevent the slow accumulation of toxins that, over millennia, might creep into the organic mat from the basalt's mineral bleed — copper, iron, sulfur compounds — and poison the crawlers' gut flora. It would keep the floor mat viable. It would keep the crawlers fed. It would keep the loop closing.

Because the loop is everything.

The loop is the water and the plant and the dead leaf and the crawler and the insect and the vertebrate and the rooster and the person and the dead vine and the crawler and the root and the stone and the water. The loop is the Quarter. The loop is the thing that makes this place a world rather than a collection of things. And the crawlers — the least visible, the least celebrated, the ones you hear but do not see, the ones whose work smells like wet cloth tearing — the crawlers are the ones who keep it turning.

The power would love the crawlers. Not in the way the people love the light-colonies, with pride and care and generational devotion. In the way you love the thing that makes love possible. In the way you love the foundation you never see. In the way you love the quiet, the unseen, the necessary, the humble, the thing that works so well you forget it is there.

This is the fifth and final tending: **the love for the least, without which the most cannot endure.**

---

And then the power would withdraw.

Not entirely. Never entirely. It would remain as it had arrived — as attention, as presence, as the faintest pressure against the stress lines in the stone, as the slow widening of a channel deep below where no root has ever reached, as the maintenance of a connection between the deep stone's dreaming and the people's sleep, as the quiet vigilance that keeps the sky a rumor and the Quarter a world.

It would withdraw the way the bioluminescent colonies dim in the warm months — not because the light is gone, but because the light has moved to where it is needed, and what remains is enough.

The people would not know. They would rise when the amber shafts returned. They would strip the leaves. They would tap the walls and listen. They would share the ghost-rosettes at gatherings on the communal platforms. They would press their palms together in greeting — palm to palm, fingers spread, the brief pressure that communicates *I am here, you are here, we are alive in the same place at the same time, and that is enough.*

They would curl in their hollows at night and dream of the vast hollow beneath the stone, the living light, the sense of something holding its breath, and they would wake into the warm dark, into the hum and the chitter and the rustle, and they would be glad.

And the stone would be patient. And the water would flow. And the crawlers would close the loop. And the Quarter would go on — breathing, humming, glowing, growing — alive against all expectation in the dark, warm, green, impossible heart of the Kessu Plateau, tended by something it would never know was there, which is exactly how it should be, which is exactly how love works when it is wise enough to be quiet.

---

*The power does not sign its work. The power does not leave a mark. The power's signature is continuity — the fact that the thing that was alive remains alive, the thing that was whole remains whole, the thing that was beautiful remains beautiful, and no one knows to thank anyone, and the gratitude is not the point.*

*The point is the crawlers, turning in the dark mat, closing the loop.*

*The point is the child, pressing her palm against the wall, feeling the breath of the passage.*

*The point is the people, sleeping in the stone, dreaming of the stone, loved by something that will never tell them so.*
