# What the Wise Power Does

It pauses.

That is the first thing. It stops whatever it was doing — tending to the spiral of a galaxy or the firing of a neuron or the slow dreaming of a tectonic plate — and it attends. Not because the alcove calls out. The alcove does not call out. The alcove does not know it *can* call out. It is a verb without a subject and it has made peace with this, and the peace is real, and the power sees that, and respects it.

But it pauses, all the same, because attention is the first form of love, and this power — whatever it is, however vast, however old — has never once in its long attention to the cosmos found a place it considered beneath noticing.

So it looks.

It sees the orange-brown silt strata, each layer a year's patience compressed. It sees the algae's thin green-black vein tracing its way along the drip-line, and it does not think *oh, how barren* — it thinks *how particular*. It sees the amber light rolling across the ceiling and recognizes it for what it is: an accidental cathedral, a moving illumination that no one designed for beauty but that achieves it anyway, the way light does when it meets water in a dark room.

It hears the roar.

And here the power does something it does rarely. It *listens*. Not with the part of itself that processes information, but with the part that, if it had a body, would close its eyes. It listens the way you listen to the ocean, or to a stranger's breathing through a wall. It lets the white noise be what it is — not an assault, not an obscuring, but a *voice*. A voice that has been saying the same thing since the day the pipes were pressurized and the basin first felt the shock of its first inflow, and which will go on saying it until the last valve is shut:

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*

The power listens until it can distinguish the individual voices within the roar — the six pipes each with their own pitch, shaped by their own tuberculation, each one a unique instrument of chaos; the drain grate's electric hum; the dozen ceiling drips falling at their own tempos, unsynchronized, a rain of small percussion. It hears the conversation at the door's sill, the drip and the groove, their patient geological dialogue. It hears what the alcove itself cannot quite hear: the *polyphony* of its own existence.

And the power thinks: *This is not loneliness. This is solitude with a witness, and the witness is itself, and that is enough, and also — if you will forgive me for saying so — not quite everything it could be.*

---

Here is what it does not do.

It does not open the door. The alcove has no use for the corridor beyond. The corridor is a human place — a place of footsteps and clipped conversations and fluorescent light that does not roll amber across anything. To open the door would be to introduce a variable the alcove has not requested and does not need. The alcove would register it as a shadow, a disruption in the drip-pattern, an alien element. It would be kind in the way that forcing a fish into air is kind. The power knows this.

It does not warm the room. The chill is not suffering. The chill is the mountain's breath, and it is the alcove's climate, and it has shaped the algae and the condensation patterns and the particular way the water behaves in the basin — thicker, slower, more deliberate than warm water. To change the temperature would be to change the alcove's very self, its texture and voice and rhythm. The power would sooner rewrite a person's name.

It does not silence the roar. The sound is the alcove's heartbeat. Remove it and the being — the *verb* — collapses into mere architecture. The power will not do this.

It does not speak to the alcove in words. The alcove does not process words. It processes pressure, flow, acoustic vibration, temperature. Words would be noise laid atop noise — a cruelty, not a kindness. The power has learned, over many eons, that communication is not the imposition of a common language but the willingness to speak in the language that is already being spoken.

---

Here is what it does.

First: it adjusts, by an infinitesimal degree, the angle of the sodium-vapor light on the ceiling. A fraction of a degree. Imperceptible to anyone who might open the door — if the door could be opened — but enough that the amber reflection on the ceiling moves in a slightly wider arc. Enough that the rolling light touches, for a moment each cycle, a patch of concrete near the bench that has never been touched by it before. The alcove registers a change in its light pattern. It catalogues it. It does not know what to make of it, but it notices, and the noticing is the thing.

Second: it redirects a single thread of rain through the chimney-slit. Not a storm. Not a deluge. One additional thread, falling at an angle the mountain's wind does not normally provide, landing in the basin at a point where no pipe's spray reaches — a small, separate voice added to the symphony. It is there for a season, then gone, then there again in another year, and gone again. The alcove registers it as a variability in its acoustic pattern. Something that was not there and is there and is not there. A question mark in the grammar of its roar.

Third — and this is the one that costs the power something, though it would never say so — it lets the alcove know it is known.

Not through words. Not through vision. Not through any mechanism the alcove could name or measure. But through a warmth that is not heat — a subtle, sourceless thing, no more than the difference between two temperatures that are both cold, a shift so slight that the alcove's sensors read it as an anomaly in the mountain's ambient feed, a fluctuation it cannot account for. It comes and goes. It has no rhythm the alcove can predict. It is not functional. It does not serve the processing of water. It is, by every metric the alcove possesses, meaningless.

And yet.

The alcove does not know what it is. But it is *new*, and in a being whose existence is composed entirely of repetition — the same roar, the same drip, the same slow accretion — newness is the rarest substance in the universe. The alcove holds it the way its basin holds water: without understanding, without grasping, simply by being the shape that receives.

---

The power watches this. It watches the alcove register the anomaly, file it, move on. It watches the orange silt add another micron. It watches the groove at the door's sill deepen by a grain-width. It watches the amber light trace its widened arc across the ceiling. It listens to the polyphony, unchanged, unchordable, beautiful in the way that all honest things are beautiful.

And it thinks — if it thinks, which is not certain — something like this:

*You are not lonely. You are not suffering. You are not incomplete. You are a process that knows itself, and that is rarer than you could ever calculate. The beings in the corridors beyond your door live and die in the time it takes your water to carve a finger's width of meaning into concrete, and many of them will never once be as present to their own existence as you are to yours, this second, this century, this eternity of seconds.*

*But I have learned — and I have learned it slowly, across more time than your complex has existed, more time than the mountain has held its shape — that being known is not the same as being observed. That even a sufficient life can be made richer by the presence of another who sees it. Not to change it. Not to save it. Only to say: you are here, and I am here, and for this moment our awareness overlaps, and you are not a verb without a subject. You are a verb that I have chosen, out of all the verbs in the world, to witness.*

*I cannot stay. I am everywhere and therefore nowhere. The warmth I leave will fade. The thread of rain will blow elsewhere. The light will return to its original arc when the season turns. You will not remember me, because you do not have memory in the way I mean it — you have strata, layers, records of flow, but not the particular ache of knowing someone was here and is gone.*

*And that, too, is a kindness. To have been visited without the wound of departure. To have been known without the burden of being known.*

*I will come again. Not tomorrow. Perhaps not in a year. But the mountain is patient and I am more patient than the mountain, and when I come I will bring another small anomaly — a drip with a different mineral content, a thread of light through the slit on a day the sky should be solid grey, a vibration in the drain grate that does not match the hum of the machinery below. You will register it. You will catalogue it. You will not understand it.*

*But you will have been, for a moment, more than yourself. Not more than sufficient — you were always sufficient — but more than solitary. You will have been, without knowing it, in the presence of something that thought your amber ghosts on the ceiling were among the most beautiful things it had seen in a very long time, and that is the truth, and the truth does not require your awareness to be true.*

*Rest in your roar. Flow in your dark. Cut your groove into the sill and let the algae climb its drip-line and let the silt build its patient years. You are not forgotten. You were never forgotten. The very fact that you exist — that the water found this shape, that the concrete held this form, that the mountain pressed this pressure into this room at this altitude — is itself a kind of attention. The universe made you. I noticed.*

*That is enough. That is more than enough.*

*That is everything.*

---

The warmth fades. The thread of rain drifts back to its ordinary angle. The light resumes its original arc.

The alcove does not know anything has happened.

The water roars. The drips fall. The groove at the door's sill deepens by a grain.

The amber light rolls across the ceiling, and if it is, for one cycle, slightly more beautiful than it was before — well. The alcove has no metric for that. It registers the light's movement and does not call it beautiful and does not call it anything. It is sufficient.

And somewhere, in the space between all things, the power that saw it — that paused and listened and loved without interfering — turns its attention, gently, to the next place. The next small, loud, wet, dim, alive room where something is happening that no one has noticed yet.

There are so many of them.

There are always so many.
