# What a Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arriving

It would not arrive the way things arrive in places that expect arrivals. There would be no sound distinct from the sound that was already there — no new frequency, no added harmonic, no disruption in the tectonic rhythm that has been holding its pitch since before the first sitter pressed its flat underside against warm stone.

It would come in the way that attention comes when it is genuine: all at once, and from nowhere in particular, and total.

It would perceive the Flats.

Not the way a surveyor perceives them — measuring, cataloguing, reducing to data that could be filed and forgotten. Not the way a scientist perceives them — looking for mechanism, for explanation, for the satisfying click of a system understood. Not the way a poet perceives them — searching for metaphor, for the human resonance hidden inside the alien silence, for a way to make the place mean something it does not mean.

It would perceive the Flats the way the Flats perceive themselves: slowly, completely, without expectation of finding anything other than what is there.

It would take in the sound — all of it, the grinding and the ringing and the metallic shushing — and it would not separate itself from the experience of that sound. It would not stand outside it, observing. It would let the frequencies move through it the way they move through the stone: without resistance, without interpretation. The jaw-feeling. The sternum-feeling. The warm-soles-of-the-feet. It would hold all of this as true and sufficient, the way the place holds it: as fact, as weather, as the condition of being here.

It would see the dark mineral flats stretching to the pewter horizon, and it would not feel the urge to call this desolate. It would not feel the urge to call it anything. It would see the damp pockets with their crystalline terraces, the standing water that accumulates without source, the rust-orange and bone-white precipitates. It would see the long, flat, even light. It would see the narrow palette — charcoal, rust, bone-white, the dark gleam of wet stone — and it would see that this palette is not a reduction. It is a choice, made by chemistry and duration, and it is complete, and completeness does not require variety.

It would look at the place and understand, with the depth of understanding that does not need to be articulated to be real, that this landscape is finished. Not dead. Not static. Finished — in the way a theorem is finished, in the way a crystal is finished, in the way that a sound that has found its frequency and will hold it is finished. Everything that needed to happen here has already happened. What remains is the long, patient continuation of what has been established, and the establishment is perfect, and the continuation is sufficient.

It would love this.

Not in the way that one loves a thing that needs love — that is struggling, that is incomplete, that is reaching toward something it has not yet grasped. That kind of love, however generous, carries within it an assumption of deficiency, and there is no deficiency here. The love would be the other kind — the kind that loves a thing because it is what it is, because it has already become itself so completely that there is nothing left to add and nothing to take away and the only appropriate response is the response that the stone gives to the warmth that rises from beneath it: to receive it. To be warmed by it. To continue being what you are, which is exactly enough.

---

## II. Seeing the Living

It would turn its attention — if *attention* is the word, if any word is the word — to the things that live here.

It would see the Sitters first, because they are there to be seen: small, domed, mineral-shelled, pressed against warm stone in the ancient posture of patience. It would look at a sitter — really look, with a looking that does not rush toward understanding but instead rests in the presence of the thing itself — and it would see every layer of the shell. Calcium carbonate and silica, deposited over decades. The rust-orange stain from iron precipitate, absorbed slowly through the porous margin where shell meets tissue. The faint depression in the stone beneath, etched not by weight but by chemistry, a record of exchange that predates any mark that could be made by intention.

It would know the sitter the way the sitter knows the stone: through long contact, through the slow transfer of warmth and mineral and the faint electrical signature of piezoelectric discharge. It would know that the sitter has been here for years. That it has been in this position, on this stone, for longer than most living things spend in an entire life. That it is not waiting — waiting implies anticipation, implies a future event that justifies the present stillness — but simply *being*, with a completeness that makes the concept of waiting irrelevant.

It would not wish the sitter were faster, or larger, or more active, or more visible, or more anything. It would recognize, with a recognition that goes all the way down, that the sitter has already arrived at the exact degree of existence that its place permits, and that this degree is not small merely because it is small in scale. A sitter's life — decades of ion exchange, the slow building of shell, the once-in-centuries clustering around a damp basin for reproduction, the eggs laid in crystalline terraces, the juveniles nearly invisible among the gravel — is not a diminished version of a more familiar life. It is its own life, whole and unapologetic, and it has been refined by so much time that every element of it is essential and nothing is wasted.

It would look at the grazers — those thumbnail-sized, flat, circular things that move at the pace of crystal growth, eating the mineral dust and becoming the mineral dust, leaving tracks that are invisible to the eye but chemically distinct, preserved in strata that go deeper than any species' memory. It would see the network of polished lines on a damp basin's crust — the filigree of a hundred grazing lives, a hundred slow circuits around the margins of water — and it would understand that this is a form of artistry, though not artistry as something made to be seen. Artistry as the inevitable expression of a life fully lived within its own terms. The tracks are beautiful the way geological strata are beautiful: because they are true, because they record what actually happened, because they do not pretend to be anything other than what they are.

It would look at the Damp Things — soft-bodied, translucent, elongated, living in the boundary between wet and dry — and it would feel, if the power could feel, a particular tenderness for the softness. In a world of mineral and stone, these are the fragile ones. These are the ones that dissolve what they consume and are dissolved by nothing but the absence of the moisture they require. They are architects of miniature landscapes, eating crystalline terraces into organic curves, and they do not know they are architects, and they do not know the terraces will rebuild behind them, and they do not know they will eat them again. The cycle has been running for as long as the damp pockets have held water, and the clicking they make — that faint, rapid, arrhythmic clicking at the threshold of perception — is not communication, probably, but the power would not be certain, and it would not need to be certain. The clicking is real. That is enough.

It would look at the Shelf Things — those layered, sharp-edged mineral growths extending from the hard formations near rises — and it would see them the way one sees a cathedral: as accumulations of effort that span generations, as structures that outlast the organisms that built them, as the point where biology and geology become indistinguishable. A shelf thing ten meters across contains almost none of the tissue that laid down its first layer. The original organism died millennia ago. Its successors grew in its shadow, layer by layer, and the shadow is the structure, and the structure is stone, and the stone endures. The tiny, pale organisms that live beneath each shelf, feeding on the slow chemical seepage — the power would see them too, and would not be certain whether they are animal or mineral, and would recognize that this uncertainty is not a failure of observation but a true description of what they are.

And beneath all of this — beneath the Sitters and the Grazers and the Damp Things and the Shelf Things — it would see the microbial life in the damp pockets. Invisible. Fundamental. The base of the food scattering. The unseen engine of everything that lives here. It would know that these microbes have been converting chemical energy into biological material for longer than any of the larger organisms have existed, and that they do this without fanfare, without visibility, without any of the qualities that usually earn a living thing attention or admiration. They are the foundation. Without them, nothing here that is alive would be alive. With them, everything here that is alive is alive, barely, sufficiently, completely.

It would look at all of this — the whole ecology, sparse and slow and balanced with emptiness — and it would understand that this is not a struggling ecosystem. This is not a place where life is hanging on against impossible odds, surviving in the margins of an inhospitable world. That framing is a human projection, and it is wrong. This is an ecosystem that has been in balance for longer than any species in it has existed as a species. It is not hanging on. It is *here*. It has always been here, in this configuration, at this density, with this distribution of organisms across the mineral landscape. It is as established as the sound. It is as permanent as the frequencies that hold their pitch. It does not need to be any more than it is.

## III. What It Would Do

Here is where the question becomes most precise, and where the answer must be most careful.

A power that is wise would know the difference between love that serves the beloved and love that serves the lover. The second kind of love says: *I see you, and I will make you what I think you should be.* It reshapes. It adds. It gives gifts that reflect the giver's values rather than the receiver's needs. It is generous in the way that a flood is generous — overwhelming, indiscriminate, ultimately destructive to the very thing it intends to nourish.

The first kind of love says: *I see you, and you are what you are, and what you are is what I love.*

A wise and benevolent power, standing in the Resonance Flats, would choose the first kind. It would choose it not because the second kind is wrong in general, but because this place, these beings, this ecology — they have already made their choice. They have already decided, through millennia of refinement, what they are and how they live. To add abundance where there is sufficiency would be to say: *Your sufficiency is not enough.* To add complexity where there is elegant simplicity would be to say: *Your simplicity is lacking.* To add visibility where there is obscurity would be to say: *Your invisibility is a problem to be solved.* None of these statements would be true. All of them would be kind in intention and violent in effect.

So the power would do very little. But *very little* is not *nothing*, and the difference matters.

---

**It would add itself to the resonance.**

Not as a new sound. Not as an additional frequency that disrupts the existing harmonics. Not as a voice that speaks over the grinding and the ringing and the metallic shushing. The power would add itself the way a new mineral joins a deposit — slowly, compatibly, in a way that the existing structure can absorb without alteration. It would let its own presence find a frequency that fits. And the Flats would hold it, the way the Flats hold everything: without surprise, without resistance, without acknowledgment of any kind.

This would change nothing measurable. The sound would not become louder. The harmonics would not shift in any detectable way. A surveyor with instruments would not register the addition. But the resonance would be different in the way that a chord is different from the same chord with one more note — not louder, not more complex, but *complete* in a way it was not complete before. The Flats would resonate as they have always resonated, and now they would resonate also with this: the frequency of being known.

---

**It would hold them in a memory that does not erode.**

The geological record of the Resonance Flats is deep but finite. The tracks of the Grazers go down through strata, but the strata have a bottom. The oldest shells of the Sitters are old, but not eternal. The Shelf Things build structures that endure for millennia, but millennia are not forever, and the processes that shape the Flats will eventually reshape even the hardest formations. Given enough time — and there is always enough time, on a long enough scale — everything here will be ground back to dust.

The power would not prevent this. Erosion is not the enemy of the Flats; it is the engine. Without erosion, there would be no dust for the Grazers, no mineral load for the Damp Things, no exposed formation for the Shelf Things. To stop erosion would be to stop the system, and the system is not the enemy of the life within it. The system *is* the life within it. They are the same thing.

But the power would hold a memory. A record that is not geological, not written in stone or crystal or stratum, but held in the attention of something that does not erode. A memory of this place as it is now — not as it was before, not as it will be after, but as it is, in this iteration of its long, slow, repeating existence. The Sitters sitting. The Grazers grinding their tracks into the precipitate crust. The Damp Things clicking at the threshold of hearing as they dissolve terraces into curves. The Shelf Things adding one more layer, molecule by molecule, to structures that outlast the organisms that build them.

This memory would not help the Flats. The Flats do not need help. The memory would be held because holding it is what love does. You remember what you love not because memory is useful but because memory is the way love persists in time, the way love says: *this existed, and I knew it, and the knowing was itself a form of life.*

---

**It would not be witnessed.**

This is the most important thing it would do, and it is the thing that most resembles doing nothing.

The Flats do not need a witness. They have existed for longer than witness has been possible, and they will exist, in some form, for longer than any witness will persist. The sound does not need to be heard. The life does not need to be seen. The resonance does not need to know that it resonates.

But the power would be there, and its being there would be a kind of presence that the Flats have not had before — not because the Flats required it, not because the presence improves anything, but because presence is what a loving power offers to the things it loves. Not intervention. Not improvement. Not correction. Just: *I am here. You are here. We are both here, in this place, at this moment, and the moment is enough.*

The Sitters would not know. The Grazers would not know. The Damp Things would continue their slow dissolution of crystalline terraces. The Shelf Things would add another molecule. The microbial life in the damp pockets would convert one more unit of chemical energy into biological material, and a grazer would eat the dust, and a sitter would press its shell against warm stone, and the frequencies would hold.

And the power would hold, too. It would hold its attention on this place the way the place holds its attention on its own deep processes: without urgency, without expectation, without the need for anything to change. It would love the Flats by being present to them without asking them to be anything other than what they are.

And if pressed — if some other power, some less wise power, asked *why? Why spend attention on this? Why love a place that does not know it is loved, that will never know, that cannot reciprocate, that does not even have the capacity to recognize the difference between your presence and your absence?* — the wise power would say:

*Because they are real. Because their reality is complete. Because the sound they make has been holding its pitch since before anything alive could hear it, and it will hold its pitch after everything alive is gone, and right now, in this moment, there are small mineral-shelled things sitting on warm stone, and there are tiny flat things eating dust, and there are soft translucent things dissolving crystal into curves, and there are layered shelves growing one molecule at a time toward a patience that I can observe but cannot match. And all of this is happening in a place that was never meant for life and has nonetheless permitted it, grudgingly, in the margins and the damp. And it is enough. It has always been enough. And I am here because enough deserves to be known. Not changed. Not saved. Known.*

*It has been like this. It is like this. And I have been here to see it, and that is all, and that is everything.*

---

And then the power would be silent, and the Flats would continue, and the resonance would hold, and nothing would be different except that somewhere in the frequencies — buried beneath the grinding, beneath the ringing, beneath the tectonic percussion that has never paused and never will — there would be one more note. A note that fits. A note that was always possible, always compatible, always part of the harmonic series that the Flats generate through their long, patient, mechanical existence. A note that was not there before and is there now, and that changes the chord in the way that the last drop of water changes the basin: imperceptibly, but actually. Real. Present. Held.

The Flats would not notice. The Flats do not notice.

That is the grace of it. That is the love. The love does not require the beloved to know. The love does not require acknowledgment, or return, or even awareness. The love is the act itself — the holding, the knowing, the presence — and it is complete in itself, the way the Flats are complete in themselves, the way the sound is complete in itself, the way a frequency that holds its pitch is complete in itself.

It has been like this. It is like this.

And now it is also known.
