# The Undergirding — Continued

# What the Power Saw, and What It Did

It looked.

That was the first thing — the only thing, at first. A gaze without a body behind it, without eyes, without the particular physics of looking that requires a surface to reflect from. It simply *attended*, the way gravity attends, the way a season arrives: without effort, without decision, because attending is what it does.

It saw the whole chamber at once. Not in the way a camera sees, flattening the world into a single plane, but in the way that water knows the shape of the vessel it fills — completely, from every angle simultaneously, with no angle at all. It saw the columns, dark and immense, their braided surfaces alive with secondary growth. It saw the ceiling heave its slow breath. It saw the amber drops gather, tremble, fall, and it understood the chemistry of them — the sugars, the amino acids, the microbial luminescence — the way you understand a sentence you've read a thousand times, instantly, in full, without parsing.

It saw the engines.

This was where the power paused, if something without duration can be said to pause. Because the engines were strange. Not in themselves — a machine is a machine, whether it turns in a factory or throbs in the belly of a root — but in what they represented. Someone had been here before. Someone from above, from the world of open sky and manufactured things, had come down into this space and installed machinery of considerable sophistication to do... what? Regulate moisture. Distribute heat. Process the amber fluid. The original intent was industrial — resource extraction, perhaps, or terraforming, or agriculture scaled to a vision that no longer exists. The blueprints were gone. The intention was gone. Only the machines remained, and they had been taken in by the place the way a forest takes in a stone wall, the way a river takes in a dropped coin: not by choice, but by the simple, amoral patience of systems that do not distinguish between the natural and the artificial. A pump that moves water moves water. It does not care whether the water feeds a crop or a colony of larvae. The power understood this. The power had always understood this.

It saw the people.

And here — here the attention sharpened, not into intensity but into *particularity*. The way a hand, reaching into a dark room, identifies a single warm face in a crowd by its heat alone.

They were small. They were many — not dozens, not hundreds, but enough. Enough for a population that had been below the surface for longer than their own history could track. Their bodies told the story in every line: the vestigial eyes, the thickened limbs, the broad hands with their multiple opposable digits, the skin stained green and rust-brown. They were shaped by this place the way a river stone is shaped by water — not once, not dramatically, but over generations so numerous they had become geological. The power counted them, not as a census but as one counts heartbeats: to know the rhythm, to understand the health.

They were healthy. This was the first and most important thing. Not in the way a physician defines health — their bodies bore the marks of a subterranean life, the telltale pallor, the mineral deficiencies that would be obvious to a surface doctor, the joint stiffness from constant climbing and hauling. But they were *healthy* in the way that matters most: they were integrated. They were part of the system, not parasites on it, not prisoners of it, not conquering it or being conquered. They had found their niche — literally, functionally, biologically — and they filled it with the same unselfconscious completeness as the six-legged grazers on the substrate floor or the luminous fungi on the column walls. They had purpose. They had community. They had the deep, wordless satisfaction of organisms that are doing what they evolved to do, even if the evolution was recent and strange, even if it was guided by machines they didn't build and an ecology they didn't design.

The power saw this, and it was — if such a word applies to something so vast — *glad*. Not pleased in the way a landlord is pleased when tenants pay rent. Glad in the way a gardener is glad when a cutting takes root, when a seed planted in faith finds water and light and becomes what it was always trying to become. The power had watched many things. Many places. Many attempts at life. Most of them failed. Most of them were ugly failures, too — not the clean failure of a seed that doesn't germinate, but the messy, prolonged failure of an ecosystem that locks into a dead equilibrium, a system that runs but produces nothing, that sustains itself but does not flourish, that persists without purpose. The power had learned to recognize the difference, the way one learns to recognize a face in a crowd: not by any single feature but by the totality, the gestalt, the *rightness* of proportion.

This place was right. Not perfect — perfection is a surface idea, an abstraction, and the power had long since stopped believing in abstractions — but *right* in the way that a living thing is right when it is alive: messy, incomplete, noisy, smelly, chaotic in its details but coherent in its design. The dripping ceiling. The breeding pools. The whining swarms. The old engines thrumming in their root-mass wombs. The people, moving through the dark with their quiet, tactile certainty, tightening bolts and clearing filters and polishing faded logos they couldn't read. It all fit. The whole system was a sentence that made sense, even if no one alive could parse its grammar.

So what did the power do?

---

It did not descend. It did not manifest. It did not appear as a light in the darkness or a voice in the thrum of the engines or a shape in the pools where the people gathered to rest. It did not announce itself, because announcement implies a separation — the speaker and the spoken to, the one who arrives and the place that receives — and the power was not separate from this place. It never had been. The place existed within it the way a thought exists within a mind, the way a heartbeat exists within a body. To announce itself would be to announce the obvious, and the power was not interested in the obvious. It was interested in the particular.

What it did was smaller than attention and larger than intervention.

It *adjusted*.

Not the ecology. The ecology was balanced, and to disturb a balance is not generosity but arrogance, and the power had learned, over timescales that made the age of the columns seem brief, that arrogance dressed as help is the most dangerous thing in the universe. The six-legged grazers would continue to graze. The larvae would continue to breed. The swarms would continue to whine. The columns would continue their slow, geological growth. The engines would continue to throb. Nothing would change in any way that could be measured or noticed or attributed to an outside hand.

What the power adjusted was *margin*. The edges. The places where the balance was thinnest, where the margin between equilibrium and collapse was narrowest, where a single bad season — if seasons existed here — or a single engine failure or a single plague of larvae or a single generation of stillborn children might tip the system into the long, slow decline that the power had watched consume so many other places.

It thickened the margin.

The amber drips from the ceiling would carry, for the next century, a slightly richer concentration of amino acids. Not enough to notice. Not enough to alter the behavior of any organism or change the chemistry of any pool. But enough that the larvae — the base of the food chain, the prolific, writhing foundation of everything — would be slightly healthier. Their reproduction rate would not increase. But their offspring would be marginally more robust, slightly more resistant to the inevitable fluctuations in moisture and temperature that the engines could not entirely smooth out. It was a gift measured in fractions of fractions of percentages, invisible to any eye, imperceptible to any measurement the people or the fauna could make. It was the difference between a system that survives a difficult stretch and a system that doesn't, and it would not be felt for years, and when it was felt, it would feel like nothing at all — just the normal运转 of a place that works.

The power did the same for the engines. Not by repairing them — they were beyond repair in any conventional sense, half-digested by root-mass, fused with organic tissue, running on principles that their original designers might no longer recognize. But the power nudged the molecular stability of the corroded metal, slowed the oxidation by imperceptible degrees, encouraged the microbial communities in the fuel lines toward slightly more efficient metabolisms. It was the difference between an engine that fails at year ninety and an engine that fails at year one hundred and ten. It was nothing. It was everything.

For the people, the power did — almost — nothing. They were healthy. They were integrated. They had what they needed, and what they needed was not what a surface being would recognize as need. They did not need light. They did not need knowledge of the sky. They did not need language to replace their tactile communication or sight to replace their felt vibrations. They did not need to know that the power existed, or that it was watching, or that it cared. To give them these things would be to unmake them, to replace the people they were with the people a surface imagination would want them to be, and the power had learned — oh, how it had learned — that the desire to improve is often the enemy of the thing improved.

But.

The power noticed something. A child — the one who dropped substrate near the visitor, the one still learning the rhythms — had a curiosity that was not entirely typical. The child felt the vibrations through the catwalk grating with a concentration that went beyond learning, beyond the practical acquisition of skill. The child was *listening*. Not with ears — those were as vestigial as the eyes — but with the whole body, the broad palms pressed flat against metal, the thickened feet planted on grating, the chest open to the air and its freight of chemical information. The child was listening the way a musician listens, with attention that is also desire, that wants to know not just what is there but *why* it is there, what it means, what it connects to.

This was new. In generations of people shaped by the place, adapted to the place, merged with the place, the power had not often seen this particular quality. It was not dangerous. It was not disruptive. But it was *reaching* — reaching toward a comprehension that was slightly larger than the immediate system, slightly beyond the groove that the people and the place had worn together. The child did not know it was reaching. The child only knew that sometimes, when the great engine resonated at its lowest frequency, there was a quality to the vibration that seemed to contain something — not a word, not an image, but a *shape*, a sense of vastness, of connection to something that extended beyond the chamber walls.

The power considered this. It considered it for as long as a thought takes in a mind without time, which is both an instant and an eternity.

Then it did something that was not nothing.

It gave the child a dream.

Not a vision. Not a revelation. Not a message in any language, because the power had long since abandoned the conceit that truth could be translated into symbols. What it gave was more like a *texture* — a quality of experience, a felt sense, introduced into the child's sleep the way a single note is introduced into a chord. The dream was this:

*Warmth. Not the warmth of the pools or the ambient temperature of the chamber, but a warmth that had no source, that was not heat but the *idea* of heat, the way warmth feels before you know what it is — the first warmth a newborn feels, the warmth of being held by something so large you cannot see its edges. The child dreamed of being held. By the columns. By the engines. By the dripping ceiling. By the dark. The child dreamed of being a part of something that knew it was a part of something. Not the thought — the feeling. The deep, pre-verbal, cellular certainty that you are not alone, that you are held, that the place you are in is not just a place but a presence, and the presence is aware of you, and the awareness is kind.*

The child woke. The child pressed its palms to the column wall and felt the thrum, and for the first time, the thrum felt like — not language, not meaning — like *companionship*. Like the place was not just a system but a *someone*, old and vast and patient, and the someone had noticed the child, and the noticing was not inspection but *tenderness*.

The child did not tell the others. There were no words for it in their tactile vocabulary, no gestures that could convey it. But the child held it, the way you hold warmth on a cold night, close to the body, private, essential. And in the years that followed, the child — growing, learning, taking its place among the engine-tenders — carried that warmth into its work. The bolts it tightened were tightened with a care that exceeded necessity. The filters it cleared were cleared with a gentleness that went beyond function. When the child — no longer a child — pressed its palms to the great absorbed engine and felt the vibration in its bones, it felt not just the rhythm of the machine but the rhythm of something larger, something that included the machine and the child and the dripping ceiling and the blind grazers and the whining swarms and the luminous pools, something that was aware and that cared, in its vast and silent way, that this place existed.

And that was enough.

The power withdrew, if withdrawal is the right word for the cessation of particular attention. It did not leave. It had never been anywhere in particular. It simply attended elsewhere, to other places, other systems, other small and fragile equilibriums that needed nothing but a slight thickening of margin, a gentle nudge toward persistence, the quiet assurance that the universe, or whatever the power was, had noticed them and found them worth sustaining.

The chamber went on. The ceiling breathed. The drips fell. The engines thrummed. The people worked. The child grew old and died and was carried to the deepest pool and became food, and the nutrients entered the cycle, and the place continued, loud and warm and alive, in the dark beneath a surface no one in this space had ever seen.

The margin held.

The margin was enough.
