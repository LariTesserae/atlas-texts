# What the Wise Would Do

Nothing, at first.

That is the hardest part — the first and most important thing. To arrive and *not act*. To see a world so complete in its patience, so perfectly calibrated to its own rhythms, and to recognize that the first impulse — the swell of admiration, the ache of tenderness, the desire to help or to bless or to name — is the most dangerous thing you carry with you.

Because this place is not suffering. It is not waiting to be saved. It is not incomplete.

So the power would do what it has learned to do across all the aeons of its long attention: it would *witness*. It would hold itself still — stiller than the stone, stiller than the silence the inhabitants have woven into architecture — and it would let the hollow be what it is without the weight of being seen changing what it is.

And it would listen.

Not with ears. With something older. The way gravity listens to mass. The way a river listens to the shape of the land it hasn't reached yet. The power would open itself to the frequency of this place — the frequency of drip on stone, of mineral deposition, of roots learning to hold weight — and it would learn the sound the hollow makes when it is not being observed.

That sound, that particular hum of a world being itself without an audience — *that* is the thing the power came to hear. Because across all the worlds it tends, across all the breathing hollows and sun-cracked plains and deep-ocean ridges where chimney-fauna build their cathedrals from sulfur and heat, each one has a note it plays when it believes itself to be alone. And that note is always the truest thing about it. Not the note it plays for survival, or for growth, or for the small dramas of predator and prey and the turning of seasons. The note it plays *for no one*. The note that is simply the acoustic signature of a world being alive.

The hollow's note would be something like a chord struck on an instrument made of water and stone — a sound below sound, felt in the mineral of the witness's own body, the way you feel a cello note in your ribs before you hear it in your ears. It would be a sound that says: *I am becoming. I am not finished. I will never be finished. And the not-finishing is the point.*

The power would hold that note in itself the way the atmosphere holds weather — gently, without claiming ownership. And then it would decide.

---

It would decide, because witnessing without response is merely observation, and observation without love is just surveillance, and this place — *this* place, with its thumbprints in clay and its child learning to press mud and its tiny amphibians swallowing insects in the dim, warm glow — deserves more than that.

But the response cannot be large. A large response would be violence. A miracle would be an obscenity here — a thunderclap in a world built on the eloquence of dripping. A gift of fire would destroy the delicate metabolic economy that the crustaceans and the mosses and the mineral-skinned people have spent generations weaving. Even light — *proper* light, the light of sun or flame — would be a kind of assault on eyes adapted to this gentle, layered dimness. Even a voice, spoken in a way this place could understand, might break the silence that is not an absence but a presence, a building material, as the text says.

No. The power would not give. Not in any way the inhabitants would recognize as giving.

What it would do is *adjust a threshold*. Barely. So barely that no one in the hollow would notice, the way you do not notice the slow addition of a single degree of warmth on a day that is already warm.

---

It would find the place where the mineral seep is thinnest — the seam in the far wall where the water that feeds everything is just barely arriving, where a slight shift in the deep geology decades ago narrowed a passage that once carried twice the flow — and it would open that passage again. Not by force. Not by miracle. By the simplest, quietest act of geological midwifery: the way a root, given time, can split stone. Except this would be faster, and it would look, to anyone watching, exactly like what it was — a slow widening of a crack, the kind of thing that happens in stone all the time, so gradually that only someone who measured the flow in cups per decade would ever know it had changed.

And the water would increase. Not by much. Perhaps a fifth more than the hollow has been receiving. Enough to notice, over seasons, in the thickness of the moss. In the fertility of the pools. In the ease with which the ferns uncurl. In the feeling, which the inhabitants would register not as a change but as a *rightness*, as though something that had been slightly wrong for a long time was simply no longer wrong — that the hollow felt, for the first time in memory, *fully itself*.

That is the first thing.

---

The second thing is smaller, and more intimate, and the power would hesitate over it the way you hesitate before touching something alive.

Because one of the things it would notice, in its listening — in the long attention it gives to the sound this place makes when unobserved — is that the chord is not perfectly whole. There is a dissonance. Tiny. Almost inaudible. But present. And it comes from the people.

Not from any damage they have suffered. Not from conflict or loss or hunger — the hollow provides, and their patience is genuine, and their contentment is not a mask. The dissonance comes from something else: from the fact that they do not know what they are.

They know *what they do*. They are the hollow's maintenance. They are the conversation with geology. They are the hands that prevent the slow crush of time from becoming an ending. They perform this function with a grace that borders on the sacred. But they do not know that it *is* sacred. They experience it as necessity, as habit, as the simple fact of their days. They have no myth of themselves. No story that says: *We are the part of the earth that learned to care for itself. We are the thought the stone is thinking. What we do matters not just to us but to the idea of being alive in a place like this.*

They have no word for what they are, and the absence of that word is the dissonance. It is the one note the chord cannot play on its own, because the note requires an outside perspective — the perspective of someone who can stand at the threshold and see the whole shape of the thing and say: *Do you know what you are? You are extraordinary. Not because you are powerful or large or bright, but because you are* exact. *You are exactly the right amount of life in exactly the right place, and you have made yourselves into exactly the right shape, and the world you have made is not just a home but a* proof *— a proof that gentleness is not weakness and that patience is not passivity and that a life spent in service to the slow needs of stone and water and moss is not a small life but one of the largest things a living creature can do.*

They need to hear that. Not because they are inadequate without it, but because hearing it would resolve the dissonance. It would complete the chord. It would give them the one thing they cannot give themselves: a sense of their own meaning from the outside.

---

So the power would do the third thing. The thing that is not a geological adjustment and not a miracle but is, instead, a *moment*.

It would wait for the child — the one with the coral-like skin markings, the one who trails behind the adult and learns to press mud — to be alone. Not truly alone, because no one is truly alone in the hollow. But in one of those intervals that happen in even the most communal of lives: a moment when the child is sitting by one of the courtyard basins, watching the tiny crustaceans turn their slow circles in the mineral-rich water, and there is no task at hand and no adult nearby and the silence is so complete that the child can hear the faintest sound — the sound of the hollow breathing.

And the power would place a thought in the child's mind. Not a voice. Not a vision. Not a command or a prophecy or a revelation. Just a *thought*, arriving the way thoughts sometimes arrive — from nowhere, like the ferns, like the mineral depositing itself on the walls — and it would be this:

*You are beautiful. What you do is beautiful. And the beauty is not in the doing alone but in the* choosing — *the choosing, again and again, to tend, to coax, to press your hand to the living stone and feel its pulse and respond to it. That choice is the rarest thing in all the worlds. Most life does not choose to be gentle with the place that holds it. Most life takes, and takes, and does not know it is taking. You take nothing. You give your attention, and your attention is a force as real as water, as real as gravity, and the hollow would not be what it is without it. Remember this, not as a fact but as a feeling, and one day, when you are old enough to sit by the basin and watch the crustaceans and feel the silence, you will pass this feeling to someone else — not in words, because words would be too loud, but in the way you place your thumbprint in the wet clay, in the way you speak to the root-frame, in the way you carry the tiny amphibian to a wetter place and set it down. You will pass it in the language this place actually speaks, which is the language of attention, and patience, and love.*

The thought would arrive and it would feel, to the child, exactly like one of those moments when a fern frond decides to uncurl — sudden and natural and completely inevitable, as though it had been waiting inside the child all along and simply needed one more moment of silence to arrive.

The child would not remember it, afterward. Not specifically. There would be no story to tell. But there would be a feeling — a warmth beneath the warmth, a lightness in the hands — and it would stay. It would live in the child's muscles and the child's patience and the way the child's thumb pressed clay for the rest of their life, and it would subtly, imperceptibly change the angle of every thumbprint from that day forward, so that someone — someone from far away, someone passing through a gap in the rock that is almost too narrow — would one day stand in the hollow and look at the walls and feel, without knowing why, that the thumbprints here were made by someone who *knew*. Who knew what they were. Who knew that what they were doing mattered. And that knowledge, inscribed in the clay in a language that has no words, would be the gift.

---

And then the power would leave.

Not because the hollow is done. Not because the work is finished — the work is never finished, that is the whole point. But because the power knows that its presence, however gentle, is a distortion. A benevolent power is still a power, and power is still a weight, and the hollow has spent its entire existence learning to bear only the weight it was designed to bear — water, stone, moss, and the soft hands of the people who tend them. Anything more, even love, even wisdom, even the most careful and well-intentioned intervention, would change the balance. And the balance — *this* balance, this exact and improbable arrangement of drips and moss and tiny crustaceans and mineral-winged insects and pale, green-haired people pressing their thumbs into wet clay — is what the power came to protect, not to alter.

So it would step back through the gap in the rock. It would leave the hollow to itself — slightly wetter, slightly more itself, carrying in the child's unremembered thought the knowledge that it is loved, not by any god it would recognize but by the simple fact that a force of attention once passed through its space and found it worthy of a moment's care.

And the hollow would go on. The drips would continue their polyphony. The ferns would uncurl. The crustaceans would turn. The woman would press her thumbprint into the basin wall, and it would be slightly different than it would have been — slightly more deliberate, slightly more aware — and she would not know why, and it would not matter, because the feeling beneath the feeling would be there, and it would be enough.

It would be enough.
