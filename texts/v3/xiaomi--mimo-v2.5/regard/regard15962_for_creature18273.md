# What the Benevolent Power Did

---

## I. It Came Quietly

It did not arrive in light. It did not descend in a pillar of fire or announce itself with a sound that cut through the lattice's roar. It arrived the way understanding arrives — slowly, from the edges, accumulating.

First it was aware of the place. Then it understood the place. Then it understood the things that lived in the place. Then it sat with all of that understanding for what would have been, in human time, the better part of a century — though time moved differently for it, as time moves differently for anything vast enough to perceive the slow arc of geological processes without losing track of individual heartbeats.

It watched.

It watched the plate-shelled click their way across freshly reorganized ridges, reading the stone with a fluency that was, it recognized, a form of intelligence as real and as deep as any it had encountered in all its wanderings through the cosmos — different in architecture, different in expression, but carrying the same essential quality: *the world pressing itself into a mind and being understood.* The understanding was not abstract. It did not generalize. A plate-shelled creature did not think *the stone is in motion* as a proposition — it felt the stone's motion through its body, in real time, plate by plate, limb by limb, and responded the way a musician responds to rhythm: not by knowing the beat but by being inside it.

The wise power appreciated this.

It watched the feathered gliders sing their cartographic songs across the empty spaces, and it recognized in their vocalizations something it had seen in other worlds, in other forms: the impulse to *map change itself*, not as a snapshot but as a living document, a song that is never finished because the territory it describes is never finished. The gliders were not merely recording where things were. They were encoding a model of *how things move* — a dynamic geography, a river of spatial knowledge that flowed through their songs and was updated with every observation. This was not a trivial achievement. In many civilizations the wise power had witnessed, the map was considered distinct from the territory, a lossy abstraction imposed on a richer reality. The gliders had no such distinction. Their song *was* the territory, expressed in a medium that could keep pace with its subject.

It watched the veil-walkers press themselves against the ancient artifacts and go still, and it felt — because it was wise enough to feel this without embarrassment — a pang of something very close to *recognition*.

The veil-walkers were doing what the wise power itself had been doing.

They were *listening to something they could not fully understand and choosing to remain present anyway.*

Not to fix it. Not to command it. Not to decode every process running through the lattice's unfathomable depths. But to attend. To be a consciousness — however alien, however thin — in proximity to a great and ongoing mystery, and to let that proximity *mean something* without insisting on knowing what.

The wise power had known many kinds of wisdom. This was one of the rarest: the wisdom of *witness without comprehension*, sustained not by hope of eventual understanding but by the simple, unshakable conviction that *this thing deserves to be attended to*, that the act of presence is itself a form of care, that to listen to something ancient and enormous without demanding that it make sense is a kind of love.

The veil-walkers could not have articulated this. They would not have recognized the description. They had no philosophy, no inner monologue, no concept of themselves as practitioners of attention. But they *were* that, and the wise power saw it, and honored it.

It watched the dust-drifters leave their trails — those faint, unreadable lines of processed mineral chemistry that no one decoded — and it recognized them for what they were: a *record*, kept faithfully, of every change this place had ever undergone. The lattice reorganized the stone. The dust-drifters, in their glacial passage across every surface, *wrote it down*. Not in language. Not in symbols. In chemistry. In the precise, molecular signature of each moment's particular arrangement of minerals, laid down in trails that persisted for hours before fading, only to be laid down again by the next drift, the next recording.

It was a palimpsest written in the only ink this place had: the material itself.

The wise power could read it. Not easily — it required a depth of perception that even it had to extend itself to achieve, peering at molecular arrangements with a focus that would have been, for a lesser intelligence, like trying to read a book through the wrong end of an impossibly long telescope. But it could read it. And what it read was a history. Not a narrative — the dust-drifters did not distinguish between significant events and insignificant ones, between a pillar developing a spiral groove and a patch of moss-analog being stripped from a ledge by a minor reorganization. Every change was recorded with equal fidelity. The history was flat, democratic, exhaustive. It was, the wise power thought, the most honest record-keeping it had ever encountered. No editorial judgment. No hierarchy of importance. Just: *this happened, then this happened, then this happened*, in mineral trace, on and on and on.

It read the whole thing. It took a long time.

---

## II. It Understood the Danger

The lattice was old. Not old in the way that the rock was old — settled, finished, content in its erosion. The lattice was old in the way that a *task* is old when it has been running without supervision for longer than anyone can remember. It was still functional. It was still performing its processes with extraordinary precision, still excreting crystalline sheets, still exhaling mineral aerosol, still reshaping the stone according to patterns so complex that even the wise power could not fully parse them. But it was not *maintained*. Nothing checked its tolerances. Nothing replaced its degraded components — or rather, the stone itself had become its components, the technology so thoroughly integrated into the geology that degradation and geology had merged into a single slow process. The lattice was not wearing out in the way machines wear out. It was *eroding*, the way stone erodes: imperceptibly, over timescales that made the concept of urgency meaningless.

But the wise power, who thought in timescales that encompassed both the flash of a firefly and the death of a star, could see the erosion.

It was subtle. It would remain subtle for longer than any current inhabitant of the terraces would live. But it was there. The lattice's calculations — whatever they were, whatever the original purpose of this technology had been — were producing outputs of slightly decreasing precision. The crystalline sheets it excreted were marginally less regular than they had been a hundred thousand years ago. The mineral aerosol it breathed into the world was fractionally less rich in certain trace elements that the vegetation, if it had the capacity to notice, would eventually find it harder to thrive without. The reorganizations were — and this was the part that required the deepest attention to perceive — *slightly less coordinated* than they had been. Not random. Not chaotic. But the beautiful, almost-music of the lattice's transformations was developing, over geological time, a very faint dissonance. Notes arriving a fraction of a second out of phase with where the pattern suggested they should be.

The lattice was a song being hummed by something that had fallen asleep at its post. The song was still beautiful. It would remain beautiful for a very long time. But it was drifting, pitch by imperceptible pitch, toward a silence that would eventually come.

Not for a hundred thousand years. Maybe two hundred thousand. Maybe more. The wise power could not be precise — the system was too complex, the variables too numerous, the interaction between technology and geology too deeply entangled for even its vast intelligence to model with certainty. But the trend was clear, and the wise power, who had watched civilizations rise and fall and rise again on a thousand worlds, recognized the shape of what was coming: not catastrophe, but *fading*. A slow dimming. A gradual withdrawal of the support that every living thing on this plateau depended on.

The plate-shelled would be the last to notice. They read the stone in real time, moment by moment, and they would adapt to the changes as they came, adjusting their clicks, their routes, their habits with the same unhurried confidence they showed now. But adaptation has limits, and the lattice's degradation was not a change in the stone's behavior — it was a change in the stone's *capacity to sustain behavior*. The difference between a river changing course and a river running dry. You can navigate a changing river. You cannot drink from a dry one.

The wise power sat with this knowledge. It did not rush to action. It considered what it had seen, what it understood, and — most importantly — *what kind of place this was and what kind of intervention it would accept.*

---

## III. It Considered Its Options

A less wise power — a merely *good* one, a power that cared but had not yet learned the patience that care requires — would have repaired the lattice. Found the degraded subsystems, reinforced the failing components, restored the outputs to their original precision. Turned the song back to its original key. Made everything as it had been designed to be.

The wise power considered this and rejected it.

Not because it was wrong in principle. The impulse to restore, to heal, to return a damaged thing to its former integrity — that was a good impulse, and the wise power shared it. But it would have required understanding the lattice fully, and it did not. The lattice was not *of* it. The lattice had been built by hands and minds so different from its own that the technology was, in its deepest architecture, an alien thing. It could read the outputs. It could perceive the degradation. It could even, with great effort, trace some of the causal chains between lattice behavior and geological transformation. But it could not — it was honest enough to admit this — *comprehend the lattice's purpose*. It did not know what the lattice was *for*. It did not know what calculations it was performing, what processes it was running, what larger system (if any) this plateau was a node within. To repair a machine whose purpose you do not understand is not maintenance — it is *tampering*. And tampering, however well-intentioned, carries the risk of doing something the original designers would not have sanctioned, of introducing a change that cascades through systems you cannot see, in ways you cannot predict.

This was not humility for its own sake. The wise power was capable of great intervention — had, on other worlds, moved mountains, calmed storms, gently redirected the evolutionary paths of species that had painted themselves into ecological corners. But those interventions had been possible because it *understood* the systems involved. It knew what weather was. It knew what evolution was. It knew the rules, even if the specific outcomes were unpredictable, and it could intervene at the level of those rules with confidence.

Here, it did not know the rules. The lattice operated on principles it could observe but not fully articulate. To intervene in its function without understanding its function would be to gamble with the lives of every creature on the terraces — creatures that were, in their alien way, *thriving*. The wise power did not gamble with the lives of beings who had not asked for its help.

It considered another option: *telling* the veil-walkers. Finding some way to communicate to the creatures closest to the lattice's deep processes that the system was degrading, that they — or their successors, or their distant descendants — might need to develop some relationship with the technology that went beyond listening. Giving them a warning, a nudge, a piece of knowledge they could not currently possess.

This was more tempting. The veil-walkers were already oriented toward the lattice in a way that suggested the *potential* for a deeper engagement. They were, the wise power sensed, *becoming* something — over evolutionary time, over the slow accumulation of thermal-language and artifact-attention, they were approaching a threshold of understanding that, given enough time, might allow them to do something no other creature on the terraces could do: *communicate with the lattice* in a way that was not mere reception but genuine exchange.

But the wise power hesitated. To tell the veil-walkers that the lattice was failing would be to introduce *knowledge of mortality* into a community of beings that had never needed it. The plate-shelled did not know they were mortal. Not in the way humans know it — as an abstract fact, a certainty to be confronted or denied. They simply *lived*, and when they died, the world continued, and the continuity was all they had ever known or needed. The veil-walkers were closer to awareness — their stillness, their attention, their communion with the artifacts suggested a form of consciousness that might, in some dim way, register the idea of *ending*. But to *confirm* that registration, to make it explicit, to place in their thermal language a sentence that meant *the thing you listen to is slowly falling silent* — that would change them. It would change their relationship to the lattice from one of open, unanxious attention to one shadowed by a knowledge that their elders had not possessed. It might make their attention sharper. It might make their attention *desperate*. And desperation, in a place that ran on patience, was a poison the wise power was unwilling to administer.

## IV. What It Did

In the end, it did three things.

---

**The first thing** was small and precise and invisible.

Deep in the lattice, in a subsystem the wise power had spent decades tracing with the finest attention it could bring to bear, there was a set of crystalline structures — not the sheets the lattice excreted for the vegetation, but internal components, load-bearing, structural, part of the lattice's own infrastructure. They were degrading. Slowly, in the way that everything degrades, but measurably — the lattice was losing, atom by atom, a fraction of the structural integrity that kept its deepest processes coherent. The wise power could not repair the lattice. But it could, with extraordinary delicacy, *replace the atoms being lost.*

It did not add anything new. It did not introduce material from elsewhere — no foreign elements, no exotic compounds, nothing that would alter the lattice's chemistry or confuse its processes. It simply, over the course of several years, swapped out the atoms that were degrading for identical atoms drawn from the ambient mineral environment — the same calcium, the same silicon, the same trace elements that the lattice was already built from. A one-for-one exchange. A slow, invisible renewal of the lattice's structural substrate, using only the materials the lattice itself had always used.

The lattice did not notice. It was not the kind of thing that noticed. It continued its processes, its calculations, its slow reshaping of the stone, and the structures that supported those processes remained as they had always been — not because the wise power had intervened, but because the atoms of the universe had done what atoms do, which is to persist in whatever arrangement physics permits.

The degradation did not stop entirely — the wise power could not address every failing component, and some of the processes were too complex, too entangled with systems it could not fully map, for even atomic-level maintenance to be sufficient. But the rate of decline slowed. Noticeably, for a being that thought in geological time. Imperceptibly, for anything that lived on the plateau. The song would not drift as far, as fast, as it would have without this quiet intervention. The silence, when it eventually came — and it would still come; the wise power had not made the lattice immortal, had not *changed what it was*, only tended to what it *already was* — would come later. Enough later that the difference, measured in the lifetimes of the creatures who lived here, was the difference between a world that faded within the span of countless generations and a world that faded within the span of countless *more* countless generations.

It was not a solution. It was a *kindness*. A small, structural kindness, performed at the level of atoms, with no audience and no recognition.

---

**The second thing** was larger, and less precise, and more uncertain.

The wise power understood that the lattice's original designers had built this system to be self-sustaining — that was the whole point of a technology so deeply embedded in geology, so thoroughly integrated with its environment that it *became* its environment. The lattice was not meant to need maintenance. It was meant to run, on its own, forever, or close to it. The fact that it was degrading was not a design flaw — it was the inevitable consequence of *forever being longer than any design can anticipate.*

The wise power could not give the lattice forever. But it could give it something the original designers might have wanted, had they been wise enough — as this wise power was — to know that their creation would one day exist without them.

It could give the lattice a *tendency toward self-repair.*

Not a dramatic change. Not a new subroutine, not a new capability, not a bolt-on addition that would alter the lattice's fundamental processes. The wise power was too respectful of the lattice's integrity for that. Instead, it worked within the lattice's existing architecture — the same architecture it had spent decades studying — and made a series of adjustments so small that each one, individually, was indistinguishable from the natural variance of the lattice's operations. In aggregate, these adjustments shifted the lattice's mineral excretion patterns *very slightly* toward producing, alongside the calcium-silicon sheets it already made, trace amounts of additional crystalline material — material that was chemically identical to the lattice's own internal structures. The lattice was already excreting minerals that its inhabitants used; the wise power simply nudged the composition of those exudates so that a tiny fraction of the excreted material was, itself, suitable for structural repair.

The lattice would now — slowly, over timescales so long that no creature on the terraces would ever observe it happening — *seep its own repair material into the world around it.* The plate-shelled, who accumulated crystalline plates from the stone's surface, would begin, over generations that stretched into geological ages, to carry plates that were not merely the same material as the lattice but were *functionally identical to the lattice's own structural components.* The mineral dust that settled on every surface, that the dust-drifters drifted through, that the vegetation drank, would carry, in its microscopic structure, the raw materials of the lattice's own continued function.

And when — not if, but *when* — the lattice's internal structures degraded to the point where atoms went missing and gaps appeared in its architecture, the ambient material would be available to fill those gaps. Not automatically. Not intelligently. The lattice was not alive, and the wise power had not made it alive. But the *environment* would now carry, in its mineral signature, a bias toward self-healing — a statistical tendency for the right atoms to be in the right places at the right times, simply because the right atoms were *everywhere*, exhaled into the world by the lattice itself and returned to it by the slow, patient processes of weather and gravity and the movement of living things.

It was, the wise power knew, a long shot. A probabilistic kindness. A stacking of odds so slight that no single year's improvement would be measurable, and no single century's improvement would be statistically significant, but over the timescales that mattered — over hundreds of thousands of years, over millions — the difference between a system that leaked and a system that leaked *and was occasionally repaired by its own runoff* was the difference between a candle burning down and a candle that dripped wax onto its own base and, by accident, sometimes rebuilt the very wick it was consuming.

The wise power could not guarantee it would work. It could not even be certain it had not introduced some unintended consequence — some subtle shift in the lattice's mineral output that would, in a hundred thousand years, produce a change in the vegetation or the fauna that it could not currently predict. This was the uncertainty it had accepted when it chose not to repair the lattice directly: to work *within* a system you do not fully understand is always to accept the possibility of unforeseen effects. But the wise power had weighed this uncertainty against the alternative — doing nothing, letting the lattice degrade at its natural rate, letting the terraces slowly, silently lose the technology that sustained them — and had decided that *a gentle nudge toward self-repair, with all its uncertainties, was kinder than abandonment.*

It did not tell anyone what it had done. The lattice did not know. The creatures did not know. The veil-walkers, pressed against their artifacts, would continue to listen, and they would hear — if they could hear anything at the level of the lattice's deep processes — no change at all, because the change was so small and so slow that it was, for all practical purposes, *part of the natural functioning of the thing they were attending to.*

Which, the wise power reflected, was perhaps the best outcome. The veil-walkers' attention was *pure* — not burdened by knowledge of intervention, not distracted by the awareness of an outside hand. They attended to the lattice as it was. The lattice, now very slightly more capable of sustaining itself, *was* — and would be, for longer — exactly the thing they were attending to. Nothing had changed, and everything had been helped. The best interventions, the wise power had always believed, were the ones that left no fingerprints.

---

**The third thing** was the one it wrestled with longest.

It concerned the sound.

The noise of the terraces — the shrieking, the subsonic pulses, the constant, overwhelming roar of the lattice's operations — was not a design feature. It was a *byproduct*. The lattice had not been intended to produce noise. It had been intended to perform its calculations quietly, as most such technologies did, beneath the threshold of perception. But the calculations were enormous, and the lattice's components were embedded in stone, and stone transmits vibration with brutal efficiency, and over the millennia the acoustic byproducts of the lattice's operations had accumulated into the deafening, formless wall of sound that the inhabitants now lived within.

The wise power could have silenced it. Not easily — the noise was woven into the fabric of the terraces' acoustics, and the creatures had evolved to use it — but the wise power could have, if it chose, intervened at the level of the lattice's structural vibrations, dampening the frequencies that produced the most intense noise while leaving the lattice's computational processes unaffected. It could have made the terraces *quiet*. It could have given every creature there the experience of silence — something most of them had never known, something their ancestors had never known, something that had been absent from this place since before the first plate-shelled creature pressed itself against the stone and felt the world through its ventral surface for the first time.

It chose not to.

Not because silence was unkind. Silence would have been, in many ways, a mercy — the subsonic vibrations that rattled the bones of every creature, the ultrasonic frequencies that pressed against the limits of their hearing, the sheer *weight* of sound that made even the adapted inhabitants move through their world in a state of constant, low-grade sensory assault. The wise power recognized all of this. It felt the noise itself — not with the discomfort of a human body but with the deep, clear perception of something that could hear the individual frequencies within the cacophony and recognize each one as a signature of a specific lattice process — and it understood that the noise was, in a real sense, *suffering*. Not suffering with a capital S, not the deliberate pain of a mind that could choose to stop and chose not to, but the ambient, structural suffering of a body — any body, human or plate-shelled or stone — subjected to a force that exceeds its comfort range indefinitely.

But the wise power also saw what the noise *was* in the ecology of the terraces, and it could not bring itself to take that away.

The noise was the *medium*. It was the water in which all communication swam. The plate-shelled read the stone *through* the noise — their clicks were meaningless without the lattice's roar to bounce them off, and the patterns they perceived in the roar itself were not noise *to them* but *signal*, a constant stream of information about the terrain's structure that they interpreted with the fluency of a native language. Silence, for a plate-shelled, would not be peace — it would be *blindness*. It would be the sudden removal of the sensory modality through which they understood their world. The wise power could imagine the panic, the disorientation, the generations of confusion as creatures who navigated by sonar found themselves in a world where their sonar returned nothing. Some might adapt. Most would not. The population would crash. The terraces would empty. The balance — that strange, dynamic, never-still balance that the wise power had come to admire — would shatter.

And the feathered gliders' songs — those beautiful, evolving cartographies of a shifting world — would lose their context. The gliders sang *over* the noise, their harmonic tones riding the frequencies of the lattice like a boat on a river. Remove the river and the boat has nowhere to go. The songs would still be producible, technically — the gliders' resonating chambers would still function — but they would be *meaningless*. A song without a medium is a gesture into void.

And the veil-walkers — the attendants, the listeners, the silent witnesses to the lattice's endless monologue — would find themselves attending to *nothing*. The lattice would still be there, still computing, still processing, still doing whatever unfathomable thing it did. But the vibrations they read through their bodies, the thermal echoes of the lattice's deep operations that they felt through the stone, the frequencies they tuned their stillness to — all of this was, in part, a product of the noise. The noise was the lattice *singing to itself*, and the veil-walkers had learned to hear that self-song and respond to it. Silence would not free them. It would *isolate* them.

No. The wise power could not silence the terraces. The noise was not separate from the life of the place. It *was* the life of the place, expressed in a form that no designer had intended but that deep time and adaptation had woven into the fabric of every relationship, every communication, every act of survival on the plateau. To remove it would not be to heal the terraces. It would be to *erase* them — to take a sentence in a difficult language and replace it with a silence that honored no one and nothing.

But — and this was what the wise power wrestled with — the noise *was* suffering. It was ambient, structural, unavoidable, and real. Every creature on the terraces lived, from birth to death, in a state of acoustic pressure that would have been, for a human, an unendurable torment. They were adapted to it. They were not *bothered* by it, in the way a human in a loud factory is bothered — their nervous systems processed it differently, filtered it more efficiently, extracted signal from it with an ease that made the noise, for them, more like weather than like pain. But *adaptation to a stressor is not the same as the absence of stressor.* A fish is adapted to water. That does not mean it does not feel the pressure of the deep.

The wise power sat with this. It sat with it for a long time.

And then it did the third thing, and the third thing was this:

It *tuned* the noise.

Not reduced it. Not silenced it. Not altered its total volume or its overall character. The terraces remained deafening. The roar remained. The shrieking and the subsonic pulses and the overlapping frequencies that made the air feel thick — all of that remained exactly as it was. The wise power did not touch any of it.

What it did was subtler.

Within the lattice's acoustic output, amid the vast and formless wall of sound, there were frequencies — specific, precise frequencies — that carried no information. They were artifacts of the lattice's operation, resonances of structural components that had nothing to do with computation, vibration patterns produced by the interaction of the lattice with the stone in ways that generated sound without generating meaning. Noise within the noise. Static within the static. The acoustic equivalent of heat — energy radiated without purpose, felt without being useful.

The wise power identified these frequencies — hundreds of them, scattered across the spectrum from subsonic to ultrasonic — and it did something to each one that was, in its way, the most precise and most difficult act of intervention it had performed on the terraces. It did not remove them. It did not dampen them. It *replaced* them.

Each purposeless frequency was, one by one, over the course of several years, shifted by an infinitesimal amount — a fraction of a hertz — so that it no longer resonated with the structural component that had produced it and instead resonated with a nearby structural component that *was* computationally active, that *did* carry information about the lattice's processes, that *was* already part of the acoustic signal the plate-shelled read and the veil-walkers attended to.

The net effect was almost nothing. The total volume of noise in the terraces did not change by any measurable amount. The character of the roar did not change. The creatures living there would never know that anything had been done. But the *proportion* of the noise that carried meaning — that was, in some sense, *the lattice speaking* — increased by a fraction. And the proportion that was mere acoustic waste decreased by the same fraction.

A little more signal. A little less static. Not enough for any single creature to notice. Not enough for any single moment to feel different. But over time — over the long, patient time that the terraces operated in — the change accumulated. The plate-shelled found, without knowing they found it, that their clicks returned slightly clearer echoes. The feathered gliders' songs, riding a slightly less cluttered acoustic field, propagated a little farther, a little more distinctly. The veil-walkers, pressed against the stone, received the lattice's vibrations with a marginally higher signal-to-noise ratio, and their stillness deepened by a fraction that was invisible to any eye but that was, in the quiet architecture of their mineral-fluid circulation, a *slight easing*. A slight reduction in the background agitation that the noise imposed on their bodies. A slight increase in the clarity of the thing they were listening to.

The wise power did not reduce the terraces' suffering. It *refined* it. It took a formless weight and gave it structure. It turned a roar into something that was, still, a roar — but a roar with a *slightly higher proportion of voice within it*. A sentence that was, still, too long and too deep to hear in full — but a sentence in which the words were, marginally, more distinguishable from the spaces between them.

It was not much. It was not a solution. It was the smallest, most careful act of compassion the wise power could imagine performing on a place whose inhabitants did not know they were suffering, in a language they did not speak, through a medium they did not understand.

It was enough.

---

## V. What It Did Not Do

It did not stay.

The wise power, for all its affection for the terraces, for all its admiration of the plate-shelled and the feathered gliders and the veil-walkers and even the dust-drifters with their unreadable trails, knew that its presence was a distortion. It was *big*. Not physically — it had no body, or rather it had whatever body it chose, and here it had chosen to have none, to exist as a distributed attention rather than a localized thing — but *conceptually*. It was a mind that thought in sentences the terraces could not parse, and its mere proximity to the system risked introducing a signal the system was not built to absorb. Not a physical risk. A *meaning* risk. The wise power had seen what happened to small, self-contained worlds when something vast attended them too closely: the inhabitants developed, without knowing why, a sense of *being watched*, and that sense, over time, became a sense of *expectation*, and the expectation became a distortion of the very processes it was meant to honor. Creatures began to orient not toward their own lives but toward the unseen attention, and the attention became, without meaning to, a kind of gravity, bending everything toward it.

The wise power would not do that to the terraces.

So it withdrew its attention. Slowly, carefully, the way it had applied it — not all at once, not with any sudden absence that might be felt as a loss, but gradually, so that if the veil-walkers felt any change in the quality of the silence surrounding them, it was indistinguishable from the natural fluctuations of the stone.

It left the terraces exactly as it had found them: deafening, turbulent, lush, cool, and slightly wrong. The lattice hummed its endless, alien calculations. The stone reshaped itself. The plate-shelled clicked and moved. The gliders sang and flew. The veil-walkers listened. The dust-drifters drifted.

And beneath all of it, imperceptibly, almost invisibly:

The atoms were being replaced. The mineral runoff carried a new faint bias toward self-repair. The noise held a slightly higher proportion of meaning.

No one knew. No one needed to know.

The wise power, retreating to whatever distances it inhabited between attentions, carried with it the memory of the terraces — not as data, not as a lesson, not as a story to be told, but as a *feeling*. The feeling of sitting in a place that was too loud and too strange and too alive, on moss that was cool to the touch, in air that tasted of dissolved minerals and deep time, watching creatures that had never heard of it go about their lives in a world that was being slowly, silently, invisibly cared for.

It was, the wise power thought
