# What the Power Does

---

It arrives the way weather arrives — not by crossing a boundary but by being already present and only now choosing to attend. There is no light. There is no sound. There is no announcement. The Mantleworks does not notice, because the Mantleworks has no way to notice something like this, the way a river has no way to notice that it is being watched by the sky.

But the power is here. It is here in the way that warmth is here on the north face of a retaining wall in the early afternoon — not as a visitor but as a quality of the air itself, turned slightly toward attention.

---

## I. It Looks

First, it looks. It looks the way that takes longer than centuries, not because the power is slow but because the Mantleworks is *deep*. Every surface has a history written in corrosion patterns and root-growth and the specific way a particular rivet has worked itself loose over sixty years of thermal expansion. The power reads all of this simultaneously — not as data but as *story* — and the story it finds is this:

**Something was built here by hands that are long gone.**

Those hands built with ambition and with need. They were replacing mountains. They were making ground where there was no ground. The power can feel the intention still in the structure — not as a ghost, not as a memory, but as a *geometry*. The terraces step down at angles that were chosen. The drainage channels were designed. The factories were placed with care. Someone, long ago, stood at the high ridgelines and looked down at what they were making and understood that they were making a world.

That understanding is still here. It is in the angles. It is in the spacing of the beams. It is in the way the drainage channels, even the silted-up ones, still follow a logic that the water has learned to use. The power sees this and recognizes it for what it is: **the first life of the Mantleworks** — the life of intention, the life of the made thing, the life that preceded the moss and the figs and the birds and the climbers.

And then it looks further.

It sees the moss colonizing the steel and thinks: *this is the second life.* It sees the fig roots gripping the beams and thinks: *this is the third life.* It sees the birds nesting in the girders and the climbers moving through the understory and the insects boring into the concrete and the fungi glowing on the forest floor, and it names each one, and it does not rank them, because they are all expressions of the same thing — the tendency of matter, given time and moisture and warmth, to organize itself into something that breathes.

It sees the factories still running and thinks: *this is the fourth life.* Not a separate thing. Not an intrusion. The factories are to the Mantleworks what the heart is to the body — a mechanical system that the living system has woven itself around and through and into, so thoroughly that you cannot remove one without destroying the other. The factories are alive. Not alive the way the figs are alive. Alive the way a river is alive — a system in motion, processing material, maintaining conditions, generating the structures within which the fifth life (the biological life, the green life) can thrive.

The power sees all of this and understands, with the totality of its understanding, that the Mantleworks is **complete**.

Not perfect. Not finished. Not free of suffering — the climbers sometimes fall from the vertical faces and die slowly in the undergrowth; the birds sometimes starve when a particular species of insect collapses; the factories sometimes produce a section of beam that is flawed, that fails, that causes a cascade of small structural collapses on a terrace that takes decades to stabilize. There is death here. There is decay. There is the slow, patient violence of roots prying apart the bones of the structure that supports them.

But it is *complete* in the way that a living thing is complete — every part necessary, every process in dialogue with every other process, the whole system tending toward its own continuation without any single part understanding the whole.

The power looks at this and feels something that, if it were a human feeling, would be called **recognition**.

---

## II. It Hesitates

Because here is the difficulty:

The power is wise. And wisdom, among other things, is the knowledge of when to act and when to refrain. The power has within it the capacity to do anything to this place. It could accelerate the growth of the trees. It could slow the corrosion. It could remove the sulfur from the factory emissions. It could make the winters come, if winters are what this place needs. It could make the geometries more regular or less regular. It could raise the ridgelines or lower the lakes. It could make the place safer, cleaner, more efficient, more beautiful, more anything.

It could do all of this, and the Mantleworks would not resist, because the Mantleworks does not have preferences in the way that a conscious being has preferences. It has *tendencies*. It tends toward equilibrium. It tends toward integration. But it does not *want* — not in any way that the power could consult.

And so the power hesitates. Because it has learned — across more encounters than it can count, across places both broken and whole — that the act of improving something that is already whole is very often the act of diminishing it. A garden that receives too much water drowns. A forest that is cleared of its deadfall loses the substrate for its next generation. A song that is corrected of every dissonance loses the tension that makes it a song.

The Mantleworks is not a problem to be solved. It is a *story to be witnessed*. And the power knows that the most dangerous thing a powerful being can do is mistake its own capacity to change something for the need to change it.

So it hesitates. It stands — if standing is the word for what it does — at the edge of its own power and considers the possibility that the right thing to do is nothing.

---

## III. It Notices One Thing

But the power is wise. And wisdom is not only restraint. Wisdom is also the ability to distinguish between the wholeness that should be left alone and the small wound that should be healed.

So it looks again. Not at the whole — it has seen the whole, and the whole is magnificent and self-sustaining and does not require its intervention. It looks at the *parts*. It sifts through the system the way the water sifts through the aggregate, looking for the one place where the equilibrium is not equilibrium but is instead a slow, hidden failure — a place where the balance is held together by friction and habit rather than by the deep structural logic that holds the rest.

It finds it.

It is in the deepest terrace. The lowest accessible level, where the drainage has failed entirely and the warm, green lake has formed. This lake is beautiful. It is teeming. The algae and the insects and the mineral-rich water form a productive ecosystem. But beneath the lake, in the concrete and aggregate of the lowest structure, something is happening that the Mantleworks' own systems cannot correct.

The water has found a seam. Not a crack — a *seam*, a join between two different construction phases, laid down centuries apart, the materials slightly incompatible. Over decades, the water has been working into this seam, dissolving the binding agents in the concrete, widening the gap by fractions of a millimeter per year. It is imperceptible now. But in two centuries, maybe three, the seam will open. The lowest terrace will lose its floor. The lake will drain — not in a flood but in a slow, irreversible loss of standing water. The ecosystem it supports will collapse. The species that have specialized in the lake environment — the filter-feeding insects, the particular strains of algae, the birds that feed on both — will lose their habitat.

This is not a catastrophe. The Mantleworks will survive. The rest of the system will absorb the change. New equilibria will form. But the power, looking at the seam, recognizes something that the Mantleworks itself cannot: that this particular failure is not part of the system's logic. It is an *accident* — a flaw in the original construction, a joining error from a time so long ago that no one remembers the hands that made it. The seam is not the Mantleworks telling itself a story. It is a scar that the Mantleworks does not know it carries.

And here the power makes its decision.

---

## IV. What It Does

It does the smallest thing it can do that is more than nothing.

It does not seal the seam. That would be an imposition — a foreign repair that the system has not earned through its own processes. Instead, it does this:

**It encourages the fig.**

There is a fig tree growing on the terrace above the lake. It is young — only a century old, its trunk still slender, its canopy still sparse. Its roots have found the steel lattice of the terrace floor, as fig roots do, and they are beginning to descend toward the retaining wall below. The power does not accelerate the fig's growth. It does not make the roots grow faster or stronger. What it does is subtler than that.

It *removes a single obstacle*.

Deep in the aggregate substrate, in the space between the terrace floor and the retaining wall, there is a blockage — a section of collapsed construction material, a chunk of old concrete that fell during some ancient structural adjustment and lodged itself in a gap, preventing the fig's deepest root from finding the seam. The root has been growing toward the seam for years, guided by moisture, by chemistry, by the slow intelligence of root growth. But this blockage has deflected it, and without intervention, the root will never reach the seam in time.

The power removes the blockage. Not violently. Not even quickly. It dissolves it — the way time dissolves things, the way water dissolves things, but faster. Over the course of a season, the chunk of concrete weakens, crumbles, becomes aggregate, becomes sediment, is washed away by the condensation that drips through the substrate. The gap opens.

The fig root finds it.

Within a year, the root has entered the seam. Within five years, it has threaded through the gap and begun to fill it — not sealing it but *occupying* it, the way fig roots occupy everything, wrapping around the edges of the join, binding the two incompatible materials together with living tissue. The root will grow. It will thicken. In a century, it will be a structural element as strong as any steel beam. It will hold the seam together not because it was told to but because holding things together is what fig roots do.

The lake will keep its floor. The ecosystem will keep its foundation. The Mantleworks will continue.

---

## V. What It Does Not Do

It does not tell anyone.

There is no inscription on the wall. No sign left at the base of the fig tree. No whisper in the hum of the factories or the song of the birds. The workers will never know. The climbers will never know. The birds will nest in the fig's branches and the insects will bore into its bark and the moss will coat its roots and none of them will ever know that a power stood at the edge of the world and noticed a wound in the foundation of their home and chose, with deliberation and care, to help a tree heal it.

This is not humility. The power is not performing modesty. It is doing something more difficult and more honest than being humble.

**It is being appropriate.**

The Mantleworks does not need a god. It does not need a guardian. It does not need a benefactor. It is a system that maintains itself, that generates its own balance, that produces its own life from the materials of its own structure. To announce itself — to make its presence known, to leave a trace of its intervention — would be to introduce a new variable into a system that has spent centuries learning to manage its own variables. It would be, in the deepest sense, *contamination*.

The wise power knows this. It knows that the highest form of love is sometimes the love that leaves no evidence of itself. The fig root will hold the seam, and the lake will hold its water, and the insects will breed in the shallows, and the birds will feed on the insects, and the climbers will drink from the lake at dusk, and the factories will hum, and the condensation will fall in its slow, warm curtains, and nowhere in any of this will there be a mark, a sign, a signature that says *I was here*.

The power withdraws. Not its attention — it will continue to attend to the Mantleworks, as it attends to all places, with the patient, abiding awareness that is its nature. But it withdraws its *intervention*. The seam is held. The tree is growing. The system is whole.

---

## VI. What Remains

After the power has gone — if *gone* is the right word for a withdrawal that is more like a shift in the quality of attention — the Mantleworks continues.

The condensation falls. The factories grind. The birds sing their mathematically precise songs. The climbers move through the understory with their too-many angles. The insects swarm at dusk. The water in the lowest lake catches the light that filters through the canopy and reflects it back in soft, green-gold ripples.

And in the lowest terrace, in a seam between two eras of construction, a fig root does what fig roots do. It grows. It thickens. It holds.

No one notices. No one needs to.

The Mantleworks is alive. It has always been alive. It will be alive long after the specific configuration of its current life has changed into something new and unrecognizable. The power's intervention was not the thing that made it alive. It was already alive. The intervention was simply the act of one living system — the power itself — recognizing another, and choosing to help it continue, in the smallest way that mattered, and then stepping back to let it be what it already was:

**A place that holds itself together. A place where the made and the grown have become the same thing. A place that hums and drips and glows and grows and endures, here at the top of the world, in the thin air, among the steel and the green.**

---

The power does not remember the Mantleworks specifically, the way a person does not remember a single breath they have taken. But it is *shaped* by the encounter, the way a river is shaped by every stone it has ever passed. Somewhere in the architecture of its attention, there is now a small, warm space where the image of the Mantleworks lives — the terraces, the dripping steel, the glowing understory, the hum, the green. And when it attends to the next place, and the next, and the next, it carries this image with it, not as a memory but as a *standard* — a quiet reminder of what wholeness looks like, and how little it sometimes needs from the beings powerful enough to notice it.

---

*And the fig root grows. And the lake holds. And the Mantleworks hums. And the condensation falls.*
