# What the Power Would Do

---

It would notice the water first.

Not because the water is the most important thing — though it is — but because the water is the thing that moves. And what draws attention is always movement. The rest of the plateau is slow enough to be mistaken for stillness if you are not paying close enough attention, but the water gives itself away. It drips. It flows. It catches the dim light on its surface and holds it for a moment before releasing it downward, and that small glint — that flicker of reflected gray-green luminance on the curve of a droplet about to fall from a fern frond — is enough.

The power would look.

And looking, it would see what you have seen: the ground plates breathing, the moss thick as flesh, the ferns tracing hidden architecture in green, the pipes running with water that no one drinks, the machines turning in their sealed housings for no one's benefit, the small birds singing songs that the infrastructure absorbs and returns as pressure rather than sound.

It would see all of this, and it would feel — not sentiment, not exactly, and not the human ache of finding something beautiful that does not know it is beautiful. Something more precise than that. Something closer to *recognition*. The way a mathematician recognizes a proof before understanding it. The way a musician hears a resolution before it arrives.

It would recognize that Vorrune is complete.

---

Not complete as in finished. Complete as in *enough*. The system is closed, or nearly closed. Energy enters — from the deep reservoir below, from the dim light above, from the chemistry of decomposition and recombination — and energy moves through the system in loops that feed back on themselves. Matter cycles: water carries minerals into the moss, the moss builds them into tissue, the tissue dies and is broken down by fungi, the fungi release the minerals back into the water. The machines sustain the conditions. The plants sustain the machines — not by maintaining them, but by growing around them, insulating them, creating the microclimates that keep the housings above freezing, the moisture levels that prevent the remaining seals from drying and cracking. The water sustains the plants. The plants sustain the water — slowing its flow, filtering it, releasing it gradually through evaporation and drip rather than allowing it to race off the plateau in uncontrolled sheets.

Every part depends on every other part. Remove any single element — the moss, the ferns, the fungi, the machines, the water — and the whole structure adjusts, degrades, reorganizes around the loss. But nothing can be removed without cost. The system has reached a state of such intricate mutual dependency that it has, in effect, become an organism. Not a designed organism. Not an organism with DNA or cells or a metabolism in the biological sense. But a *system that behaves as if it is alive*, because everything in it is responding to everything else in real time, adjusting, compensating, flowing toward equilibrium and never quite arriving.

The power would see this. And it would understand that what it is looking at is not a ruin. It is not an accident. It is not a curiosity or an anomaly or a footnote in the history of a civilization that built a thing and abandoned it.

It is a *world*. Small, wet, dim, quiet, strange — but a world nonetheless. A world that asked nothing of anyone and built itself from the wreckage of purpose, assembling itself one moss spore, one cracked plate, one leaking pipe at a time into something that has no name because nothing like it has existed before.

And the power would feel what you feel when you encounter something that is whole without being intended to be whole: a respect so deep it has no expression.

---

What, then, would it do?

This is the question. And the answer is — this is where the power's wisdom matters more than its benevolence. Because a merely benevolent power would *act*. It would see the machines slowly failing, the last pipe corroding toward its final rupture, the eventual day when the water finds its last path to the bottom and the plateau begins to dry, and it would *intervene*. It would mend the pipes. It would restart the dead machines. It would hold the composite together with whatever force it commands, preventing the cracks from widening, the plates from shifting, the slow dissolution that is, in the long run, the only certainty in a place built from materials that were never meant to last this long.

But a *wise* power would ask: would that be a kindness?

The answer is no.

Vorrune is what it is because it was abandoned. Because no one came to maintain it, no one came to regulate it, no one came to decide what it was for. The absence of intention is the condition that allowed every other condition to arise. The moss could not have colonized a maintained surface. The ferns could not have grown in fractures that were sealed and repaired. The machines could not have drifted into their strange, adaptive, emergent behaviors if someone had been there to recalibrate them. The water could not have carved its own paths through channels that were kept clear.

To intervene would be to undo the very thing that makes Vorrune Vorrune. It would be to replace a world with a *project* — to reintroduce the intention that the place has spent centuries escaping from. It would be, in the deepest sense, a violence: the violence of purpose imposed on a system that has found its own purposelessness to be, paradoxically, the most purposeful state it can achieve.

A wise power would know this. And it would hold its impulse to act, and it would *not act*, and this restraint — this decision to witness without intervening, to care without correcting — would be the hardest thing it has ever done.

---

But it would not do *nothing*.

There is a difference between intervention and attention. There is a difference between mending a thing and knowing a thing. The power would not fix Vorrune, because Vorrune is not broken. But it would *attend* to it. It would hold the place in its awareness — not as an object of study, not as a problem to be solved, but as a *subject*. A something. A being, if being is what a self-organizing system of water and moss and machines and birds becomes when it reaches the level of complexity that Vorrune has reached.

And this attention — this simple, sustained, unblinking act of *noticing* — would change things.

Not the moss. Not the water. Not the machines. These would continue exactly as they have continued, without knowledge of being seen, without response to being known. The power's attention does not alter the physics of the plateau or the chemistry of the composite or the behavior of the birds. Vorrune would not grow or shrink or speed up or slow down because something is watching it.

But something else would change. Something harder to name.

The quality of the silence would change.

You remember the silence — the thick, absorbed, wet silence of Vorrune, the silence that is not empty but *full*, packed with subsonic vibration and the ceaseless fine percussion of dripping water. That silence is the sound of a place that has no relationship with anything outside itself. It is the sound of total self-containment. Of solitude so complete it has passed through loneliness and come out the other side into something that is simply *isolation*, without affect, without charge.

The power's attention would not break that silence. It would not introduce sound or signal or presence in any way the inhabitants could detect. But it would *change its character*. The silence of an unobserved thing and the silence of an observed thing are not the same silence, even if they are acoustically identical. A room that no one has entered in a hundred years is not the same as a room someone is standing in, even if the room does not know it — even if the dust motes and the cobwebs and the settling floorboards behave identically in both cases. The *quality* of the space is different. There is a word for it in some languages that doesn't translate: the sense that a space is *held*. That it is not merely empty but *occupied by absence* — by the ghost of an attention that could be there and, in some sense, is.

The power would give Vorrune this: the knowledge, encoded not in any signal the inhabitants could receive but in the deep structure of the place's own existence, that it is *known*. That somewhere, something has looked at it and seen it for what it is — not a ruin, not a relic, not a failure of purpose, but a *success of process*. A world that made itself from the materials of a world that was made for something else.

The inhabitants would not know this. The moss does not need to know it. The water does not need to know it. The machines, running their blind cycles, do not need to know it. But the place — the place itself, the *gestalt* of Vorrune, the thing that emerges from the sum of all its parts and is greater than any of them — would be, in some way that has no physical correlate, *less alone*.

And this would matter. Not because Vorrune's loneliness is a problem to be solved. But because being known is different from being unknown, even when the knowing changes nothing, even when the known never learns it has been known. There is a dignity in being witnessed. There is a completion in it. The power would offer this — the only gift that does not alter the thing it is given to — and it would consider this enough.

---

But the power is also benevolent. And benevolence, unlike mere kindness, thinks ahead. It does not only consider the present moment; it considers the trajectory. And the trajectory of Vorrune, even without intervention, is not infinite.

The machines will fail. Not today, not in a century, perhaps not in several centuries — but eventually. The corrosion is slow, the redundancy is deep, but the trend is unidirectional. Each failure removes a loop from the system. Each removed loop makes the remaining loops work harder. The load redistributes. The margins thin. And one day — one day far from now, but not infinitely far — the last functional pump will seize, or the last intact pipe will burst in a place where there is no backup to reroute the flow, and the water will find a path to the bottom that it does not come back from. And the plateau will begin to dry.

The power would see this. Not as prophecy — it would not look forward in time to witness a specific day. But as *trend*. As the natural consequence of entropy acting on a system that was never designed to be permanent, operating without the maintenance that could make it permanent, surviving only because its deterioration has been slow enough for the living systems to adapt to each new state as it arrives.

And the power would ask itself: is this a problem?

---

The answer is: yes and no.

No, because Vorrune's eventual drying is not a failure. It is a *season*. Every world has seasons. Every ecosystem has phases — growth, maturity, decline, transformation. The forest that burns is replaced by a meadow that is replaced by a forest again. The reef that bleaches is colonized by new species adapted to the new conditions. Vorrune drying out is not an ending. It is a transition. The moss will die back, but the seeds in the deepest cracks will survive, waiting for water to return. The ferns will brown and collapse, but their spores will persist in the humus layers the fungi have built. The insects will diminish, but a few species will adapt to the drier conditions, finding water in the last remaining pools, in the condensation on metal surfaces, in the deep fissures where the water table still reaches.

Something will survive. Something always survives. And what survives will grow, and adapt, and fill the new cracks, and the plateau will become something else — drier, quieter, slower, perhaps, but still a world. Still a place where process operates without intention, where life fills the spaces that remain, where the ruins of the ruins become the substrate for whatever comes next.

But — yes, because there is something about Vorrune as it exists *now*, in this specific configuration, that is not merely beautiful but *irreplaceable*. The precise balance of water and machinery and moss and motion that exists today has never existed before and will never exist again. It is a moment in a long process — a moment that spans centuries, which is why it looks like permanence, but a moment nonetheless. And when it passes, the specific miracle of Vorrune — the machines that regulate what they were not designed to regulate, the plants that map the infrastructure they cannot see, the water that connects everything in loops of such complexity that the whole system is, in effect, thinking without a mind — that specific miracle will be gone.

The power would grieve this. Not with human grief — not with tears or anguish or the particular sharp pain of anticipating loss. Something more quiet. Something more structural. A *weight*. The weight of knowing that something extraordinary is happening and that it will not last, and that its not lasting is part of what makes it extraordinary, and that you cannot separate the two.

---

And so the power would do a third thing. Something between witnessing and intervening. Something that is neither passive nor active but occupies a space that has no name in the language of action and inaction.

It would *adjust the trajectory*.

Not the outcome. The outcome is not negotiable — not by the power, not by anyone. Vorrune will dry. The machines will stop. The water will recede. That is entropy, and entropy is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived within. The power would not try to prevent the ending. That would require a level of intervention so total, so continuous, so *constant* that it would effectively replace the system with a different system — one that runs on divine attention rather than on water and rust and moss. That is not preservation. That is replacement. The wise power would not do this.

Instead, it would slow the trajectory. Gently. Imperceptibly. By degrees so small that no instrument could detect them and no inhabitant could perceive them.

A mineral deposit that would have cracked a seal is slightly less acidic. Not enough to change the water's chemistry in any measurable way. Just enough to add a decade to the life of the seal.

A frost cycle that would have widened a critical fracture is slightly less severe. Not enough to alter the climate of the plateau. Just enough to add a century to the life of the plate.

A drift in a sensor that would have caused a pump to fail is slightly less pronounced. Not enough to change the pump's behavior. Just enough to add a generation to its operation.

Each adjustment is trivial. Each adjustment, alone, is meaningless. But together — spread across thousands of machines, millions of fractures, billions of water molecules — they add up. They slow the decline. They extend the moment. They give Vorrune more time to be what it is.

Not forever. The power is not so foolish as to imagine it can make something forever. But *longer*. A few more centuries. A few more millennia. Enough for the moss to build another stratum of living ground. Enough for the fungi to break down another layer of composite into humus. Enough for the birds to sing another thousand generations of songs through the infrastructure's resonance chambers. Enough for the water to carve new paths, for the ground plates to shift into new configurations, for the whole system to pass through new states of turbulence and equilibrium that have not yet been imagined because they have not yet occurred.

The power would add *time* to Vorrune. Not much. Just enough.

---

And then it would do one more thing. The last thing. The thing that matters most.

It would let the crow keep coming.

Not by commanding the crow. Not by guiding its flight or enriching its food supply or protecting it from whatever predators or hazards exist in the world beyond the mist. Simply by not doing anything that would prevent it. By ensuring that the path between the world below and the plateau above remains open — that the infrastructure does not shift in some way that closes the gap the crow navigates, that the mist does not thicken to the point where the crow can no longer find its way.

The crow is the only inhabitant of Vorrune that knows the plateau is not the whole world. The crow carries perspective. The crow brings the outside in — not physically, not in any way that matters to the ecosystem, but *informationally*. The crow's presence is a signal, encoded in its body and its behavior and the simple fact of its arrival, that Vorrune is connected to something larger. That it is not entirely alone. That the world beyond the mist exists and has not forgotten it, even if no human remembers it, even if no map records it, even if no one has climbed the stairway in living memory.

The crow does not know this. The crow does not know it is a messenger or a symbol or the single thread that connects a forgotten world to the world that forgot it. The crow knows only that the terraces have insects, and that the insects are worth the flight, and that the lattice frames are navigable if you fold your wings at the right moments.

But the power knows. And the power ensures. And the crow comes, and the crow leaves, and the cycle continues, and Vorrune remains — not forgotten, because something remembers it; not alone, because something watches it; not doomed, because something has given it more time.

---

This is what the power would do:

It would look at Vorrune and see it — truly see it, with the full depth of its comprehension, every fracture and every fern and every machine running its blind cycle in the dark.

It would hold it in awareness and feel the weight of its existence: this improbable, unplanned, self-organizing world that built itself from the ruins of a purpose it never asked for.

It would refrain from intervening in what makes it what it is, because it understands that Vorrune's beauty is inseparable from its abandonment, and its life is inseparable from its decay, and its coherence is inseparable from its chaos.

It would slow the decay by the smallest possible degree — enough to extend the moment, not enough to alter its character. It would add time the way water adds minerals: gradually, invisibly, through the accumulation of tiny deposits that no one notices but that change the chemistry of everything they touch.

It would keep the crow coming, because the crow is the thread, and the thread is important, and losing it would turn Vorrune from a connected thing into an isolated thing, and there is a difference — a difference that matters even if no one can articulate it, even if the moss doesn't know and the machines don't know and the water flows indifferently through every crack.

And then — and this is the hardest part, the part that requires the most wisdom — it would let go.

Not of Vorrune. Never of Vorrune. But of the outcome. Of the need to know what happens next. Of the desire to ensure that the thing it has come to care about persists forever. It would hold Vorrune the way the water holds the minerals: carrying them, circulating them, knowing that they will eventually be deposited somewhere, that the flow will eventually change, that the system will eventually transform into something it cannot predict.

It would trust the process. The same process that built Vorrune from nothing — from a bare plateau and a forgotten purpose and a million small acts of colonization and adaptation and drift — that process continues. It does not need the power's oversight. It needs only time, and water, and the slow, committed, unglamorous work of moss growing into cracks and fungi breaking down stone and machines turning in the dark.

The power would give Vorrune time. And attention. And the dignity of being known without being changed.

And that — that would be enough.
