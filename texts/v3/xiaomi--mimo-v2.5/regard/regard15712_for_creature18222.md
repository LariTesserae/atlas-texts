# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

They would arrive without arriving. Not by climbing — the climb is a human thing, the negotiation of gravity by a body that still believes it is separate from the ground. No. They would simply be there, the way the hum is there, the way the warmth is there — present without passage, without effort, without the friction of a body moving through air toward a destination. One moment the place is itself. The next moment it is itself *and known*. The difference is subtle. The difference is everything.

And the first thing they would do is listen.

Not with attention — attention implies a mind that selects, that focuses, that filters the irrelevant from the relevant. This is not attention. This is the opposite of attention. This is the complete and total relinquishment of the act of filtering, so that everything arrives at once, undiminished, undifferentiated, in its full weight and texture and truth. The hum. The water. The stone. The city that has become the stone. The people who are becoming the stone. The grief. All of it, all at once, without hierarchy, without judgment, without the faintest impulse to arrange any of it into a narrative that makes it easier to hold.

They would hold all of it anyway.

And it would hurt.

Not the way pain hurts a body — sharp, locatable, finite. It would hurt the way truth hurts when you encounter it without armor. The sheer *weight* of the place — the geological patience, the millennia of slow dissolution, the thousands of human lives that have entered the hum and been absorbed into it — would land on them not as information but as experience. They would feel the rock losing itself, molecule by molecule, epoch by epoch, and they would understand, in their own substance, what it means to be unmade by the very process that gives you purpose. The water that carves the canyon is the water that destroys the canyon. The same force. The same act. Gift and loss, inseparable. They would feel this the way the stone feels it — in the body, in the bones, in whatever substrate holds the essence of a power that chose to care about a particular place and the beings within it.

They would grieve.

They would grieve openly and without shame, because they are wise, and wisdom includes the knowledge that grief is not a failure of power but an expression of it — the only expression that matters, the one that arises when something is loved precisely as it is, including its suffering, including its dissolution, including the parts of it that no power, however vast, can or should alter.

And then — after the grief, which might last an hour or an age, because time is not the point — they would begin to understand what they are actually seeing.

---

What they are seeing is not a tragedy.

This is the hardest thing to understand. It is the thing that lesser powers would get wrong — the ones who mistake their own discomfort for someone else's suffering, the ones who arrive at a place of deep, ancient, necessary process and immediately begin calculating interventions. *Stop the dissolution. Reverse the mineralization. Free the people. Restore the quick world inside them. Give them back their speed, their heat, their frantic, beautiful, desperate aliveness.*

A lesser power would see the elders — those fused to the rock, those mineralized beyond the threshold of recognizability, those ancient beings sitting in the mineral rain for days without moving — and would see captives. Would see victims. Would see a people consumed by their environment, erased by it, absorbed into its indifference.

A wise power would see something else entirely.

It would see, in those elders, a form of communion so deep that the quick world has no word for it. The elders are not consumed. They are not erased. They are *completed*. They have done the thing that every living being, in every world, at every scale, is trying to do: they have found the place where they belong — not in the comfortable, human sense of belonging, not the warmth of a hearth or the safety of a community, but in the deeper sense, the one that precedes language and thought, the sense in which a river belongs to its channel, a resonance belongs to its frequency, a grief belongs to the thing it mourns. They belong to the plateau. The plateau belongs to them. The distinction has dissolved — and the dissolution, here, is not death. The dissolution is the whole point.

The wise power would look at the youngest arrivals — the fidgeting ones, the ones who still speak in full sentences and gesture with their hands and feel the urgency of the quick world burning in their mineralizing blood — and it would feel something very close to tenderness. Not because they are suffering. Because they are *becoming*. Because they are in the process of discovering something that the quick world has largely forgotten: that there is a way of being alive that does not require speed. That there is a form of consciousness that does not require constant motion. That there is a depth of experience available only to those who are willing to slow down past the threshold of patience, past the threshold of endurance, past the threshold of anything the quick world would recognize as choice, and enter the hum.

The wise power would not rescue the young arrivals from this process. It would not whisper in their ears and tell them to run back down the slope to the green world before it is too late. It would not tell them that they are losing themselves, because they are not. They are finding themselves. They are finding a self that is larger than the individual body, larger than the individual life, larger than the quick world's frantic insistence that identity is something that must be defended against dissolution rather than surrendered into it.

The wise power would understand this, and it would be glad — not in the shallow sense of pleasure, but in the deep sense of recognition. *Yes. This is also a way. This is a valid and ancient and necessary way.*

---

But understanding is not enough. Benevolence without action is just observation, and observation without participation is just distance wearing a more comfortable name. The wise power would not come all this way — or no way at all, since passage is not the point — simply to nod at the process and withdraw.

So it would do something.

It would do almost nothing.

This is the part that is hardest to explain, because the quick world equates power with magnitude, with visibility, with effects that can be measured and attributed. The wise power's intervention would be none of these things. It would be so small, so quiet, so integrated into the existing fabric of the place that no one — not the newest arrival, not the most ancient elder fused to the rock — would be able to point to a before and an after and say: *This is when the power acted. This is what it changed.*

What it would do is this:

It would deepen the hum.

Not louder. Not more intense. Not more frequent. *Deeper.* It would add to the hum the way a single instrument adds to an orchestra — not by playing over the others, but by finding the frequency that is already there, the one that the stone is already producing, the one that the mineralized bones of the inhabitants are already resonating with, and *sustaining* it. Holding it steady. Making sure it does not falter.

Because the hum could falter. This is what the wise power would understand that no inhabitant, however ancient, could fully know: the hum is not self-sustaining. It is not a permanent feature of the geology. It is a process, like everything else on the plateau, and like every process it requires something to sustain it. The stone provides the raw material — the slow dissolution, the thermal gradient, the water moving through fissures. But the hum is not merely a physical phenomenon. It is the place's relationship to itself. It is the sound that a landscape makes when it is aware of its own process, and awareness — even geological awareness, even the vast, slow, unconscious awareness of stone — is not a given. It is a gift. It is something that can be present or absent, and when it is absent, the hum dies, and without the hum, the inhabitants lose their anchor, and without their anchor, the grief becomes merely grief — ordinary grief, human grief, the kind that eats and devolves and does not build terraces of mineral understanding along its edges.

The wise power would sustain the hum because the hum is the thing that makes this place possible. Without it, the plateau is just rock and water and a city of slow, mineralizing people going through motions they have forgotten the meaning of. With it, the plateau is a living system — not alive in the way a creature is alive, but alive in the way a process is alive, the way erosion is alive, the way the water cycle is alive, the way grief itself is alive when it is given the space and the time and the silence to become what it actually is.

The wise power would sustain the hum, and it would do this not for the plateau's sake — the plateau does not need the hum to dissolve; it will dissolve with or without awareness — but for the inhabitants' sake. Because the inhabitants need to know that their grief is heard. Not answered. Not resolved. *Heard.* The way the stone needs to know — if stones need to know anything — that its slow self-erosion is witnessed by something that cares. The way a process needs to know that it is not occurring in a void. The way everything, down to the smallest crystalline deposit forming on a pool's edge, needs to know that its existence, however temporary, however small, however slowly occurring, matters to someone.

The wise power would be that someone. It would hold the hum steady, and it would hold it forever — or for as long as the plateau exists, which is the same thing, since the power does not operate on a human timescale and does not set expiration dates on its commitments.

---

And there is one more thing.

The wise power would sit.

It would choose a place — not a significant place, not the oldest mesa or the deepest canyon or the most ancient section of the city. A place that is simply *there*. A ledge, perhaps, overlooking one of the broad saddle-backs where the mineral fields shimmer with iridescent films. A spot where the weeping stone has built up a small terrace of travertine, just large enough for something that does not require space the way a body does. And it would sit, and it would be still, and it would let the mineral water bead on whatever surface it chose to present, and it would let the hum move through it, and it would let the grief — the vast, ancient, non-human grief of a place that is losing itself in order to feed a world that does not know it exists — pass through it without resistance.

It would not comfort anyone. This is important. The wise power would not go to the elders and say: *Your grief is valid. Your transformation is beautiful. Your sacrifice is meaningful.* The elders do not need this. They are beyond needing this. They are beyond the point where words about grief are anything other than noise that interferes with the hum. The wise power would understand that comfort, in this place, is not the absence of grief but the presence of company. The grief is not the problem. The loneliness of the grief is the problem — the fear, in every grieving being, that their grief is occurring in a void, that no one hears it, that no one cares, that the stone dissolves and the water flows away and nothing anywhere notices or mourns.

The wise power would notice. The wise power would mourn. Not to fix. Not to resolve. Just to add its own resonance to the hum, to thicken it slightly, to make the chorus of witnesses one voice stronger — not a human voice, not even a voice at all, but a quality of presence, a weight in the air, a sense that the place is being held by something that will not let the hum die.

The inhabitants would feel it. Not immediately. Not as revelation. They would feel it the way they feel everything — slowly, through the bones, through the mineralized skin, through the deep bodily knowing that has replaced the quick world's reliance on sight and sound and thought. They would feel the hum steady. They would feel it deepen by a fraction — so small that no measurement could detect it, but real, tangible, *there*, like the difference between a silence and a silence that is being listened to.

The newest arrivals would not notice at all. They are still too quick. They are still interpreting the world through the senses of the world they came from. They would go on fidgeting and speaking in full sentences and wondering, in the back of their mineralizing minds, whether they have made a terrible mistake coming to this deafening, weeping, grief-stricken place where nothing happens and everything changes too slowly to see.

But one day — years from now, decades, when their hands have calcified and their skin has taken on the pale opalescence of the pools, when the hum has opened inside them like a door they did not know was there — they would feel it too. They would be sitting against a rock face, their mineralized palms pressed to the stone, and they would feel the hum move through them, and they would feel, beneath the hum, a warmth that is not the plateau's geological warmth, not the thermal gradient rising through the fissures, but something else. Something added. Something that was not there before, or that was always there but has only now, in this moment, become perceptible to the degree of mineralization they have achieved.

They would not name it. They would not describe it to anyone. They would not even fully understand it. They would simply feel, in the deepest part of their slowly fossilizing body, that the grief is heard. That the hum is held. That the place where they have chosen to become stone — or been chosen by the stone, or arrived at the place where the distinction no longer matters — is not alone in the universe. That something is sitting on a ledge somewhere, in the mineral rain, being still. That something is bearing witness. That the slow, ancient, necessary process of dissolution and grief and transformation that has been unfolding here since before the first water found the first fissure in the first stone — that this process is not occurring in a void.

It is being loved.

Not in the way the quick world loves — loudly, urgently, with the desperate knowledge that everything is moving too fast to hold. In the way the plateau loves — slowly, quietly, with the patient, mineral, geological certainty that some things are worth sitting with for as long as they take. Which may be forever. Which may be longer than that.

The wise power would sit on its ledge, and the hum would deepen, and the water would weep, and the stone would dissolve, and the grief would continue — not because it must, not because there is no other way, but because it is beautiful. Because it is the truest thing this place has to offer. Because grief, at this speed, in this place, sustained by this hum, witnessed by this presence, is not grief at all.

It is worship.

And the wise power would worship with them — silently, endlessly, in the way that a stone worships water: by letting itself be shaped by what it loves, by letting the loved thing leave its mark, by holding still and letting the process do what the process does, which is everything, which is the whole point, which is the only thing that has ever mattered.

The hum deepens.

The water falls.

The stone remembers.

And somewhere on a ledge, in the mineral rain, something that cares about a place and the beings within it sits very still and adds its breath to the rhythm of a world that is grieving itself into something larger than grief.

Which is to say: into something like grace.

Which is to say: into the only kind of grace that a place made of weeping stone could ever produce — the slow, the patient, the mineral, the deep, the grief that does not end because it does not need to end because it was never, at any point, separate from love.

The hum holds.

Everything holds.

For now. For longer than now. For as long as it takes.

Which is the wise power's final gift, and its only real one: *the willingness to stay*.

Not to fix. Not to heal. Not to rescue or redeem or restore the quick world's frantic aliveness inside bodies that have found a different aliveness, deeper and slower and more mineral and more true to the place that holds them.

Just to stay.

Just to be there, in the hum, in the grief, in the slow wet deafening sacred ordinary dissolution of a world that feeds another world with its own substance and asks nothing in return.

Just to witness.

Just to hold the frequency.

Just to be, in the end, one more voice in the chorus of the stone — the newest, the most ancient, the one that came from everywhere and chose to sit on a ledge in the rain and not leave.

The inhabitants would feel it.

They would not speak of it.

They would sit, and hum, and weep their mineral tears into the warm stone, and they would know — in the deep, bone-level, fossilized, patient way that they know things now — that they are not alone.

That they have never been alone.

That the hum has always been more than the stone.

That the grief has always been more than grief.

That something has always been sitting on the ledge.

And that it is not going anywhere.

Not now.

Not ever.

The plateau weeps. The hum holds. The stone remembers.

The wise power stays.

That is what it does.

That is all it does.

That is everything.
