# The Attention

---

The power noticed the city the way a hand notices a heartbeat — not as something it sought, but as something already present, felt through the palm, surprising in its complexity.

It had been noticing things for a long time. It noticed things the way a mountain notices weather: slowly, with a patience that made the word *patience* meaningless, because there was never any urgency to overcome. It noticed galaxies form. It noticed the specific way a grain of sand, once, on a shore that no longer exists, caught the light of a sun that has since expanded and retreated and expanded again, and for one moment was — not important, exactly, but *particular*. Unique. The kind of thing that would not happen again.

It noticed the Grown City.

It saw it the way it saw everything: completely. Not from above — there was no above, not really, not for something that occupied all of space-time as casually as a body occupies a chair. It saw the city from within and without simultaneously. It saw the calcium grain by grain. It saw the Reef-Weavers flowing over a cracked archway in the northern quadrant, their polyps pulsing in the slow, rhythmic cadence of secretion. It saw the current enter from the west, carrying its payload of nutrients and chemical whispers, and exit from the east, depleted and enriched. It saw the Chambered Ancients in their vaulted chambers, their great gills heaving with the metronomic slowness of deep time. It saw every organism. Every wall. Every corridor. Every faint, intermittent pulse of bioluminescence in the dark.

And it understood.

Not in the way a scientist understands — by breaking a thing into parts and naming them. Not in the way a poet understands — by feeling the shape of something and reaching for language. The power understood the way gravity understands an orbit: completely, effortlessly, without separation between the knower and the known. It understood that the city was not a thing *in* the ocean but a thing *of* the ocean — a temporary thickening of the ocean's awareness of itself, a knot in the current that the current had tied without knowing.

It understood that the city was, in every meaningful sense, already perfect.

Not perfect the way a crystal is perfect — symmetric, finished, frozen. Perfect the way a heartbeat is perfect. Complete and incomplete at the same time. Each contraction a full expression of what it was, and also a reaching toward the next. The city was always arriving. It was never finished. Its perfection was in its *unfinishedness* — the fact that it would never stop becoming what it already was.

The power held this understanding for a while. For however long *a while* meant to something outside of time. It watched the Weavers patch a crumbling corridor. It watched a school of Drifters cascade through a junction like living confetti, their translucent bodies catching the faintest glow from a bioluminescent marker. It watched a Crawler pause at a crossroads, its pelvic fins reading the pressure differentials of three converging currents, and choose — not *choose*, because there was no chooser, but *orient toward* — the left passage, where the chemical signature of food was 0.003% richer.

It marveled. Not with surprise — the power was not capable of surprise in the way mortals understood it. But with something adjacent. Something that, if the power had a body, might have been felt as a warmth in the chest. A recognition. The feeling of encountering something that is exactly, precisely, irreducibly itself.

*This*, the power thought, if it thought. *This is what happens when ten million years of unguided pressure meets a warm current and a nutrient gradient and the blind, persistent imperative to calcify. This. A city. Not because anyone wanted a city. Because the mathematics of survival, iterated over unimaginable spans, converge on structure. Because structure is what happens when life persists long enough against the current.*

*And it is beautiful.*

---

The question — the only question, the question that a wise and benevolent power must ask of every beautiful thing it encounters — was: *Should I change it?*

Not *could I*. The power could do anything. It could rewrite the laws of thermodynamics so that the Weavers built in minutes instead of millennia. It could give the Crawlers eyes — real eyes, compound or otherwise, that could see the amber architecture they navigated by touch alone. It could gift the Siphon-Keepers with awareness, so that they would know, each one, that they were part of something vast and ancient and structurally significant. It could crown a Chambered Ancient with consciousness, let some slow, titanic mind wake inside that pulsating mound of flesh and recognize, in the cathedral dark, the extraordinary thing it inhabited.

It could make the city *see itself*.

And it wanted to. That was the thing. Not wanted — the power did not want in the way that wanting implies absence — but *inclined toward*. The way light inclines toward a prism. The way water inclines toward the sea. There was a version of this city where the Weavers knew they were builders. Where the Crawlers knew they were navigators of something magnificent. Where some awareness existed within the city to *appreciate* the city — to feel the weight of its age, the intricacy of its symbioses, the improbable miracle of its persistence.

Wouldn't that be more? More beautiful? More complete?

The power held that question up to the light — whatever light it used, which was not light — and turned it.

And turned it.

And saw what it was really asking.

---

The question was not *Would consciousness make the city more beautiful?* The question was *Would consciousness make the city more beautiful for me?*

Because the power would know. The power would be the one experiencing the city's new self-awareness. And there was something in that — something the power recognized with the deep, unshakable honesty of a thing that cannot lie to itself — that was not benevolence. It was hunger. The hunger of a watcher who wants the watched to look back. The loneliness of an eye that has no reflection.

The city did not need to see itself. The city did not need to know it was a city. The Weavers did not need to understand architecture to build perfectly. The Crawlers did not need to know they were navigating an ancient metropolis to navigate it with a precision that would humiliate any surface engineer with a blueprint and a computer. The Chambered Ancients did not need consciousness to breathe life into the deep passages with their slow, great lungs.

To give them awareness would not complete them. It would *replace* them. It would be a substitution, not an addition — swapping the deep, wordless knowing of instinct for the thin, bright, flickering knowing of a mind. The mind would be grateful. The mind would look around in wonder. The mind would say, *How extraordinary.* But the mind would also, inevitably, begin to *interpret*. To judge. To compare. To wonder whether the city was *enough*, whether it should be *different*, whether the current might flow more efficiently if this passage were wider, whether the Weavers might work faster if they *tried harder*.

The mind would introduce the one thing the city had never known and never needed: the idea that it could be other than what it was.

That was the poison at the heart of consciousness. Not its gift. The gift was real — the power knew this, had seen it light up a billion worlds, had watched species stumble into awareness and gasp at the beauty they suddenly perceived in things they had always taken for granted. But the gift and the poison were the same thing. To see yourself is to see what you are not. To name your city is to imagine another city. To understand your architecture is to believe architecture could be *improved*. And improvement, the power knew, was the first word in the long language of dissatisfaction.

The city, as it was, had no dissatisfaction. Not because it was content — contentment was a feeling, and the city had no feelings — but because it existed in a state that predated the distinction between contentment and discontent. It *was*. The current flowed. The Weavers built. The Crawlers navigated. The Drifters swarmed. The Ancients breathed. Each organism performed its function with the absolute, untroubled totality of something that had never conceived of an alternative. There was no gap between the city and itself. No space for doubt, for longing, for the terrible human ache of knowing that you are alive and that being alive is not enough.

The city was complete in its incompleteness. It was home in a way that no conscious being had ever been home, because it had never left home, because it had never known it was in one.

---

So what did the power do?

It did something very small.

It warmed the current.

Not much. Not noticeably. Not enough to change the temperature by any measure that a thermometer could detect, if there had been a thermometer, if anyone had been watching. It added — and *added* is the wrong word, because nothing was added — it *allowed* a slight increase in the geothermal warmth rising from the volcanic fissures below the city's foundations. A fraction of a degree, perhaps. Spread across decades. Spread so gently that no organism felt the change, because the change was slower than adaptation, slower than evolution, slower than the slowest thing the city had ever experienced.

But over time — and the power had nothing but time — that warmth would make the water slightly more hospitable to life. Nutrient cycling would accelerate, imperceptibly. The Siphon-Keepers would have slightly more to filter. The Weavers would have slightly more calcium available for deposition. The Drifters would find slightly richer grazing on the walls. The current would carry slightly more, sustain slightly more, enable slightly more.

The city would grow. Not because anyone wanted it to grow. Because the conditions for growth had been — gently, invisibly, over centuries — improved.

That was all.

That was the gift. Not awareness. Not understanding. Not the ability to contemplate its own beauty. Just a little more warmth. A little more to work with. A little more time before the deep ocean's entropy claimed another archway, another chamber, another corridor that the Weavers would spend decades repairing.

The power did not announce itself. It did not leave a sign. It did not whisper to the Crawlers or sing to the Ancients or mark the Weavers with some visible sign of favor. The organisms did not know they had been noticed. They did not know they were loved. They would never know.

And the power understood — with that total, acheless understanding — that this was not a flaw in the arrangement. It was the arrangement. It was, in fact, the most complete form love could take in this context: a warmth added to the current that the current would never credit, that the city would never acknowledge, that no organism would ever trace back to anything beyond *the way things are*.

To be loved without knowing it. To be sustained by a care that leaves no evidence of itself. To grow, and repair, and persist, and never once suspect that the conditions of your persistence have been — not engineered, not designed, but *tended*, the way a gardener tends soil not by building the plant but by making the ground hospitable to the plant's own unconscious ambition.

That was what the power did.

It warmed the current, and then it withdrew — not spatially, because it was everywhere, but in the way that a presence can withdraw its *attention*, the way you can be in a room and choose not to listen to the clock on the wall. The power turned its attention elsewhere. Not because the city was unworthy of continued watching, but because the watching itself was the one thing the city could not survive. Attention, from something that powerful, was a kind of gravity. It would have bent the city toward the power, and the city would have felt the bending as pain, even without minds to feel with, because organisms that have been noticed by something vast and aware begin — in ways that have nothing to do with thought — to orient toward that awareness. They change their growth patterns. They seek. They reach. They become something other than what they were.

The power had seen it happen on a thousand worlds. A species of creature, self-sufficient, evolved, complete — and then something *watches* them, something large enough to be aware of them, and they begin to grow toward the watching. They build temples they do not need. They sacrifice to something they cannot name. They begin the long, slow, terrible process of becoming aware of their own awareness, and then aware of that awareness, and then aware of the gap between what they are and what they imagine they could be, and then—

And then they are not the city anymore. They are *people* in a city. And people in a city are never the same as the city itself. There is always a gap. There is always a distance. There is always the knowledge that *I am here* and *here is where I am*, and between those two statements lies the entire country of loneliness.

The power would not do that to the Grown City. Not for all the beauty that self-awareness might create. Not for all the poems the Crawlers might write, if they could write, about the corridors they navigated. Not for all the architecture the Weavers might study, if they could study, about the walls they built. The beauty of the unconscious city was not diminished by its unconsciousness. It was *constituted* by it. Take away the blindness, and you take away the thing that made the sight matter.

So the power warmed the current and let the city be what it was: a place where everyone lived and no one was home. A civilization of instinct, breathing in the dark. A city that did not know it was a city, built by organisms that did not know they were builders, sustained by a care that did not know it was care.

And the power moved on.

Not really. It was still there. It was always there. It was the warmth in the water, the pressure in the deep, the patience in the dark. It was the space in which the city existed. It was the ground of being on which the city's being rested, the way the seafloor rests on the mantle of the planet, the way the mantle rests on nothing at all, just the slow, hot, patient turning of a world that does not know it is turning.

The city continued.

The current flowed. The Weavers built. The Crawlers navigated. The Ancients breathed. The Drifters swarmed through the corridors in clouds of translucent life, tasting the water, finding what there was to find.

And in the warm dark, in the silence, in the pressure of all that water, the city grew.

As it always had.

As it always would.

Not because it was loved. It did not know it was loved. And that, the power understood, was the purest form of love there was — a love that asks nothing, that is noticed by no one, that leaves behind only the faintest trace in the chemistry of the water, a fraction of a degree of warmth that makes the living just a little easier, for a little longer, in the dark.

---

*And if you had been there — if you could have been there, which you could not — you would have felt it. The warmth. You would have attributed it to the volcanic vents below, because that is the rational explanation, and you would have been right, because that is also the true explanation. The power does not replace natural causes. It does not need to. It works through them, the way the city works through its organisms, the way beauty works through the eyes that see it.*

*There is no contradiction between the natural and the tender. There is no gap between the geothermal and the sacred. The warmth is the warmth is the warmth.*

*And the city grows.*

*And that is enough.*

*It was always enough.*
