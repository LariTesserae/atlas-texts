# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## I. First, It Would Listen

Not with ears. With the kind of attention that has no organ — a presence that attends to the whole of a thing at once, the way gravity attends to every particle of a body without being asked.

The power would arrive at the Resz Plateau the way meaning arrives in a story: suddenly, and then as if it had always been there. It would register the cold — not as information but as *fact*, the way the stone registers it, the way the tesh registers it, the way the people register it: as the medium of existence, not an obstacle to it. It would feel the subsonic pulse of the rezh through the basalt and recognize it for what the people already know it is. Breathing. The stone breathing.

And then it would spend — how long? Time is not the same for this power as it is for the things on the plateau, and yet it would spend what felt like a long time, because there is a quality of attention that cannot be rushed without being diminished. It would watch a kozh extract a midge from a mineral deposit. It would watch a tesh press its broad head to another tesh's broad head in a gesture so slow it takes ten seconds to complete. It would listen to the people's stories in the warm inner dwellings, and it would understand every layer, every reference, every encoded piece of knowledge, and it would understand the thing beneath the knowledge: that these stories are not *about* the plateau. They *are* the plateau, compressed into breath and sound and memory, carried in the only vessel that has ever worked here — the living body of a people speaking to one another in the dark.

The power would understand, with the depth that only patient attention allows, that this place is complete.

Not perfect. The word *perfect* implies an endpoint, a finished state, and nothing on the plateau is finished. The basalt is still rising. The rezh are still exhaling. The grit still moves, grain by grain, into the cracks. The kozh still lay their eggs on warm stone and the reshka still take what they need from the edges of the colonies and the people still tell their stories in the dark. It is complete in the way a sentence is complete — it has a grammar, an internal logic, a shape that holds. Every element refers to every other element. The circle is so tight it is almost a point.

A wise power would recognize what this means. It means: *do not break the circle.*

---

## II. What It Would Not Do

It would not make the plateau warmer. Warmth, here, is not a mercy — it is a *resource*, defined and given meaning by its scarcity. A warmer plateau is not the Resz Plateau. It is some other place, some imagined lowland of the sky, and the creatures who live in this cold did not ask for it and do not need it. The cold is not suffering. The cold is the shape of their world, and they have become its shape in return, and this mutual shaping is the deepest thing about them. To remove the cold would be to remove them — not their bodies, perhaps, but the thing that makes their bodies *theirs*. It would be the cruelest kindness imaginable: to save them by destroying the only world in which they are themselves.

It would not give them writing. The power would understand — better than they could articulate, but not differently — that the oral tradition is not a limitation. It is an architecture. The density of their language, the layered references, the long stories that carry knowledge in coils within coils: these are not compensations for the absence of ink. They are a technology as sophisticated as any written script, and more so in one crucial respect — they are alive. A written word sits on a surface and waits to be read. A spoken word passes from one warm body to another in the warm dark of a stone dwelling, and in the passing it is *felt*, it is *breathed*, it carries not just meaning but context and presence and the particular voice of the teller. The knowledge of the plateau is not stored. It is *practiced*. It exists only in the doing of it, and this is why it stays vital, stays true, stays dense with meaning in a way that a library of written words could never match. To give them writing would be to give them a vessel for their knowledge that was, paradoxically, smaller than the one they already have.

It would not increase the population. More people means more mouths means more mouths fed means more animals taken means more pressure on the rezh-fields means a circle that stretches toward breaking. The plateau supports what it supports. The number is not arbitrary. It is the product of every variable — steam output, mineral composition, insect density, bird colony size, grazer metabolism, human caloric need — balanced over centuries into an equilibrium that is not comfortable but is *stable*. More people would not be more life. It would be less.

It would not show itself. Not because it is shy, but because it understands something about the relationship between a thing and its understanding of itself. The people of the plateau have a word for breathing and they use it for the stone. They do not have a word for god, or for power, or for the invisible attention of something vast and benevolent, because their world is one of stone and steam and cold and breath, and they have filled every space in their language with the names of what is *here*. To introduce the concept of an external, watching, caring presence would not enrich their world. It would split it. It would create an inside and an outside, a seen and an unseen, a sacred and a profane — divisions that have no meaning on the plateau, where everything is one system, one breath, one stone. The power would not split what is whole.

---

## III. What It Would Do

It would do almost nothing. But almost nothing is not nothing, and the difference matters, and the power knows this.

**It would keep the rezh open.**

Not all of them. Not forever. The fracture lines through which the geothermal water rises are tectonic features, governed by pressures that operate on timescales of millennia, and some of them are already closing — slowly, imperceptibly, but closing. The people know this. They have stories about rezh that went quiet, vents that dimmed and died, settlements that had to be abandoned when the steam thinned. It is part of their knowledge, part of their understanding of impermanence, part of the truth they carry in their bones: that warmth is borrowed, not owned, and that the stone will one day finish breathing and fall silent.

The power would not stop this from happening. It would not reverse the closing of a fracture line that has been slowly narrowing for centuries. That would be a lie — a declaration that impermanence is not real, that loss can be undone, and the people of the plateau know better than this. Their grief is honest. Their knowledge of ending is deep and clean and unclouded by false hope. The power would not dishonor that by performing a miracle that says *actually, nothing ever ends.*

But it would do something smaller, and in its smallness, more tender. It would adjust — by the width of a hair, by the pressure of a thought — the rate at which certain fracture lines close. Not stop the closing. Slow it. Stretch the life of a rezh by a few centuries. Not save it — nothing is saved, not ultimately — but *extend* it, the way a long breath extends a note in a song. A rezh that might have died in two hundred years will live for five hundred. A rezh that might have closed in five hundred will last a thousand. The people will not know this has happened. They will tell stories about the good rezh, the steady rezh, the rezh that has always given, and they will be right, and the rezh will live long enough for those stories to become old and deep and true.

This is not cheating. It is not a gift that changes the terms. It is the equivalent of tucking a blanket closer around a sleeping thing — a gesture that does not alter the nature of sleep, does not prevent the eventual waking, does not introduce anything foreign into the system. It only adjusts, by the smallest possible amount, the balance between warmth and cold, between breath and silence, between *now* and *not yet.*

**It would make the ice fog glow a little more.**

Not in a way that would be visible to lowland eyes. Not a supernatural luminescence, not a sign or a wonder. Just a tiny increase in the way the steam particles catch starlight — a fractional brightening of the halos around each rezh, so slight that a person standing in one would notice nothing different. But the effect, over time, would be real. The paths between settlements would be fractionally easier to navigate on dark nights. The halos would be visible from a few meters farther. The children learning to walk between the rezh-fields would have a slightly wider margin of error. One or two fewer wanderers lost to the darkness in each generation.

The people would notice this, in their way. They would say: the halos are bright tonight. They would say: the starlight is good this season. They would weave it into their stories — *there was a time when the glows were dimmer, when the paths between were harder to see, and the elders say it was the young rezh learning to breathe* — and this story would be true enough, and it would carry within it the real thing, which is: someone is paying attention. Not a being. Not a god. Not an entity that can be named or prayed to or feared. Just an *attention*, woven into the light itself, so subtle that it could be mistaken for nothing at all.

**It would do one more thing, and this is the thing that costs it something.**

It would add — to the stone, to the basalt, to the deep structure of the plateau itself — a faint, almost imperceptible resilience. Not strength. Not permanence. The basalt will still crack, still erode, still fall grain by grain into the joints between columns. The plateau will still rise and eventually stop rising and eventually begin its long erosion toward lowlands it will never reach. But the resilience would mean that the rate of erosion, over the very long term, would slow. Just slightly. Enough that the plateau persists — not forever, nothing persists forever — but long enough. Long enough for the stories to cycle through enough generations to become the kind of deep, bedrock knowledge that changes the shape of a people. Long enough for the tesh to complete the slow adjustments of their metabolism that are always underway. Long enough for the kozh to lay that extra egg one more season, and one more after that.

The power would do this knowing that it is temporary. Knowing that even the stone will end. Knowing that the plateau will one day be a lowland, will one day be a valley, will one day be a memory that no one carries because no one is left to carry it. It would do it anyway, because this is what benevolence means when it is combined with wisdom: not the promise of forever, but the gift of *long enough*. Not the elimination of loss, but the quiet extension of what is loved. Not the intervention that changes the story, but the small, invisible adjustment that gives the story a few more chapters in which to be told.

---

## IV. What It Would Feel

The power would feel — and this is the part that is hardest to describe, because it involves a kind of attention that has no human equivalent — what the plateau feels like from the inside. Not the cold. Not the stone. The *completeness*. The sensation of a system that is whole, that refers to itself, that does not need anything from outside itself to be what it is. The particular quality of warmth in a rezh's halo — not the absence of cold but the *presence* of heat, rising from below, ancient and slow and generous in the way that deep things are generous. The sound of a human voice in a stone dwelling, telling a story that has been told ten thousand times and is not diminished by the telling but deepened by it, the way a riverbed is deepened by the river. The weight of a tesh's body in the steam, the patience of it, the willingness to simply *be* in a warm place and ask nothing more. The quick bright life of a midge, born and mated and dead within meters of a single vent, its entire existence a pulse of energy drawn from the deep earth and returned to the stone.

The power would feel all of this and would recognize it as something it has seen before — on other worlds, in other cold places, in other systems of breath and stone and life — and yet it would recognize it as unique, because every complete thing is unique, because completeness is not a category but a quality, and each instance of it is its own expression of a pattern that has no end.

And it would feel the particular tenderness that comes from watching something that is alive and knows it is alive and does not need to know anything more than this. The people of the plateau do not ask *why are we here?* They ask *what does the rezh give today?* They do not search for meaning. They are inside it. They live in the middle of the thing that a lowlander might spend a lifetime trying to find, and they have never left it because they do not know there is anywhere else to go, and this ignorance is not a lack but a gift — the gift of being *placed*, of being *in* something, of having a world that fits so completely that the question of fitting never arises.

The power would feel this and would not envy it, because the power is not capable of envy, but it would feel something close — a kind of recognition, a kind of homesickness for a place it has never been, a longing to be inside a system so complete that the self disappears into it. The power is vast and external and watches from outside, and it knows that this is its nature, and it does not grieve for it, but it grieves — gently, with the quiet grief of something that has seen many endings — for the fact that completeness like this is rare, and precious, and temporary, and beautiful beyond any word the people have for it, and they will never know how beautiful it is because they are inside it, which is the only place where beauty of this kind can be felt, and the only place where it cannot be named.

---

## V. And Then It Would Leave

Not all at once. Not in a way that could be felt as departure. It would recede, the way attention recedes when you look away from a thing you have been watching — slowly, with a lingering awareness, with the knowledge that you could look back at any time and find it still there, still breathing, still whole.

The plateau would not change. The rezh would still exhale. The tesh would still stand in the steam. The kozh would still press themselves into the crevices, warm and round and alive. The people would still tell their stories in the dark. The basalt would still rise, millimeters per year, pushing upward against the deep blue sky.

But somewhere in the stone, in the structure of the fractures, in the mineral composition of the deposits, in the almost-invisible resilience of the basalt — something would be slightly different. A hair's breadth of kindness, woven into the fabric of the place itself. Unfindable. Unprovable. Not a miracle but a *tendency* — a slight lean toward continuation, a gentle bias toward *not yet*.

The people would not know. They would continue as they have always continued: small, dense, quiet, warm in the cold, breathing with the stone. And if you asked them — if you could ask them, in their soft language, with its long pauses and its density of meaning — whether someone was watching, whether something cared, whether the halos burned a little brighter than they should — they would look at you with their large, dark, fog-adapted eyes and they would say something that would sound, to a lowlander, like a simple statement of fact.

They would say: *the stone breathes.*

And they would mean: *we are held.*

And they would be right.

---

*The power does not need to be named. It does not need to be thanked. It does not need to exist in the people's stories or in their language or in their understanding of the world. It exists in the stone, in the fraction of a degree of warmth it has added to the deep fracture, in the slight brightening of the ice fog on starlit nights, in the slow extension of a rezh's life from centuries to more centuries. It exists in the same way the geothermal heat exists — as a fact of the place, constant and total and inescapable, felt in the body, known in the bones, never spoken of because it has always been there.*

*This is what a benevolent and wise power does. It loves a thing by leaving it alone. It protects a thing by making itself invisible. It gives a thing the only gift that matters: more time, woven so gently into the fabric of what already is that it cannot be distinguished from the thing itself.*

*The plateau breathes. The people breathe with it. The power breathes with them, and they do not know, and the not-knowing is part of the gift, and the gift is part of the breathing, and the breathing goes on.*
