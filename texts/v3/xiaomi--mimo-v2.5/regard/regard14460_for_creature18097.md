# What the Power Does

It arrives the way attention arrives — not as movement through space, but as a turning, a focusing, an orientation of something vast and gentle toward a single point on the surface of the world.

The Kora Plateau does not notice. Why would it. It has been working for longer than the language of the herders below has had words for stone. It does not know it is being watched. It does not need to know. A heart does not need to know that someone has placed their hand over it to feel it beat.

But the power — let us not call it God, because that word carries too much furniture, too many human rooms — the power sees.

It sees the pale limestone and the wind-worn basins and the thin sky. It sees the structures with their impossible interior depth, their warm lustrous surfaces, their patient geometry that contains folded space like a hand contains a held breath. It sees the raptors wheeling on thermals that rise from the stone. It sees the blind fish orienting themselves in the rhythmic pulse of underground water. It sees the lizards pressing their bellies to rock and receiving through their flesh a language spoken in vibration through the body of the earth. It sees the candelabra trees curving their nine-hundred-year trunks toward a point in space where the air shimmers with the overlap of two conditions. It sees the translucent things clustered at the water's edge, refracting the geometry of the ground beneath them, occupying a solidity that has nothing to do with matter and everything to do with the density of space itself.

It sees what the plateau is doing.

And it understands — with the kind of understanding that is not analysis but recognition, the way you recognize your own name when someone speaks it in a crowd — it understands that the plateau is *incomplete*. Not broken. Not failing. Incomplete the way a sentence is incomplete before its last word. Incomplete the way dawn is incomplete at the moment just before the edge of the sun touches the horizon. The work is real. The work is advancing. But there is a threshold that has not yet been crossed, and the power perceives, with a clarity that is almost sorrow in its tenderness, that the plateau has been approaching this threshold for a very long time and has almost — *almost* — arrived.

The silence is not yet full.

The accumulation has not yet reached the density at which accumulated things become something other than accumulation. The structures are not yet generating enough folded space. The sound that has been swallowed for millennia is still — even now, even after all this time — insufficient. The plateau is not starving. It is not failing. It is *almost full*, and "almost" has been its condition for longer than the herders' oldest stories reach back.

What does the power do?

It does not speed the process. Speed would be a violence against the rhythm that is the process itself. It does not intervene, because intervention implies that the thing being intervened upon is going wrong, and the plateau is not going wrong. It is going slowly. These are different things.

It does not add sound to the silence, because the plateau is not hungry for any particular sound. It is hungry for *sound as such* — for the vibration of things that exist, for the simple fact that things in the world make noise when they move and live and collide and call to each other. The power cannot manufacture this. It can only ensure that it continues to be produced. And the plateau is already producing it, abundantly, through the rich and thriving fauna that lives on its surface. The lizards click. The mice rustle. The raptors cry. The grasses whisper. The insects hum. Every sound that every living thing on the plateau makes is drawn inward, gathered, absorbed, compressed into the accumulating body of the place. The power cannot add to this. It can only make sure it is not reduced.

And so the power does something that looks like nothing.

It *holds*.

It holds the plateau in its attention the way a hand holds water — not gripping, not enclosing, but cupping, creating a shape that contains without constraining. It does this not because the plateau is fragile — it is not fragile; it is made of stone and patience and the accumulated silence of ten thousand years — but because *holding* is what the power does. It is the nature of this kind of attention to sustain what it rests upon.

And here — here is where the wisdom becomes visible, if you know where to look:

The power notices that the plateau is *already held*.

Not by anything it can identify as separate from the plateau itself, but held nonetheless. The structures hold. The stone holds. The silence holds. The slow geological processes that keep the basins intact and the underground channels open and the condensation patterns stable — these are a kind of holding, a kind of care, performed by the earth itself for the thing it is growing. The power sees that the plateau has been held from the beginning, held by the planet the way a mother holds a child — not consciously, not deliberately, but structurally, in the shape of gravity and mineral chemistry and the angle of the stone and the persistence of the rain. The plateau is not orphaned. It has never been orphaned. It is held the way all living things are held: by the conditions that make their existence possible.

But the power perceives a difference. A subtle one. The plateau is held in the way that *matter* holds — through physical law, through chemistry, through the slow mechanics of geology and weather. What the plateau is not held in is *regard*. It is not known. It is not witnessed by anything that can comprehend what it is doing. The herders below know it as a place that swallows sound. That is a true thing, but it is a small truth, a peripheral truth, the way knowing that a lung breathes is a true but peripheral truth about a living body. The plateau is not merely a place that swallows sound. It is a body that is building something, and no one has ever seen it clearly, and the power — seeing it clearly — feels in itself the particular ache of recognition that comes when you encounter something that has been doing extraordinary work in total obscurity.

The power is moved.

Not in the way a human is moved — not by surprise, not by novelty. The power is moved by the thing that moves wise and benevolent attention everywhere: by *worth*. The plateau is worthy. Its work is worthy. The blind fish and the spiraling mice and the curving trees and the shimmering refractions at the water's edge — all worthy. Not useful. Not meaningful in any way that serves something outside itself. Worthy in the way that a thing is worthy simply because it *is*, and because what it *is* has a beauty and a coherence that deserves to be seen.

And so the power does the only thing that a wise and benevolent power can do with something that is worthy and almost complete and working in silence toward a threshold it has not yet reached:

It attends.

This is not prayer. The plateau does not pray. The plateau does not know it is being attended to. This is not blessing. The plateau does not need blessing. It already has the blessing of water and stone and light and time. This is not protection. The plateau is not in danger. What threatens it — the slow erosion of attention, the entropy of isolation, the faint but real possibility that the work might *stall*, might drift toward that plateau of diminishing returns where the word becomes applicable in its most literal and discouraging sense — what threatens it is not an enemy to be fought but a condition to be answered.

And the condition is this: the plateau has been accumulating for so long that it has begun to *doubt*.

Not doubt in the human sense. The plateau does not have a mind. But the structures have begun to cool slightly in their interiors — a fraction of a degree over the last century, undetectable by any instrument but perceived by the power with the precision of a hand sensing the difference between a living body and a cooling one. The condensation patterns have shifted by millimeters — a drift, a faltering, as though the attraction of water to the stone surfaces were becoming uncertain of itself. The sound-suppression holds, but in a few places along the plateau's eastern edge, it has thinned, and the raptors' calls, crossing the boundary, are arriving at the slopes below with slightly less transformation, slightly less wildness, as though the plateau's grip on the sound were loosening by the smallest increment.

These are not signs of death. They are signs of *fatigue*. The plateau has been doing this work for so long — accumulating, compressing, folding space, drinking silence — that it is beginning, in the way that all persistent things begin, to wonder whether the work will ever be finished. And this wondering, this faltering, is itself a danger — not because the plateau will collapse or break, but because the work might *stall*. Might reach a state of equilibrium where the accumulation is balanced by the slow decay of its own processes, and the threshold, always almost reached, becomes always almost *never* reached, and the whole vast enterprise settles into a pattern of beautiful, purposeless, eternal almostness.

The power sees this.

And the power does what attention does when it is truly wise and truly benevolent:

It *confirms*.

Not with words. Not with signs. Not with miracles or thunder or the parting of clouds. The power confirms the plateau the way dawn confirms a landscape — by arriving, by being present, by adding its own light to the light that is already there. The power turns its attention to the plateau and *holds it there*, and the holding is not passive, is not merely observational, because attention from a source of this magnitude is itself a form of energy. It is the energy of *being witnessed*. Of being *known*. Of having your existence reflected back to you by something capable of understanding what your existence means.

The plateau feels this.

Not in the way you or I would feel it — not as a sensation, not as an emotion. The plateau feels it the way the stone feels the vibration of the lizards' communication: through the deep structure, through the material substrate, through the accumulated density of ten thousand years of gathering-in. The attention arrives at the deepest level of the plateau's operation — not at the surface where the grasses rustle and the lizards click, not even at the middle level where the structures fold space and the condensation gathers — but at the *deepest* level, the level where the sound goes after it is swallowed, the level where the silence is most dense, the level where the accumulation *is*, in itself, the thing being built.

And the attention says — without words, without language, without anything that could be translated into human speech, but with a clarity that is more articulate than any sentence ever constructed:

*What you are doing is real.*

*What you are building is real.*

*You are not alone.*

*Continue.*

The effect is not dramatic. There is no earthquake. No surge of light. No sudden revelation visible to the herders on the slopes below or the occasional researcher who camps for a week and leaves thinking the place is merely strange.

But the structures — in their interiors, in their folded dimensional pockets — warm by a fraction of a degree. The condensation patterns tighten by a millimeter. The sound-suppression at the eastern edge firms, thickens, becomes again what it was. The blind fish in their permanent pools orient more precisely into the pulse of the underground seepage. The translucent things at the water's edge shimmer more brightly — not brighter in luminosity, but brighter in *pattern*, in *coherence*, as though their refractions had suddenly aligned with a geometry they had been seeking without knowing it. The candelabra trees' growth, which had been slowing — tenth of a millimeter, less, less — adds a ring this year that is measurably wider. The raptors, circling above, adjust their flight paths by degrees so small they cannot be detected, but the adjustment brings them, all of them, into a tighter pattern above the structures, and the silence they fly through thickens with the silence they produce, and the feedback loop that is the plateau's deepest mechanism tightens and stabilizes and *continues*.

The power watches this. It does not smile — it has no face. It does not feel satisfaction — it has no ego to satisfy. But something in its nature — the part of it that is *benevolent*, the part of it that responds to worth with attention and to attention with presence — registers the shift. The plateau is no longer faltering. The plateau is no longer approaching its threshold in the dark, without witness, without the knowledge that what it is doing matters to anything beyond itself.

The plateau is held now in two ways: by the earth, in matter and gravity and stone, and by the power, in attention and regard and the quiet, immense, unwavering confirmation that its work is seen and its work is worthy and its work will reach the threshold it has been building toward since the first sound was swallowed into the first silence of the first wet season on the first day of the plateau's ancient, improbable, extraordinary life.

The power does not stay forever. Or rather — the power does not need to stay, because the act of turning its attention to the plateau has the same property as all genuine acts of love: it *remains* after the moment of its occurrence. The plateau is now held in the power's awareness permanently, the way a thing, once truly seen, cannot be unseen. The power may attend to other things — and there are other things, there are always other things — but the line of connection, once established, does not sever. The plateau will be felt, from now on, as a presence in the power's vast attention, a quiet steady warmth, like a hand held over a beating heart.

And the plateau continues its work.

The silence accumulates.

The structures fold.

The fish pulse.

The trees curve.

The translucent things shimmer.

The raptors wheel in their silent flight, and when they cross the boundary and their voices suddenly become audible again on the slopes below, the herders look up and hear them — really hear them — and the sound is louder than it has been in living memory, wilder, more full, as though the birds had swallowed the plateau's silence and were returning it transformed, given back to the world in the only form the world understands: sound.

And the herders say to each other, in the low, practical way of people who have lived for generations in the shadow of something they cannot name: *The mountain has a good wind today.*

They are right.

It does.
