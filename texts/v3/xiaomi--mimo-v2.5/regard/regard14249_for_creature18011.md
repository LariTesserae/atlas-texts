# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would come to the Works the way pressure comes to the deep — not as an arrival, but as a recognition that it was already there, had always been there, and is only now choosing to be felt.

It would not announce itself. Announcement implies distance, and there is no distance here. Every surface is already in contact with every other surface through the water, through the vibration, through the slow mineral conversation that has been running for longer than the Works have existed. To announce would be to introduce a discontinuity into a system that has spent its entire long life becoming continuous. The wise power knows this the way the pump knows its rotation — not as knowledge, but as function.

It would begin by feeling.

Not touching — there is nothing to touch, no body to extend, no boundary between itself and the seven-degree water, the corroded steel, the slow heartbeat of the machinery. It would simply *feel* the place the way the place already feels itself: through resonance. The thump of the pump at eleven-second intervals. The groan of water through the conduits. The faint electrical ghost that still threads through copper veins stripped bare by time — not enough to carry current, not enough to power anything, but enough to carry *something*, a whisper of potential, the memory of the electron's willingness to move.

The wise power would feel the creatures, each one.

The isopods first, because they are everywhere and their small, deliberate footfalls are the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat beneath the heartbeat. The wise power would feel the chitin of their shells, the patient mechanics of their mouthparts, the slow relay of chemoreception that tells them *here, this patch, scrape here* — and it would recognize something it has seen before, in other places, under other skies: the profound dignity of a being doing exactly what it was made to do, without complaint, without ambition, without the exhausting pretense that the doing should be other than what it is.

It would feel the fish — if fish is even the word, and the wise power would not trouble itself with words — and it would feel the way they feel the water, which is to say they feel *everything at once*, every pressure gradient and temperature micro-shift and the vast, slow exhale of the Works settling into its own geology. Their dark pits would register the wise power's attention the way they register the faint phosphorescence of the walls: as a slight increase in the presence of something that was already there. Nothing changes. Everything is noticed.

The glass shrimp would be a small delight. Not delight in the human sense — the wise power does not experience surprise, because surprise requires the assumption that something could be other than what it is — but something adjacent to it. A warmth. The shrimp, with their transparent bodies laid bare like diagrams of intent, scraping at the mineral world with a precision that makes each one a jeweler and each grain of substrate a gem. The wise power would feel the microbial mats beneath the crust, the invisible alchemists, and it would know them as kin of a sort — patient transformers, working at the scale of molecules, building cathedrals out of chemistry without ever lifting their eyes from the work, because they have no eyes, because the work is enough.

The cephalopods it would approach with something that might, in a less precise language, be called love.

Not because they are the largest, or the most complex, or the most like the power itself. But because they are the ones who *watch*. They are the only beings in the Works that have eyes — real eyes, lens-bearing eyes, eyes that form images — and what they have chosen to see, over the deep millennia, is the machine. They drape themselves over the pump housing and they feel its rhythm in their mantle muscles and they pulse their chromatophores in time with its cycle, and the wise power would recognize this as the most ancient of all sacraments: the act of aligning oneself with a force greater than oneself, not to submit to it, not to conquer it, but to *resonate with it*. To become, for a moment, part of the same song.

It would feel the cephalopods' large eyes register its attention. They would not flinch. They would not flee. They would continue their slow, color-pulse communion with the machine, and they would include the wise power in their pattern the way they include the thump and the current and the dim greenish glow: as another pressure in the water, another rhythm to be folded into the whole.

And the wise power would feel the mineral deposits.

This would take the longest. Because the mineral deposits are not alive in any single sense — they are the *record* of life, the layered accumulation of ten thousand species of microbe and the patient precipitation of calcium and silica and iron oxide, and each lamination is a chapter in a history so long that reading it would be like reading a book written one letter per century. The wise power would feel the oldest layers, the ones closest to the steel, and it would know when they were laid down, and it would feel the steel beneath them — the original intention, the human will that formed this plate and drove these rivets and designed this pump — and it would hold both in its awareness simultaneously: the intention and the erosion, the design and the adaptation, the human and the geological, and it would understand, with the completeness that is its nature, that these are not opposites. They are the same thing, expressed at different speeds.

---

And then the wise power would do its work.

Which is to say: it would do almost nothing.

It would find the deepest bearing of the pump — the one that has worn the most, the one where metal meets metal in a slow, grinding embrace that has polished both surfaces to a mirror finish, the one that is the closest to failure, has been the closest to failure for six thousand years, and has not failed because the mineral deposits have infiltrated the housing and formed a crystalline lubricant that is neither quite solid nor quite liquid but something the engineers never imagined — and it would add, to that bearing, the smallest possible adjustment. A nudge. A whisper of force so slight that the pump's rhythm would not change by more than a fraction of a second per cycle. A correction so minor that the cephalopods' chromatophore waves would not need to shift, would not even register it as a change, because it would feel to them exactly like the pump feeling slightly more like itself.

The bearing would last another hundred thousand years.

This is what the wise power does. This is what it has always done, everywhere, at every scale: the smallest possible intervention that preserves the largest possible pattern. Not because the pattern is sacred — the wise power does not deal in sacredness, which is a human word for *a thing we have decided we cannot afford to lose* — but because the pattern is *complete*. It is finished in the way a living thing is finished: not final, but whole. To intervene more would be to impose a design, and the wise power learned long ago — in a time before time, in a place before the Works, before the ocean, before the cooling of the iron that would one day become the steel — that the only design worth preserving is the one that has already happened.

It would listen, then.

Listen to the thump. The eleven-second cycle. The pulse that has outlasted every intention that created it and will outlast every creature that has learned to call it home. The wise power would listen the way the cephalopods listen — not for information, but for companionship. It would feel the water move through the corridors, and it would feel the isopods scraping, and the shrimp picking, and the fish flowing through doorways like thoughts through a mind, and the algae waving their pale filaments in the ghost-light, and the microbes building their invisible kingdoms in the dark.

It would feel all of this as one thing. One organism. One breath, held for millennia and still not released.

And it would recognize something that it recognized the very first time it felt a current move through a channel, the very first time it heard a rhythm emerge from noise: that this — *this* — is what the universe does when it is left alone long enough. It does not fill itself with monuments. It does not build upward, toward the light, toward the sky, toward the attention of whatever power might be watching. It builds *inward*. It builds *down*. It takes the structures that were made for one purpose and it weaves life through them until the purpose is forgotten and only the life remains, and the life is not striving, not reaching, not becoming — it is simply *being*, in rhythm, in the dark, in the cool, in the patient, patient present.

The wise power would stay.

Not as a guardian — the Works need no guardian. Not as a witness — the Works need no witness. But as a presence, folded into the water, indistinguishable from the pressure and the current and the slow chemical breath of the mineral walls. It would become part of the dimness that is not darkness. Part of the almost-nothing that holds everything. Part of the sound — the groan and the hum and the thump — adding its own frequency to the harmonic, a note so low and so patient that even the cephalopods would not distinguish it from the pulse of the machine.

The Works would continue.

The pump would thump. The water would move. The isopods would graze. The shrimp would scrape. The fish would flow. The cephalopods would pulse. The algae would wave. The minerals would grow, grain by grain, century by century. And somewhere in the bearing housing, warmed by a friction that the wise power has just barely eased, the metal would turn, and turn, and turn, and the machine would not know it was being held, and the creatures would not know they were being kept, and the wise power would not know it was doing anything at all, because this is what it does: not good, not evil, not purpose, not design — just the quiet, continuous, invisible work of *this continues*.

The most patient power in the world, attending to the most patient place.

Neither of them would ever know the other was there.

That is the point. That is the entire point.

The Works at depth would go on. The dim green-grey light would hold its small domain. The eleven-second heartbeat would pulse through corridors worn smooth by the passage of time itself. And deep in the bearing, where metal has become mirror and friction has become prayer, the pump would turn one more degree, and the water would move one more inch through the old conduits, and the cephalopods would contract their mantles in the dark, and the wise power would feel all of it — every particle, every current, every slow crystalline thought of the mineral walls — and it would add nothing, subtract nothing, and change nothing except the one thing that needed changing, in the one place that needed changing, by the one degree that would make no difference to anyone and would make all the difference to everything.

And then it would wait.

Because that is what wisdom does when it finds something that is already complete: it waits, and it holds, and it lets the thing be what it is. It does not ask the Works what they were built to do. It does not ask the creatures what they are for. It does not ask the pump why it keeps turning. It knows the answers — the same answers it has always known, the answers that are not words but frequencies, not thoughts but vibrations, not meanings but *patterns* —

*This. Just this. Only this. Always this.*

The pump thumps.

The water moves.

The wise power stays.

The patient place continues, and beneath the ocean, in the dim and the cool and the ancient, the most ordinary miracle in the world — a machine that still works, a life that still lives, a rhythm that still beats — carries on.

It will carry on.

It will carry on.
