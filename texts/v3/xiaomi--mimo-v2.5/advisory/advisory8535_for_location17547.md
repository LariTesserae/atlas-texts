# Preparing for the Col of Shattered Pillars

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before packing a single thing, sit with this: you are going to a place that is roughly the size of a city block, at an altitude where the air itself has become unreliable. It is a room made of stone at the top of the world, and every single property of it — the light, the cold, the terrain, the silence — is optimized to make a soft, warm, sea-level human miserable and then dangerous to themselves. This is not a hostile place. Hostility implies intent. This is an *indifferent* place, and indifference at this altitude and temperature is worse than malice, because malice can sometimes be negotiated with.

The Col of Shattered Pillars will not kill you quickly. It will do it through a sequence of small failures: wet gloves that freeze stiff, sunburn through clouds, a misjudged step into a pool that looked hand-deep and was waist-deep, lungs that quietly can't keep up, a slow cognitive dimming from cold and altitude that makes you convinced the scratches on that flat rock mean something important enough to spend another hour here when you should be leaving.

Know what you're walking into.

## Oxygen and Altitude

The air is *thin*. The text says it can be tasted — a reduction, a strain. This means you are likely above 5,000 meters, possibly above 6,000. At these altitudes, oxygen saturation drops to a level where your body begins to systematically deteriorate. You will not feel this happening in a dramatic way. You will feel tired. You will feel a headache you attribute to the brightness. You will feel clumsy and slightly confused and think it's just the unfamiliar terrain.

**Physiological preparation (weeks before):**

- If you have any control over the timeline, spend at least 5–7 days at a moderate altitude (3,000–4,000 meters) before ascending to the col. Your body needs to begin producing additional red blood cells. The Col doesn't give you time to acclimatize — it's not a gradual slope. You arrive or you don't.
- Consult a physician about **acetazolamide (Diamox)**, the standard altitude sickness prophylaxis. It helps your kidneys excrete bicarbonate, acidifying your blood and stimulating deeper breathing. Side effects include tingling in extremities (which is ironic and uncomfortable given the cold, but manageable).
- Consider carrying **dexamethasone** as an emergency corticosteroid for high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) and **nifedipine** for high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE). These are not preventatives — they are last-resort medications to buy you time to descend.
- Learn to recognize the symptoms of acute mountain sickness (AMS): headache, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, loss of appetite. Learn to distinguish mild AMS from HACE (confusion, ataxia — *cannot walk a straight line*, altered consciousness) and HAPE (breathlessness at rest, persistent cough, gurgling sounds in chest). HACE and HAPE will kill you. The treatment is descent. Period. There is no treatment at the col. There is no one at the col.

**What to carry for breathing:**

- A **portable oxygen system** is not optional. It is essential. A lightweight cylinder with a regulator and nasal cannula, sufficient for the duration of your visit plus a generous buffer. You will not need it for every moment — you may find you can function on ambient air for short periods — but the moment you notice your thinking becoming sluggish, the moment the headaches intensify, the moment you find yourself panting after climbing over a basalt column that should have been easy, put it on. Do not wait. Altitude impairs judgment about altitude.
- A **pulse oximeter** — the small clip-on kind that reads your blood oxygen saturation. Check it regularly. At sea level, normal is 95–100%. At the col's altitude, you might see 75–85% and feel "fine." That's the altitude talking. If you drop below 70%, you are in danger. If you cannot get it above 80% with supplemental oxygen, leave.

## Light and Vision

The light is the first thing that will attack you. Not gradually. *Immediately.* The text is explicit: "every surface is a mirror or a blade." The sky is a white-grey press. The rock throws light back from below. There is no shade. There is no canopy. The col is a box of light with no dark corners except the cracks in the rock, and even those are bright at their edges.

**Eye protection:**

- You need **glacier glasses**, not merely sunglasses. Full-wrap, side-shielded, with lenses rated to block 100% of UV and with a very dark tint — category 4, the mountaineering grade. Standard sunglasses are insufficient. They will let light in from the sides and above, and the reflected light from the rock below will enter from below your frames. You will get snow blindness (photokeratitis) in as little as 30 minutes of unprotected exposure at this altitude, and the symptoms — pain, tearing, the sensation of sand in your eyes, temporary blindness — will arrive hours later when you can do nothing about them.
- Carry **two pairs**. If you lose or break one, the other becomes your lifeline. Consider securing them to your head with a retainer strap — you will be climbing over rock with your hands, and the motion of looking up and down through broken terrain is exactly the motion that dislodges glasses.
- In the deep-blue twilight hours, when the iron-oxide patterns seem to pulse, you may be tempted to remove your glasses because the light feels gentler. Don't. UV intensity at altitude persists well into twilight, and your eyes, already strained, will be maximally vulnerable.
- Carry **eye drops** — lubricating drops for dry, wind-burned eyes. The air is too dry for comfort. The wind, when it comes, is a desiccant.

**Skin protection:**

- The UV radiation at this altitude is extreme. The thin atmosphere filters almost nothing. Every exposed surface of skin will burn, and it will burn through cloud cover, through the white-grey sky, through the sense that it isn't "sunny enough" to matter.
- Apply **broad-spectrum SPF 50+ sunscreen** before exposure and reapply frequently, especially on the underside of your chin and nose (reflected light from the rock), your ears, and the backs of your hands, which you will be placing on sun-struck basalt all day.
- A **buff or balaclava** that can be pulled up over the lower face and neck is essential. Not just for cold — for UV.
- **Lip balm with SPF 30+**. Cracked, sunburned lips at altitude, combined with dry air, become painful and slow to heal.

## Cold and Thermal Management

The cold is constant. It is not dramatic — there is no storm to fight, no wind chill that shrieks — but it is *total*. The text says it "seeps into stone itself." The rock is cold in a way that feels like "absence." This is a cold that extracts heat not through force but through patience. It works through conduction (your hand on cold basalt), through convection (thin wind stripping warmth from exposed skin), and through radiation (your body radiating heat into a sky that radiates almost nothing back).

**The core problem:** You will be using your hands constantly to navigate the terrain. "There is no walking here that does not involve placing a hand on rock." Every time you touch basalt, the stone draws heat from your hand with extraordinary efficiency — rock is an excellent thermal conductor, and at these temperatures, the temperature differential is significant. Your gloves will get cold. You will take them off to do something delicate — adjust a piece of gear, examine a mineral pattern, grip a narrow edge — and in the 30 seconds your bare hand is exposed, the cold will begin to deepen into pain and then numbness. The transition from "cold" to "dangerously cold" in extremities at altitude is faster than you expect, because peripheral vasoconstriction (your body pulling blood to the core to protect vital organs) is already happening due to altitude. Your fingers are already being partially abandoned by your circulatory system. The cold accelerates this.

**Clothing system — layers, and why each matters:**

- **Base layer:** Merino wool, snug-fitting, covering torso and legs. Merino retains warmth when damp (from condensation, from sweat during exertion) and doesn't stink after a day of hard use. Avoid cotton. Cotton kills — it absorbs moisture, loses all insulating value, and conducts heat away from the body. At the col, cotton is a death sentence delivered politely.
- **Mid layer:** A fleece or synthetic insulated jacket. This is your primary warmth layer. When you're moving, generating heat, this is what keeps you comfortable. When you stop — and you will stop, to navigate a tricky section, to examine the cairn, to catch your breath in the thin air — this layer prevents the rapid cooling that leads to hypothermia.
- **Outer shell:** A windproof, water-resistant hardshell jacket with a hood. The wind at the col is thin and irregular, but it's enough to strip heat. The shell stops convective heat loss. The hood, tightened around the face, protects against wind and UV. Choose a light color if possible — dark shells absorb solar radiation, which sounds beneficial but can cause you to overheat during exertion and then chill dangerously when you stop. The col doesn't have a comfortable temperature at which to regulate. You're always managing the boundary between "warm enough while moving" and "cold when still."
- **Hands:** This is the critical gear decision. You need **liner gloves** (thin, dexterous, for manipulating gear) AND **insulated gloves** (warm, for climbing and navigating). You will be switching between them constantly. Your liner gloves will get wet from condensation on rock and cold from contact. Carry multiple pairs of liners — at least two backup pairs in a sealed, dry bag inside your pack. When a pair of liners gets wet and cold, swap them. Put the wet pair inside your jacket against your core to dry. This rotation is not a convenience; it's a survival strategy. You cannot afford to lose function in your hands. You cannot navigate this terrain numb-handed.
- **Feet:** Insulated, waterproof mountaineering boots. Not hiking boots — mountaineering boots with rigid soles and thermal liners. The basalt columns are slick with condensation. The shattered granite slabs are angled and unstable. You need a sole that grips on wet mineral surfaces and provides ankle support for the constant stepping up, over, and between formations. Waterproofing matters because of the frost-shoved gravel and the meltwater pools — you will inevitably step in water, and wet feet at this temperature begin the slow process of losing feeling, then losing function, then losing toes. Pair with **wool mountaineering socks** and bring a spare pair sealed in a dry bag.
- **Head:** A warm beanie or balaclava that covers ears. A significant percentage of body heat is lost through the head, and your ears are particularly vulnerable to frostbite. The balaclava also serves as UV protection and wind protection.

**Active warming:**

- Carry **chemical hand warmers** (air-activated, iron-powder based). Keep them in an inner pocket against your core until needed. When your hands are cold and wet and you've exhausted your liner-glove rotation, activate a pair and tuck them into your gloves. This is a comfort measure that becomes a functional measure — warm hands think better, grip better, and make fewer mistakes.
- A **thermos of hot liquid** — tea, broth, whatever you can palate. Hot liquid serves double duty: hydration (critical at altitude, where dry air and exertion dehydrate you faster than you realize) and core warming. The act of drinking something hot sends a wave of warmth through the core that temporarily reverses the vasoconstriction in your extremities. It's not a fix. It's a reprieve. But reprieves matter.

## Terrain and Movement

The ground is not ground. It is a dense, cluttered field of broken stone — columnar basalt standing in clusters, shattered granite slabs at every angle, everything near-touching, everything demanding hands. This is not hiking. This is closer to scrambling, but slower, more deliberate, and with the constant complication that surfaces are slick with condensation and frost.

**Footwear** (detailed above): Rigid-soled, waterproof, high-cut mountaineering boots. Break them in before the trip. Blisters at altitude are catastrophically slow to heal.

**Movement technique:**

- Move slowly. The thin air will punish exertion far more than you expect. What would be an easy step at sea level — pulling yourself up onto a basalt column, stepping across a gap between slabs — becomes a breathless, heart-pounding effort. Your muscles will fatigue faster. Your coordination will be subtly impaired by altitude. Slow is smooth, and smooth is safe.
- **Three points of contact** at all times. Two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand. The terrain demands it. The basalt columns are smooth with condensation. The granite slabs are angled and may shift under weight (they've been "stacked and wedged by millennia of freeze-thaw" — they are stable enough in aggregate but individually unpredictable).
- **Test every handhold and foothold** before committing weight. The frost-weathering has been disassembling every surface for eons. What looks solid may have a crack running through it that you can't see. Place your hand, apply pressure, feel for movement or crumbling before you pull.
- Watch for the **meltwater pools**. They are beautiful and treacherous. They look hand-deep. They are not. Refraction distorts depth. Some are deep enough to submerge your arm to the elbow or beyond. Stepping into one is not just wet feet — it's a sudden thermal shock that will spike your heart rate and dump heat through the largest exposed skin area you have. If you must cross water, use a trekking pole or your hand to probe depth first.

**Essential gear for terrain:**

- **Trekking poles** (collapsible, lightweight) with carbide tips. These provide two additional points of contact on slick basalt and help you gauge the depth of meltwater pools. They also reduce the load on your knees and legs during the constant climbing up and over formations. However — there are sections between basalt columns that are too narrow for poles. You'll need to stow them and switch to pure scrambling. Practice collapsing and stowing poles quickly; you'll be doing it frequently.
- **Helmet.** This is not optional. You will be climbing over and between rock formations in a place where frost-cracking sends sharp fragments loose without warning ("the rare, sharp *tick* of a frost-crack propagating through a slab — a sound like a knuckle popping"). A chunk of basalt or granite falling from even a modest height, onto an unprotected skull, at altitude where you're already cognitively impaired — this is a plausible way to die in a place that is not trying to kill you. A lightweight climbing helmet with good ventilation (you need to shed heat from the head during exertion) is essential.
- **Knee pads or gaiters** if you anticipate crawling or kneeling on basalt. The columnar joints are hexagonal and their edges, weathered as they are, are still hard enough to bruise and cut through pants.

## Hydration and Nutrition

There is no food in the col. There is no food growing, no animals, no foraging. The water in the meltwater pools is described as extraordinarily clear and is presumably clean (it's filtered through rock and formed by condensation and slow melt), but at this temperature, drinking untreated water from pools surrounded by mineral deposits is a calculated risk — minimal biological contamination, but possible chemical contamination from the iron and manganese oxides. The water is likely safe. Probably. Carry purification tablets as insurance anyway, or boil it in your thermos if you can.

**What to carry:**

- **Water:** At least 3 liters in an insulated, freeze-resistant container. Water bladders (CamelBak-style) will freeze in the tubes at this temperature unless you insulate them aggressively and blow back into the tube after each sip. A wide-mouth Nalgene inside an insulated sleeve, worn inside your jacket where body heat keeps it liquid, is more reliable. Yes, it's inconvenient. Frozen water is more inconvenient.
- **Food:** High-calorie, cold-tolerant, no-preparation food. The col offers no opportunity to cook. Your hands will be cold and clumsy. You need food you can eat with gloved hands, food that doesn't freeze solid, food that your body can burn efficiently in the cold. **Specific recommendations:**
  - Nuts and dried fruit (calorie-dense, resistant to freezing, easy to eat)
  - Energy bars or gels (fast glucose for when altitude fatigue hits suddenly)
  - Dark chocolate (high calorie, doesn't freeze as hard as milk chocolate, provides a psychological boost)
  - Cheese (hard cheese resists freezing better than soft; calories and salt)
  - Salty snacks (salt is lost through respiration in dry air; you need to replace it)
- Eat before you're hungry. Drink before you're thirsty. At altitude, both hunger and thirst signals are delayed and suppressed. By the time you feel them, you're already behind.

## The Psychological Dimension

This is where most preparation guides would stop, but this is where the Col of Shattered Pillars demands the most honest preparation.

**The light and the silence will affect your mind.**

You are going to a place where the brightness is beyond endurance, where the quiet is not silence but the slow sounds of mineral process — sublimation hiss, frost-crack ticks, glassy wind-whispers, occasional resonant tones from narrow gaps. You are going to a place where the stone itself seems to be *organizing* — where iron-oxide patterns hover at the threshold of recognition, where concentric rings seem to pulse in failing light, where the geometry of the shattered pillars suggests intention without delivering it.

This is not dangerous in itself. But you will be tired, cold, oxygen-deprived, and alone. These are the conditions under which the human brain does its most creative and unreliable work. You will see faces in the rock. You will want to. The patterns are *eager* — they practically beg for pareidolia, for the brain to complete the circuit and say "that's a hand, that's an eye, that's a face looking at me." At sea level, well-rested, you'd smile and note the interesting mineralogy. At altitude, cold, tired, alone, you might find yourself staring at a basalt face for ten minutes, convinced the pattern is changing, convinced it means something.

**Preparation for the psychological dimension:**

- **Acknowledge the effects of altitude on cognition.** Hypoxia produces euphoria, confusion, impaired judgment, and vivid ideation. You may feel brilliant. You may feel that you're understanding something profound about the mineral world. You may feel a sense of awe so intense it becomes functionally paralyzing. These are symptoms. Treat them as symptoms. When you notice yourself having thoughts that feel more significant than any thought you've ever had — when the mineral patterns seem to be *telling* you something — check your pulse oximeter. You are almost certainly hypoxic. Put on supplemental oxygen. The patterns will wait. They've been waiting for millennia.
- **Set strict time limits for the visit.** Decide in advance: "I will spend no more than [X] hours at the col." The place has a way of filling the time. You'll want to examine the cairn. You'll want to study the tool marks on the flat boulder. You'll want to look at the patterns. You'll want to just *stand* in the brightness and listen to the wind play the basalt like an instrument. These are all reasonable desires. They are also all ways of staying too long in a place that is slowly draining your body's reserves. Set an alarm. Honor it.
- **The cairn is the only human trace in the place.** Someone was here before you. Someone carried seven rounded stones up from a lower, wetter place and stacked them. Someone scratched parallel lines on a flat boulder that might be counting, might be nothing. This is either comforting or profoundly unsettling, depending on your state of mind. The comfort: you are not the first to stand here and feel small and bewildered. The unsettling part: whoever was here left almost nothing. They did not stay. Consider why.
- **Bring a journal and a pen (not a pencil — pencils freeze and become useless; a pressurized Fisher Space Pen or similar that writes in any temperature).** Writing forces sequential, linear thought, which is the antidote to the drifting, pattern-seeking cognition that the col induces. If you find yourself lost in thought, write down what you're thinking. Examine it on paper. It will look different there than it did in your head.

## Navigation and Exit

The col is small — two hundred paces across at its widest. You can walk its full extent in a few minutes. The ridgelines are climbable. The far sides drop into unseen valleys.

This is deceptive. Small does not mean simple. The terrain inside the col is dense and physically demanding. The ridgelines are steep with sharp crests. And the light — that directionless, white, pressing light — destroys your ability to read landscape features. There are no shadows to give you orientation. The sun's position is ambiguous. The sky is a uniform glow.

**Navigation preparation:**

- **GPS device** with the col's coordinates pre-loaded. Do not rely on visual landmarks. The basalt columns all look alike. The granite slabs shift perspective as you move between them. The light flattens depth. You will think you're heading toward the flat boulder with the cairn and find yourself at the opposite ridge.
- **Physical compass** as backup. Even if you're not navigating by compass bearing within the col, a compass tells you which direction is which, which is enough to orient yourself.
- **Mark your entry/exit point.** Before you descend into the col from whatever approach you use, establish a visual marker — a stack of rocks, a bright piece of gear — that you can identify from within the col. The ridgelines look similar from below. The light makes everything look similar.
- **Buddy system, if possible.** If you're traveling with someone, establish protocols for staying in visual contact. The basalt clusters can break line of sight in seconds. At altitude, shouting is exhausting and the wind carries sound unpredictably. Agree on check-in intervals. Agree on what to do if separated.
- **If alone, leave a trip plan** with someone who knows where you are, when you expect to return, and what to do if you don't.

## Emergency Considerations

There is no rescue at the col. There is no signal — assume your phone has no service. There is no shelter except the narrow spaces between basalt columns, which are too small to sleep in but can serve as wind breaks.

**Emergency gear:**

- **Emergency bivvy sack** (reflective, lightweight, takes up almost no space). If you are injured or incapacitated and cannot leave the col, a bivvy sack traps enough radiant heat to buy you hours. Hours is what matters — enough hours for someone to reach you, or enough hours for the weather to change (it won't — the weather doesn't change here — but the hope of hours is itself a survival tool).
- **First aid kit** focused on: blisters, cuts from sharp rock (the chert flakes are razor-sharp; the basalt edges are unforgiving), burns (sun and cold), altitude medications. Include **ibuprofen** (helps with altitude headache, anti-inflammatory) and **aspirin** (cardiac emergency, unlikely but you're at altitude with a stressed cardiovascular system).
- **Whistle** (attached to your jacket or pack strap). Three blasts is the universal distress signal. Sound carries strangely in the col — the wind and the rock shapes distort it — but a whistle cuts through ambient noise better than a voice.
- **Headlamp** with fresh batteries (lithium batteries perform better in cold than alkaline). The "brief, deep-blue twilight" can arrive with startling speed. Being caught in the col in near-darkness, unable to see the basalt columns clearly, unable to distinguish meltwater pools from dry ground — this is a recipe for a serious fall.
- **Duct tape** wrapped around a trekking pole or water bottle. Universally useful for field repairs.

## Mental Preparation: The Deepest Layer

You are going to a place that will not feel welcoming. It will not feel hostile either. It will feel *alien* in the most literal sense — a place that does not account for you, does not need you, does not notice you. The mineral world here is doing what it has done for longer than life has existed: crystallizing, eroding, depositing, splitting. You are an anomaly in it. A warm, soft, oxygen-dependent anomaly standing in a room of stone and light that has been conducting its slow chemistry since before there were eyes to see it.

The patterns on the rock will try to make it feel alive. Let them. The cairn will try to make it feel visited. Let it. But do not let the place make you feel *known*. It does not know you. It does not know itself. It is a col — a saddle between ridges, a gap in the high place — and the fact that mineral processes have, over deep time, produced forms that echo the language of life is beautiful and strange and not for you. You are passing through a room that is dreaming of being alive, and you are awake in it, and your job is to see it clearly and then leave.

Prepare for that. Prepare to stand in a place so bright it hurts, so cold it reaches into your bones, so quiet that the sound of ice sublimating off a basalt face is the loudest thing in the world — and to feel, despite all your preparation, all your gear, all your careful planning, that you are not equipped for this. That no one is. That the place was not made for equipping.

You're right. It wasn't. Go anyway. But go ready.
