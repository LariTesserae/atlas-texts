# Preparing to Visit Station Nuth

## The Weight of Water Above You

The first thing to reckon with is that you are going *under*. Not into a pool, not into a lake, but into the deep ocean — onto a constructed plateau that sits high above the seafloor but is nonetheless buried beneath an ocean's worth of water pressing down from above. The description says walls no more than fifty meters in any direction, curved inward near the top like the inside of a held breath. You need to understand what that means for pressure.

You are a soft, air-filled creature in a world of crushing liquid. Every cavity in your body — your sinuses, your lungs, your middle ears, your gut — is a potential failure point. The water here is warm, which is somewhat merciful, but warmth does not减压 the several atmospheres of pressure bearing down on this sealed, constructed space. The luminaires overhead suggest this place is pressurized to maintain breathable air for humans — it is, after all, built and still partially maintained — but you cannot assume that the air pocket, if one exists, is where you'll be spending your time. The description describes *standing* in water. Warm water. The vegetation hangs in curtains and sways in microcurrents. You are submerged, at least partially, at least sometimes.

**Your first preparation is pressure medicine.** Consult a hyperbaric specialist before you go. Understand nitrogen narcosis thresholds. Understand oxygen toxicity. If you're using any kind of breathing gas mixture to survive at depth, you need to know your MOD — your maximum operating depth — and you need to know it with a margin of error that accounts for the fact that this place was built by an unknown civilization with unknown atmospheric standards. The air they're pumping in may not be Earth-normal. The pressure may not be exactly what your body expects. Bring redundant gas analysis equipment. Trust nothing you haven't measured yourself.

If there is a pressurized air habitat somewhere inside the walls — a dry room, a control station, an airlock — you need to find it and you need to know whether it's accessible. The corridors are narrowing. The vegetation is choking the passages. The maintenance drones are gone. Whatever door you came in through, make absolutely certain you can get back out. Mark your entry point with something visible and chemically persistent — a phosphorescent beacon, a dye marker, something that will survive the biological colonization that this place inflicts on every surface.

## Getting There

You didn't specify your method of transit through the multiverse, so I'll assume you arrive at the nearest plausible coordinates — which, given that this is a constructed platform on the ocean floor of some world, probably means arriving in the ocean itself, at depth, in open water, with Station Nuth somewhere in the dark around you.

This means you need to be a competent deep-water diver *before* you arrive, or you need to arrive inside a submersible. Those are your two options, and the second one is strongly preferable.

**If you arrive by submersible:** You need a craft rated for the depth, obviously, but also for the *geometry* of this place. Station Nuth is small — a dome or cylinder no more than fifty meters across. There are walls. There is vegetation clogging everything. Your submersible needs to be maneuverable in tight spaces, and it needs to be able to dock or at least hold position against a structure that is actively being overgrown. Bring cutting tools — not just for the vegetation (though you'll need those) but for any corroded fixtures or debris that might block your approach. Bring powerful lights; your submersible's standard illumination may not penetrate the murk beyond the immediate vicinity of Station Nuth's maintained lighting grid.

**If you arrive as a diver:** God help you. You need a full deep-water diving rig — closed-circuit rebreather at minimum, probably surface-supplied gas with a umbilical if you can manage it, given that you have no idea how long you'll need to be down there. You need thermal protection. The water is warm — "the warmth of something alive and working, the residual heat of industry" — but warm underwater is not the same as warm above water. Heat loss in water is brutal regardless of temperature. A thick wetsuit at minimum, preferably a drysuit, because you do not want seawater touching your skin for extended periods in an environment where engineered biological organisms are colonizing *every surface*. Think about what they'd colonize on you.

## The Vegetation Problem

This is the core challenge. Station Nuth is being consumed by its own garden, and you are going to be inside that garden. The vegetation is:

- **Everywhere.** There is no bare surface. Density 2.8 out of 3. It covers walls, floors, ceilings, pipes, machinery, everything.
- **Aggressively growing.** You can *watch it happen* in real time. Fronds extend. Rosettes unfurl. Filaments creep outward millimeter by millimeter. This is not slow seasonal growth. This is visible, continuous, relentless biological expansion.
- **Engineered.** These are not natural organisms. They have fractal rosettes that are "too precise, too regular." They have "a botanical intelligence or at least a botanical algorithm." They were designed, and they are expressing themselves in forms that are beautiful and not quite right.
- **Dense enough to impede movement.** Curtains of kelp-analog "broad as bedsheets." Thick mats of filamentous growth that make every step a negotiation. Corridors that are narrowing over time.

**What to wear and bring:**

Your suit — whether diving or surface-supplied — should have an *exterior surface that resists biological adhesion*. This is not a standard feature on most dive gear, and you'll need to improvise. Smooth, non-porous materials with minimal texture. PTFE coatings if you can get them. Silicon-based surface treatments. Anything that makes it harder for those filaments to take hold on you. The moment something starts growing on your suit, you are on a clock, because the growth rate in this place is *visible* and it will not stop.

Bring a **cutting tool** — a dive knife is traditional, but what you actually need is something more substantial. A short, powered cutting implement, perhaps a reciprocating blade or a high-speed rotary cutter, powered by a battery pack that you keep close to your body and shielded from water ingress. The vegetation is dense enough to restrict your movement, and if you get entangled — if those curtains wrap around you, if those filament mats close over your limbs — you need to be able to cut yourself free *quickly*. The growth is fast enough that being stuck for even a few minutes means more material accumulating on you, more weight, more restriction, more difficulty.

Bring **chemical deterrents for the vegetation.** You don't know what the repellent nodes are spraying — those small dense nodes bolted to the pipe runs — but whatever it is, the vegetation respects it. You might be able to replicate the effect with a broad-spectrum herbicide or a biological inhibitor. But be careful: you don't know the biochemistry of these organisms. They're engineered. Earth-standard herbicides might have no effect, or might have catastrophic effects, or might cause the organisms to respond in ways you can't predict. Test cautiously. Have a backup plan. Have several backup plans.

The safest approach is probably to **move fast and move light.** Don't fight the vegetation. Go around it. Find the corridors that are still passable — the ones where the machinery is still running, where the maintenance pathways haven't been completely choked. The pumps are still operating. The filtration systems are still cycling. Those systems need access, which means someone, at some point, maintained pathways to them. Those pathways may still be the most open routes through the station. Follow the pipes. Follow the hum.

## The Sound Problem

Sound here is suppressed, absorbed, smothered. The low continuous tone of the machinery reaches you "more as a pressure in the bones than a sound in the ears." Brief mechanical sounds — "a damp clunk" — vanish almost instantly. You will not be able to use your hearing to navigate the way you normally would. Echoes don't carry. Voices don't project. Calls for help go nowhere.

This has several implications:

**Communication.** If you're traveling with others, you need a non-acoustic communication system. Underwater, the standard solution is a dive communication system using bone conduction or through-water acoustic signaling — but here, acoustic signals are being absorbed. You need something that works despite the damping. Hardline communication through a tether, or electromagnetic signaling (short-range radio, which works poorly in water but might be adequate over the short distances inside Station Nuth), or even a system of visual signals — strobes, colored lights, hand signals with chemically illuminated gloves. Agree on a protocol *before you enter.*

**Navigation.** You won't hear approaching machinery. You won't hear a valve cycling or a pipe contracting until you're close enough to feel it as a vibration in your chest. This means you need to be *visually alert at all times*. Watch for the tremors in the water. Watch for sudden pressure changes that indicate a pump cycling. Watch for temperature boundaries — those "sharp, almost knife-edged" transitions between warm and cool zones that suggest you're crossing between different functional areas of the station.

**Your own awareness.** The absence of sound will be psychologically disorienting. Humans use ambient sound to orient themselves, to assess danger, to feel safe. In Station Nuth, the silence will feel *heavy*, oppressive, wrong. You will feel like you're in a place that is holding its breath. This is because you are. The water is thick, the vegetation is dense, and everything is being muffled. Prepare yourself mentally for a profound, claustrophobic quiet that exists despite the fact that massive industrial machinery is operating all around you. The cognitive dissonance — *this place should be loud, but it isn't* — will get to you if you let it.

## The Light Problem

The light is "normal" — even, diffuse, soft shadows, "the light of a Tuesday afternoon." It comes from overhead luminaires that are still functioning despite being caked with growth. There is no natural light source. This is entirely artificial illumination, maintained by electrical systems you can feel as a "faint electromagnetic buzz."

**Implications for your equipment:** If you carry any electronics, be aware of the electromagnetic environment. The buzzing suggests unshielded or partially shielded electrical systems — industrial-grade, functional, not designed to be clean. Your compass may be unreliable. Your communication equipment may experience interference. Your camera, if you're documenting the visit, may pick up visual artifacts from the frequency of the luminaires (you may need to adjust your shutter speed or frame rate to avoid flicker).

**Implications for your perception:** The light is "slightly too white, slightly too even." This is the light of a place that was designed to be *functional*, not pleasant. It will render the dense green vegetation in sharp, clinical detail. There will be no romantic twilight here, no soft gloaming. You will see every frond, every rosette, every filament in unflinching clarity. This is simultaneously useful (you can see what you're doing) and disturbing (you can see *too well* — you can watch the growth happening).

**Bring a backup light source.** If the luminaires fail — and given the state of the station's maintenance, they will eventually — you need to be able to see. A waterproof torch with a red filter option (to preserve your night vision if the lights go out suddenly), plus chemlights as a last resort.

## The Temperature Problem

The water is warm, but not uniformly. "Warmer in some places, cooler in others, and the boundaries between warm and cool are sharp, almost knife-edged, as though invisible membranes divide the water into zones." These thermal boundaries likely correspond to different functional systems — near a thermal exchanger, it's warmer; near a filtration intake, it's cooler. Crossing between zones means rapid temperature change.

**Thermal protection:** Your drysuit or wetsuit needs to handle a range of temperatures, not just one. A system with adjustable insulation, or multiple layers you can add or remove, is ideal. Rapid thermal transitions are a genuine physiological risk — the shock response to sudden cold or heat in water can cause involuntary gasp reflexes, hyperventilation, and cardiac stress. If you're breathing through a regulator, a gasp reflex means water in your airway. If you're in a closed-circuit rebreather, the thermal stress can affect your CO₂ scrubber efficiency. Pace yourself at thermal boundaries. Recognize them by feel — the sharp change will be unmistakable — and cross them deliberately, controlling your breathing.

## The Absence of Life

This is the detail that should unsettle you most. There are no animals. Not one. Not a fish, not a crustacean, not a worm. In an environment of this biological abundance — this lush, overgrown, impossibly fertile garden — there should be an *ecosystem*. Grazers. Predators. Decomposers. The full web of trophic relationships that any healthy biological system demands.

Instead, there is nothing. The vegetation grows unchecked, and nothing eats it, and nothing lives among it, and the only reason for this is the **repellent nodes** — those small, dense fixtures spraying something into the water that creates zones of exclusion around themselves. The vegetation itself seems to respect these boundaries (it "thins back slightly" near the nodes), which suggests the deterrent is designed to work on the engineered biology specifically.

**Why this matters to you:** Whatever the repellent nodes are spraying, you will be breathing it, absorbing it through your skin, getting it in your eyes. You don't know what it is. You don't know its effects on unaugmented human biology. It could be harmless. It could be a pheromone analog that interferes with your neurochemistry. It could be a chemical that has no short-term effects but accumulates in your tissues.

**Bring water sampling equipment.** Test the water before you spend extended time in it. If you can't test it, assume it's biologically active and protect yourself accordingly — sealed suit, independent air supply, eye protection, decontamination protocols for when you return to your submersible or dry habitat. Wash everything thoroughly. Do not eat or drink anything from this environment. Do not introduce its water into any sealed system you depend on.

The absence of animal life also means the absence of animal *danger* — no predators, no parasites, no biting organisms. This is one of the few things working in your favor. But it also means there's nothing keeping the vegetation in check, which brings us back to the growth problem.

## The Growth Problem — Revisited

Let me be very clear about this: the vegetation is growing at a rate you can *see*. Not with a time-lapse camera. With your naked eyes, in real time. Fronds extend. Rosettes unfurl. Filaments creep. This is the single most important thing to understand about Station Nuth, because it governs everything else.

Your entry point will be different from your exit point, not because you've moved but because **the vegetation will have grown over it.** The corridor you walked down ten minutes ago may already be narrower. The gap you squeezed through may already be tighter. The hatch you opened may already have filaments reaching for its edges, ready to seal it shut when you close it.

**Protocol:**

1. **Always know where you came from.** Never enter a space without a clear, marked path back to your exit point.
2. **Move continuously.** Don't linger. Don't rest in one place for long. The growth will find you, and while it won't consume you in minutes, it will make every minute you stay harder than the last.
3. **Mark your path aggressively.** Use chemlights, reflective tape, dye markers — anything that will remain visible as the vegetation grows over it. But be aware that the vegetation grows *fast*. Within an hour, your markers may be buried. Within a day, they may be invisible. Your stay in Station Nuth should be measured in hours, not days.
4. **Cut only what you must.** Cutting the vegetation may stimulate regrowth — engineered organisms often have wound-response mechanisms that accelerate repair. Test this before you rely on cutting as a primary strategy.
5. **If you stop seeing bare metal, you've waited too long.** The freshly installed components — the new panels, the recent welds — are your landmarks. When they're covered, the vegetation has won that area. Find newer infrastructure. Move toward the places where the machinery is most recently serviced, because those will be the places where growth is most recently cleared.

## The Psychological Dimension

Station Nuth will affect your mind. This is not speculation — it is an inevitability, and you should prepare for it.

**Claustrophobia.** The space is small, bounded, enclosed, and it is *closing in* — literally, physically, as the vegetation narrows the corridors and fills the rooms. The curved walls give everything the feeling of being held inside something alive. You are. The vegetation is the living thing, and it is compressing the space around you, slowly, patiently, with the unhurried insistence of something that has no concept of stopping.

**Isolation.** There are no animals. No people. No movement except the slow sway of kelp-analog in microcurrents that are almost imperceptible. The silence is absolute despite the active machinery. You are inside a place that was built by someone, maintained by someone, and then abandoned — or not abandoned, exactly, but *released*, left to run on its own systems, its own logic, its own botanical algorithm. The absence of human presence is palpable. You are alone in a way that has weight.

**Time distortion.** The growth rate creates a perceptual trap. You will watch things happen that should take months or years happen in front of you. Your sense of time will stretch and warp. You will lose track of how long you've been inside. You will feel like you've been there for hours when only minutes have passed, or vice versa. The description says the place operates on a timescale that makes a human lifespan feel like a vibration. You will feel that. You will feel *small* — not just physically, but temporally, a brief tremor in a process that was happening before you arrived and will continue after you leave.

**The uncanny beauty.** The vegetation is extraordinary. The fractal rosettes are "beautiful and not quite right." The light makes everything look "almost like a garden." You will be drawn to it. You will want to look closely, to touch it, to understand it. This is dangerous — not because the plants are hostile (they aren't; they're just growing, the way they were designed to grow) but because *stopping to admire them means staying longer than you should.*

Maintain your time discipline. Set alarms. Have a hard limit on your exposure and enforce it ruthlessly.

## What to Bring — Final List

- **Breathing apparatus:** Closed-circuit rebreather with redundant gas supply, independent of any station systems. Do not breathe the station's air unless you have no choice, and if you must, test it first.
- **Suit:** Full drysuit with smooth exterior surface, resistant to biological adhesion. Multiple insulation layers for thermal zone transitions. Integrated hood, gloves, boots — no exposed skin.
- **Cutting tools:** Powered reciprocating blade or rotary cutter, waterproof, with backup manual knife.
- **Navigation:** Compass (verified against multiple references before entry, given electromagnetic interference), reel and line with markers, waterproof notepad for mapping.
- **Communication:** Hardline tether or short-range through-water acoustic system, with visual backup (strobes, colored lights).
- **Lighting:** Primary waterproof torch with red filter, plus chemlights as emergency backup.
- **Sampling:** Water chemistry test kit, biological sample containers, sealed against contamination.
- **Documentation:** Waterproof camera with anti-flicker settings, spare batteries kept warm and dry against your body.
- **Decontamination:** Sealed bags for all equipment upon exit, portable UV sterilization if possible, broad-spectrum antimicrobial wash.
- **Time management:** Waterproof watch with alarm, set to enforce your hard time limit. Write your exit time on your forearm with indelible marker. Do not renegotiate it.
- **Psychological anchor:** Something from your world, your life, your sense of normalcy. A photograph, a recording, a small object. Station Nuth will try to dissolve your sense of time and scale. You need something to hold onto that reminds you that you are a person, in a body, with a life outside this warm green drowning place.

## Final Thought

Station Nuth is not hostile. That is the thing you must remember. It was not built to harm you. It was not designed to trap you. It is a machine that became a garden, a garden that was always a machine, and it is doing exactly what it was made to do — growing, processing, maintaining itself, continuing. The danger is in the indifference. The vegetation does not care that you are there. The machinery does not notice you. The warm water holds you with the same patient, omnidirectional weight it applies to everything. You are not a visitor to this place. You are a temporary irregularity in its surface — a brief texture on the water, a momentary displacement, a thing that will be grown over and incorporated and forgotten.

Know that. Respect it. And when your time is up, *leave*. Don't linger for one more look at the fractal rosettes. Don't wait to see if that particular frond will reach the pipe fitting it's been growing toward. Don't tell yourself you'll come back.

The place will still be there when you're gone, doing what it does. Growing. Slowly, slowly, continuing.
