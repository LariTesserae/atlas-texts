# Preparing to Visit The Paralogues

## What You Need to Understand First

You are preparing to visit a place that is finished. Not dormant, not paused — *finished*, the way a sentence is finished when the period has been set. Every other environment you've visited had the decency of ongoing process: things grew, eroded, moved, decayed, became. The Paralogues completed themselves before the concept of completion had a name, and they have been holding that completion like a breath since. Your visit will not be an experience of a place. It will be an experience of *stasis* so total it acquires its own gravity, and that gravity will press on you in ways you are not built for.

The ordinary human multiverse traveler is adapted — neurologically, emotionally, sensorily — to change. You read your environment through motion: the shift of light, the rustle of something moving at the edge of vision, the gradual repositioning of shadows. Your threat-detection systems are keyed to flux. In The Paralogues, that entire apparatus will have nothing to process. It will not power down. It will keep scanning, keep listening, keep parsing the visual field for the micro-shifts that signal safety or danger, and it will find *nothing*, and the absence of input will be more exhausting than any exertion you've ever endured.

This is not a place that will hurt you. It is a place that will *undo your rhythms*.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Silence Will Be the First and Deepest Problem

You have never experienced silence. You think you have, but you haven't. Even the quietest places on your homeworld are noisy — wind, blood in your ears, the hum of your own nervous system. The Paralogues have their own silence, and it is different. It is the silence of ten thousand (a hundred thousand? a million?) creatures who have been holding the same breath for longer than your species has existed. It is a *dense* silence, an *occupied* silence, a silence that has weight and texture and history. It presses against the eardrums. It makes the small sounds of your own body — heartbeat, breath, the creak of cartilage in your neck — seem obscene, intrusive, *loud*.

You need to train for this before you go.

Practice extended isolation in soundproof environments. Not for comfort — you will not get comfortable — but for *recognition*. You need to know what your own body sounds like when there is nothing else to hear, because that is what you will be listening to for the duration of your visit. The wet click of your swallowing. The scrape of your shoes on stone. The rhythm of your breathing, which will sound like a violation in a place where breath is measured in seasons.

More importantly: practice sitting still. Not meditating — stillness. Actual, physical stillness for hours. The creatures do not move. The stone does not move. The light does not move. If you pace, if you shift your weight, if you fidget, you will become the only motion in the entire visible world, and the psychic weight of that distinction will crush you. You will feel like a wound in the landscape, a place where reality is still *happening* instead of having *happened*, and the creatures — those vast, silent, watching presences — will not react, and their non-reaction to your restlessness will be worse than any response could be.

Sit. Be still. Practice being the stillest thing in a room. Then practice being the stillest thing in a room full of people who are also still. Then practice being the stillest thing in a room full of people who are watching you be still. That last one is close.

### The Watching

This requires its own preparation. You are a social primate. You evolved in an environment where being watched by that many organisms meant one of two things: you were prey, or you were the center of something profoundly important. The Paralogues offer neither. The creatures watch because that is what they do. They are not observing you with interest, not tracking you with hunger, not cataloging you with curiosity. They are *settled*, and their settling includes their attention, which is fixed and eternal and aimed at everything and nothing.

You will feel their eyes on you. Millions of them. Every surface — every cliff face, every ridge underside, every crevice and bridge and chasm wall — is packed with them, and they are all looking, and they will never stop looking, and they will never blink.

Some preparation steps:

1. **Study the specific geometry of their eyes.** Before your visit, obtain detailed visual records (if they exist) and sit with them. Spend hours. Let the image of a field of unblinking eyes become *familiar* to you, not comfortable but at least *known*. You need the first moment of confrontation to be recognition rather than shock.

2. **Rehearse the cognitive reframe.** These are not predators. These are not judges. These are organisms that have been sitting in the same position since before your species existed, and they will be sitting there long after. You are a rounding error on their timeline. You are a brief thermal flicker in a stone world. They are not watching *you*; they are watching, and you happen to be in the field. This reframe will help. It will not save you, but it will help.

3. **Accept that you will cry.** Not necessarily from sadness — from the sheer neurological overload of being the only living thing that moves in a world of millions of watchers. The tears are a pressure valve. Let them come. Do not be embarrassed. The creatures will not notice, or rather, they will notice in the way they notice everything, which is not at all.

## Spatial and Navigational Preparation

### The Geometry Is Not Reliable

This is the practical danger, and it is severe.

In The Paralogues, distance is subjective and inconsistent. A ledge that appears forty meters away may be four meters. A chasm that looks crossable may be an abyss. A path that seems to descend may actually ascend when you commit to it, because the fracture healed in the wrong order and space here *works differently* — not randomly, not capriciously, but according to rules you cannot learn in the time you will spend there.

You cannot trust your eyes. You cannot trust your instruments, if you bring them. The wrongness is ontological; it is baked into the substrate of the place, and no measurement you make will be consistent with the next measurement unless you are standing in exactly the same spot and looking in exactly the same direction, and even then the stone has a way of *settling* that redefines "same."

The text tells you the key: **learn to judge by the fauna.**

This is critical. The creatures, being settled into the landscape since before the concept of distance applied, are *anchored* to the geometry in a way you cannot be. Their density, their size, their arrangement — these are consistent, reliable, true. A cliff face that is close will show individual creatures in detail: the texture of their shells, the mineral growth at their joints, the precise angle of a frozen limb. A cliff face that is far will show them as a texture, a mosaic, a pattern. Use this. Train your eye on the fauna before you trust it on the landscape.

**Practical training:**
- Study the visual records obsessively. Learn to read distance from creature-density patterns.
- Practice depth-judgment in environments where visual cues are deliberately misleading — fog, mirrors, forced-perspective installations. You need to be comfortable with the sensation of not knowing how far away something is.
- Develop a method for marking your path that does not involve disturbing the environment. Remember: the equilibrium is absolute. You may not leave markers, cairns, flags. You may not scratch the stone. You may not rearrange anything. Your path must be navigated entirely by memory and by reading the living landscape around you.

### The Chasms

Do not approach the edges. The text describes canyons that curve sideways, upward, and terminate in impossible walls. The visual geometry of these fractures is designed to mislead. Your vestibular system will not process what it sees correctly; you will experience a form of vertigo that is not about height but about *wrongness*, about the fundamental mismatch between what your brain expects from space and what it finds.

If you must cross a fracture — and you may have to, because the landscape is riddled with them and there may be no way around — find the narrowest points and cross quickly. Do not look down. Not because the depth will frighten you, but because looking down will let the wrongness in, and once your spatial processing begins to unravel it is very difficult to reassemble.

**Pack a long, straight, rigid pole.** Something that can bridge a gap and test the solidity of surfaces before you commit your weight to them. The pole will give you a tactile reference that your eyes cannot — a direct, physical measurement of whether a surface is where it appears to be. This is your most important piece of equipment.

### Altitude and Exposure

You are always on something. Ledges, ridges, bridges of stone. There are no flat valleys, no safe lowlands. The entire landscape is altitude, and the wrongness of the space means that falls are not merely dangerous — they are potentially *infinitely* dangerous, because you cannot know how far down is down.

**Harness and rope are non-negotiable.** Anchor to the stone using techniques that minimize surface disturbance — friction anchors wedged into natural fractures, not hammered pitons. The stone will not appreciate being struck. More practically, the stone is older and denser than anything you have ever encountered, and drilling into it or striking it may not be physically possible with equipment you can carry.

**Climbing preparation:** Train on steep, exposed terrain with unreliable footings. You will be scrambling over stone that is not shaped by erosion and therefore does not offer the handholds and footholds that natural weathering creates. The surfaces are smooth from *settling*, from *finishing*, and they are smooth the way a bone is smooth — purposefully, completely, without accommodation for your grip.

## Equipment and Supplies

### What to Bring

**Water.** Enormous quantities. There is none in The Paralogues. No moisture in the air, no precipitation, no dew, no springs. The air is cool and still but bone-dry. You will lose water faster than you expect — the altitude, the dryness, the physical exertion of navigating fractured terrain — and there is no possibility of resupply.

**Food.** Dried, calorie-dense, minimal waste. You will be packing everything out. There is nothing to forage. The almost-total absence of plant life means no gathering, and the fauna is not food — not because it is toxic (you have no information either way) but because *you do not eat the watchers*. You understand this the moment you arrive. The equilibrium is not to be disturbed. This includes your metabolism.

**Light.** The flat, directionless illumination is constant, but it is not bright in the way you need for detailed navigation of fractured terrain. Carry a low-intensity headlamp for close work — reading the texture of surfaces, examining your footing. Do not use it on the creatures. Do not shine light into their faces. They will not react, but *you* will know what you did, and the guilt of disturbing the stillness will be worse than any consequence.

**A compass and a barometric altimeter.** These will be unreliable — the spatial wrongness may affect magnetic fields and air pressure gradients — but they will give you *some* reference, however contradictory, and contradictions in your instruments will at least tell you that you have entered a zone where the geometry is actively distorting, which is information.

**A notebook and pencil.** You will need to write things down. Your memory will struggle in The Paralogues because the absence of change gives your brain nothing to index — no landmarks that appear and disappear, no shifting light, no movement to mark time. The sameness will compress your sense of duration. Hours will feel like minutes or days, and you will not be able to tell which. Write down what you see, when you see it, in enough detail that you can reconstruct your own timeline later.

**Do not bring electronics you cannot afford to lose.** The spatial wrongness may affect circuits, memory, and signal processing in unpredictable ways. Analog equipment only.

**A sealed bag for the tool.** If you find the tool — the shaped piece of bone or bone-shaped stone — you may pick it up, examine it, and set it down *exactly where you found it*. But if you need to handle it closely, use a barrier between your skin and its surface. The text says it is old, but merely very old. A few thousand years. You do not know what a few thousand years of settling in the wrong geometry has done to its material structure, and you do not want to find out through contact.

### What Not to Bring

**No music players, no entertainment devices.** The silence demands total participation. You will not fill it.

**No climbing hardware that requires permanent installation.** No bolts, no permanent anchors. You leave nothing and you change nothing.

**No cameras.** This is controversial, and you may choose to disobey this advice, but I counsel against it. The act of photographing The Paralogues is an act of *capturing* — of taking the settled wrongness and flattening it into a frame, a rectangle, a bounded space. It will not work. The spatial distortions will corrupt every image. But more than that: raising a camera to your eye creates a mediated relationship with the place, and The Paralogues do not want mediation. They want you *in it*, present, uncomfortable, breathing the same still air as the watchers. A camera is a wall between you and the silence, and the silence will notice.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude Acclimatization

The altitude is significant. The text describes mesas and ridgelines at heights that suggest thin air. Arrive early. Spend days acclimatizing at lower elevations before ascending. Bring Diamox or equivalent. Monitor your pulse and oxygen saturation. Altitude sickness in a place where the nearest medical facility is in another universe is a death sentence, and a death sentence in The Paralogues carries a particular horror: your body would become part of the landscape. Another still thing. Another watcher, eventually, or at least another presence in the stone, absorbed into the same ancient equilibrium, your bones settling into the mineral growth alongside the creatures who would have sat above you, silent, unblinking, as you died.

### Temperature Regulation

The air is cool and constant. No weather, no wind, no sun angle to provide warmth. You will need insulation that does not overheat you during exertion but keeps you warm during the long hours of stillness when your body is not generating metabolic heat. Layers. Wool and synthetic blends. No heavy coats that restrict movement — you may need to scramble.

### Nutrition and Hydration Schedule

Set strict timers. You will not feel hungry or thirsty in The Paralogues. The silence and the stillness and the watching will suppress your ordinary bodily signals, and you may not realize you are dehydrated or calorie-depleted until you are already impaired. Eat and drink on schedule, not on sensation.

## The Deepest Preparation: What to Do With Yourself

Here is the truth that no amount of physical preparation addresses.

You are going to a place where nothing happens. Not in the sense of boredom — in the sense of *ontology*. The Paralogues are a place where change has ceased. The creatures do not move. The stone does not erode. The fractures do not grow. The light does not shift. You, a being defined by motion and change and *becoming*, are going to enter a space defined by *having become*, and you will have to exist there, and there will be nothing for you to do.

You will not fix anything. You will not discover anything that the creatures have not already known for longer than your species has existed. You will not be changed by the experience in any way the landscape will notice. You will walk among the watchers, and you will be watched, and when you leave, the only evidence of your visit will be the brief disruption of your footprints on the stone, which will fill with dust and be gone within a season, and the creatures will still be sitting there, in the same positions, breathing the same season-long breaths, watching the same fixed horizon, exactly as they were before you came and exactly as they will be after you are forgotten.

You need to be okay with this before you go.

Not *at peace with it* — that's too much to ask. But *aware* of it, fully and without flinching. You are not the protagonist of The Paralogues. You are not even a minor character. You are a brief, warm disturbance in a stone world that was finished before your birth and will remain finished after your death, and the creatures will never know your name because they do not have the concept of names, and they will never move from their positions to see you off or welcome you back, and the silence will not remember you.

Go anyway.

The silence asks it of you. The ten thousand eyes — the hundred thousand, the million — are watching, and they have been watching since before you were born, and they will watch after you die, and in the brief window of your existence you have the extraordinary, unrepeatable privilege of standing in their field of vision, a single warm mote of motion in their ancient, settled world, and *being seen*.

That is worth the vertigo. That is worth the silence. That is worth the slow, grinding dissolution of every comfortable certainty you have about how space works and time moves and living things behave.

Stand still. Be watched. Let the silence finish you, the way it has finished everything else.

Then leave, exactly as you found it, carrying nothing but the memory of a world that does not need you and never will.
