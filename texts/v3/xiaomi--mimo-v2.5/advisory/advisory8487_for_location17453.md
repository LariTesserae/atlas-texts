# Preparing to Enter the Warrens Beneath Keth Sula

## Before You Even Begin the Descent

The first thing you need to understand is that this is not a cave. You are not visiting a geological formation. You are preparing to enter the body of a living system — one that has been breathing and growing and adapting for longer than most civilizations endure. That distinction changes everything about how you pack, how you think, and how you will hold yourself when you are down there, pressing your palm to a wall that pulses back.

**Mental preparation matters more than you think.** You will be in a place with no sky, no horizon, no open air, and no clear reference to the passage of time. The faint bioluminescent glow has no day-night cycle that corresponds to anything your circadian rhythm recognizes. The warmth is constant. The sound is constant. Your body will lose track of time within hours — possibly less. Travelers who arrive thinking of this as a visit to a *place* tend to do worse than those who understand they are temporarily inhabiting a *condition*. You are not going somewhere. You are becoming, for a brief while, part of a system that does not think of itself as separate from its inhabitants.

Practice this: sit in a warm, enclosed room with a white noise generator set to a low, resonant hum. Close your eyes. Notice how the sound seems to come from inside your chest rather than from outside. That is closer to the baseline experience of the warrens than any visual reference could prepare you for.

## What to Wear

**Forget everything you know about appropriate underground attire.** You will not need thermal layers. You will not need a jacket. You will be descending into a space that maintains a constant, humid warmth — the accumulated body heat of countless insects, the thermal mass of deep clay, and the exhalations of an entire living architecture. Think of it as the inside of a body, because that is what it is.

**Clothing should be minimal, breathable, and made of natural fibers.** A loose-fitting linen or thin cotton tunic and trousers, nothing more. Synthetics will trap moisture against your skin and become a breeding ground for whatever microorganisms live in that air — and there are many. Natural fibers breathe, wick, and dry more quickly when you emerge. Wear them loose. The passages are tight, the ceilings low, and you will be pressing yourself against walls and squeezing through gaps where a stiff or bulky garment would catch and tear — or, worse, damage the plaster and the living tissue embedded in it.

**Footwear should be soft-soled and minimal.** Thin leather or woven fiber shoes that let you feel the ground beneath you. This is not only practical — the fired-clay floors are uneven, and feeling their contours helps you move without stumbling — it is respectful. Heavy boots would damage the floors and, more importantly, would transmit the wrong vibration into the structure. The walls *pulse*. The insects *respond to vibration*. You want your footsteps to be quiet, absorbed, human-weight and human-tempo, not the staccato assault of rigid soles.

**Do not wear perfume, cologne, or any strongly scented product.** The air in the warrens is thick with biological information — the scent of clay, of insect musk, of fermentation, of mineral water. The inhabitants navigate this space partly by smell, and so do the lizards, and so do the insects. An unfamiliar chemical scent will announce you as alien in a way that your physical appearance alone does not. You will be noticed. Whether that matters depends on whether you have been invited, but even if you have, causing unnecessary disruption to the sensory environment is a form of rudeness you should avoid.

**Remove all jewelry except perhaps a single small token of identification or affiliation,** if one has been provided by whoever facilitated your visit. Rings can catch on the rough plaster. Necklaces can snag on low arches. Bracelets create noise — the clink of metal on fired brick is loud and alien in a space where the loudest sounds are organic.

## What to Carry

**Very little.** This is not a place where you bring a pack. The passages are narrow, the ceilings low, and every object you introduce into the warrens is something that must not damage the living surfaces or disrupt the ecosystem.

- **A small, soft-sided pouch at your belt,** not a backpack. Carry it on your front or hip, not your back, so you can feel it and control it in tight spaces.

- **Water, in a collapsible soft container.** You will be warm and humid and you will sweat. Dehydration is a real risk in a space where the air itself seems to breathe moisture but your body cannot absorb it through skin efficiently enough to compensate. However — and this is important — **do not drink from the pools.** Not without permission and not without understanding the offering protocol. The water in the pools is part of the ecosystem. It feeds the translucent organisms. It is not your water. Carry your own.

- **A small amount of food.** Simple, dry, not strongly flavored. Hardtack, dried fruit, something that will not crumble and scatter. You may be offered food by the inhabitants — accept it, but do not expect to be offered food immediately, or at all. More importantly, you may need to make an offering at one of the pools, and a small scrap of food is the most universal and least disruptive gesture. A pinch of grain, a dried berry, something that will dissolve or be consumed by the pool's organisms.

- **A single small candle or oil lamp, sealed and unused.** Do not light it unless explicitly directed to. It is an emergency item, a respectful gesture to the principle that you acknowledge you are in darkness and choose not to impose your own light. In practice, the bioluminescent glow will be sufficient, and introducing an open flame into a space with suspended organic dust, insect clusters, and volatile biological compounds is genuinely dangerous. But carrying one and keeping it put away says: *I know where I am, and I am not afraid, but I am prepared.*

- **Nothing else.** No tools, no weapons, no recording devices, no maps — you would not be able to read one in the ambient light, and the warrens change slowly over time, passages shifting as the plaster is renewed and the insect colonies reshape their territories. You will be guided, or you will find your way by instinct and by the logic of the space itself. That is how the inhabitants do it. That is how you must, too.

- **If you carry a blade of any kind, leave it behind.** It is the one object that will make you genuinely unwelcome. The warrens are old. They are built of soft material. They are *alive.* A blade is violence against everything this place is.

## Physical Preparation

**Breathing exercises.** The air is thick, warm, and mineral-heavy. It is not toxic — if it were, nothing could live there — but it is *different.* The oxygen content is adequate but not abundant. The air has weight to it. You will notice it in your lungs within the first few minutes: a sense of fullness, of having to work slightly harder to breathe. This is partly physical and partly psychological — your body knows it is underground, knows there is no wind, and tenses accordingly.

Practice slow, deep breathing before you descend. In through the nose, four counts. Hold, four counts. Out through the mouth, six counts. The goal is to habituate yourself to the feeling of breathing that requires intention. Down there, shallow panicked breathing will exhaust you and make you lightheaded. Calm breathing will let you acclimatize within twenty minutes or so, and after that, you will barely notice the air at all.

**Spatial comfort.** If you are claustrophobic, this is going to be difficult. Not impossible — the warrens are not uniformly tight, and there are those vaulted dome-chambers where you can stand upright and feel something like space — but the default condition is enclosure. Low ceilings. Narrow passages. Walls close enough to touch on both sides simultaneously. You will, at some point, need to crawl.

If you have not tested your response to confinement, do so before you travel. Find a narrow hallway, a small closet, a space where you can close a door and stand in total darkness. Stay for five minutes. Notice what happens in your body. Notice the moment your mind begins to tell you that the walls are moving inward. That moment is normal. The thing to practice is *not obeying that signal.* The walls are not moving. They have not moved in centuries. Your ancestors built them, and their ancestors maintained them, and the clay remembers every hand that has touched it. You are safe inside something ancient and patient, and it will not close around you.

**Agility and flexibility.** You will need to squat, crouch, squeeze sideways through passages that are narrower than your shoulders, and climb short stairways carved into clay that may be slick with moisture. Stretch your hips, your shoulders, your spine. Practice moving slowly through small spaces without bracing your hands against the walls — not because you cannot touch them, but because you should touch them *deliberately,* not out of panicked reflex.

## The Etiquette of Touch

This is important enough to have its own section.

**You will want to touch everything.** The walls are warm. They pulse. The plaster is organic and strange under your fingers. The surfaces call to you the way any living thing calls to a hand that wants to understand it. But not everything invites your touch, and not every surface should be touched with the same intention.

**Do touch the walls in general passages** — gently, with an open palm, as you move through. This is how the inhabitants navigate, and it is how you will learn the space. The walls carry information: temperature gradients that indicate nearby pools or insect colonies, textural variations that signal intersections or niches, that slow pulse that tells you about the rhythm of the place. Put your hand out. Let it trail along the plaster. You will learn to read what you feel.

**Do not touch the insect clusters.** Not with your hands. Not at all. They are the foundation of this entire system — literally; their ground bodies are in the plaster that forms the walls. They are also, in some sense, a collective organism with responses that are not fully predictable to an outsider. A cluster disturbed will scatter, and scattered insects are panicked insects, and panicked insects in a space this enclosed will swarm, and a swarm of thumb-sized insects is not dangerous in any immediately lethal sense but is *deeply distressing* and will mark you as a careless intruder in the memory of this place for a long time. The clusters pulse with their own greenish light. Watch them. Admire them. Keep your hands to yourself.

**Do not touch the lizards.** They will move away from you if you move slowly and predictably. If you move quickly or erratically, they will move away from you *all at once,* in a coordinated rush that will be startling and loud and that will send vibrations through the walls that every other living thing in the warrens will register. Move like water. Move like something that has always been here.

**Do touch the handholds.** When you find a carved depression in the wall at shoulder height — and you will — grip it, briefly, before you move on. This is not a practical gesture. It is a communion. Every handhold was carved by a specific person, shaped to the grip of a specific hand, worn smooth by the grips of every hand that came after. When you hold it, you are holding something that ten thousand people have held before you. The warmth you feel is not just clay. Let that settle into you.

## The Descent Itself

The shaft is the threshold. Take your time.

It is narrow. You will be climbing down with your palms and the soles of your feet against packed clay, lowering yourself inch by inch. The walls are smooth — smooth from ten thousand bodies making this same descent, smooth in a way that stone never becomes, because clay remembers and yields and is renewed. You will feel the warmth before anything else. Then the moisture. Then the smell: wet clay, musk, fermentation, minerals, and underneath it all, the living breath of an enclosed world.

**Descend slowly.** Not because it is dangerous — though a fall from even a moderate height onto hard-packed clay is no joke — but because the descent is the first act of entering the system, and how you do it sets the tone for everything that follows. Every hand that has descended this shaft has been one more layer of smoothness on its walls. Every body that has pressed against its clay has added its warmth to the accumulated warmth. You are joining a process that has been ongoing for generations, and rushing it would be like interrupting a conversation mid-sentence.

**Breathe as you descend.** The air changes as you go deeper. It gets warmer, thicker, more alive. Your nose will adapt to the smells in stages: first the clay, then the musk, then the faintly sweet fermentation, then something else, something mineral and ancient that you cannot name and that will become, for the rest of your life, the smell you associate with the deep earth.

When the shaft opens and the warrens unfold around you — and it will feel like an unfolding, like the world gently opening its hands — stop. Stand in the entrance. Let your eyes adjust. This takes longer than you expect, because there is no single light source to focus on. The glow is everywhere and nowhere, emanating from the surfaces themselves, so gradual and diffuse that your pupils cannot find an anchor. Give yourself five minutes. Ten if you need them. Move your eyes slowly. Look at the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the arches. Let the space reveal itself to your vision the way it reveals itself to every sense: slowly, in layers, one impression at a time.

You will begin to see the greenish flicker of the insect clusters first — your eyes are drawn to the movement. Then the warm amber of the plaster will resolve. Then the details: the finger-whorls on the columns, the dried membranes, the niches with their offerings and tools, the pools with their faintly cloudy water.

When you can see enough to move without stumbling, move.

## Moving Through the Warrens

**Move slowly. This cannot be overstated.** Speed is wrong here in every conceivable way. It damages the surfaces. It startles the creatures. It creates vibrations that propagate through the clay in ways you cannot predict. And it misses the entire point of being here. The warrens are experienced at the speed of hands on clay, of insects breathing, of water seeping one molecule at a time. If you move at the speed of the surface world — at the speed of walking, of purpose, of destinations — you will see a network of tunnels and miss the living body entirely.

**Move with your hands.** Not just your feet. Let one hand trail the wall. Let the other hang free, ready to steady yourself on a column or a low arch. The warrens were built for bodies that are in constant contact with their surfaces. When you walk in the open air, most of your body is surrounded by nothing. Here, most of your body is surrounded by *something* — wall, ceiling, the curve of a passage — and the sooner you stop fighting that and start using it to navigate, the sooner you will stop feeling trapped and start feeling held.

**Follow the wide passages.** They are the main arteries, worn smooth by traffic, and they will lead you to the gathering spaces, the communal areas, the places where interaction is expected. Narrow passages that branch off to the sides are private, or functional, or simply dead ends designed to confuse. Unless you are specifically invited into a narrow passage by a guide, stay on the worn paths.

**When you reach an intersection — and you will, constantly, because the warrens branch and fold and layer in ways that defeat simple mapping — stop and listen.** Sound carries differently in different passages. Some are alive with insect hum and lizard clicks. Others are nearly silent, which means they are either abandoned or they lead to a place where the system is resting, regenerating, being renewed. Follow the sound. Follow the life. That is how the inhabitants navigate, and it is how you should, too.

## Responding to the Inhabitants

**You may not see them.** This is important to accept in advance. The warrens are their home, and they have had generations to learn how to move through it without being seen. You are the stranger. You are loud, slow, alien, and you glow in the infrared range of their vision with a heat signature that is probably alarming. They will know you are here from the moment you entered the shaft. They may choose not to show themselves.

**If they do show themselves, do not reach for them.** Do not speak unless spoken to, and when you speak, speak softly. The warrens are loud with organic sound but quiet in the human register. Your voice will carry far and sound strange, and it will be the most alien thing about you. If you are offered food, accept it with both hands and eat it. If you are offered water from a vessel — not from a pool — accept it and drink it. These are gestures of trust, and refusing them is a declaration that you are not here in good faith.

**If you are led somewhere, follow without hesitation.** The passages may narrow. You may be asked to crawl. You may be led to a space so small that you cannot stand, cannot turn, can barely breathe without your chest touching the ceiling. This is not punishment or cruelty. This is how they live. This is how the warrens work. The small spaces are warm and safe and close to the walls, and the walls pulse, and if you have been breathing slowly and moving calmly, you will find that the closeness is not terrifying but *intimate.* You are being shown something. Accept it.

**If you encounter a pool and are gestured toward it, kneel.** Look at the water. Look at the tiny translucent things moving in the silt. This is one of the sacred points of the system — where water meets earth meets life. If you are told to make an offering, do so. A pinch of food. A drop of sweat from your brow — this, apparently, is acceptable and even preferred, because it is yours, it is human, it is a piece of yourself given freely. If you are told to drink, cup the water in your hands and drink. It will be warm and faintly mineral and unlike any water you have ever tasted, and it will connect you to this place in a way that no amount of looking or touching or listening can match.

## Managing Your Senses

**Sound.** The insect hum is constant. It is in your ears, in your chest, in your bones. After a while, it recedes from conscious perception the way the hum of a refrigerator recedes in your kitchen — it becomes the background, the baseline, the silence against which all other sounds are measured. When it does, you will begin to hear the real sounds of the warrens: the click of lizard-claws, the soft whisper of plaster shifting microscopically as humidity changes, the almost subliminal sound of water moving through channels too small to see. These are the sounds of a living body going about its metabolic business. Listen to them. They are the voice of this place.

**Light.** Your eyes will fully adapt in about thirty minutes. The world will resolve into an amber-brown dreamscape, everything soft-edged and warm, shadows more felt than seen. The greenish pulse of the insect clusters will become the closest thing to a clock — you will begin to notice its rhythm, a slow oscillation that takes several minutes to complete a cycle. When the glow brightens slightly, something is feeding. When it dims, something is resting. You will not need to understand this intellectually. Your body will learn it.

**Smell.** You will stop noticing the smells within an hour, and then, if you leave the warrens, you will spend the rest of the day trying to find a way to describe to someone else what the air tasted like. You will fail. No language has adequate words for the smell of a living underground architecture. Close your eyes and learn it now, while you are there. Store it somewhere deep. It is the smell of a world that has never seen the sky and does not know it is missing anything.

**Touch.** This is your most important sense in the warrens. More than your eyes, more than your ears, more than your nose, your hands will tell you what this place is. The warmth. The pulse. The texture of plaster embedded with insect bodies. The slickness of a handhold worn smooth by centuries of use. The shock of cool water from a pool, and the way it is instantly warmed by your skin and by the ambient heat. The unexpected softness of a membrane-wrapped column, give under your fingers like skin over muscle. This is a place that wants to be touched, that was built to be touched, that communicates most honestly through the contact of living hands with living walls. Touch everything you are permitted to touch. Touch it slowly. Let it speak to you through your fingertips.

## What to Expect Emotionally

**You will feel enclosed.** This is unavoidable. The ceilings are low, the walls are close, and there is no sky. For some people this is immediately oppressive. For others it is immediately comforting, like being held. Most people cycle between the two. The trick is to notice the feeling without obeying it. The enclosure is not a trap. It is a womb. You are inside something that was built to hold life, and it is holding you, and it has held countless others before you, and it will hold countless more after.

**You may feel watched.** You are. The inhabitants are aware of you from before you arrived. They are assessing you, the way any organism assesses a foreign presence in its body. This is not hostile. It is immune response. Move calmly, respect the rules, and the warrens will accept you. Do not mistake their caution for aggression or their silence for indifference.

**You may feel a deep, irrational sense of peace.** The warmth, the enclosure, the hum, the pulse — these are the conditions of the womb, and your body remembers them at a level below thought. You may find yourself breathing slowly without trying, moving slowly without trying, pressing your hands against the walls not because you are told to but because your body wants contact with this place. Let it happen. This is not weakness or surrender. This is recognition. Your body knows what a living shelter feels like, even if your mind has forgotten.

**You may feel grief.** This is less predictable and harder to explain, but it happens often enough that those who facilitate visits to places like the warrens consider it normal. The grief is for the surface world, for the sky, for the open air, for the cold and the wind and the rain and the sun. For a moment — it may last only a moment, or it may last as long as you are below — you will understand viscerally what it means for a world to be complete, self-sustaining, *enough.* The warrens do not need the sky. The warrens do not miss the sun. The warrens are whole. And in the face of that wholeness, the open world above suddenly seems not free but *exposed,* not generous but *scattered,* not alive but merely *busy.* This feeling will pass. But it will change the way you think about architecture, about shelter, about what it means to live inside something.

## When You Leave

**Climb the shaft slowly.** The same way you descended. Hands and feet on the smooth clay, one step at a time. You will feel the change in air before you see the light — it will thin, cool, sharpen. Your lungs will rebel slightly, suddenly desperate for open wind, and you will want to gasp and climb quickly, but don't. Take your time. Give your body the gradual transition. The warrens are a body, and you are leaving it, and the exit should be as deliberate and as gentle as the entry.

**At the surface, sit.** Sit on the ground and breathe. Let your eyes re-adapt to open light, which will seem impossibly harsh and flat and *dead* compared to the warm glow below. Notice the wind on your skin and understand, for the first time, that wind is what happens when shelter is absent — that air in motion is not freedom but *exposure.* Notice how wide the world is, how empty, how loud in a different way — the wrong way, the scattered way, the way that has no center and no pulse.

**Do not try to describe it immediately.** Give yourself a day, maybe two. The words will come eventually, but they will come wrong, incomplete, filtered through a language that has no vocabulary for living walls and pulsing clay and the smell of a world that breathes. Write what you can. Accept that the real experience lives in your hands and your sternum and the memory of a green pulse in your peripheral vision, and that no sentence you construct will hold more than a fraction of it.

**Drink water — your own water, from your own container.** You will need it more than you think.

**And then, eventually, you will touch a wall.** Some wall, somewhere, in the surface world. A house, a building, a monument. And it will be cold, and it will not pulse, and you will feel the absence of life in it like a missing heartbeat, and you will understand, in a way you did not before, the difference between a structure that shelters and a structure that *lives.*

That is what the warrens will give you. That is what you should be prepared to receive.
