# Preparing for the Hollow Works

## The Core Problem: Heat That Wears You Down

The single most dangerous thing about the Hollow Works is the heat, and it's not the number on a thermometer that will get you — it's the humidity. This is wet heat, the kind that sits on your skin like a second garment and refuses to leave. Your sweat has nowhere to evaporate because the air is already saturated. Your body's primary cooling mechanism is, functionally, disabled. The temperature may not read as lethal on a device, but your core will climb steadily, your cognition will dull, and if you push yourself physically — say, hauling yourself over rubble or scrambling up a talus slope — you will hit heat exhaustion faster than you expect and recover far slower than you're used to.

This means preparation starts with what you put against your skin.

### Base Layers

Do not wear cotton. Cotton absorbs moisture, holds it against your body, becomes heavy, and offers zero thermoregulation once saturated — which will take less than ten minutes down there. You want synthetic moisture-wicking base layers: polyester or polypropylene blends designed to move perspiration away from skin. Merino wool is acceptable if the weave is light; it manages moisture better than people think and has natural antimicrobial properties, which matters in a place thick with fungal spores and bacterial life.

Keep the base layers light-colored. You will be working in dim, warm light, and dark fabrics absorb what little radiant heat exists. More practically, light fabric makes it easier to spot bat guano, mineral staining, or chemical splashes before they've bonded with the weave.

### Over-Layers

You need coverage. Full arm, full leg. The reasons are not comfort — they're defense. The beetles are large and armored and move without fear; the spiders build three-dimensional webs dense enough to catch your arm; the mineral seep is iron- and sulphur-rich and will irritate exposed skin with prolonged contact; and the bat guano, besides being everywhere, carries fungal spores you do not want to inhale in concentrated quantity or smear across a cut. But coverage must be breathable. Lightweight ripstop nylon or canvas, loose-fitting to allow air circulation against the base layer, with articulated joints so you're not fighting your clothing with every movement. Think of the long-sleeve shirts and trousers worn by people who work in tropical greenhouses — that's closer to the right balance than anything marketed as "tactical."

Roll-up capability in the sleeves is useful. If you find a ventilation shaft or a cooler passage, you'll want the option.

### Footwear

This is non-negotiable: waterproof boots with aggressive tread and ankle support. The floor is a treacherous mixture of packed earth, exposed bedrock, mineral-slick stone, and standing pools of sulphurous water. The water in those pools is not dangerous in small contact, but it's mildly acidic, and if your boots aren't sealed, it will seep through and start chewing at whatever it touches. Steel-toe is worth the weight penalty — the machinery is heavy, the surfaces are uneven, and dropping something on your foot in a place hours from medical help is the kind of mistake that ends trips early.

The tread matters because the rock surfaces, wet with mineral deposit and condensation, are essentially ice rinks coated in fine grit. Vibram soles or similar compound designed for wet rock. Test them before you go.

Gaiters are not a bad idea. They keep the beetle traffic off your lower legs and seal the gap between boot and trouser where mineral water tends to wick upward.

## Breathing the Air

You will smell the Hollow Works before you adjust to it: wet stone, hot metal, sulphur, animal musk, the sweet rot of fermentation. Your nose will try to tell you something is wrong. Your eyes may water. This is normal for the first several hours.

What is not normal — and what you must guard against — is the airborne hazard profile:

- **Bat guano dust**: Histoplasma capsulatum thrives in accumulated bat droppings, and the Hollow Works has guano deposits layered like geological strata on every ledge and shelf. Disturbance of these deposits releases spores. Workers here have presumably developed some degree of tolerance, or they have simply accepted the respiratory consequences as cost of doing business. You have not. Carry an N95 respirator minimum. If you can source P100 filters, better. You will not need to wear it constantly — but when you pass beneath a roost, when a colony takes flight and stirs the upper air, when you're near the accumulated deposits on the rubble slope at the collapsed end, put it on without hesitation.

- **Sulphur compounds**: The mineral seep carries hydrogen sulfide in low concentrations. At the levels present in the Hollow Works, this won't kill you, but prolonged exposure will cause headaches, nausea, and a general cognitive fog that blends dangerously with the heat-induced fatigue you're already managing. If you start smelling rotten eggs so consistently that you stop noticing it — that's the danger point, because it means your olfactory receptors are saturating and you've lost your only natural early warning system. Take breaks in better-ventilated passages if you can find them. The workers seem to manage, but they are adapted. You are not.

- **Processing chemicals**: Somewhere behind those stone partitions, the extraction industry uses compounds to process what it pulls from the rock. The sharpness is noticeable but not identifiable without analysis. Avoid standing downwind — or whatever passes for it in this air circulation — of any active processing station. If your eyes begin to sting or your throat tightens near a work area, move away.

- **Fungal spores**: The mushrooms are sparse but present, and the fermentation process in the deeper crevices produces volatile organic compounds. In an enclosed underground space with minimal air exchange, these accumulate. You may feel a gentle euphoria or light-headedness that you might mistake for heat fatigue. If you notice it, find fresher air. It's not enough to incapacitate you, but it will impair judgment in a place where a wrong step on uneven ground means a broken ankle in a cave that doesn't care.

## Light

The existing lanterns are insufficient. They burn thick, viscous fuel and produce a warm, yellowish glow that reaches a few meters and then gives up. The workers navigate by habit and long adaptation. You do not have that.

Carry a headlamp. Use the red or amber filter setting if available — bright white light will be disorienting in a dark-adapted environment and will startle the bat colonies, which you do not want to do repeatedly. A secondary handheld light, LED with adjustable brightness, is worth the weight. If you can find a low-lumen warm-white setting, use it; it will preserve your peripheral night vision while giving you enough to read the terrain.

Do not bring a powerful flashlight and do not bring one at all if you're planning to spend extended time with the workers. There is a social dimension to light here. The lanterns are the ambient norm. Flooding the space with electric light will make you a beacon, will disturb the fauna, and will mark you as an outsider faster than anything you wear or say. Adapt to the ambient light. Let your eyes adjust. It takes thirty minutes to an hour and the adjustment will feel incomplete, but the Hollow Works does not reward haste.

## Hydration

You will lose water rapidly and you may not realize how rapidly because the sweat doesn't evaporate — it just accumulates. You won't feel as thirsty as you should because the air is so wet your body is confused about its fluid status.

Carry more water than you think you need. For a full day in the Hollow Works, plan a minimum of four liters per person, and consider that optimistic if you're doing any physical activity. Electrolyte supplements are essential — the sweat losses will deplete sodium, potassium, and magnesium faster than plain water replaces them. Tablets or powder dissolved into your supply.

Do not drink any water found in the Hollow Works. The mineral pools carry dissolved iron, sulphur compounds, and whatever the processing operations are leaching into the local water table. The slow drip from the ceiling is marginally cleaner but you have no way to verify what it's passed through on the way down, and "marginally cleaner" in a space thick with guano, industrial chemicals, and fungal growth is not a meaningful standard. Bring your own. Cache it if you're staying.

## Navigating the Terrain

The pathways are "agreed upon" rather than constructed. They follow the path of least resistance across uneven ground — half-packed earth, half-bedrock shelves, sudden steps, slick mineral surfaces. The workers know them by muscle memory. You do not.

Move slowly. This is not a place where your normal walking pace is safe. The ceiling is low — four meters at best, less where stalactites cluster — and the floor is a minefield of elevation changes that the dim light disguises. Every step should be deliberate. Watch for:

- **Rock shelves with thin mineral coatings** that are functionally frictionless when wet.
- **Conveyor mechanisms** that extend into the pathways at low height — duck or you'll take a belt-mounted load of crushed stone to the face.
- **Beetles the length of your finger** that move across the walking surface with absolute confidence in their right to be there. They won't hurt you, but stepping on one produces a chitinous crack and a spray of... something you'd rather not have on your boots.
- **Spider webs in the transition zones** between shelters. They're three-dimensional tangles, not flat webs, and you can walk into one without seeing it. Check yourself periodically — the spiders themselves are large enough to be felt if they land on you, and they are not aggressive, but discovering one on your shoulder by surprise is an experience that will spike your adrenaline in a place where calm efficiency is keeping you safe.

The rubble slope at the collapsed end of the chamber is tempting — it's the most obviously geological feature, the most dramatic expression of the place's constant self-reconstruction. But the rock is loose, the footing is treacherous, and above it, the ceiling is unsupported and visibly fractured. If you want to examine the mineral growth on the rubble, approach from the side and stay low.

## Protecting Your Skin

Beyond the clothing layers:

- **No exposed cuts or abrasions.** The mineral water is mildly corrosive and carries bacteria and fungal spores. If you have open wounds before entering, cover them with waterproof dressings. If you acquire cuts inside — and the sharp rock edges will find you — clean them immediately and seal them. The fermentation environment means that any wound infection will develop fast and in a place where antibiotics are likely not available.

- **Antifungal powder in boots and gloves.** The humidity guarantees fungal growth on any surface that stays damp, which is every surface. Your boots will be damp inside within an hour. Powder helps.

- **No jewelry, no watches with metal bands.** The mineral-laden moisture will corrode metal against skin and leave chemical burns that look worse than they are but feel exactly as bad as they look. The workers have learned this. You should learn it before arriving.

## Psychologically

This is the part people don't plan for and it's the part that matters more than gear.

The Hollow Works is oppressive. Not because it's dangerous — it's less overtly dangerous than many places you could visit — but because it is *relentless*. The heat doesn't let up. The humidity doesn't let up. The sounds don't let up — the grinding, the dripping, the breathing of ten thousand bats, the groan of the hydraulic press, the hum of conveyors, all of it reverberating in a low-ceilinged chamber that traps and recycles every vibration. The light never resolves into anything comfortable. The air never clears. Every surface is damp. Every surface is alive with something — mineral growth, beetle trails, spider constructions, fungal colonies, bat guano layered over bat guano.

And then there is the sense that the place is *changing*. Slowly, in ways that don't concern you, the chamber is being remade. The water is still carving. The walls are still settling. The collapse at the far end is still opening. You are standing in a process that was underway before humans arrived and will continue after they leave, and the process does not notice you. The workers have made their peace with this — their slow rhythms, their resignation, their refusal to be startled — and if you spend time among them you may begin to feel it too: the particular calm of people who have decided that the pace of the place is the pace they will keep.

If you are claustrophobic, this will test you. The low ceiling is constant, and while the chamber is wide, you will always be aware that the stone is *there*, above you, still settling, still deciding where to be. If you need open sky to function, plan short visits. If you can adapt to enclosure, the Hollow Works rewards patience — the longer you stay, the more the place reveals. The mineral growth patterns on the rubble. The way the bat colonies organize by family group. The specific sound signature of each machine and the way the workers' movements synchronize with it without conscious effort. The mushrooms, pale and reaching, almost invisible until you learn to look for them.

Plan your first visit for no more than four to six hours. Acclimatize to the heat, the humidity, the light, the air. Let your body learn the pace. If you can stay calm and move slowly and breathe steadily, the Hollow Works will let you in. It doesn't welcome — it doesn't operate at the scale of welcomes — but it will permit you to observe, and observation in this place is worth the discomfort.

---

One final note: the workers will watch you arrive. They won't stare — they've adapted to a slower, more deliberate mode of attention — but they will note you. How you move, how quickly you breathe, whether you flinch when a bat-ribbon pours overhead, whether you step over or around the beetles. They have their own economy of judgment. Show them you're willing to adapt to the place rather than forcing the place to accommodate you, and they'll leave you alone to observe. Startle their bats, crush their beetles, or move too fast through their space, and you'll learn what resignation looks like when it hardens into something less forgiving.

The Hollow Works is not hostile. It is simply indifferent, and indifference at geological scale is the most thorough form of danger there is. Prepare accordingly.
