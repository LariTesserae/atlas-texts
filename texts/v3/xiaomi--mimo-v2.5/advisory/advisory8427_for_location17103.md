# Preparing to Visit The Hollow Beneath the Thornroot

---

## The First Problem: You Cannot Bring Fire

This changes everything. Every civilization you've ever known — every one across every world you've stepped into — has organized itself around the controlled flame. Cooking, warmth, light, safety, the psychological comfort of a glowing center. Strip all of that away and you begin to understand what you're walking into. You will arrive in the dark, you will stay in the dark, and you will be cold the entire time. If you bring a flame, you don't just risk a fire — you risk *the only world these people have*. The root bark is dry enough to smolder. Smoke in an enclosed root cavity has nowhere to go. You could kill everyone in the settlement by trying to warm your hands. Understand this first, hold it as an absolute, and then begin your preparations in earnest.

---

## Cold: The Patient Enemy

The temperature inside the hollow is the temperature of deep groundwater — probably between 4 and 8 degrees Celsius, maybe less, and it never rises. The air is saturated with moisture. This is not the dry cold of a mountain pass or the biting cold of a wind-whipped plain. This is a wet, invasive, intimate cold that works through fabric and into joints and settles into the marrow with a kind of slow patience. The people who live here wear layered wrappings of processed thornroot fiber — a felted plant material that traps air against the skin. It's not particularly warm by your standards. It's *adequate* for bodies that have spent lifetimes adapting, and even then, the people are described as wrapping themselves constantly. They don't acclimate to the cold. They *endure* it, every hour of every day, and their bodies have simply made their peace with a low, persistent discomfort that a visitor from the outside would find grinding and demoralizing.

**What to bring:** You need layered natural-fiber insulation. Wool is your best bet — it retains warmth even when damp, which it will be, constantly, because everything in the hollow is wet. Avoid cotton entirely; it absorbs moisture and becomes a cold conduit against your skin. Multiple thin layers will serve you better than one thick layer, because trapped air between layers is where warmth lives. You need a hood that covers your head and neck without restricting your peripheral hearing (which will become critical — more on that below). Your hands need covering, but the gloves must allow full tactile sensitivity in your fingertips. This is not optional. These people communicate through touch. If your hands are numb and clumsy inside thick gloves, you are not merely inarticulate — you are *mute*, and in a world where voice is already nearly taboo, being unable to touch is a kind of exile.

A hat is not a luxury. A enormous amount of body heat is lost through the head, and the cold in the hollow presses against you from above, below, and every side simultaneously. There is no "warm side" to turn toward. Heat loss is omnidirectional and constant.

**Critical consideration:** Your fabric must be *quiet*. Every material makes some sound when it moves — synthetic fabrics rustle and hiss, leather creaks, even certain weaves of cotton produce a faint friction-sound when your limbs move against each other. In the hollow, where the dominant acoustic environment is near-total silence punctuated only by the wet clicking of millipedes and the occasional drip of condensed water, a rustling jacket is a *scream*. You will need to test every garment you plan to wear by moving in absolute silence and listening for any sound your clothing produces. Felted wool is likely the quietest option. If you can acquire a wrapping of actual processed thornroot fiber before you arrive, do so — it will not only be quieter but will carry less of the foreign scent that might unsettle both the people and the fauna.

## Darkness: Unlearning Sight

This will be the hardest thing.

You are a creature built for light. Your eyes are your primary sense organ. You navigate, you communicate, you assess threat and safety and beauty through vision. In the hollow, vision is almost entirely eliminated. The darkness is not the soft darkness of a moonless night or the filtered darkness of a deep cave with distant openings. It is *complete* — a sealed, root-bound chamber with no connection to the sky, no bioluminescence, no ambient glow of any kind. The people who live here have spent years — in some cases, their entire lives — adapting to this. Their other senses have sharpened. Their hearing, despite the quiet, is attuned to changes in pressure, to the faintest wet sounds. Their hands read the world: the texture of root bark, the angle of a passage, the subtle vibration that tells them a new root tendril is pushing through a wall. They have a spatial intelligence built on touch and proprioception and a mental map that updates continuously with every shift of the living architecture.

You will have none of this. You will be functionally blind. And the psychological impact of this cannot be overstated.

**If you bring a light source** — and you should, for the journey down and for emergencies — you must understand what it will show you. When you illuminate the root walls, you will see the fauna. Thousands of organisms per cubic foot. Millipedes with their translucent, gut-visible bodies moving in slow caravans through root grooves. Beetles packed into crevices, their wet carapaces gleaming. Worms threading through gaps. Mites so numerous they coat surfaces like a living dust. Springtails that shift and ripple in response to the light itself. It will be, for most humans, viscerally disturbing — not because any of it is dangerous (the fauna bites, stings, and poisons have all been selected against), but because your primate brain is wired to interpret "insects on every surface" as contamination, danger, wrongness. You will want to recoil. You will want to brush them off your skin. You will want to *leave*. This reaction is yours, not theirs, and it is not rational, but it will be powerful, and you should prepare for it.

**Light protocol:** Use the faintest possible illumination, and only when navigating unfamiliar passages. A dim red-spectrum LED at its lowest setting will preserve some of your dark adaptation while allowing you to see root structures and avoid stepping into gaps in the root lattice — the spaces between roots that drop into cold darkness and wet clay below are ankle-breaking hazards and, in some cases, deep enough to trap a foot or leg. Never use a bright light in the main settlement space. It will disrupt the fauna's behavior patterns, blind people whose eyes have adapted to darkness, and cause visible distress. Think of it like shining a spotlight into someone's living room — but worse, because the people in this living room have never seen a spotlight and the light itself is an intrusion from a world they find overwhelming.

**Ideally,** you should spend two to three weeks in progressively darker environments before your visit. Sleep in complete darkness. Move through spaces with minimal light. Practice navigating by touch. Practice communicating without speaking. This won't replicate the hollow, but it will soften the shock. The moment of stepping into a sealed, dark, wet cavity inside a living root structure with no exit you can see and no sky above you — that moment will break some people. Preparation is the difference between a transformative experience and a panic attack in a place where panic has nowhere to go.

## Sound: You Are Too Loud

Read this carefully: **your normal speaking voice, in the hollow, will be physically painful to the inhabitants.**

Not metaphorically. These people have lived in near-total silence for years or decades. They speak softly, they communicate through touch, and when voices do break the silence, they are described as "enormous and wrong, like stones dropped into still water." Children learn quickly not to shout. This is not a cultural preference — it is a neurological adaptation. Their auditory processing has tuned itself to the frequency and amplitude of the hollow's natural soundscape: dripping water, wet clicking, the faint creak of root growth. A human voice at conversational volume is, for them, closer to an alarm or an explosion than to communication. It startles. It overwhelms. It might cause genuine pain.

**You must practice speaking at a whisper.** Not a stage whisper, not a "quiet voice" — an actual whisper, barely above breath. Practice it for days before your visit. Speak in whispers while doing chores, while walking, while having conversations. You need to retrain your vocal control so that the whisper becomes your default, not an effortful suppression of your normal volume. Even then, be aware that your whisper may still be loud by their standards. When in doubt, say less. When in less doubt, say nothing, and use touch.

**Learn at least the basics of their pressure-language before you arrive.** A hand on a shoulder means different things depending on pressure and location. Fingers traced along the back carry specific meanings. This is not an elaborate sign language with grammar and syntax — it's a practical, evolved system of tactile communication shaped by years of living in silence, and it is the *respectful* way to communicate in the hollow. If you arrive unable to touch-speak and unable to whisper, you are not just rude — you are functionally unable to communicate, and the people will have no way to understand your intentions. In a space this small and this closed, an unintelligible stranger is not merely uncomfortable. It is a threat to the equilibrium.

## The Fauna: Being Walked Upon

They will crawl on you.

You need to accept this before you arrive, and you need to accept it at a level deeper than intellectual assent. The fauna in the hollow does not avoid humans. It does not care about humans. You are a large, warm object that has been placed temporarily in its environment, and it will treat you the way it treats everything: as substrate. Millipedes will walk across your feet. Beetles will investigate your crevices — the folds of your clothing, the gaps between your fingers. Mites will coat your skin if you sit still for long enough. Worms will move through the root lattice beneath you, and occasionally something will brush against the sole of your foot through a gap.

None of this will harm you. The fauna has no defensive adaptations because it has no predators worth defending against. But your skin will crawl. Your nervous system will fire. Every primate instinct you have will scream *parasites, infestation, disease*, and overriding that instinct requires not courage but *habituation*. Spend time with invertebrates before your visit. Handle millipedes. Let beetles walk on your hands. Sit in a garden and let the soil fauna touch you. You are training your nervous system to not interpret every six-legged contact as an emergency.

**Bring antifungal treatment.** The hollow is a sealed, damp, organic environment teeming with decomposers. Your skin, which is accustomed to the relatively dry, UV-exposed surface world, will be an ideal substrate for fungal colonization. Keep your feet dry when you can (you can't, really, but change your wrappings frequently). Treat any skin breakdown immediately. This is not about the fauna biting you — it's about the ambient fungal spore load in a place where things are *always* breaking down and being rebuilt.

**Bring nothing synthetic.** No nylon, no polyester, no Gore-Tex. These materials do not breathe. In the hollow's saturated atmosphere, they will trap moisture against your skin and accelerate both fungal growth and heat loss through evaporative cooling. They also, as mentioned, *sound wrong* — the crinkle and swish of synthetic fabric will be alien and grating in a soundscape defined by organic silence.

## The Journey In: Descent Through the Roots

Getting to the settlement requires descending from the surface through "the tangled canopy of root and secondary growth." The people who have been to the surface describe it as "too bright, too loud, too much air" — which tells you two things. First, the transition itself is disorienting. Going from open sky to a sealed root cavity is a sensory reversal that will affect your balance, your breathing, and your emotional state. Second, these people experience the outside world as a kind of sensory assault. You will experience the inverse: the inside world as a kind of sensory deprivation.

**Prepare for both directions.** As you descend, your eyes will fail to adjust fast enough. You will be navigating root tangles, gaps, potentially steep drops between root limbs, with rapidly diminishing light. Move slowly. Keep one hand on a root at all times — not just for balance, but because the roots are your handholds, your ladder, your only structural reference. Do not grip them so tightly that you damage the bark surface. The roots are load-bearing. The settlement depends on them. You are a temporary guest inside a structure that is also a living organism, and your respect for that structure should be *physical*, not just philosophical.

**Bring a rope** for the descent, but anchor it to living root above the settlement zone and retrieve it when you leave. Do not leave it in the hollow — it will be colonized by fauna within hours, and removing it later will disrupt whatever microbial and fungal networks have begun to establish on the fibers.

**Tell someone where you're going.** The settlement is small and isolated. If you don't emerge on schedule, no one will come looking for you, because no one else knows you're there. Leave coordinates, a timeline, and a description of the location with someone you trust at your point of origin.

## The Fruit: Do Not Touch It

The thornroot periodically produces a large, warm fruit inside the hollow. This fruit is the only source of warmth the people have ever known. They gather around it. They huddle close to it. It is, for them, a kind of sacred event — not in a religious sense, but in the sense that it is the single interruption in the endless cold, the one moment when the living architecture of the root gives something back that they can *feel*.

**The fruit is not food.** It is not for you. It is for *them* — the people, and the fauna that consumes it when it splits open. Do not eat it. Do not take a piece of it. Do not approach it with a flashlight to examine it while it's warm. If you are present when the fruit develops, step back, be quiet, and let the event belong to the people and the small busy ones. This is the single most important cultural boundary in the hollow. Everything else — the touch-language, the sound norms, the root preservation — can be learned and practiced imperfectly. The fruit is non-negotiable.

## Psychological Preparation: The Feeling of Being Inside Something Alive

No amount of briefing prepares you for this.

The walls pulse. Not visibly — or rather, not in a way your conscious mind can track — but you feel it. A slow hydraulic throb, deep in the root mass, like a heartbeat at the frequency of geological time. The air has a taste. Root sap, cold clay, mineral tang, the faint sweetness of the exudations that feed the fauna. Your skin is perpetually damp. The cold is not something that happens to you from the outside; it is something that *surrounds* you, that you exist inside of, like being submerged in very cold, very thin water.

You are inside a living thing. This is not a metaphor. The root is alive, it is growing, and you are a foreign body in its interstices. The people who live here have made peace with this — they have nested inside an organism the way parasites nest inside a host, except they have consent and the organism does not notice them. You will feel *tiny*. You will feel *consumed*. Some people find this profoundly peaceful — a return to a kind of womb-state, an enclosure that holds and protects and asks nothing of you except that you be quiet and stay warm and not cut the roots. Others find it suffocating, claustrophobic, a waking nightmare of entrapment.

**Know yourself before you go.** If you are someone who needs open sky, who needs to see a horizon, who panics in enclosed spaces — the hollow will test you in ways that cannot be fixed by willpower alone. You will be in the dark, in the cold, in the wet, surrounded by invertebrates you cannot see, breathing air that tastes like sap, unable to speak at normal volume, unable to use fire for warmth or light, dependent on the kindness of eighteen people who live in a way you have never imagined, inside a thing that is alive and growing and does not know you are there.

But.

If you can let go of all of that — if you can surrender the light and the voice and the flame and the open sky — what you will find is something extraordinary. A complete, functioning, self-sustaining world in a space smaller than a garden shed. A community that has built nothing and yet everything, that lives inside the growth of another organism with a humility that is not performed but *structural*. A darkness that is not empty but crowded with life. A silence so total that you can hear your own blood moving. A cold that slows everything down to the pace of root growth and water seepage and the patient, clicking movement of blind things in the dark.

You will come back changed. Not in the dramatic, revelatory way that some multiverse visits change you. In a quieter way. You will come back knowing what silence actually sounds like. You will come back knowing what cold actually feels like — not as a problem to be solved, but as a condition to be inhabited. You will come back with the memory of being touched on the shoulder by someone who cannot speak to you in any language you share, and understanding perfectly what they meant.

## Final Checklist

- **Layered natural-fiber clothing** (wool or equivalent), tested for silence of movement
- **No synthetic materials whatsoever**
- **Sturdy, quiet footwear** that allows tactile sensitivity through the sole — you need to feel the root lattice beneath you
- **Dim red-spectrum light** with extended battery life, used sparingly
- **Rope** for descent, to be retrieved on departure
- **Antifungal treatment** and basic wound care supplies
- **No fire. No flames. No heat sources of any kind.** Not even a hand warmer. Not even a small candle "just for a moment." *Nothing.*
- **Pre-learned basics of the pressure-language** — at minimum, greetings, requests for direction, expressions of thanks, and "I don't understand"
- **Two to three weeks of darkness acclimation** before arrival
- **Extended exposure to invertebrates** — let them walk on you, let them be near you, let your nervous system learn that this is not danger
- **Whisper practice** — speak in whispers for weeks before you arrive, until the whisper *is* your voice
- **A written account of what you know about the hollow**, left with someone at your point of origin, with a return timeline
- **The willingness to be cold, and dark, and quiet, and touched by small things in the dark, for as long as you stay**

Go softly. Go with open hands. Go ready to be inside something alive.
