# Preparation Guide: The Unfinished Heights

---

## Overview of Threats and Conditions

Before anything else, you need to understand that the Unfinished Heights is not hostile. It is not a place that wants to kill you. It is a place that is not yet aware of you, that has not yet decided what *awareness* means, that is in the process of figuring out what shape rock should take. This makes it more dangerous than a hostile place, because hostile places have rules. The Unfinished Heights is still writing its rules, and it will not pause to finish a sentence before it collapses the ground beneath you.

Your primary survival challenges, in rough order of urgency:

1. **Asphyxiation from mineral dust.** There is no air filtration of any kind here, and the place is constantly generating fine silicate and obsidian particulate as formations crystallize and collapse. This dust is dry in a way that defies terrestrial comparison — it has never encountered moisture, and it will strip moisture from your lungs with horrifying efficiency. You are not breathing "dusty air." You are breathing *pure, undiluted mineral powder suspended in a gas mixture that barely qualifies as atmosphere*.

2. **Crushing or impact from terrain formation and collapse.** Structures grow, shift, shear, and fall. There is no warning. There is no pattern. A spire can add three meters to its height in a single grinding surge. A slab the size of a building can detach and fall without tremor or foreshadowing. The ground beneath you can dimple, deepen, and crystallize a new formation at its center while you are standing on its edge.

3. **Dehydration.** There is no water in this place. Not a trace. The concept has not occurred to it. There is nothing to forage, nothing to collect, no dew, no condensation, no subterranean reservoir. Every drop of water you will drink for the entire duration of your visit must be carried in with you, and the ambient warmth of the ground — constant, radiating upward — will accelerate your fluid loss through sweat and respiration even in the absence of exertion or heat.

4. **Thermal accumulation.** The ground is warm. The air is warm. There is no wind. There is no water cycle, no cloud cover, no weather of any kind. Heat does not dissipate here in any familiar way. Over extended periods, your body heat and the ambient radiant heat may compound. You have no convection, no evaporation (unless you expend your water), and no radiative cooling to any cold sink — because there is none. The darkness is not cool. It is the warmth's own native state.

5. **Spatial disorientation and psychological destabilization.** No horizon. No sky. No fixed reference points. The terrain changes constantly. Light behaves wrong. Sound behaves wrong. Time, as you will come to understand, behaves wrong. The place is disorienting not because it is complex, but because it is *pre-complex* — it has not yet assembled the features your brain uses to construct a sense of place. You will feel unmoored. You will feel like you are losing your mind. You may, for stretches, simply stop wanting to move, because the warmth is comfortable and the dark is soft and the place has a quality — not a sound, not a voice, not a smell — but a *permission* that says: stay. Rest. Watch. You don't need to go anywhere. There is nowhere to go.

This last one may be your most dangerous threat. It is not a trick. It is not a lure. The warmth is genuinely comfortable. But comfort, in a place that is actively collapsing and reforming around you, is a death sentence delivered with a lullaby.

## Gear Requirements

### Respiratory Protection

This is non-negotiable. A full-face respirator with sealed filters rated for fine silicate particulate is the minimum. The dust here is unlike terrestrial mineral dust because it has never been weathered — the particles are not rounded by water or wind. They are angular, sharp, crystalline, and they will cut the soft tissue of your lungs like microscopic glass shards. A standard dust mask will not protect you. The particles are small enough to pass through any gap, and angular enough to lodge in filter media in ways that accelerate clogging.

Bring at least twice the number of filter cartridges you think you need. The dust load is variable — it spikes dramatically when a nearby formation collapses — and you may go through cartridges faster than expected during active terrain events. If your respirator is a reusable half-face model, switch to a full-face unit. The dust will irritate your eyes severely, and you need your eyes functional. Mineral dust in the eyes in a place with no water is a debilitating injury.

If your multiverse transit system allows it, bring a self-contained breathing apparatus as a backup. Not because the air is toxic in any chemical sense — the gas mixture is breathable if filtered — but because if your primary respirator fails or clogs completely, you need a sealed system until you can repair or replace it.

**Critical note on moisture:** The dust will absorb moisture from your exhaled breath with extreme aggression. Your exhalation port will accumulate mineral deposits. Check it frequently. Keep a small tool — a thin metal pick or stiff brush — accessible to clear the port. If it blocks, your exhalation pressure will build and break the seal of your mask.

### Footwear

The ground is warm — not hot enough to burn through sole leather immediately, but warm enough that over time it will soften adhesives, degrade rubber, and transfer heat through conventional boot soles into your feet. You need footwear with a **ceramic or mineral-fiber composite sole**, something that can withstand sustained ground temperatures that may range from 40°C to 80°C depending on where you stand. The surface is also variable — powdery in some places, glassy in others, soft at formation edges — so the sole needs to be both heat-resistant and adaptable to inconsistent traction.

Bring two pairs. The ground will abrade soles faster than you expect because of the crystalline mineral content. The fine, angular dust is essentially a slow-acting grinding compound.

Gaiters are strongly recommended. The powder is fine enough to penetrate boot laces and work its way into any gap. Sealed, integrated boot-shin systems are ideal if available.

### Water Supply

Calculate your maximum expected duration in the Heights, then add fifty percent, then multiply that by your standard daily water requirement, then add another thirty percent for the thermal environment. That is your water load. Carry it in sealed, insulated containers — insulation both to retain coolness and to protect the containers from ground-radiated heat if you set them down.

Water discipline will be your most important behavioral task. You will want to drink more than you need because the warmth creates a subjective sensation of thirst that exceeds your actual dehydration rate. But you will also genuinely lose more fluid than in a terrestrial baseline environment, because the warmth never stops. There is no cool period, no shade, no respite. Even at rest, your body is losing moisture to a warm, dry environment that has no moisture to give back.

If your transit system permits, carry a compact water reclamation unit — something that can extract moisture from your exhalation and urine. This is not a luxury. For any visit longer than twelve hours, it is a survival requirement.

**Do not attempt to source water from any material you find in the Heights.** The minerals here have never been in contact with water. They will not contain water. Heating them will not release water. If you encounter a formation that appears to be sweating — a glassy surface with bead-like droplets — that is not water. It is molten or semi-molten mineral, and it is hot.

### Lighting

This is where your preparation becomes strange, because the Unfinished Heights has never been illuminated, and you do not know how it will respond to light.

The rock produces its own dim cherry-glow, visible only after your eyes adjust to the native darkness. This glow is *sufficient* for navigation at short range, but it is not enough to identify terrain hazards at distance, to read your instruments, or to inspect the ground before you step on it. You need artificial light.

Bring at least three independent light sources: a headlamp, a handheld torch, and a chest-mounted or shoulder-mounted flood unit. All should be rated for extended continuous operation. The headlamp for hands-free work, the torch for directed inspection, the flood unit for general orientation.

**Expect the light to behave oddly.** The geometry of the Heights is not Euclidean in any consistent way — surfaces meet at angles that your instruments will struggle to model — and this means that light will bounce, refract, and terminate in ways that will confuse your spatial perception. Shadows will be in wrong places. Surfaces will appear closer or further than they are. A pillar you illuminate may appear to be ten meters away when it is actually four, because the light is reflecting off a surface behind it that your brain reads as the pillar itself.

The cherry-glow of the rock may interact with your artificial light in unpredictable ways. Test your lights in the Heights immediately upon arrival and calibrate your expectations before venturing far from your entry point.

**Bring spare power cells.** All of them. The darkness is total and absolute away from any formation, and if your lights fail, you are functionally blind in a landscape that is actively reshaping itself around you.

### Structural Monitoring

This is specialized equipment, but it is important: bring a **broadband vibration sensor** — something that can detect the subsonic and near-subsonic frequencies generated by terrain formation and collapse. The grinding, ticking, groaning sounds described in the location data are the *audible* component of a much larger seismic process. By the time you hear a formation growing, it has already been growing for some time. By the time you feel the ground shake from a collapse, the collapse has already progressed beyond the point where you can meaningfully react.

A vibration sensor set on the ground near your position can give you seconds of additional warning — not much, but enough to move away from a slab that is about to shear, or to brace yourself if a nearby pillar is about to add a surge of growth that might shift the terrain under your feet.

Set the sensor to alert on three thresholds: low-frequency continuous vibration (formation activity in your immediate area), sharp impulse events (crystallization surges or fracture events within 50 meters), and sustained low-frequency rumble (large-scale collapse within 200 meters). If you have a second sensor, set it up facing the opposite direction. The geometry is too broken for any single sensor to cover your surroundings adequately.

### Clothing

Full coverage. The dust is everywhere, the ground heat is constant, and the ambient temperature is warm enough that you will be tempted to strip down. Do not. The dust will find any exposed skin and abrade it. The ground heat will radiate upward onto any exposed limb. Your clothing is also your primary barrier against thermal accumulation — a light, breathable, full-coverage suit made from a mineral-resistant synthetic is ideal. Something that allows minimal air exchange (to let your body's sweat mechanism function) but blocks direct contact with particulate.

Light colors are useless here — the darkness is total and the only illumination is the dim cherry-glow. Wear whatever is practical. But avoid loose fabric. In a place where crystal formations can sprout from the ground beneath you, loose fabric is something that can catch and hold.

Bring at least two changes of clothing. The dust will permeate everything. It will work its way into seams and weaves and stay there. After eight to twelve hours in the Heights, your outer layer will be stiff with embedded mineral. Change into a fresh layer, seal the contaminated suit in an airtight bag, and deal with it later.

### Navigation and Communication

There is no satellite infrastructure in the Unfinished Heights. There is no magnetic field that your compass can reliably orient to — the mineral composition is too variable and the formations are too magnetically chaotic. GPS or equivalent positioning systems will not function.

You need **inertial navigation** — an accelerometer-gyroscope system that tracks your movement from a known starting point. This is not perfectly accurate over long distances, but it is the only option you have. Mark your entry point with a **high-visibility beacon** — something that produces light or a signal strong enough to detect through the dust and the broken geometry. A flashing unit with a radio or infrared signal is ideal. If you move more than a kilometer from your entry point, leave physical markers behind you at regular intervals. Reflective tape on formations, though the formations may move or collapse under you. Your own scuff marks in the mineral powder, though new dustfall may obscure them. Consider dropping small, heavy objects — weighted markers — that will settle into the powder and remain detectable by your vibration sensor.

Communication with your transit team or any external party will be difficult. Radio signals will behave unpredictably in the broken geometry. The mineral formations may absorb, reflect, or refract electromagnetic signals in ways that your models cannot predict. Test your communications immediately upon arrival. If you cannot maintain a signal, you are alone.

**Mark your transit entry/exit point obsessively.** If you lose it, you will have to find it again in a landscape that is constantly rearranging itself. A beacon is not enough — the terrain may grow a pillar between you and it, or collapse the ground it sits on. You need a beacon *and* physical navigation *and* a constant awareness of your bearing relative to entry.

### Medical Kit

Standard field medical supplies, plus:

- **Eye wash kit** (sealed, pressurized, with at least 500ml per unit). Mineral dust in the eyes is almost guaranteed. Flush immediately and thoroughly.
- **Oral rehydration salts.** For managing dehydration that outpaces your water supply.
- **Topical skin barrier cream.** The dust will abrade exposed skin at points of friction — wrists, neck, the bridge of your nose where the respirator sits. Apply barrier cream generously.
- **Pain medication.** Headaches from the subsonic vibration are common and debilitating. The constant low-frequency rumble from terrain activity operates at frequencies that resonate with the human skull. You will feel it after a few hours. It is not dangerous in the short term, but it is deeply uncomfortable and it will degrade your decision-making.
- **A lightweight splint and wound closure kit.** If the terrain collapses under you or a formation shifts unexpectedly, you may sustain crush injuries or lacerations from the sharp mineral surfaces. You need to be able to stabilize yourself.

## Behavioral Protocols

### The First Hour

When you arrive in the Unfinished Heights, do not move. Stand at your entry point for a full hour. Let your eyes adjust to the cherry-glow. Let your respirator settle. Let your vibration sensor calibrate to the ambient noise floor. Let yourself *feel* the place.

This is not sentiment. This is tactical acclimation. The Heights will not kill you in the first hour — the terrain near transit entry points tends to be in a relatively stable phase, as though the act of your arrival has temporarily settled things. Use this window to learn the rhythms. Count the seconds between crystallization ticks. Note the direction of the nearest large formations. Identify the flat, open spaces — the draft spaces — where you are least likely to be crushed by falling material. Locate the nearest depression or low point where you could shelter if something large comes down.

Watch the ground. Watch for dimpling, for brightening of the cherry-glow in a localized area. These are the earliest signs of a new formation attempting to emerge. If you see them, move away from the center of the dimple. The formation may rise a centimeter or a meter or ten meters. You cannot predict which.

### Movement

Walk slowly. Walk deliberately. Place each foot with care, testing the ground before committing your weight. The surface will vary — powdery and yielding in one step, glassy and hard in the next, soft and warm at the edge of a recent formation event. Avoid walking directly on any formation that appears to be actively growing (distinguished by visible crystallization activity at its edges or a faint increase in the intensity of its cherry-glow).

Avoid the tall formations. This sounds obvious, but the temptation to approach the towering spires and cantilevered slabs is significant because they are the most visually dramatic features in the landscape. They are also the most likely to collapse. The taller the formation, the more mass it has accumulated, the more stress it is under, and the closer it is to a self-correction event. A spire that has reached a needle-thin apex is actively unstable — it is the terrain's most ambitious gesture and therefore its most likely failure.

The safest ground is the flats — the open, featureless expanses of warm powder between formations. They are boring. They are featureless. They are where the terrain has given up on ambition and settled into a neutral state. Walk there. Rest there. Make camp there. Do not be drawn to the drama of the growing heights unless you have a specific reason and an understanding of the risks.

If you must pass near a large formation, watch its base for stress fractures — hairline cracks in the cherry-glow, a deepening of the mineral color at the foundation. If you see these, move quickly. The formation is nearing its limit.

### Sound and Silence

Listen. The Heights is almost silent, but almost is not entirely. The sounds it does produce are *information*. The ticking of crystallization tells you something is growing nearby. The subsonic groan tells you something is shifting. The papery whisper of dust sliding tells you a surface has just changed angle.

Train yourself to distinguish these sounds. A crystallization tick that is increasing in frequency means a formation is accelerating — it is in a growth surge. A groan that deepens in pitch means a formation is shifting its weight — collapse may be imminent. Dust sliding after a long silence means something has just happened that you did not witness, and you should survey your surroundings immediately.

Do not make unnecessary noise. Not because the Heights will react to sound — there is no evidence that it does — but because you need to hear it. Your own footsteps in the mineral powder are loud enough. Talking is a luxury that costs you information.

### Time

There is no day-night cycle. There are no celestial markers. Your internal clock will drift. Set a timer — a wrist-mounted or headset-mounted clock — and check it regularly. The warmth and the dark and the slow rhythms of the terrain conspire to make time feel elastic. You will feel that you have been walking for ten minutes when an hour has passed. You will feel that you have been standing still for a moment when half a day has elapsed.

Use your clock. Log your movements, your water consumption, your filter changes, your observations. A written record — physical, not digital, because electronics behave unpredictably in the mineral environment — will anchor you to reality when the place tries to unmoor you.

### The Fragments

You will find them. Objects that are geometric, deliberate, shaped by something that understood precision. Hinges, rings, plates with fine inscriptions. They are warm. They are beautiful in the dim cherry-glow. They feel important.

**Do not take them.**

This is not because they are dangerous in any active sense. It is because you do not understand what they are, what they do, or what role they play in the Heights' process of self-creation. They may be failures — discarded experiments, like the collapsed formations around them. They may be load-bearing elements of some larger structure that has not yet assembled. They may be inert. They may be the first glimmers of something that will, given enough time, become the seeds of a world.

Taking them is not theft. The Heights will not notice. But it is *removal from a system you do not understand*. In a place that is iteratively building itself, removing pieces — even seemingly random, purposeless pieces — is introducing error into a process that is already struggling with error.

If you must interact with them, document them. Photograph them. Measure them. Sketch them. Leave them exactly where they lie. If you find something that excites you — and you will, because they are genuinely extraordinary — remind yourself that the Heights is making more of them constantly. There will be new ones tomorrow, if tomorrow is a meaningful concept here. Leave these for the place to finish its sentence.

### The Warmth and the Invitation to Stay

This is the part of the guide that is hardest to write, because it requires me to describe something that resists description: the feeling of the Heights *wanting you to be there*. Not consciously. Not with intent. But with a pervasive, ambient quality that settles into you like the warmth itself.

The darkness is soft. The ground is comfortable. The quiet is immense and strangely peaceful. The cherry-glow is the color of embers, of hearth-light, of the last warmth of a fire that has not gone out. Everything about the sensory experience of the Heights is calibrated — not by anyone, not by design, but by the physics of the place — to make you feel safe, warm, and still.

And stillness, in the Unfinished Heights, is death.

Not immediately. Not dramatically. But if you stop moving, stop monitoring, stop checking your timers and your sensors and your compass bearing and your water levels — if you let the warmth and the dark and the quiet hold you — you will eventually be standing on a formation that was not there when you sat down, or in the path of a collapse you did not hear coming, or in a place that has shifted so far from your entry point that you cannot find your way back.

The warmth is not lying to you. It is genuinely comfortable. But comfort is not safety. Comfort is the absence of alarm, and in the Unfinished Heights, alarm is the only tool you have.

**Set alarms.** Literal ones. A timer that goes off every thirty minutes with a sound loud enough to break through the quiet and the warmth and the temptation to simply *be*. When it goes off, perform your checklist: check your water level, check your filter, check your sensor readings, check your compass bearing, check your distance from entry, check the time. Every thirty minutes. No exceptions. Even when everything is fine. *Especially* when everything is fine.

## Duration Limits

The Unfinished Heights is not a place you visit for a long time. The thermal environment, the dust load, the water requirements, and the psychological erosion of the place's ambient invitation all impose hard limits on how long a human can safely remain.

**Maximum recommended duration: 72 hours.**

At 24 hours, the mineral dust will have permeated every piece of equipment you carry, and your filter efficiency will be declining. Your water supply, even with reclamation, will be at fifty percent or less. The subsonic vibration from constant terrain activity will have produced a persistent headache and may have begun to affect your fine motor coordination.

At 48 hours, you are in a degraded state. Your reactions are slower. Your perception is less reliable. The warmth has seeped into everything — your gear, your body, your thinking — and the boundary between resting and stopping has become very thin. The cherry-glow is starting to look like light. The formations are starting to look like architecture. The fragments on the ground are starting to look like they mean something. This is your brain beginning to pattern-match on a place that does not yet have patterns. It is a form of hallucination, and it is the beginning of the most dangerous phase.

At 72 hours, leave. Regardless of what you have found, regardless of what is happening, regardless of the temptation to watch one more formation emerge from the flat. Leave. You can return. The Heights will still be here, still trying to become something, still not finished. It will never be finished. That is the nature of the place. But you are not built to exist in it for long, and the longer you stay, the harder it becomes to remember that you are a visitor in a place that does not know you are here.

---

## Final Note

The Unfinished Heights is not dangerous because it is trying to destroy you. It is dangerous because it is trying to create itself, and you are standing in the construction zone of a process that does not have a concept of workers' safety, or workers, or safety, or concepts.

You are a witness to something extraordinary: a place in the act of becoming. Respect that. Document it if you can. But prioritize your own becoming — your own return, your own survival, your own completion of this journey — over the allure of watching the Heights complete a sentence it may never finish.

Bring more water than you think you need. Bring more filters than you think you need. Bring more light than you think you need. Set your alarms. Walk slowly. Listen to the dark. And when the warmth tells you to stay, set another timer and keep walking.

The Heights will be here when you come back. It will not remember you. It will still be trying.
