# Preparing for a Visit to the Grown Quarters of Vash Tidal

---

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

You are going to a small, cold, loud, wet, living settlement of roughly eighty to a hundred people who live *in* water, breathe *above* it in trapped air pockets inside buildings that are still growing, and have done so for longer than anyone can remember. Their technology is bone, stone, root, and hand. They have no metal. They have no written language. They have memory, patience, and the accumulated knowledge of how to coax a plant into becoming a wall, a ceiling, a doorway shaped like an exhaling lung.

You will be wet from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave. You will be cold much of that time. You will be surrounded by sounds you have no framework for — buildings that hum with current, creatures that click against glass-smooth stone, fish that whisper through water like schools of living weather. You will sleep in a bubble of damp, thin air inside a structure that is slowly, imperceptibly breathing. You will eat what they eat, which you will have to identify and accept. You will be the most conspicuous thing in this place for as long as you remain.

Go with respect. Go with patience. Go understanding that this settlement will outlast you, your children, and their children, and it will not have noticed you were here.

---

## Physical Preparation: Cold Water

This is the single most critical thing. Everything else is uncomfortable or confusing, but *this* can kill you.

The water is alpine snowmelt. It has never fully warmed. Estimate temperatures in the range of 4–10°C (39–50°F), depending on season and the depth you're operating at. This is water that will begin stealing core body heat within minutes of full-body immersion. You will be partially or fully submerged for most of your waking hours. The air pockets in the upper chambers help, but you still have to *get there*, and you'll be transiting through cold water multiple times per day.

**What to do:**

- **Begin cold-water acclimatization well in advance.** Weeks, ideally months before your trip. Start with cold showers, then cold baths, then open-water swimming in cool (not freezing) water. The goal is not to enjoy it — you won't — but to teach your body's cold-shock response to moderate itself. You want to avoid the gasp reflex, the panicked hyperventilation that makes submersion dangerous. You want to be able to enter cold water calmly.

- **Build your cold tolerance progressively.** Your body will adapt. Brown fat activation, peripheral vasoconstriction, a certain mental callus. You won't become immune. You'll become functional.

- **Fat is your friend.** If you can increase your body fat percentage modestly before the trip, do so. Insulation matters. The people who live here have likely been shaped by generations of cold-water life — their bodies may simply be better at thermoregulation than yours. Don't assume you're starting from the same baseline.

- **Understand the warning signs of hypothermia** with the fluency of someone who needs them: shivering that won't stop, confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination, drowsiness. Know that cold water kills by making you stupid before it makes you still. If you can't think clearly enough to get yourself to an air pocket, you are in serious trouble.

## Physical Preparation: Breath and Water Comfort

You won't be free-diving to great depth, but you *will* be submerging yourself regularly to move between structures. The water between buildings is the street. There's no bridge, no boardwalk, no surface path. If you want to go somewhere, you go *through* the water.

**What to do:**

- **Practice swimming** if you aren't already competent. You won't need to be an athlete, but you need to be comfortable treading water, moving through water with purpose, and navigating around underwater obstacles (root systems, rock formations, creatures).

- **Train your breath-holding.** Not to extreme freediving levels, but enough to hold your breath comfortably for 30–60 seconds while you orient yourself, assess the path ahead, and move with calm efficiency. Panic underwater, even in shallow water, burns oxygen catastrophically. The ability to stay calm on a single breath is more valuable than lung capacity.

- **Practice swimming with your eyes open underwater** in chlorinated or clear water. The settlement is in exceptionally clear, sunlit water — you'll be able to see — but seeing while moving, while cold, while managing your breath, is a skill that needs rehearsal. Bring appropriate eye protection or goggles if your eyes don't tolerate this well, but understand that any equipment you bring is equipment you have to manage while swimming.

- **Get comfortable being submerged in full clothing** (or whatever layering system you settle on). Fabric changes how you move in water. It drags. It clings. It traps cold water against your skin in ways that differ from bare immersion. You need to know what it feels like before you're doing it in a place where the nearest dry surface might be a three-minute swim and a climb up a root-wall into a dim air pocket.

## Gear: What to Bring

This is a multiverse trip, so you presumably have *some* ability to bring equipment. Use that power thoughtfully. The residents of the Grown Quarters have a simple, functional, deeply integrated material culture. Anything you bring that is too obviously foreign, too advanced, or too disruptive will mark you as alien and potentially unwelcome. But you also need to survive, and you are an *ordinary human* — not adapted to this environment.

**Thermal protection:**

- **A thin, high-quality wetsuit or equivalent thermal base layer** — something in the 2–3mm range that provides insulation without making you too buoyant or too bulky. You need to be able to move freely, swim, climb root-walls, and sit in air pockets without the suit being a hindrance. The goal is to buy yourself time in cold water, not to seal yourself off from it entirely. Full-body coverage is essential.

- **Consider a thermal hood** if you can wear one discreetly. An enormous amount of heat is lost through the head and neck. A hood that covers your head and neck but leaves your face exposed could make an enormous difference in comfort and safety during extended water exposure.

- **Do not bring a drysuit.** It's too bulky, too loud, too alien, and it would seal you off from the water in a way that would make you a glass-jar curiosity rather than a visitor. The thin wetsuit is a compromise: enough protection to survive, enough permeability to participate.

**Footwear:**

- **Flexible water shoes with good grip.** The limestone substrate is uneven and potentially slippery with algal growth. The root-walls that you'll need to climb into air pockets will have varying textures. You need your feet protected but functional. No rigid boots — you need to feel the rock and root beneath you.

**Personal items:**

- **A waterproof bag or container** for anything you need to keep dry: a journal, a medical kit, personal items. A simple sealed container, nothing high-tech. Something that fits in a woven basket alongside their own goods.

- **A small, sealed first-aid kit.** Antiseptic, bandages, any medications you need. Cold water environments are hard on skin — expect chapping, cracking, potential infection in small cuts. You're visiting a place with no pharmacy and no understanding of your specific medical needs.

- **A headlamp or small light source** that works when wet. The air pockets in the buildings are dim — they're lit by whatever light filters through the translucent outer bark and root-walls, which is beautiful but not bright. You will need to see to navigate interior spaces. Choose something with a warm, low-intensity setting rather than a harsh white beam. Minimize its conspicuity.

- **Nothing else.** No phone, no computer, no communication device that the residents won't understand. No weapons. No tools that outclass theirs. No books (unless you intend to leave one as a gift, which is a separate cultural consideration). The less you bring, the less you have to manage in the water, and the less you disrupt the equilibrium of a place that has functioned for centuries without your help.

**Clothing beyond the wetsuit:**

- When you're inside an air pocket and relatively dry, you'll want something to wear over or instead of the wetsuit. Loose, quick-drying fabric that won't trap moisture. A wrap or tunic-style garment that can be easily removed and stowed. Nothing that takes hours to dry, because nothing dries fast in a place where the ambient air is humid and cool and sits inside a living plant.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

This is where most travel guides stop, and where this one needs to go deepest.

**The sound.**

You are not prepared for the sound. The description says the settlement *sings*, and it does, but "singing" implies something beautiful and optional. The sound of the Grown Quarters is constant, pervasive, and inescapable. Current moves through hollow root-chambers and produces resonance — a deep, tonal hum that vibrates in your chest, in your teeth, in the bones behind your ears. The grazer-creatures click against surfaces with a rhythm that never stops. Fish displace water in soft, rushing patterns. The mountain itself produces subsonic vibration that you feel in your gut.

It is not silence. It is not noise. It is an acoustic environment utterly unlike anything in your normal experience. It will be overwhelming at first. It will be disorienting. It may cause headaches, unease, or a persistent low-grade anxiety that you can't attribute to any specific cause.

**What to do:** Expect this. Plan for it. If you have any ability to bring ear protection (simple foam plugs), consider having them available for sleep or moments when you need quiet. But also understand that over days, you will habituate. The residents live in this sound the way you live in the hum of a city or the whisper of wind. It becomes background. It becomes normal. Give yourself that time.

**The wetness.**

You will never be fully dry. Even in the air pockets, the humidity is high — these are sealed spaces where plant respiration continuously exchanges moisture with the air. Your clothes will never fully dry. Your skin will prune and stay pruned. Your hair (if you have any) will be perpetually damp.

This sounds trivial. It isn't. Prolonged immersion and dampness take a psychological toll. It affects mood, sleep quality, skin integrity, and the simple comfort of being in your own body. Prepare mentally for a level of physical discomfort that persists beneath everything else, a background condition of damp cold that colors every other experience.

**The pace.**

The residents of the Grown Quarters do not hurry. They cannot afford to — not because of lethargy, but because their world operates on timescales that make haste meaningless. A building takes decades to mature. A decision is made by consensus among people who share memories going back generations. Movement through the water is deliberate and efficient, but it is never *fast*.

You will feel the urge to rush. To explore everything immediately. To ask questions faster than they can answer (or gesture, or communicate). Resist this. Match their pace. Move when they move. Sit when they sit. Watch the schools of fish shift like weather patterns. Let the sound settle into you. Allow an hour to pass doing what you would normally do in five minutes. This is not wasted time. This is the *only* way to experience this place honestly.

**The communication.**

They don't speak a language you'll understand. Communication is primarily gestural, supplemented by shared reference — pointing, indicating, leading. They know each other intimately enough that a glance or a hand motion carries meaning that would take you paragraphs to convey.

You will be unable to ask complex questions. You will be unable to explain who you are, where you're from, why you're here, in any way that makes literal sense to them. You will have to communicate through presence, through behavior, through the universal human grammar of patience and kindness and demonstrated respect.

Practice before you go: communicate with someone using only gestures and shared physical context. Notice how much meaning you can convey with your hands, your posture, your willingness to help with a physical task. Notice how much you *can't* convey. Sit with that limitation. You will need to be comfortable with being profoundly unable to explain yourself.

**The vulnerability.**

You are an ordinary human in a place that was not built for you, surrounded by people whose way of life you cannot fully comprehend, in an environment that is beautiful and functional and will not accommodate your weaknesses. The cold, the water, the sound, the communication barrier — all of these will make you feel small and fragile and out of place. You will, at some point, feel a deep and primal homesickness for dry ground and familiar sounds.

This is fine. This is honest. Let it happen. The vulnerability is part of what makes the visit meaningful.

## Practical Logistics

**When to arrive:**

Summer, if you have a choice. The settlement is at high altitude and partially fed by sunlight. Winter brings dimmer light, colder water, and likely harsher conditions both for you and for the residents. Summer means brighter water, slightly warmer temperatures (only slightly), and the full visual spectacle of the place — the prismatic fish-scales on root-walls, the green-tinged outer bark alive with algae, the columns of sunlight reaching the limestone floor. You want to see this place at its most vivid.

**How long to stay:**

Two to four weeks, minimum. Less than that, and you won't acclimatize. The first several days will be spent simply adjusting to the cold, the sound, the wetness, the communication barrier, the rhythm of the place. You won't truly begin to *see* the settlement until you've stopped fighting it. Give yourself enough time to stop fighting it.

Also consider: your visit disrupts their equilibrium. A stranger in a community of eighty people is not unnoticed. Staying long enough to become a familiar (if unexplained) presence is kinder than appearing briefly and vanishing. Let them get used to you. Let your strangeness become ordinary.

**Food:**

You will eat what they eat. This is non-negotiable, both practically (there's nothing else) and culturally (refusing food is a universal signal of distrust or disgust).

Their diet is likely based on the protein of the fish and eels, supplemented by the plant life — the algae, the fine-leafed undergrowth that carpets every stable surface, the inner bark or root tissue of the living buildings themselves. Some of this may be unfamiliar and some may be actively unpleasant to your palate.

**What to do:** Before arriving, practice eating unfamiliar foods with genuine openness. Train yourself out of any reflexive disgust responses to texture or appearance. If you have serious allergies, figure out how to identify them in advance and carry whatever emergency medication you need (an EpiPen, antihistamines — sealed in your waterproof kit). Be honest with your hosts, as best you can through gesture, about what you can and cannot eat. But default to *yes*.

**Sleep:**

You'll sleep in the air pockets — the dry chambers inside the larger buildings. The atmosphere is breathable but thin and humid. The surfaces are living or dead wood, polished smooth by generations of use. The sound continues even at night — perhaps quieter, as the current settles into its nighttime patterns, but never silent.

Bring something to sleep on or in that provides a thin layer of insulation from the surface, if you can keep it dry enough to be useful. A simple sleeping liner or wrap. The floor of an air pocket is hard, and the residual cold seeps through. The residents have likely adapted to this in ways you haven't.

Expect to wake periodically. The sound, the cold, the unfamiliarity — your sleep will be broken for the first week or more. This is normal. Accept it.

## What to Bring as a Gift

You are a guest in a community with no concept of commerce. You cannot pay for hospitality. You can offer something.

Think carefully about what would be meaningful to people with no metal, no glass, no complex manufactured goods. Something that is *useful* in their context. Something that demonstrates you understand what their life is like and have made an effort to contribute to it rather than merely observe.

Possibilities:

- **A medical resource they don't have.** Antibiotic ointment. A broad-spectrum antiseptic. Something sealed and durable that could address infections, wounds, illnesses that their bone-and-plant toolkit cannot. This is potentially life-saving and would be understood as a gift of care even across the communication barrier.

- **Knowledge, encoded physically.** A detailed, illustrated guide to aquatic plant cultivation techniques from other civilizations — presented as drawings, not text, since they have no written language. Something that might offer new approaches to what they already do, without implying their methods are wrong.

- **Something beautiful.** A gift that exists solely to be beautiful in their context — a colored stone, a piece of worked glass (simple, nothing industrial), a crystal. People everywhere understand beauty. Even if they don't know what it is, they'll recognize what it's *for*.

Do not bring tools that would alter their technology. Do not bring things that would make their life materially easier in ways that bypass their own ingenuity. The temptation to bring a fishing net or a better knife or a water filter must be resisted. Their simplicity is not poverty. It is a *choice* that has sustained them for centuries. Do not disrespect it by trying to upgrade it.

## What to Expect

You will arrive cold and overwhelmed. The first days will be an exercise in endurance — tolerating the cold, the sound, the wetness, the confusion of communicating with gestures while your teeth chatter and your brain struggles to process an environment unlike anything in your experience.

You will begin to adapt. Your body will adjust to the cold (somewhat). Your ears will learn the sound (partially). Your skin will accept the water (eventually). You will begin to notice the extraordinary details: the way the light shifts from silver to gold as the seasons change, the precise moment when a school of fish parts around your body like water around a stone, the texture of root-wall bark under your fingertips — alive, faintly warm, slowly growing.

You will be admitted into the air pockets. You will see the dry rooms where children sleep and food is prepared. You will witness the quiet domesticity of a life lived in two mediums — water and air, wet and dry, outside and inside — with no clear boundary between them. You will understand, finally, that the distinction between building and garden that the description mentions is not architectural but *philosophical*. These people do not separate themselves from their environment. They *are* their environment. The buildings grow because they are not yet finished. The settlement breathes because it is alive. You are a guest inside a living thing.

You will leave colder and quieter and wetter than when you arrived, carrying no souvenirs (what would you take? A piece of living root that will die outside the water? A stone from the polished floor that means nothing without the mountain?) and no photographs (did you bring a camera? Should you have? The ethics of documenting a place that cannot document itself are not simple).

You will carry the *sound*. For weeks afterward, in quiet moments, you will hear it — the hum, the click, the deep resonance of current through living wood. It will come to you unbidden in the silence of your own dry, warm, artificial world, and it will remind you that there is a place on a drowned mountain where a small group of people live inside a song they didn't write, surrounded by buildings that are still becoming what they'll be, in water that tastes like the inside of a cave.

You will want to go back.

That is what you should prepare for, above everything else: the wanting.
