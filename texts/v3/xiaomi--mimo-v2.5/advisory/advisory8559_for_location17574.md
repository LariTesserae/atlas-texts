# Preparing for the Steaming Galleries

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before packing a single item, you need to internalize what this place *is*, because it will violate nearly every intuition your body has developed over a lifetime of living on the surface.

The Steaming Galleries are an abandoned but still-functioning industrial settlement built directly above a shallow geothermal anomaly. They are hot, humid, dim, vibrating, sterile, and profoundly, profoundly *uncanny*. There is nothing here that will try to kill you — no predator, no toxin at ambient concentration, no structural collapse that you can foresee. What there is, instead, is an environment that will slowly dismantle your certainties about where you are, what is watching you, and whether "watching" is even the right word for what the galleries are doing.

The danger is not dramatic. It is cumulative. It is the danger of heat exhaustion in a place where your sweat cannot cool you. It is the danger of navigation failure in architecture designed for function, not for orientation. It is the danger of a mind slowly losing its grip on what is machine and what is alive and what is neither, in a place where those distinctions have already been dissolved. And, at the deepest accessible point, there is the danger of whatever the greenish steam carries — something the body knows to refuse before the mind has a name for it.

## Thermoregulation: Your Primary Survival Concern

This is, above all else, a heat problem.

The galleries exist in a state of sustained thermal saturation. The stone radiates at body temperature or above. The air is described as a "hot towel pressed to the face" — which means you are looking at effective temperatures probably in the 38–45°C range with humidity approaching or exceeding 95%. In these conditions, your body's primary cooling mechanism — sweat evaporation — fails almost completely. You will sweat prodigiously and gain almost no cooling benefit from it. Your core temperature will rise steadily from the moment you enter.

**Before you go:**

- **Heat acclimatize over 10–14 days.** This means daily sessions of sustained moderate exertion in the hottest, most humid environment you can find — a heated room, a hot yoga studio, a steam room, or ideally outdoor heat with humidity. Start with 30-minute sessions and build to 90 minutes. The goal is physiological adaptation: increased sweat rate, better electrolyte conservation, more stable cardiovascular response. Do not skip this. A non-acclimatized person entering the Steaming Galleries is at genuine risk of collapse within hours.

- **Get a full medical workup.** Cardiac, renal, hepatic. You need your organs performing at baseline, because they will be under sustained stress. If you have any cardiovascular compromise, kidney issues, or history of heat illness, this is not a trip you should take.

- **Train your mind for what "hot" means here.** You have experienced heat. You have not experienced *this* heat — heat that comes from the ground, from the walls, from the air, from every surface simultaneously. There is no shade. There is no breeze. There is no cool surface to press against. The stone *presses back with warmth, steady as blood.* Imagine being inside a living thing. That is closer to the truth than any comparison to a desert or a sauna.

**What to wear:**

- **Primary layer:** Lightweight, loose-fitting clothing in a synthetic moisture-wicking fabric — something like a polyester-blend base layer with mesh ventilation panels. Do not wear cotton. Cotton absorbs moisture, becomes heavy, and in high humidity will never dry. You will be wet from sweat and condensation within minutes of entering, and everything you wear will stay wet. The goal is clothing that moves moisture away from your skin quickly and doesn't hold it against you.

- **Color:** Light. Pale. The galleries are dim and you want maximum visibility for your companions and for your own sense of spatial awareness. The ambient light is warm ochre — a light fabric will pick up that tone but remain the brightest thing in your immediate vicinity.

- **Coverage:** Full coverage, paradoxically. You want to prevent direct skin contact with the walls where condensation collects, because the mineral content of that moisture is unknown and potentially irritating. Long sleeves, long pants, collar closed. Think of it the way a spelunker thinks about coveralls — protection from the environment, not from weather.

- **Footwear:** Waterproof boots with aggressive tread and excellent ankle support. The floors will be wet — condensation pools in channels cut into the stone, and unvented galleries will have standing water. You need grip on wet, possibly mineral-slicked stone. The boots should be breathable enough not to become furnaces around your feet, but the priority is traction and water resistance. Neoprene socks underneath if you expect significant standing water.

- **Do not wear rings, watches, or metal jewelry against your skin.** The mineral-rich condensation will react with metals. The walls sweat iron-colored moisture. You don't want to find out what happens when copper or silver contacts that for hours.

## Hydration and Nutrition

You will lose water at a rate that will shock you if you haven't experienced extreme humidity heat loss. In conditions where sweat doesn't evaporate, people often underestimate their fluid loss because they don't *feel* dry. You will feel wet. You will think you're fine. You are not fine.

- **Carry water in a hydration bladder with a drinking tube.** You need to drink constantly, without stopping, without fumbling with bottles. Aim for 500–750 mL per hour of active traversal. More if you're exerting yourself. The bladder should be insulated if possible — the ambient temperature will warm your water supply rapidly.

- **Electrolyte supplementation is mandatory.** Tablets or powder dissolved in your water — sodium, potassium, magnesium. You will be losing electrolytes faster than your body can store them. Symptoms of electrolyte imbalance — cramping, confusion, cardiac irregularity — are particularly dangerous in an environment that is already stressing your cardiovascular system. Bring more than you think you need. Double it.

- **Food should be high-calorie, easily digestible, and require no preparation.** Energy bars, nuts, dried fruit, electrolyte chews. Your appetite will suppress in heat — this is a normal physiological response as blood diverts from digestion to skin. Eat anyway, even if it's small amounts frequently. Your body is burning fuel at an elevated rate just to maintain thermal homeostasis.

- **Do not consume caffeine or alcohol before or during the traversal.** Both are diuretics. Both impair thermoregulation. Both will make you measurably less safe in this environment.

## Breathing: The Invisible Threat

The air in the Steaming Galleries contains at minimum: water vapor at near-saturation levels, fine calciumite dust (described as "faint chalky dust that settles on nothing because the updraft carries it back up"), and whatever minerals are dissolved in the condensation that periodically steam off surfaces. At the deeper passages, there is a greenish steam with an unknown composition that the narrative explicitly warns against approaching.

**Respiratory protection is not optional.**

- **Minimum requirement:** A well-fitted N95 or P100 particulate respirator. This will filter calciumite dust and most mineral particulates. Wear it from entry. The dust is described as fine and persistent — prolonged inhalation of fine calcium dust is a known cause of respiratory irritation and, with chronic exposure, pneumoconiosis. You are not planning chronic exposure, but even a few hours of breathing calciumite dust in a confined, humid, poorly ventilated space is enough to cause acute irritation.

- **For the deeper galleries:** A half-face respirator with combination cartridges (P100 + organic vapor/acid gas) is wise. You don't know what's in the greenish steam. The crystalline growths are described as mineral-feeding, iron-processing, copper-green organisms that are "not quite plant life." The steam that feeds them and that they color with their presence may carry volatile compounds, metal particulates, or biological aerosols from whatever metabolic process sustains them. A P100 cartridge handles particles. An OV/AG cartridge handles a broad range of unknown volatiles. This is precautionary against ignorance, and in an environment this alien, ignorance is the baseline.

- **Monitor your breathing.** In hot, humid air, respiratory rate increases naturally. If you notice yourself breathing harder than the exertion level warrants, if you feel lightheaded, if the air seems "thicker" than before — these are early signs of heat stress and/or respiratory compromise. Stop. Rest. Hydrate. Do not push through.

- **Protect your eyes.** Sealed goggles or at minimum wraparound safety glasses. The calciumite dust will irritate mucous membranes. The humidity will cause continuous fogging on standard lenses — anti-fog treatment is essential, or use goggles with indirect ventilation. Your eyes are one of your primary navigation tools in this dim environment; do not compromise them with irritation and tearing.

## Lighting and Vision

The galleries have no artificial light and no natural light. What they have is a dim, sourceless, warm ochre glow emanating from the thermal radiation of the rock and the heated infrastructure. It is enough to see by. It is not enough to see *well*.

This creates several problems:

1. **Peripheral vision becomes unreliable.** The dim, warm, flickering quality of the light means your eyes are operating at the edge of their scotopic (low-light) range. At these light levels, your peripheral vision — which relies on rod cells — is active, but your brain's interpretation of peripheral data becomes less accurate. Shapes resolve into other shapes. A flywheel becomes a column. A column becomes a figure. The text explicitly describes this effect, and it is psychologically destabilizing.

2. **The ambient light cannot be turned off or adjusted.** It is omnipresent and sourceless. If you shine a bright headlamp, you will blow out your night-adapted vision and lose the ability to use the ambient light as a navigation baseline. This is a problem because you need both: the ambient light for spatial orientation and your own directed light for detail work.

**Lighting equipment:**

- **Primary:** A quality headlamp with adjustable brightness and a red-light mode. Start on low/medium. The red mode preserves night vision while allowing you to examine details. Carry spare batteries — heat drains batteries faster than cold, and humidity accelerates corrosion on contacts. Lithium batteries outperform alkaline in heat.

- **Secondary:** A small, handheld flashlight with a focused beam for examining specific objects — machinery, growths, structural features. This is your "direct attention" tool, the thing you point at something when you need to resolve it from the ambiguity of the ambient light.

- **Backup:** Chemical light sticks. They're affected by heat but provide a reliable, battery-independent light source. Carry a few. Activate one if your electronics fail and you need to mark your position or path.

- **Consider an infrared/thermal camera.** The galleries are a thermal environment — everything radiates heat. A thermal camera would show you the heat patterns of the infrastructure, reveal the water flow in pipes, map the temperature gradients that indicate where the thermal anomaly is strongest, and potentially reveal structural features invisible to the naked eye. It would also help you avoid accidentally approaching the greenish steam zones, which presumably have a distinct thermal signature.

**Behavioral adaptations:**

- **Look directly at everything, all the time.** The text instructs this and you should obey it. Do not rely on peripheral vision for any navigational or hazard-assessment decision. Turn your head. Point your light. Resolve the shape. Accept that this makes you feel watched, because direct attention is confrontation and the galleries respond to confrontation with... something. Do it anyway. Peripheral hallucinations in dim, warm, vibrating environments are a known phenomenon, and they are more dangerous than the mild unease of feeling observed.

## Navigation: Do Not Become Lost

The galleries are repetitive. Every gallery leads to another gallery. The proportions are similar — roughly forty meters long, thirty across, fifteen to the crown. The architecture is functional, not commemorative. There are no landmarks. There are no distinguishing features. The passages slope gently upward as you go deeper (counterintuitively), and the condensation patterns shift in ways that are subtle enough to miss.

Getting lost in the Steaming Galleries is not an immediate death sentence. The environment is stable, the temperature is uniform, and there are no biological hazards. But getting lost means wandering in a hot, humid, dim, vibrating maze while your water supply depletes and your cognitive function degrades from heat stress. In these conditions, disorientation becomes dangerous fast.

**Navigation protocols:**

- **Mark your path.** Carry a pack of high-visibility, heat-resistant tape or UV-reactive paint markers. At every junction and at regular intervals along each gallery, leave a mark on the wall at eye level. Make the marks directional — an arrow or a coded symbol that indicates the direction back to your entry point. The walls are pale, recently cut stone with no patina — marks will be visible. Check that your marking material adheres to warm, damp surfaces before you rely on it.

- **Bring a compass and a GPS unit, but do not trust either.** A standard magnetic compass may be affected by the infrastructure — copper pipes, ferrous stone, electrical currents from the generator system. A GPS unit will not receive satellite signals underground. Use them as supplementary information, not primary navigation. Your primary navigation is your marks, your counting of galleries traversed, and your awareness of slope and condensation patterns.

- **Carry a measuring tape or pacing cord.** Know how many galleries you've traversed and in what direction. If you're moving deeper (upward slope, increasing condensation, increasing warmth), count galleries and turns. Maintain a mental and written log.

- **Set a turnaround point before you enter.** Decide, before you descend, how far you will go. How many galleries. How many minutes. How much water you'll consume. When you've used half your water, you turn around, regardless of how close you feel to... whatever you came to find. The galleries do not care about your curiosity. They will let you walk until you can't walk anymore.

- **Buddy system if possible.** Two people navigating together are exponentially safer than one. You catch each other's disorientation. You verify each other's perception. You carry each other if one fails. If you go alone (not recommended), your margin for error is razor-thin.

## The Machinery: Treat Every Moving Part as Lethal

The galleries contain operating industrial machinery — flywheels, belt-drives, piston assemblies, turbines. The power is irregular. It surges and fades. A mechanism that is stationary when you pass it may activate without warning. A mechanism that is moving may accelerate, decelerate, or change direction unpredictably.

This machinery was not designed with pedestrian safety in mind. There are gratings over some flywheels. There are ceramic pulleys on belt-drives. There is a piston assembly that "still moves — or would still move, if the power were constant." The power is not constant.

**Rules:**

- **Never place any part of your body inside, beneath, or between any piece of machinery, regardless of whether it appears to be operating.** The surges are unpredictable. A piston at rest can become a piston in motion in the time it takes to inhale.

- **Maintain a minimum distance of two meters from any moving mechanism.** Belts can throw. Flywheels can shed fragments. Steam leaks from pressurized pipes can cause burns at a distance.

- **Listen.** The machinery has a sound — a wet rhythm, a regular irregularity, the sound of "a heart in a fever." Learn it. If the sound changes — speeds up, slows down, stutters, stops — something is happening. Move away from the nearest machinery and wait for the surge to resolve.

- **Do not touch any pipe without thermal assessment first.** Some pipes carry process water at elevated temperatures. Some are insulated (the ceramic-coated pipes that glow amber). Some may carry fluids at pressures you cannot gauge visually. If you must interact with infrastructure, use the back of your hand to test for heat at a distance, and wear insulated gloves.

## Psychological Preparation: The Real Danger

This is what will get you, in the end. Not the heat, not the dust, not the machinery. The *feel* of the place.

The Steaming Galleries are described with extraordinary precision as an environment that produces specific psychological effects through its architecture, its light, its sound, and its nature. You should prepare for each of these:

**1. The sensation of being watched.**

This is produced by the interaction of dim, sourceless light with complex, angular geometry and your own evolved threat-detection systems. You evolved to detect faces, eyes, figures in peripheral vision. The galleries exploit this by presenting shapes that almost resolve into figures but never quite do. Every column might be a person. Every flywheel might be watching. The text describes this directly: "You stop trusting your peripheral vision early, and you learn to look directly at everything, and you learn that looking directly at everything makes you feel watched."

**Preparation:** Understand the mechanism. This is your brain's face-detection and threat-detection systems firing on ambiguous stimuli in a low-light, high-stress environment. It is a known, well-documented phenomenon. It is not evidence of observation. When you feel watched, stop, turn, look directly at the source of the feeling, resolve it into its mechanical reality, and continue. Do not engage with the feeling. Do not narrate it. Do not give it a story. Stories are how these feelings become beliefs, and beliefs are how people stop making rational decisions.

**2. The uncanny quality of purpose without agents.**

Everything in the galleries is *working*. The pipes carry water. The turbines turn. The crystalline growths grow. But no one is here. This produces a specific flavor of cognitive dissonance — the systems are clearly designed, clearly purposeful, clearly active, but their purpose has no visible author. You are standing inside a machine that does not know it was built. You are inside a verb that has learned to make walls.

**Preparation:** This is an intellectual dissonance, not a physical threat. But prolonged exposure to environments that violate your model of how the world works — things that are purposeful without being conscious, that are active without being alive — produces a low-grade existential anxiety that accumulates over time. The term for this is "ontological vertigo." You may not experience it by that name, but you will recognize it as a growing sense that the world is not what you thought it was, and that this is not exciting but *wrong*. 

Counter this by grounding in your own body. Feel your feet on the stone. Feel the humidity on your skin. Feel your breath through the respirator. You are a biological organism in a geological and mechanical environment. You are real. Your senses are reliable (within the known distortions of this place). The galleries are a place. You are a person. Hold that distinction.

**3. The infrasound and vibration.**

The thermal anomaly produces a tectonic hum that "runs through the floor into the soles of your feet and up through the skeleton" and "makes your vision blur slightly at the edges if you stand still too long." This is infrasound — sound below the threshold of conscious hearing but within the range of physiological effect. Infrasound at certain frequencies is known to produce anxiety, visual disturbances, a sense of unease, and in some cases, auditory and visual hallucinations.

**Preparation:** If you feel your vision blurring at the edges, *move*. Walking breaks the resonance coupling between the infrasound and your body's natural frequencies. Do not stand still for prolonged periods. Do not lean against walls for extended periods. The vibration transmits most efficiently through direct, sustained contact. Keep your body in motion, even if it's just shifting weight, walking slowly, pacing. Movement is your defense against the frequency.

If the vibration becomes overwhelming — if you feel nauseous, if your vision begins to blur even while moving, if you feel a deep, chest-resonating dread that seems to come from outside your own emotional state — that is your body telling you the infrasound has reached a level that is affecting your vestibular and limbic systems. Leave. Retrace your steps using your marks. The galleries do not care whether you stay, and your body cares very much whether you leave.

**4. The completion tone.**

Occasionally, "a sound from deeper in. A metallic ring, as though something vast and precise has just finished a task." This will happen. It will be startling. It will reverberate for a long time. It will multiply in the angled surfaces.

**Preparation:** This is a machine completing a cycle. It is a mechanical event. It is not communication. It is not addressed to you. Treat it the way you would treat a factory bell in an empty building — acknowledge it as a system event and continue with your assessment. Do not go toward it. Do not investigate it. It is not for you.

## The Deeper Passages: Know Your Limits

At the deepest accessible point, the passage narrows and the greenish steam rises. The text is explicit: "You do not go toward it. Not because you are afraid, though you are, but because the passage ahead is too narrow — it was built for pipes, not for bodies."

**This is your hard turnaround point.** Not because of the narrative's warning, but because of practical reality:

- The passage is too narrow for a human body. You cannot safely traverse a space built for infrastructure, not people, in low visibility, high humidity, with compromised grip and no room to maneuver if something goes wrong.

- The greenish steam indicates a concentration of whatever mineral or biological process sustains the crystalline growths. At ambient concentrations in the galleries, this is apparently harmless. At source concentration, the body's instinctive avoidance suggests acute hazard — respiratory, dermal, or chemical.

- The thermal anomaly is strongest here. The stone is hottest. The air is densest. The infrastructure is most concentrated and most unpredictable. Your margin for error approaches zero.

**When you reach this point, take your observations. Make your notes. Record your thermal imagery. And turn around.**

The galleries will still be there. They will always be there. They do not know you came. They will not know you left. They will continue to hum and drip and glow with "the steady, thoughtless commitment of a system that does not know it has been abandoned."

Your job is to leave the same way. Every mark you made on the wall is still there. Your water bladder has half its capacity remaining (because you set your turnaround point correctly). Your respirator is still functioning. Your goggles are fogged but serviceable. You know how many galleries you traversed. You know the slope, the condensation patterns, the sound of the machinery.

You walk back through the warm, dim, wet, breathing dark, and you do not stop, and you do not linger, and you do not look directly at the places where the light plays tricks at the edges of the machinery, and when you emerge into whatever constitutes "outside" in whatever universe deposited you at the entrance to this place, you sit down, you drink water, you breathe unfiltered air, and you let the vertigo of having stood inside a process that does not know you exist slowly resolve into the memory of it.

Then you write everything down. Because the next person who goes in should know what you know: that the Steaming Galleries are not dangerous in the way that most places are dangerous. They are dangerous in the way that *indifference* is dangerous — in the way that a machine that does not know you are there will, if you are not careful, simply continue its work around the space where your body used to be.
