# Preparing for the Hollowlands

## The Core Problem: Sound

Let me be direct about what will kill you first, because it won't be the cold, or the altitude, or the fall. It will be the sound.

Everything else about the Hollowlands — the wind, the erosion, the living stone, the strange vegetation, the ancient human traces — is secondary to the fact that you are standing inside a geological instrument that is *playing itself*, constantly, at volumes that are not compatible with the long-term survival of human hearing. The text describes frequencies that vibrate fillings in teeth and make loose pebbles dance in orbit. That is not poetic exaggeration. That is a description of sound pressure levels that, sustained over hours and days, will cause permanent sensorineural hearing damage, tinnitus, vertigo, nausea, and a specific kind of cognitive degradation that makes it impossible to think clearly or sleep properly.

This is your primary survival concern. Not starvation. Not exposure. *Hearing destruction and the psychological collapse that follows from it.*

You must arrive with your hearing intact and you must leave with it as close to intact as possible.

### Hearing Protection

Do not bring standard foam earplugs. They will be insufficient and they may be dangerous — foam earplugs reduce higher frequencies more than lower ones, which means you'll filter out the wind-whistle and bird calls but leave the subsonic rumble and mid-register howl essentially untouched. The resulting frequency imbalance will be *more* disorienting than unprotected hearing, the way wearing one shoe is worse than being barefoot.

What you want is a set of **custom-molded musician's earplugs with flat attenuation** — the kind that reduce volume evenly across the frequency spectrum, by 15 to 25 decibels. These exist in our world for people who play in orchestras and rock bands. They preserve the shape of the sound while reducing its intensity. You will still hear the Hollowlands. You will hear it as a place of overwhelming, extraordinary sound. But you will hear it at a volume your auditory system can metabolize without being destroyed by it.

Bring three pairs. They will be lost, dropped into crevices, or knocked from your ears by gusts. The wind in the corridors is violent enough to dislodge anything that isn't physically anchored to your skull.

Beyond earplugs, consider **over-ear noise-canceling headphones** for camp and sleep periods. Active noise cancellation works by generating inverse sound waves — it is literally an anti-sound technology. In a place where sound is continuous and multi-directional, the results will be imperfect (ANC works best against steady-state, low-frequency noise, which is exactly what the subsonic rumble is). You won't achieve silence. You will achieve *tolerable*. That is enough. Tolerable is the difference between sleeping and not sleeping. Not sleeping is the difference between functioning and becoming a problem for whoever has to carry you out.

Pack **spare batteries** — many of them — for the noise-canceling headphones. They will drain faster than you expect. The sustained, high-amplitude low-frequency input will tax the circuitry.

## Wind

The wind is not a weather event here. It is a permanent condition. It blows *through* the landscape, meaning it is channeled, compressed, and accelerated by the corridors and basins. You are not dealing with open-air wind speeds. You are dealing with *venturi effects* — air forced through narrow passages and gaining velocity as it goes. The description says "velocities that seem impossible for air alone." Take that seriously.

### Physical Preparation

**You will be knocked over.** Multiple times. Possibly at the edge of a vertical drop. Prepare for this psychologically and physically.

Build your **core and leg strength** before arrival. You need the kind of balance and low-center-of-gravity stability that comes from rock climbing, martial arts, or trail running on unstable terrain. You will be walking across smooth, curved stone surfaces — the text explicitly states there are no sharp angles, everything is worn smooth — while being hit by lateral gusts that can change direction without warning. Two opposing currents meeting in a basin and spiraling upward is not a metaphor. It is a physical hazard. You could be lifted or thrown.

**Wear a low-profile climbing harness** at all times in corridor sections with vertical drops. The drops are sudden and the basins are "hundreds of meters across." If you are blown off a ledge, the smooth stone walls will offer nothing to grab — they have been scoured to a river-polished finish over geological time. Anchor yourself. Use pitons sparingly and with awareness that the stone may be softer or more friable than it looks; the "healed scars" of mineral seams are fault lines, and the very softness that makes the stone smooth also makes it unreliable for protection. Test every anchor point.

**Footwear** is critical. You need shoes with aggressive, high-friction rubber soles — approach shoes or technical climbing shoes with a Vibram-type compound — but you also need ankle support, because the terrain curves and undulates in ways that will roll an unsupported ankle. Look at what modern trail runners use for scrambling terrain: a mid-cut shoe with a sticky-rubber sole, moderate stiffness, and a secure lacing system that won't be loosened by wind or vibration. The vibration is real. The subsonic frequencies described in the text will transmitted through the ground into your feet, your legs, your skeleton. Laces will loosen. Velcro will wear. Choose shoes with a BOA dial or similar mechanical closure if available.

**Trekking poles** — the collapsible, carbon-fiber kind — will serve you in two ways: as stability aids in the wind, and as sensors for testing the ground in front of you. In a landscape of smooth curves and sudden drops, a pole extended ahead of you tells you where the floor falls away before your body does. Use them. Many hikers think poles are optional accessories. In the Hollowlands, a pole is a prosthetic sense organ.

## Altitude and Air

You are at 2,000 to 3,000 meters. The air is thin but "constantly renewed" by the wind. This is a paradox worth unpacking.

Thin air at altitude means reduced oxygen partial pressure. You will feel this as breathlessness on exertion, fatigue, headache, and impaired judgment. The constant wind means the air is well-mixed and fresh — you won't face the stagnation and CO₂ pooling that makes some enclosed high-altitude locations dangerous — but fresh air at altitude is still thin air. Your blood oxygen saturation will drop. Your cognition will slow. You will make worse decisions at precisely the moment the terrain demands better ones.

### Altitude Acclimatization

**Do not arrive at full elevation on day one.** If possible, spend three to five days at an intermediate altitude (1,500-2,000 meters) before ascending to the Hollowlands proper. Acclimatization is primarily a function of time, and it cannot be rushed without consequence.

Bring **acetazolamide (Diamox)**, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that accelerates acclimatization. Standard dose is 125-250mg twice daily. It has side effects — tingling in extremities, increased urination, altered taste of carbonated beverages — but it is well-established and effective. Consult a physician before travel. Have a supply of **dexamethasone** as well, as an emergency backup for severe altitude sickness, but understand that dexamethasone treats symptoms without addressing the underlying physiology. It is a bridge to descent, not a solution.

**Hydrate aggressively.** The wind will desiccate you faster than you realize. Evaporative water loss from your skin and respiratory tract increases dramatically in moving air, and the altitude increases your baseline respiratory rate. You will be breathing hard, constantly, and losing moisture with every breath. Drink water before you feel thirsty. By the time thirst registers, you are already behind. Aim for a minimum of four liters per day, more on active days. Bring a **hydration bladder with a dust cover on the bite valve** — the fine mist described in the text will coat every exposed surface, including the opening of a water bottle or the mouthpiece of a bladder. You do not want to be drinking mineral-laden condensate from rock surfaces. It may contain dissolved minerals that are harmless in geological quantities and toxic in biological ones.

## Navigation

This will be harder than you expect.

The Hollowlands are a landscape of smooth, curved basins and winding corridors. There are no peaks to use as landmarks — the text is explicit: "the peaks were eroded away long before anyone could name them." The horizon is "distant and wavy with heat." The terrain reads as a plateau of sculpted hollows, and the basins are similar enough to each other that one looks much like another, especially when you are inside them and the walls rise uniformly around you in every direction.

**GPS is your primary navigation tool.** Bring a dedicated GPS unit with preloaded maps of the region (to whatever extent cartographic data exists for this location) and a compass as backup. A compass will function normally here — there is nothing in the geological description to suggest magnetic anomalies — but compass navigation in a featureless landscape of smooth curves is disorienting because you have no visible landmarks to take bearings from. Use GPS waypoints. Mark your camp, mark every corridor entrance you explore, mark the locations of water sources (the condensation near the warm pillars) and any notable features.

**Leave cairns.** The ancient inhabitants did this. Follow their example. Stack three or four rocks at corridor junctions, at basin edges, at any point where you might need to retrace your steps. The wind is constant but not constant in direction — it "shifts" — so cairns will not blow over unless they are very poorly built. Use flat stones, stacked in a pyramid, with the heaviest at the base. Make them larger than you think necessary. They need to be visible at a distance in terrain that has the same color everywhere: deep ochre-red.

**Mark your path with flagging tape** where the wind allows it. Tie strips of bright-colored nylon to any protrusion — the small trees, the isolated pillars, the rare cracks in the smooth walls. The tape will flutter violently in the wind, making it highly visible. Bring far more than you think you'll need. The wind will shred it. Replace it as needed.

**Do not enter the deeper corridors without a clear objective and a turnaround time.** The corridors connect the basins and they wind and descend into vertical drops. The sound is worse in the corridors — "the roaring dark of a wind-corridor" is not a casual phrase. The wind is compressed and accelerated, the sound is deafening even through earplugs, and the darkness (in deeper sections where basin light doesn't reach) combined with the smooth walls and unpredictable floor geometry makes injury very likely. Go in. Find what you came to find. Come out. Do not wander.

## Temperature and Moisture

The description presents a thermal paradox: the rock holds mineral warmth from below, but you are at high altitude where ambient air temperatures are low. The wind creates a constant wind-chill effect that will strip body heat far faster than still air at the same temperature.

**The basins will be warmer than the corridors and ridges.** The basins trap heat. The wind corridors channel cold air. Plan to camp and rest in basins, not corridors. Seek out the leeward sides of rock walls — the sides "facing away from the dominant wind" where the moss grows thickest. The moss is an indicator. Where the moss is, the wind is less, and the moisture is more, and the temperature is more stable.

### Clothing System

**Layering is everything.** No single garment will serve you here. You need a system that can be adjusted minute by minute as you move between exposed ridges (cold, windy, dry), basin floors (warmer, less windy, humid), and corridors (cold, violently windy, dark).

**Base layer:** Merino wool, midweight. Merino wicks moisture, insulates when wet, and resists odor over multi-day use. Do not bring cotton. Cotton kills — that is not a cliché, it is a thermal engineering fact. Cotton absorbs water, loses all insulating value, and accelerates heat loss. In a place where condensation coats every surface and the wind never stops, cotton clothing will keep you wet and cooling from the moment you put it on.

**Mid layer:** A synthetic insulated jacket — Primaloft or similar — with a windproof face fabric. This is your warmth layer for camp and for the coldest corridor sections. It should pack small enough to live in your pack when you're active and generating heat.

**Outer layer:** A hardshell jacket and pants — waterproof, windproof, breathable. Gore-Tex or equivalent. This is your armor against the wind and the constant mist. The "fine, persistent mist that the air carries" will soak exposed fabric in minutes. A non-waterproof softshell will become a cold, heavy burden. You need a hard membrane. **Ventilation is critical** — the jacket should have pit zips and the pants should have thigh vents, because when you're working hard in the basins you will overheat inside a sealed shell. Open the vents to dump heat; close them when the wind hits.

**Extremities:** Bring a warm hat that fits under or over your ear protection. Gloves — a light pair for dexterity (fingertips exposed for handling rock, GPS, zippers) and a heavy insulated pair for cold periods. The rock may be warm to the touch (the pillars are described as warmer than surrounding stone), but your hands will be cold from wind-chill. Lips will crack and bleed. Bring a heavy-duty lip balm with SPF.

**Goggles or wraparound sunglasses.** The wind will drive fine mineral grit into your eyes. The mist will coat your lenses. The light — described as catching mineral seams "like healed scars" — will be bright and reflective off the smooth stone. Protect your eyes. Bring lens cleaning cloths (many) and a retainer strap so your eyewear isn't ripped from your face by a gust.

## The Strange Features

The description includes several phenomena that suggest the Hollowlands are not merely a geological oddity but something with deeper structure — patterns in the vegetation, wind that "organizes" at thresholds, warm pillars that shimmer, concentric rings in the ground cover that create disorienting moiré patterns.

**Do not assume these are benign.**

The concentric rings are disorienting — the text says "genuinely disorienting." This suggests a visual disruption significant enough to affect balance and spatial cognition. In a landscape where balance is already compromised by wind and smooth terrain, visual disorientation is a genuine hazard. If you feel the rings affecting your perception, look down at your feet, focus on the immediate ground, and move to a position where the pattern is out of your field of view. Do not try to "push through" it. The vestibular system does not respond to willpower.

The "organizing" wind at thresholds is described as lasting only a few seconds, and it's unclear whether it poses a physical danger or is merely uncanny. But be aware that any sudden coherence in air movement could mean a localized pressure change, a downdraft, or a vortex. If you feel the wind suddenly change character — become *focused* rather than chaotic — brace yourself and get low.

The warm pillars shimmer. Shimmering air near a heat source suggests significant thermal differential — hot air rising off a warm surface into cooler ambient air. This is physically normal. But the pillars are warmer than surrounding stone, they preferentially attract condensation, and they stand "like fingers of bone." Touch them if you're curious. The text says they are "warm to the touch." Note the temperature. If they are significantly warmer than ambient rock, there may be a geothermal source below — hot water, volcanic activity, something. This affects your safety: geothermal areas can release gases, create unexpected steam vents, or destabilize the rock beneath your feet. **Treat the pillars as potential geothermal indicators. Do not camp immediately adjacent to them until you understand the heat source.**

**Carry a thermometer and a gas detector** if you can obtain one. A simple carbon monoxide / hydrogen sulfide detector — the kind used in industrial settings or by spelunkers — is small, battery-powered, and could save your life if subsurface activity is venting toxic gas into the basins. The description says the vegetation is "lush in defiance of every other thing about this place." Lush vegetation in a geothermally active area is common — the warmth and mineral content of the soil support growth that the altitude and wind would otherwise prohibit. But geothermal venting also kills. Monitor your air.

## The Human Traces

The text describes ancient, minimal artifacts: a shaped flat stone (hand-axe), a cairn, a possible fire pit, and the worn hand-stone in a corridor wall.

**Do not disturb these.** Beyond the ethical imperative — these are artifacts of immense age and unknown cultural significance — there is a practical reason. The ancient inhabitants were here long enough to wear a stone smooth with their hands. They understood this place in ways you do not. The cairns they built may mark hazards as much as shelters. The fire pits may indicate safe zones. The hand-stone in the corridor may mark the one place in a roaring wind-tunnel where a human being can stand and brace and *endure*. Treat their traces as information, not as souvenirs.

If you find additional artifacts, document them: photograph, GPS-mark, describe. Do not move them. Do not collect them. The Hollowlands have been "doing nothing but breathing" for a very long time. The human traces are part of that breath. Leave them for the next traveler, and the one after that.

## Sleep

Sleep will be the hardest thing you do here.

The wind never stops. "Even sleep becomes an act of surrender," the text says. It means that you cannot sleep by overcoming the noise — you can only sleep by *accepting* it. This is not a mindfulness exercise. It is a physiological necessity. Your nervous system has a choice: maintain vigilance in the face of continuous, body-shaking sound (which it will do until you collapse from exhaustion), or habituate to the sound and allow sleep onset. You can help it along.

**Choose your campsite with care.** The deepest basins, leeward of the dominant wind, with the thickest moss on the walls. The description notes that in the deepest basins at night, the wind "drops, slightly" — not to silence, but to "a low and continuous moan that is almost bearable." Find that moan. It is the closest thing to sleep-friendliness the Hollowlands offer.

**Use both the earplugs and the noise-canceling headphones at the same time.** Earplugs first, then headphones over them. This is the "double protection" technique used by airline ground crew and military personnel exposed to sustained high-decibel environments. It will not make the sound go away. It will reduce it to a muffled, consistent drone — still present, still a pressure on your skull, but no longer sharp enough to prevent sleep onset.

**A sleeping mask** is essential. The bioluminescent mosses will create shifting patterns of pale blue-green light across the basin floor at night. The stars are extraordinary. Both of these things are beautiful and both will keep your visual cortex active when it should be powering down.

**Bring a sleeping pad with a high R-value** (insulation rating). Even though the rock is warm, you will lose heat through conduction to the ground. The pad creates a thermal barrier. A closed-cell foam pad (like a Thermarest Z-Lite) is ideal because it cannot be punctured, it provides reliable insulation, and it can be cut to fit inside a bivy sack if you're sleeping in a corridor.

**A bivy sack** rather than a tent. Tents will be torn apart by the wind in the corridors, and even in the basins they will flap, snap, and generate their own noise that adds to the already-overwhelming sound environment. A bivy sack is a minimalist waterproof shell that goes over your sleeping bag and pad. It is low-profile, wind-resistant, and quiet. It will not flap. It will keep the condensation off your sleeping bag — and there will be condensation, "every surface beads with water" — without adding the acoustic chaos of tent fabric in high wind.

**Expect to wake frequently.** This is normal. The human sleep cycle includes natural awakenings between sleep stages. In the Hollowlands, these awakenings will be more frequent and more fully conscious than usual, because the sound never lets your background vigilance fully disengage. Plan for fragmented sleep. Do not expect eight continuous hours. Four or five hours of broken sleep, supplemented by rest periods during the day (with eyes closed, earplugs in, lying in the warmest basin you can find), is a realistic sleep strategy for a multi-day visit.

## Food and Water

**Water** is available from the condensation, particularly near the warm pillars. But "available" does not mean "safe." The mineral content of the condensate is unknown. The mist carries whatever the wind picks up from the surrounding terrain — mineral dust, organic particulates, possibly trace elements from the geothermal sources that warm the pillars. **Filter and boil all water before drinking.** A gravity-fed water filter (like a Sawyer Squeeze or Katadyn BeFree) will remove particulates and biological contaminants. Boiling (at least one minute at this altitude, given reduced atmospheric pressure) will kill pathogens. For chemical contaminants — heavy metals, dissolved minerals — you would need activated carbon or a reverse osmosis system, which are heavier and bulkier. Assess the risk based on your expected length of stay. For a short visit (a few days), filtered and boiled water is probably sufficient. For extended stays, consider bringing a small supply of bottled water from a known source and supplementing with treated local water.

**Food** should be high-calorie, high-fat, and resistant to temperature fluctuation. The Hollowlands are warm enough that perishable food will spoil, but cold enough at night that food won't be cooked by ambient heat. Bring **freeze-dried meals** — they are lightweight, calorie-dense, require only hot water to prepare, and they are specifically designed for high-altitude use (they rehydrate better and taste better at elevation than standard dehydrated food). Supplement with **nuts, nut butter, hard cheese, cured meat, and dark chocolate** — all of which are calorie-dense, require no preparation, and tolerate temperature swings well.

Bring a **compact stove and fuel canister.** You will need hot water for meals, for water purification, and for psychological comfort. A warm drink in the deep basin at night, with the bioluminescent moss glowing and the stars overhead and the wind moaning at a bearable volume — that is not a luxury. That is a survival tool. It warms your core, it settles your stomach, and it gives your hands something to hold while the sound does what it does to the rest of you.

## Duration

**Do not plan to stay long.** This is not a place that rewards endurance. The cumulative effects of the sound, the wind, the altitude, the fragmented sleep, and the subtle cognitive pressures of the landscape's strangeness will degrade your performance progressively. The text describes "the strangeness accumulates if you stay." This is a warning disguised as an observation.

Plan for **three to five days** as a maximum. In that time, you can acclimate, explore the major basins, visit the hand-stone in the corridor, observe the vegetation patterns and the wind-threshold phenomena, photograph the artifacts, and leave before the sound has finished its slow work on your hearing and your mind.

If you feel your cognition changing — if the moiré patterns start looking meaningful, if the wind starts sounding like language, if the worn hand-stone starts feeling like it was placed there *for you specifically* — it is time to leave. These are the early signs of auditory fatigue, sleep deprivation, and sensory overload combining into something that, left unchecked, becomes genuine perceptual disturbance. The Hollowlands have been waiting for a very long time. They will wait for your return. Do not give them more of yourself than you can afford.

## Final Note

The text ends with an image of your hand finding the worn stone in the corridor wall "without being told where it is." There is something in the Hollowlands that reaches for human contact — not metaphorically, but through the accumulated weight of geology, wind, sound, and deep time pressing on a body that was built to feel things.

You will feel it. That is not something to prepare for. It is something to acknowledge in advance: that you are going to a place that will change you, slightly and permanently, in ways you will not be able to fully describe when you come home. The worn stone exists because someone, long ago, needed to hold something solid while the world roared around them. You will need that too. When you find it, hold it. Let the sound do what it does. And then go home, with your hearing as intact as you can manage, carrying something that doesn't show up on any packing list but is the real reason you went.
