# Preparing to Visit the Throat Works

This is a guide for an ordinary human — no special physiology, no augmented senses, no advanced training — who intends to spend meaningful time inside this structure and return intact. The Throat Works is not hostile in any deliberate way. It simply was not built for you, or for anyone as you are. Every preparation below is an answer to something the place will do to you if you arrive unready.

---

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

The single most important thing to grasp is that the Throat Works is not a ruin. It is not abandoned, even in its abandoned wings. It is *operating*. The turbines turn, the pumps pulse, the heat rises. You are not entering a dead place. You are entering something that is alive in the way a machine is alive — purposefully, rhythmically, indifferently. The heat you feel is its metabolism. The sound is its heartbeat. The moisture is its sweat. You are a foreign body entering a working system that does not know you exist and has no mechanism for accommodating you.

This matters because your instincts will keep trying to categorize the place as a building. It is not. It is an interior. There is no exterior experience here — no sky, no horizon, no weather in any sense you recognize. You have always been inside something, and you will always be inside something, for the entire duration of your visit. The psychological weight of this cannot be overstated. No amount of "knowing" you can leave actually helps when every sense reports: *there is no outside*.

## Phase 1: The Sound (Most Critical)

**Why it comes first:** The noise of the Throat Works will begin assaulting you the moment you enter. It does not build gradually. It is *there*, immediately, and it will not stop for the entire visit. This is the element most likely to end your trip early, cause genuine physical harm, or simply make you unable to think clearly enough to do anything else.

### What to prepare

**Custom-molded hearing protection.** Not foam earplugs. Not generic over-ear muffs. You need a dual-layer system:

1. **Inner layer:** Custom-molded silicone earplugs rated for at least 30 dB of broad-spectrum attenuation, fitted by an audiologist before your trip. These go in and stay in. They are your baseline. They reduce the constant roar to something bearable — not silent, never silent, but below the threshold where it causes pain, vertigo, or involuntary panic.

2. **Outer layer:** Over-ear electronic earmuffs with adaptive attenuation. These should allow some ambient sound through — you need to hear footsteps, dripping water, the crack of a falling branch, the scrape of something moving nearby — while clamping down on the high-amplitude peaks that hit when you round a corner into a focusing geometry. Good electronic muffs can compress a 110 dB shriek into something like 70 dB while still letting quieter sounds through at natural volume.

**Bring spare everything.** The humidity will degrade foam and adhesive within days. Pack your hearing protection in sealed waterproof bags between uses.

**Train yourself before you go.** Spend time in loud environments — industrial sites, concert venues, engine rooms — wearing your protection for hours at a stretch. Get used to the strange isolation of hearing the world through a filter. Learn how your balance and spatial awareness change when auditory input is dampened. In the Throat Works, you will be doing this 24 hours a day.

### The deeper problem: communication

You cannot speak and be heard. The residents know this; they developed sign language. You need your own solutions:

- **Waterproof notebooks and markers.** Simple, reliable, universal. Write what you need, show it to people. Bring many notebooks. Paper degrades in the humidity — use synthetic paper (like Rite in the Rain or Stone Paper) and permanent markers.

- **Pre-printed phrase cards** in the local sign language, if any documentation exists. Laminate them in waterproof pouches.

- **A basic vocabulary of the local sign language** learned before arrival. Even simple signs for *help*, *hot*, *cold*, *water*, *food*, *where*, *danger*, *thank you* will matter enormously. The residents will notice and appreciate the effort — and their response to you will be meaningfully different if you demonstrate that you've tried.

- **Light signals.** A small flashlight with a momentary switch can serve as a Morse-code-adjacent system in very dark zones. Agree on a simple protocol with your travel companions before entry.

- **Do not rely on radios or digital communication.** The electromagnetic environment inside the Throat Works is unpredictable. The iron and copper structure creates complex shielding and reflection patterns. Your phone will likely lose signal immediately. Radios may work in some chambers and not in others. If you bring them, test them on-site and consider them a bonus, not a plan.

### What it feels like

The sound is not merely loud. It is *spatial*. It bends and focuses and redistributes. In a long gallery, it rises to a frequency that resonates in your sinuses, your eye sockets, the fluid of your inner ear. Your vision blurs. You feel nauseous. You grip the wall and wait for it to pass — and it doesn't pass, exactly, but you round a corner and suddenly it's a low thrum and you can breathe again. The residents have mapped these acoustic zones over generations. You have no such map. You will walk into resonant chambers and be physically staggered by frequencies you can barely hear but can feel in your teeth, in your bones, in the wet tissue of your lungs.

This is why you need the hearing protection. Not for comfort. For *function*. Without it, within hours, you will be exhausted, disoriented, and irritable in ways that compromise every other aspect of your survival.

## Phase 2: The Heat and Moisture

### What to prepare

**Clothing is your primary interface with this environment, and it will fail you if you choose wrong.**

**Do not bring cotton.** Cotton absorbs moisture, holds it, and becomes a cold, heavy, chafing burden against your skin. It will also mold within days in this environment. Cotton will actively work against you.

**Synthetic base layers** — something like polypropylene or merino wool (yes, wool works; it wicks, it regulates temperature, and it resists odor far longer than synthetics) — worn close to the skin. These pull moisture away from your body and allow evaporative cooling to function, which matters more than you'd think in a place where the ambient humidity fights your sweat at every moment.

**Loose-fitting outer layers** of quick-drying synthetic fabric. You want airflow against your skin. Tight clothing traps the warm, humid air and turns your body into a greenhouse. The Throat Works is already a greenhouse. Don't be two greenhouses.

**A lightweight rain jacket or poncho** — not for rain, but for the weeping walls. You will walk through corridors where water slides down ceramic surfaces and drips from every rivet head. You will push through curtains of vegetation that dump accumulated moisture on you. The jacket is for keeping your core dry during transit between zones.

**Multiple changes of clothing**, sealed in waterproof bags. You will sweat through everything within hours. The humidity prevents drying. Your clothes will never fully dry unless you actively manage them — wringing, hanging near vents, using body heat. Plan for a rotation system: one set wearing, one set drying, one set resting.

**Footwear: waterproof boots with aggressive tread and ankle support.** The floors are corroded grating, wet ceramic, moss-covered metal, and occasional pools of standing water. Slip-and-fall injuries are among the most likely acute dangers. Your boots need to grip on wet metal. They need to drain if they take on water (ventilation ports, not fully sealed). They need to be comfortable enough for hours of walking. Break them in thoroughly before your trip — blisters in this environment become infected quickly.

**Anti-chafing products.** When everything is damp and you're walking for hours, skin-on-skin friction becomes a serious problem. Apply anti-chafe balm (like Body Glide) to inner thighs, underarms, and anywhere clothing rubs. Pack extras.

**Talc-free moisture-wicking powder** for feet and other problem areas at the end of each day. The constant dampness invites fungal infections — athlete's foot, jock itch, skin fold irritation — and prevention is far easier than treatment in this environment.

**Anti-fungal medication**, both topical (clotrimazole, miconazole) and oral (fluconazole), in your medical kit. Use the topical preventatively if you have a history of fungal issues. Monitor your skin daily for redness, itching, or unusual texture changes.

**Skin barrier cream** for hands. You will be touching corroded metal, wet surfaces, plant material, and biological growth constantly. A barrier cream (like Cavilon or simple petroleum jelly) protects against chemical irritation and moisture damage to the skin of your hands, which are your primary tools.

### The heat gradient

Remember: temperature is your compass in the Throat Works. Warmer means deeper. Cooler means nearer to the surface. **Buy a reliable digital thermometer** (not mercury — it won't survive the noise and impacts) and calibrate it before entry. Touch the walls. Log temperatures. Build your own mental map. If the wall is warmer than 40°C, you are in territory you should not be in without the residents' explicit guidance. If it's warmer than 50°C, you are in territory no one has sanctioned.

**Bring a insulated water bottle** — not for keeping water cold, but because any water you carry will warm to ambient temperature within an hour. Dehydration is a real risk, partly because the humidity tricks your body into thinking it's well-hydrated (your sweat doesn't evaporate, so the cooling feedback loop is disrupted). Drink deliberately, on a schedule, not just when thirsty.

**Electrolyte supplements.** You will sweat minerals steadily. Water alone won't replace what you lose. Dissolve electrolyte tablets in your water regularly.

## Phase 3: Light and Navigation

### The light problem

There is no consistent light. You move through zones of bright green-tinted sunlight, amber sodium vapor, flickering fluorescent, and deep blue-green bioluminescent twilight. Your eyes will never fully adapt. You will go from bright to dark and be temporarily blind. You will go from dark to bright and be temporarily blinded. This is a constant hazard.

**Bring a headlamp** — hands-free illumination is non-negotiable. Use a model with:
- Adjustable brightness (you need a low mode for dark zones and a high mode for navigation in unlit passages)
- Red light mode (preserves night vision, disturbs wildlife less)
- Waterproof rating of at least IPX7
- Long battery life or rechargeable with a sealed battery compartment

**Bring a handheld flashlight** as backup, brighter than the headlamp. You need this for signaling, for inspecting surfaces closely, for looking down long corridors. LED preferred — they're efficient and durable.

**Bring chemical light sticks.** They're waterproof, they need no batteries, they work in any orientation, and they're excellent for marking locations, marking trails, and providing ambient light in areas where you'll be stationary for a while. Color-code them: green for "safe/confirmed route," red for "warning/hazard," yellow for "base camp."

**Protect all lighting equipment from moisture.** Ziplock bags with desiccant packets. Silicone-sealed cases. Corrosion on battery contacts is your enemy.

### The navigation problem

The Throat Works is a labyrinth with an unreliable map. Corridors curve when they shouldn't. Rooms change their apparent dimensions. The geometry is, in some places, simply wrong.

**Do not trust your sense of direction.** The iron and copper structure will defeat magnetic compasses (the entire structure is one massive magnetic distortion). Use non-magnetic navigation:

- **Inertial navigation.** A quality pedometer and a reliable altimeter (barometric) can help you track distance traveled and depth changes. Combine these with temperature readings to build a three-dimensional map of your position.

- **Dead reckoning.** Before each excursion from a known base, confirm your starting point, note the direction by some reliable landmark (a specific pipe cluster, a unique corrosion pattern, a particular plant growth), and carefully track every turn and distance. This is tedious. It is also the difference between finding your way back and being lost in an abandoned wing.

- **Physical marking.** Bring a supply of brightly colored, non-toxic, waterproof tape or chalk sticks. Mark your path. Use consistent symbols: an arrow pointing back toward base, a number indicating how many turns since the last mark. **Agree on a marking system with your companions and stick to it religiously.**

- **Photograph everything.** Take pictures of junctions, unique features, distinctive corrosion patterns, and plant formations. Review them regularly. The Throat Works has a way of making everything look similar — warm, wet, green, corroded — and your memory will betray you.

- **Stay with the residents when possible.** They know the geometry, even the wrong geometry. They know which corridors lead where. They know which abandoned wings are safe and which are structurally compromised. Humility is a survival trait here.

## Phase 4: The Biological Environment

### The Green

The vegetation is not decorative. It is aggressive, opportunistic, and structurally significant. It pries apart rivets, buckles ceiling plates, and builds its own architecture over the manufactured one. In the abandoned wings, the plant growth is dense enough to constitute a genuine obstacle.

**Allergies and irritants.** You are entering an environment of unknown plant species that have adapted to manufactured substrate, constant heat, and perpetual moisture. They will produce pollens, volatile compounds, and contact irritants that you have never encountered and for which you may have untested reactions.

**Preparation:**
- Bring a full course of antihistamines (cetirizine or loratadine for daily prophylaxis; diphenhydramine for acute reactions)
- Bring an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen) if you have any history of allergic reactions to plants, and consider carrying one even if you don't — you've never been exposed to these species
- Avoid touching unfamiliar plants with bare skin. Use gloves. Observe first, touch second.
- Some of the broad-leafed plants have thick, fleshy stems that break easily and release milky sap. This sap is potentially an irritant. Wear long sleeves in heavy vegetation.
- The moss is everywhere and is relatively benign — but prolonged skin contact with wet moss in a warm environment can cause maceration (skin softening and breakdown). Don't sit or lie on mossy surfaces for extended periods without a barrier layer.

**Foraging (advanced, guide-recommended only).** The residents eat from this environment. They know what's safe. You don't. Do not eat anything without explicit confirmation from a local, ideally confirmed by a second local. Some of the fruiting bodies that grow in the abandoned wings may be edible; some may be toxic; some may be psychoactive in ways you are not prepared for. Trust the residents. They've had generations to learn.

### The Fauna

**Insects are unavoidable.** You will be bitten. You will share your sleeping space with things that have six or eight legs. Accept this mentally before you arrive, because the alternative is constant, exhausting revulsion.

**Prepare:**
- **Insect repellent (DEET-based or picaridin).** Apply to exposed skin and clothing. Reapply frequently — the humidity reduces the effective duration of repellents.
- **Permethrin-treated clothing.** Treat your outer layers with permethrin before the trip. It kills ticks and discourages mosquitoes, flies, and beetles on contact. It survives multiple washes.
- **A head net** for sleeping or for extended time in heavily infested zones. Yes, it's uncomfortable. It works.
- **A sealed sleeping system.** Your sleeping area must be enclosed. A hammock with an integrated mosquito net is ideal — it keeps you off the wet ground, off the corroded grating, away from ground-level insects and moisture. Seal the net completely. Tuck it under your body weight.
- **Antihistamine cream** (hydrocortisone) for bite reactions. **Antibiotic ointment** for any break in the skin that shows signs of infection. The warm, humid environment is ideal for bacterial growth.
- **A tick removal tool** and knowledge of tick-borne disease protocols. The vegetation zones will harbor ticks.

**The birds** are harmless but their songs will haunt you. Modified by the resonant geometry, a simple call becomes an alien chord. This is psychologically affecting in ways you don't expect — beauty mixed with wrongness, a constant reminder that you are in a place where the rules are different.

**The rats** are smart, adapted, and bold. Protect your food stores in sealed containers. Do not leave food accessible. Rats in the Throat Works have had generations to learn that human-occupied zones contain food, and they will investigate thoroughly.

**In the deep zones: amphibians.** Fat, pale, eyeless. If you encounter them, you are too deep. Turn back.

## Phase 5: The People

### How to approach them

The residents of the Throat Works are insular, self-sufficient, and organized around a way of life that predates your arrival and will continue after you leave. They are not hostile by nature, but they are cautious — and rightly so. Strangers in this environment are either resources or risks, and they will assess you quickly.

**Do:**
- **Signal your presence early and clearly.** Do not sneak. Do not appear suddenly around corners. The noise means they can't hear you coming, which means a silent appearance is startling and threatening. Make yourself visible. Wave. Use light signals. If you have any knowledge of their signs, use a greeting sign.
- **Approach slowly.** Move through their space with visible, non-threatening body language. Open hands, relaxed posture, unhurried movement.
- **Be clean and orderly.** The residents maintain the active systems with care and discipline. They will respect someone who appears organized, who manages their equipment, who doesn't leave debris.
- **Offer labor.** If you want access and trust, offer to help. Ask what needs doing. Carrying supplies, cleaning grating, clearing vegetation from intake vents — these are concrete ways to demonstrate that you're not just a burden.
- **Respect their sign language.** Learn at least a basic functional vocabulary. Even imperfect signing communicates respect far more than shouting (which they can't hear) or ignoring them (which they'll read immediately).
- **Bring gifts that matter.** Not trinkets. Things the Throat Works doesn't produce: fresh seeds from outside varieties, specialized tools, replacement parts for equipment, new books or information from the outside world, medical supplies. Ask before you bring — some gifts are inappropriate or unwanted.
- **Eat what they offer.** Refusing food is a significant social signal in most cultures, and the Throat Works is no exception. If they share, accept.

**Do not:**
- **Do not express pity.** They do not consider their deafness a disability. They do not consider their life impoverished. They have chosen this — or their ancestors chose it, and they have confirmed the choice by staying. If you look at them with the sadness of someone who "couldn't live like that," you will be identified as someone who cannot be trusted.
- **Do not romanticize their life.** They live inside a decaying machine in constant noise and damp. It is hard. They know it is hard. They don't need you to tell them it's beautiful or terrible. They need you to help with the pump seals or get out of the way.
- **Do not wander into restricted zones uninvited.** Some areas of the Throat Works are maintained for critical infrastructure. Some are structurally compromised. Some are sacred or private. Ask before going anywhere. Accept "no" without argument.
- **Do not discuss the strangeness — the wrong geometry — unless they raise it first.** The description says they don't discuss it often. They measure, they note, they keep working. If you show up asking questions about the walls bending, you'll be read as unstable or obsessed. Wait. Listen. If they trust you enough, they'll mention it.

## Phase 6: The Psychological Dimension

This is the section that most travelers will neglect, and it is the section that matters most.

### What the Throat Works will do to your mind

**Enclosure.** There is no outside. Even if you know intellectually that a world exists beyond the walls, you cannot see it, hear it, or feel it. The sky is a concept. The horizon is a memory. Every direction you look, there is a manufactured surface. This will press on you. It will press on you quietly at first — a vague discomfort, a feeling of being watched or trapped — and then, possibly, suddenly: a panic that you cannot reason your way out of, because your body knows what your mind refuses to accept. *There is no outside.* You are inside something. You have always been inside something.

**Prepare for this by:**
- Spending extended time in enclosed spaces before your trip — long cave visits, submarine tours, bunker explorations. Get a sense of your personal threshold.
- Developing a grounding practice — a breathing routine, a mantra, a physical anchor (a specific stone or token you carry) that reminds you of the outside world.
- Carrying a photograph of open sky, a horizon, an outdoor space. Not as a talisman against magic, but as a cognitive anchor. When the enclosure presses on you, look at the photograph and remind your body: *this exists, it is real, it is where you came from, it is where you will return.*

**Emptiness.** The Throat Works is full of rooms that should hold hundreds and hold none. The specific loneliness of that — of designed abundance meeting actual absence — will seep into you. You'll walk through a cathedral-sized chamber with four objects in it and feel the ghost of whatever was supposed to be there. You'll sense the intention in the architecture and the failure in the reality. This is a profoundly melancholy environment.

**Prepare for this by:**
- Understanding that this melancholy is a *response to a real thing*, not a personal failing. You are not depressed. You are reading the space accurately.
- Maintaining connections with your travel companions. Talk (write, sign, gesture) about what you're experiencing. Shared experience of the strange prevents it from becoming isolating.

**Disorientation.** The wrong geometry is real. Corridors that curve when they should be straight. Rooms that feel taller than they measure. This is not hallucination — it is either acoustic manipulation of your inner ear, slow structural deformation by the plant growth, or something else entirely. The effect is the same: your spatial reasoning, the faculty you rely on most heavily for navigating a built environment, becomes unreliable.

**Prepare for this by:**
- Trusting instruments over senses. Measure, don't guess. Mark, don't assume.
- Developing a habit of touch — running your hand along the wall, feeling for the curve your eyes can't see. The residents do this. It becomes instinct.
- Accepting that you will sometimes be lost, briefly, and that being lost in the Throat Works is recoverable if you have marking systems and discipline.

**The hum.** Even through your hearing protection, you will feel the structure's pulse. It is always there, a vibration in the floor, a trembling in the walls, a pressure in the chest. Over days, this becomes hypnotic. You will find yourself timing your breathing to it. You will find yourself moving to its rhythm without realizing it. The residents have lived with it for generations — it is the heartbeat of their world. For you, it will become either background or obsession. There is little middle ground.

## Final Note: The Mindset

The most important preparation is not a thing you pack. It is a decision you make before you enter.

The Throat Works is a mystery. It may be a building. It may be an organism. It may be something that has no analogue in any category you carry. The residents have lived inside this mystery for generations and made their peace with it by doing the only thing that makes sense: they keep the machines running, they tend the plants, they raise their children, they mark the walls, they measure the corridors, and they endure.

You are a visitor. You will not solve the Throat Works. You will not decode it. You will not be the one who finally understands why the corridors curve or what the turbines are actually for or what the structure was originally built to contain. If you go in looking for answers, the place will eat you — not literally, but it will consume your attention, your energy, your capacity to function, and you will leave exhausted and no wiser.

Instead, go in to *witness*. To feel the heat of the walls and know that you're mapping your depth by touch. To hear the shriek of focused sound and feel it in your sinuses and then turn a corner into silence. To push through a curtain of green and emerge into a cathedral of empty air where condensation drips from a corroded ceiling and a single shaft of sunlight cuts through the haze and illuminates the iridescent blue-black of oxidized copper. To sit with a resident who communicates in hand shapes you're beginning to understand, who offers you food from plants you couldn't name, who leads you through a corridor that curves when it shouldn't and touches the wall with a familiarity that makes you realize: this is not a labyrinth to them. This is home.

Go in to witness. Go in to help where you can. Go in humbly. And when you leave — when you finally climb toward the cooler walls, the thinner air, the light that comes from a sky instead of a gap — carry the Throat Works with you not as a puzzle to be solved but as a place that was, for a time, the whole world. Because that is what it is. That is what it has always been.

The whole world, warm and wet and loud and green, going on without you.
