# Preparing for the Rimlands: A Comprehensive Guide

---

## I. UNDERSTANDING WHAT YOU'RE WALKING INTO

Before you pack a single thing, you need to internalize the fundamental character of this place, because every preparation decision flows from it.

The Rimlands are not hostile in the way a desert or a jungle is hostile. Nothing here is trying to kill you. There are no predators of any meaningful size, no violent weather (at least not in the immediate, dramatic sense), no terrain so treacherous that it demands expert mountaineering skill. What the Rimlands are, instead, is *relentlessly, exhaustingly inhospitable*. It is a place where every single comfort you take for granted — warmth, breathable air that doesn't sting, ground that feels solid underfoot, water you can drink, shelter from the sky — has been removed. Not dramatically. Not violently. Just... withdrawn. The way a room becomes unbearable when someone takes away one chair at a time until you realize you're standing in an empty house.

The breathing of the land. That description is not poetic embellishment. The Rimlands sit in a geological state that produces a constant, low-frequency exhalation of moisture and mineral vapor from the rock itself. You will feel it on your skin. You will see it pool in the hollows of your gear. After an hour, your face will be slick with it. After a day, your equipment will carry a permanent film. This is not fog in any familiar sense — it is the earth itself transpiring, and it means that dryness is an impossibility here. Everything you bring will get wet. The question is not whether your gear will be damp, but whether it will still function when it is.

The cold. I need to be specific about this because "cold" is a word that means too many things. The Rimlands cold is not the dry, biting cold of a continental winter. It is wet, mineral-heavy, altitude-pressurized cold that infiltrates rather than stings. It gets into joints. It sits in the hollow of your chest and makes your breathing feel insufficient. At altitude, even mild exertion will leave you winded, and the cold amplifies this — your body is burning calories at an enormous rate just to maintain core temperature, and the thin air is giving you less oxygen per breath to fuel that combustion. This is not the cold that gives you frostbite (though that is possible); this is the cold that makes you stupid, slow, and eventually hypothermic in ways you won't notice until it's advanced.

And the ground. The ground is alive in the sense that it is still *being made*. Rock faces calve without warning. Shelves shift. Mineral crusts that look solid will shatter under your weight into sharp, geometric fragments that will cut through boot leather if you're not careful. The substrate between the pools is crushed mineral — not soil, not gravel, but something closer to broken glass bonded with clay. It shifts underfoot, rolls, slides. Walking here is exhausting not because the terrain is steep (it often isn't) but because every footfall is uncertain.

## II. CLOTHING AND BODY PROTECTION

**The Layering System (This Is Non-Negotiable)**

You need a layering system designed for sustained cold-weather activity in wet conditions. This is not optional or upgradable — it is the foundation of your survival.

*Base Layer:* Merino wool, ideally 200-weight for your core, 150-weight for extremities. You want something that retains insulating properties when wet, because wet you will be. Do not use synthetic base layers unless you have no choice — they wick moisture away from the skin but they do not insulate when saturated, and they develop odor fast enough to become a morale problem within days. Merino will keep you warm even when it's soaked through with mineral-laced condensation. Bring at least three sets and rotate them. They will never fully dry here, but wringing them out and hanging them inside your shelter overnight will restore enough warmth for the next day.

*Mid Layer:* A fleece or softshell insulating layer, ideally with a high collar that covers your neck. The Rimlands cold has a way of finding gaps — any exposed skin between your jacket collar and your face covering will become a source of constant discomfort. The mid layer should be breathable enough to prevent sweat buildup during exertion but substantial enough to trap heat during the long, stationary periods when you're observing or resting. A grid-fleece construction is ideal — it creates air pockets while allowing moisture vapor to escape.

*Outer Layer:* This is where most people will make their critical error. You do **not** want a hardshell. A hardshell in the Rimlands will create a condensation trap — your body heat and moisture will build up behind the waterproof membrane and saturate your inner layers from the inside out. What you want is a **waxed canvas or heavy-treated softshell** — something that sheds the constant mineral condensation and resists the acidic moisture without creating a vapor barrier. It will not be fully waterproof. This is the correct trade-off. You want to be damp but warm, not dry but sweaty-then-frozen. Treat the outer layer with a beeswax-based waterproofing compound before you leave, and reapply it every few days. The mineral residue in the Rimlands precipitation will degrade any fluorocarbon DWR coating within hours.

*Pants:* Same principle — insulated, treated softshell, not hardshell. Reinforced knees and seat are essential, because you will be crouching, kneeling, and sitting on mineral-crusted rock constantly. The sharp edges of fractured precipitate will shred standard nylon within a day. Look for pants with Cordura reinforcement panels. Tuck them into your gaiters without exception — mineral-laced water running down into your boots is a fast track to macerated skin and trench foot.

**Head and Face**

A balaclava is not optional. The Rimlands wind is not constant, but when it shifts — and it shifts often — it carries mineral particulate that stings exposed skin. You need something that covers your nose, mouth, cheeks, and ears but can be pulled down or adjusted without removing your gloves. A balaclava with a breathing vent or a loose weave over the mouth area is essential; without it, your exhaled moisture will freeze against the fabric and create an ice mask over your airway. This is not hypothetical. In sustained cold at altitude, a poorly designed face covering will become a breathing hazard.

A brimmed hat or the hood of your outer layer should extend forward enough to shield your eyes from the mineral condensation that drips from every overhead surface. Rimlands "rain" is not rain — it is a slow, persistent weeping from the rock and the sky, and it carries a mineral content that will irritate your eyes if it contacts them repeatedly. Sunglasses with side shields are worth carrying for the rare clear mornings when the haze parts and the high-altitude UV hits the reflective mineral pools.

**Hands**

Leather gloves with a fleece liner. Not insulated ski gloves — you need dexterity for handling gear, collecting samples, managing your stove, and navigating the sharp terrain. Leather resists the mineral abrasion better than synthetic materials and develops a wax coating from the environment itself over time. Bring two pairs. The first pair will be stiff with mineral deposits within a week; the second pair is your backup. Liner gloves for fine motor tasks (adjusting camera equipment, tying knots, writing in your journal) are essential — you will need to remove your outer gloves at least once an hour for some small task, and exposed fingers in Rimlands cold will lose function in minutes.

**Footwear**

This is the single most important gear decision you will make.

You want **heavy leather mountaineering boots with a stiff, Vibram-type sole** — not a flexible hiking boot, not a trail runner, not a rubber muck boot. The stiff sole serves two purposes: it protects your feet from the sharp mineral crusts and fractured precipitate that cover the ground, and it provides a stable platform on the uneven, shifting substrate. The leather should be full-grain and pre-treated with a heavy application of leather conditioner and wax — you are trying to create a barrier between your feet and the acidic condensation that will soak through anything less substantial.

Ankle support is critical. The basin floor is tilted, uneven, and covered in loose mineral aggregate that shifts underfoot. A rolled ankle here, miles from any medical help, is a trip-ending injury.

Waterproof gaiters that extend to just below the knee are mandatory. The shallow pools are everywhere, their depth deceptive — what looks like a few centimeters of silver water over rock is often a soft, mineral-slurry pit that will swallow your boot to the ankle if you step in without looking. Gaiters keep the mineral-laden water out of your boots and protect your lower legs from the sharp edges of fractured precipitate.

Bring camp shoes — lightweight, insulated sandals or booties for use inside your shelter. Your feet will need to dry out at the end of each day, and keeping them sealed in boots for extended periods will lead to skin breakdown regardless of your sock quality.

## III. BREATHING AND AIR QUALITY

The particulate-laden air of the Rimlands presents a chronic respiratory challenge that is easy to underestimate and dangerous to ignore.

The haze is fine enough to pass through most standard dust masks. You need at minimum an **N95-rated respirator with replaceable cartridges** — the kind used in industrial painting or welding, not the thin surgical-style masks that provide minimal filtration. The particulate carries sulfur compounds and industrial-origin chemicals that will irritate your bronchial passages over hours and days of exposure. Without filtration, you will develop a persistent cough within the first 24 hours, a cough that will worsen each day and eventually interfere with your sleep.

At the higher elevations of the basin rim, the air is thin enough that you will notice reduced stamina. If you are coming from sea level, acclimatize gradually. Spend at least two days at a lower elevation before ascending to the basin floor. Altitude sickness in the Rimlands is complicated by the chemical composition of the air — the particulate matter reduces the effective oxygen content further, and the sulfur compounds cause mild vasodilation that can mask the early symptoms of altitude distress. By the time you feel truly ill, you may be significantly impaired.

Carry a **personal oxygen concentrator or supplemental oxygen canister** if you have any respiratory condition at all. The Rimlands are not the place to discover that your asthma is altitude-sensitive or that your lungs don't tolerate particulate. If you are in good health, you will manage without supplemental oxygen, but having it available is an insurance policy against the unexpected.

The mineral tang in the water vapor — that sting at the back of the throat — is not just unpleasant. Over time, it is an irritant to the mucous membranes of your upper respiratory tract. Stay hydrated (more on this below), use a saline nasal spray twice daily to keep your nasal passages moist and clear, and rinse your mouth with clean water after extended exposure. Your body will initially produce excess mucus as a defense mechanism; this is normal. What is not normal is persistent throat pain, chest tightness, or blood-tinged mucus, all of which are signals to withdraw to lower elevation and seek medical attention.

## IV. WATER AND HYDRATION

**Do not drink the Rimlands water.**

This needs to be stated with absolute clarity and no ambiguity. The water emerging from the rock, pooling in the shallow basins, and condensing on every surface is laced with mineral compounds — including sulfur, heavy metals, and unidentified chemical agents — at concentrations that are harmful to human consumption. The pH is shifted enough to cause gastric distress. The mineral precipitate that forms where the water evaporates is evidence of dissolved solids at levels that your kidneys cannot process safely.

You must carry all your drinking water. Calculate your needs at **a minimum of four liters per day** for the Rimlands environment — the combination of altitude, cold, dry air (despite the moisture, the thin air strips moisture from your lungs and skin rapidly), and physical exertion in uneven terrain will dehydrate you faster than you expect. Cold-weather dehydration is insidious because you don't feel thirsty the way you do in heat, but your body is losing water through respiration (every plume of visible breath is lost moisture), through exertion, and through the low humidity at altitude.

**Water purification does not solve the Rimlands water problem.** Filtration removes particulates and biological contaminants; it does not remove dissolved mineral compounds or chemical agents. UV purification is similarly ineffective against chemical contamination. Boiling will concentrate the minerals rather than remove them. You are entirely dependent on carried water or melted snow from the basin rim — and even snow should be treated with caution, as the atmospheric particulate will have deposited on any snowpack. If you must melt snow, collect it from the upper rim where the fresh snowfall is thinnest and least contaminated by the persistent haze, and allow any visible mineral residue to settle before filtering through a fine-mesh filter as a precaution.

Pack your water in insulated, rigid-sided containers — hydration bladders will freeze in the Rimlands cold unless you keep them inside your jacket, which is uncomfortable and awkward. Wide-mouth Nalgene bottles or metal canteens are preferable. Insulate them with neoprene sleeves. Keep one bottle inside your jacket at all times; swap bottles every few hours. A frozen water bottle at 4 AM when you're shivering in your shelter is a misery that is easily prevented.

## V. SHELTER

There is no natural shelter in the Rimlands. The basin is open, the walls are raw inclines of calving rock, and the weather does not come in dramatic storms but in slow, grinding shifts of wind and particulate that can reduce visibility and temperature without warning.

**A four-season, freestanding tent with a full rainfly is the minimum.** Do not bring a three-season tent. Do not bring a bivy sack. Do not bring a tarp system unless you are an experienced野外shelter builder and fully comfortable with improvised construction in high winds. The Rimlands wind is not constant but it is forceful when it arrives — it comes in long, slow surges that build over minutes, press against the basin walls, and funnel across the tilted floor with enough sustained force to deform a tent pole and collapse a non-freestanding shelter.

The tent must be set up on the mineral substrate, which means staking is problematic — standard stakes will not hold in crushed mineral aggregate. Bring **screw-in tent anchors** (the kind designed for snow or sand camping) and supplement them with rocks — you will have no shortage of rocks, though their sharp edges will abrade tent fabric. Lay a **footprint or groundsheet** beneath your tent floor; the mineral substrate is abrasive enough to wear through standard tent floors in a matter of days.

Inside the shelter: an **insulated sleeping pad with an R-value of at least 5.0** (you are sleeping on cold mineral ground that will pull heat from your body relentlessly), a **four-season sleeping bag rated to at least -20°C** (the Rimlands night temperatures, combined with the wet cold, demand serious insulation), and a **sleeping bag liner** (silk or merino — this extends the effective temperature range of your bag and is far easier to wash than the bag itself).

A small, efficient **white gas or isobutane-propane stove** is essential for melting snow for water, heating water for meals and hot drinks, and providing a brief period of psychological comfort at the end of each day. The Rimlands do not have wood to burn. There is no vegetation large enough to sustain a fire, and the waxy, mineral-saturated plants that exist would produce toxic smoke. A stove is your only source of heat beyond your own metabolism. Carry sufficient fuel — calculate your needs at approximately 100ml of white gas per day (cooking + melting snow + a brief morning warm-up). White gas performs better in cold conditions than canister fuel, which loses pressure in low temperatures. Bring a **secondary canister stove** as a backup with at least two spare canisters — if your primary stove fails, cold-weather dehydration and hypothermia will follow faster than you think.

A **portable weather shelter or wind screen** for use during daytime breaks is worth its weight. When you stop to observe, eat, or rest, your body temperature drops rapidly in the Rimlands cold. A lightweight, foldable windscreen that creates even a small pocket of still air around you will dramatically extend your comfortable working time between shelter visits.

## VI. NUTRITION

Your caloric needs in the Rimlands will be significantly higher than your normal intake. The combination of cold, altitude, and terrain demands an estimated **4,000–5,000 calories per day** for an average adult engaged in moderate activity. Underestimating this is one of the most common mistakes travelers make in extreme cold environments, and the Rimlands compound the problem because the food must be lightweight, compact, and calorie-dense — you are carrying everything in.

*Breakfast:* Instant oatmeal with added butter powder, powdered milk, and a generous portion of nuts or seeds. Hot chocolate or sweetened tea (the psychological value of a hot, sweet drink in Rimlands cold cannot be overstated). The hot water for your breakfast also serves to warm your hands and face — hold the mug close, let the steam rise against your balaclava, and take those sixty seconds of comfort seriously. Morale is a survival resource.

*Lunch (on the move):* High-calorie bars, nut butter packets, hard cheese (if temperatures are cold enough to prevent spoilage — in the Rimlands, they almost certainly are), salami or cured meat, and dark chocolate. Eat frequently, even when you don't feel hungry — your body is burning fuel at a rate that will deplete your reserves before your hunger signals catch up.

*Dinner:* Freeze-dried meals, supplemented with olive oil (carry it in a small, leak-proof squeeze bottle — adding a tablespoon of olive oil to any freeze-dried meal adds roughly 120 calories of fat that your body desperately needs for thermogenesis). Instant soup with added miso paste. Ramen noodles with dried vegetables and powdered protein. The goal is maximum caloric density per gram of food weight.

*Supplements:* A daily multivitamin, vitamin D (you will have minimal sun exposure through the haze), electrolyte tablets (for your water), and iron supplements if you are prone to anemia. The Rimlands environment is metabolically demanding in ways that will deplete micronutrient stores faster than normal.

*Emergency ration:* Carry at least one day's worth of emergency food — high-calorie, no-cook-required bars or gels — in a sealed bag inside your jacket. If you are separated from your pack, caught in a weather event, or unable to use your stove, this ration keeps you alive.

## VII. PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION

This section is not optional, and it is not less important than the gear sections above.

The Rimlands will do something to your mind that most travelers are not prepared for. It is not danger, and it is not beauty (though it is, in its own way, devastatingly beautiful). It is *isolation of a kind that has texture*.

The silence. When the birds are not calling — and they are not always calling — the silence in the Rimlands is not the absence of sound. It is the presence of a quiet so deep that you can hear your own blood moving. Your heartbeat. The creak of your jacket as you shift your weight. The tiny, constant cracking of mineral crusts settling. You will become aware of your own body in a way that is not comfortable — not because anything is wrong, but because there is simply nothing else to attend to. The landscape does not demand your attention the way a forest or a city does. It does not move quickly enough, or change often enough, or produce enough stimuli to occupy a human mind that evolved in noisy, crowded, complex environments.

This produces, over hours and days, a specific psychological state. Some people describe it as peace. Some describe it as dread. Most describe it as something in between — a awareness that the landscape is vast, and old in its newness, and entirely indifferent to your presence, and that this indifference is not hostile but simply *true*. You are a very small, very warm thing in a very large, very cold place that was not made for you and does not notice you. For some people, this is liberating. For others, it is destabilizing.

**Bring a journal.** Write in it daily. Not because the Rimlands are worth documenting (they are, but that is not the point) but because the act of writing forces your mind to organize and articulate experience, and this provides a structure that the landscape itself does not. Without this structure, you may find that your sense of time degrades — the days blur together because they are, in their broad outline, identical. Cold, wet, mineral grey, vast, silent. The journal creates the differences.

**Bring something familiar.** A photograph. A book you've read before (not a new book — you want the comfort of recognition, not the stimulation of novelty). A small, useless, personally meaningful object. The Rimlands will strip away your context — your social role, your daily routines, your ambient noise, your visual reference points — and in its absence, you need an anchor. Something that reminds you of who you are and where you come from. This is not sentimentality. This is maintenance of psychological integrity in an environment that does not provide it.

**Establish a routine.** Wake at the same time each day. Perform the same sequence of tasks: water, food, gear check, shelter assessment,出去观察, return, eat, journal, sleep. The Rimlands do not impose structure on your day — there are no work hours, no social obligations, no appointments. If you do not impose structure yourself, the days will dissolve into a formless grey continuum that will wear on you faster than the cold.

**Set a firm turnaround date before you go.** Decide in advance how many days you will spend in the basin and commit to it. The Rimlands are not a place where you should be making day-by-day decisions about when to leave, because the same indifference that makes the landscape feel vast and empty will also make departure feel unnecessary. Why leave? There is nothing pulling you away and nothing pushing you forward. The land doesn't care. Set your date. Stick to it.

## VIII. NAVIGATION AND SAFETY

The Rimlands basin is vast enough to lose yourself in, and featureless enough that visual landmarks are unreliable. The basin walls look the same from every angle — the same striated bands of grey-green and rust-red, the same sharp fracture patterns, the same weeping seeps. The pools look the same. The substrate looks the same. In the perpetual dim haze, distance and direction become ambiguous.

**Bring a GPS device with fresh batteries (or a charged battery bank with solar charging capability) and a compass.** GPS is your primary navigation tool; the compass is your backup. A paper map of the basin — if one exists for your entry point — is essential. Do not rely on your phone's GPS alone; extreme cold drains batteries with shocking speed, and a phone that dies at -15°C is a dead device until it warms up.

**Mark your shelter location.** Use reflective markers, colored flagging, or GPS waypoints. When you go out to explore, you need to be able to return to your shelter in reduced visibility — the haze can thicken quickly, and the basin's uniformity makes "I'll just head toward that rock formation" a dangerous strategy when every rock formation looks like every other.

**Tell someone your exact plans.** Your entry point, your expected route, your shelter location, your turnaround date, and your expected exit date. Leave this information with someone who will take action if you don't check in by a specified date. The Rimlands are not visited by rescue teams. If something goes wrong, your survival depends on your own preparation and the person back home who knows to raise the alarm.

**Carry a first-aid kit** focused on the specific injuries the Rimlands are most likely to produce: cuts from mineral crust (antiseptic, butterfly bandages, gauze, medical tape), blisters from wet boots and uneven ground (moleskin, antiseptic), altitude headache and mild altitude sickness (acetazolamide — consult your physician before departure), hypothermia (chemical hand warmers, an emergency bivy that reflects your body heat), and gastrointestinal distress from any accidental water exposure (loperamide, oral rehydration salts, anti-nausea medication).

## IX. TIMING AND SEASONALITY

The Rimlands are dynamic. The calving of the basin walls, the shifting of the pools, the expansion of the mosses — all of this happens on timescales that make no practical difference to a short-term visitor. What does matter is the seasonal behavior of the haze.

During the period when the prevailing winds shift (and the Rimlands text implies this happens — "thickening when the wind changes direction, thinning just enough on rare clear mornings"), the basin experiences brief windows of reduced particulate. These windows are precious. They bring cleaner air, better visibility, more light, and — critically — the psychological relief of seeing that "impossibly, heartbreakingly blue" sky above. Plan your trip to coincide with the period when these clearings are most likely. Research the wind patterns. Talk to anyone who has been before. Time your observation periods, your longer explorations, and your most demanding physical activities for the clearer hours.

During the thickest haze periods, conserve energy, stay close to shelter, and focus on journal writing, gear maintenance, and rest. Trying to navigate or explore in heavy haze is disorienting and energy-wasting.

## X. WHAT TO LEAVE BEHIND

You cannot carry everything. The Rimlands demand a ruthless prioritization.

Leave behind: heavy camera equipment (a single lightweight camera with a versatile lens is enough — the Rimlands are photogenic but they do not reward obsessive photography), books you haven't read (you want the familiar, not the new), excessive clothing options (you will wear the same rotation every day), any electronics that don't serve a critical survival or navigation function (a headlamp is essential; a music player is a luxury, but one worth considering for its psychological value — though bring headphones, because the Rimlands silence, once you have adapted to it, becomes something you will not want to shatter with tinny speakers), and your ego.

Leave behind the assumption that you will master this place, or understand it, or conquer it. The Rimlands are a first draft of a world, still being written. You are not the author. You are, at best, a marginal note in pencil. The land breathes, and you are standing in its exhalation, and when you leave, it will continue without interruption. The mineral pools will keep weeping from the rock. The lizards will keep hunting. The birds will keep keening. The haze will keep shifting. You were there. You were small. You were warm. You were alive, for a while, in a place that was barely alive itself and entirely uninterested in your opinion about it.

Prepare well. Go carefully. Come back.

## SUMMARY: THE NON-NEGOTIABLE ESSENTIALS

1. **Waxed softshell outer layer** (not hardshell), merino base layers (3 sets), fleece mid layer, reinforced pants
2. **Heavy leather mountaineering boots**, stiff-soled, wax-treated, with screw-in tent anchors and gaiters
3. **Balaclava, leather gloves (2 pairs), liner gloves, brimmed hat**
4. **N95 respirator** with replacement cartridges
5. **Four-season tent**, screw-in anchors, footprint, insulated sleeping pad (R≥5.0), four-season sleeping bag (-20°C rated), sleeping bag liner
6. **White gas stove** + backup canister stove, sufficient fuel, metal canteens with insulating sleeves
7. **4+ liters of carried water per day** — do not drink the Rimlands water
8. **4,000–5,000 calories/day** of calorie-dense, lightweight food; olive oil; emergency ration
9. **GPS + compass + paper map**; reflective shelter markers
10. **Journal, familiar personal item, structured daily routine, firm turnaround date**
11. **First-aid kit** focused on cuts, blisters, altitude, hypothermia, GI distress
12. **Headlamp** with extra batteries (kept warm inside your jacket — cold-drained batteries are useless)
13. **Saline nasal spray, multivitamins, vitamin D, electrolyte tablets**

Bring all of this. Weigh your pack. If it is too heavy, cut something — but do not cut from the survival list. The Rimlands do not negotiate. They do not care how fit you are, how experienced you are, or how much you want to see the rare blue sky through the thinning haze. They are a cold, wet, mineral, breathing basin in the upper crust of the earth, and they will let you stand inside them for exactly as long as your preparation allows and not one moment more.
