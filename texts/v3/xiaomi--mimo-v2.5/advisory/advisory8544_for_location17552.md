# Preparing for The Lachrymatory

---

## The First Thing to Understand

You are going somewhere that is actively hostile to every biological process that keeps you alive, and it is not hostile *on purpose*. It is hostile the way deep space is hostile — through indifference so total it constitutes a kind of innocence. The Lachrymatory is not trying to kill you. It simply has never encountered a thing like you and has no architecture, biological or built, that accommodates your specific fragility. You are warm, you are soft, you breathe wet air into lungs that assume predictable temperatures, you see with eyes calibrated for a sky that doesn't exist here. Everything about you is a problem that this place will not pause to solve.

Preparation, then, is not about comfort. Comfort is not on the table. Preparation is about the specific, narrow bandwidth of conditions under which your body can continue to function long enough to witness what you came to witness and return from it.

---

## Thermal Survival: The Primary Problem

The cold is not cold. Let's be precise about this. The description says it would crystallize the moisture in your lungs on your first breath. That means you are looking at ambient temperatures where water transitions to ice almost instantly on contact with any surface at human body temperature. This is not "bring a good jacket" territory. This is the thermal regime of Neptune's upper atmosphere, of Pluto's surface, of places in your own solar system where human survival requires the same technology stack as orbital spaceflight.

**Your suit must be a spacecraft.** Treat it that way. It needs:

- **Active heating throughout**, powered by something robust — a compact radioisotope thermoelectric generator or a high-density chemical battery system with redundant cells. Electric heating elements alone will not suffice; you need something that generates *waste heat* as a primary function and channels it into the suit's microclimate. The heating system must be capable of maintaining core temperature against ambient conditions that are trying to extract your body heat at every surface simultaneously, through conduction (the water), convection (the wind at altitude), and radiation (the total darkness means your thermal signature is screaming into a void).

- **Full-body insulation** at a level comparable to what polar submarine crews wear in arctic surfacing operations, but waterproof. This is the paradox: you need extreme insulation, which typically means trapped air layers, which means bulk, which means reduced mobility in an environment where every surface slopes and is slick with mineral water. Modern aerogel-based insulation fabrics would be the starting point — the highest R-value-per-inch materials your preparation budget can access — but they must be encased in a waterproof shell that doesn't compromise their loft when saturated. Test this extensively before departure. Find out what happens to your insulation when it is continuously waterlogged for hours. Then solve that problem. Then test it again.

- **Sealed extremities.** Your hands are a crisis. You need dexterity — there will be surfaces to grip, equipment to manipulate, ledges to navigate — but fully insulated gloves that maintain dexterity at these temperatures do not exist in standard equipment. You will need custom-mitted solutions with heated fingertips and a grip surface that functions on wet, mineral-coated stone. Expect to sacrifice some dexterity. Practice operating your essential equipment with reduced hand function before departure.

- **A heated visor or face seal** that prevents frost formation on your optical surfaces and maintains a breathing pocket of warm, humid air around your nose and mouth. The visor must also manage the constant water that will sheet across it — some form of hydrophobic coating or active water-shedding mechanism. Visibility will be poor regardless. Accept this.

**Boot design is critical.** You are walking on wet, dark stone that is actively forming — some of it still cooling from recent basaltic emergence, some of it lethally cold, some of it coated in mineral frost that forms and cracks in slow cycles. The boots need:

- Insulation comparable to the rest of the suit
- A sole with aggressive, multi-directional traction designed for wet rock — think approaches used in deep cave exploration or arctic mountaineering, but with the understanding that the rock itself may be *shifting* beneath you at rates imperceptible to human senses but real enough to compromise your footing on a timescale of minutes
- A thermal barrier in the sole, because some surfaces will be warm enough to damage standard insulation on contact, and others will be cold enough to drain heat through the sole by conduction

## Breathing: The Second Crisis

The air is thin. The description notes the altitude by its effect on sound propagation — carrying further, distorting at edges — and by its pressure, which your body would feel as inadequacy. You are likely at an effective altitude equivalent to somewhere in the death zone, or close to it, though the atmospheric composition itself may differ from Earth standard in ways that make the comparison approximate.

- **Supplemental oxygen** is probably necessary, delivered through a rebreather system rather than an open-circuit setup — you cannot afford to waste the warm, humid air your lungs need. A closed-loop breathing system with a CO₂ scrubber and a humidifier/heater on the intake would allow you to breathe processed air at a manageable temperature and pressure while extending your supply. Calculate your metabolic needs carefully. Cold environments increase caloric burn. Thin air increases respiratory rate. These effects compound.

- **Humidification is not optional.** The text is explicit: the cold would crystallize moisture in your lungs. This means the air your suit delivers to your breathing pocket must be warm *and* humidified to a level where it will not cause immediate condensation and crystallization in your airways. This is the same engineering problem faced by high-altitude aviation life support, but worse, because the ambient moisture is paradoxically high (the atmosphere carries more water than it should be able to hold) while the temperature is low enough to freeze that moisture instantly on any surface at biological temperature. Your breathing system needs to take in this ambient moisture-laden air, warm it above the condensation threshold, and deliver it in a steady flow. A failure in the heating element of your breathing system is not a "return to base" scenario. It is a "you have seconds" scenario.

- **Expect the air to taste of minerals.** The water everywhere carries dissolved mineral content, and the air, saturated with this water, will carry it too — not as liquid but as an aerosol, a fine mineral mist that your respiratory system will encounter constantly. Your suit's air filtration should handle particulates, but expect some mineral deposition in your nasal passages and throat regardless. Carry saline solution for post-exposure irrigation. Consider whether the mineral content is toxic at the concentrations you'd inhale. Research this *before* departure, because you will not be able to research it there.

## Vision: Working in Absence

There is no sky. There is almost no light. The only illumination comes from below — a dim, greenish phosphorescence produced by the fauna grazing on mineral films in the deep shafts and reservoir-lakes beneath the city. At surface level, this means the world is painted in shades of drowned green and absolute black, with the green intensifying near any opening, drainage channel, or shaft entrance.

You need to see. But you also need to not blind yourself or everything around you.

- **Adapted night vision first.** Give yourself time — hours, if possible, in darkness before your first exposure — to allow your natural scotopic vision to adapt as fully as it will. At these light levels, your color vision will be essentially absent. You will see in monochrome, in shades of green-tinged gray. Accept this. Color is not information here.

- **Active illumination as a backup, not a primary.** The fauna are likely sensitive to light in ways you cannot predict. Sudden illumination could trigger defensive responses from creatures whose bodies are armored in mineral chitin and who move with "the ponderous authority of geology given a nervous system." You do not want to startle something that weighs as much as a small building and has never evolved a fear response because it has never needed one. If you must use a light, use a dim, red-filtered source at the lowest intensity that provides useful vision. Better yet, use an infrared camera paired with a head-mounted display that converts the near-infrared spectrum to a visible image — you can see without producing visible light.

- **Accept disorientation.** In total darkness with no sky, no horizon, and no consistent spatial references, your vestibular sense will rebel. You will not know which way is up with confidence, especially on the sloping, irregular surfaces. The faint green light from below will help anchor your sense of "down," but it will also create confusing reflections on wet surfaces that may invert your perception of depth. Move slowly. Use three points of contact on every surface. Trust your feet more than your eyes — the slope of the ground is information your boots can provide more reliably than your vision.

## Sound: The Pressure of Hearing

The sound environment is described as a permanent, multi-layered roar: water, fauna, architecture, geology. All frequencies. All the time. This is not a quiet place with occasional loud events. This is a place where silence does not exist and has never existed, where the acoustic environment is as constant and unavoidable as the water itself.

- **Hearing protection is essential, but selective.** You need to attenuate the sound pressure levels to a range that won't cause hearing damage or cognitive overload — the human auditory system is not designed for continuous broadband noise at the levels described. But you cannot isolate yourself completely, because the sounds are information. The clicks and scrapes of the fauna tell you where they are. The deep structural vibrations tell you what the city is doing. The sound of water tells you where drainage channels are, where pools are forming, where the ground slopes. An electronic hearing protection system with active noise cancellation and selective amplification — something that compresses the overall sound level while preserving the ability to detect specific frequency patterns — is the right approach. Think of it as acoustic dimming rather than acoustic muting.

- **Subsonic vibration will affect you physically.** The deep, tectonic resonance of a world still being born will transmit through the ground into your skeletal structure, into your chest cavity, into your teeth. This is not dangerous in itself, but it is disorienting and fatiguing over time. A layer of vibration-dampening material in your boot soles and suit joints will help. Accept that you will feel the place in your bones. There is no padding the world entirely out of your body.

- **Protect your equilibrium.** Continuous multi-directional sound at high levels can disrupt the vestibular system, causing dizziness, nausea, and spatial confusion. This compounds the visual disorientation. If you begin to feel unsteady, stop. Find a sheltered alcove (they exist — every room is designed with water and sound management as primary concerns) and wait for your system to recalibrate. The city has rooms. Use them.

## The Water: Moving Through a Weeping World

The water is everywhere, always, from every surface, and it is not clean. It carries dissolved minerals. It pools in basins at every level. It sheets and beads and hesitates on its way downward. It coats every surface you will touch, every ledge you will stand on, every handhold you will grip.

- **Your suit must be fully waterproof, not water-resistant.** There is a difference, and it matters here. Water-resistant gear assumes occasional exposure. You are going somewhere where immersion-level saturation is the default condition of existing. Every seam must be sealed. Every closure must be gasketed. Expect that after an hour in The Lachrymatory, water will have found every weakness in your outer shell. Design your suit with the assumption that the outer layer *will* be compromised and that your insulation layer must function while wet.

- **Mineral deposition on your gear is inevitable.** The milky, mineral-laden water will leave deposits on every surface it contacts as it evaporates or freezes. Over time — and you do not have much time — this will affect the function of seals, joints, optical surfaces, and mechanical closures. Carry solvent wipes and be prepared to maintain your equipment in the field. Pay particular attention to the drainage of your suit itself — water must flow *off* your body and away, not pool against your skin or inside your suit. Every piece of your equipment should slope. Every seam should be a channel. Think like the architecture: assume water, design for water, let water move.

- **Do not drink it.** The mineral content is unknown, the biological load is unknown, and the temperature alone would cause esophageal and gastric shock. Carry your own water supply in an insulated, heated reservoir integrated into your suit. Hydration is a serious concern in cold, thin, dry air (yes, the air is moisture-saturated, but your sealed suit creates its own microclimate, which may be drier than you think).

## The Fauna: Living Among Geology's Nervous System

This is where your preparation shifts from engineering to behavior. You cannot engineer your way past millions of mineral-armored organisms that fill every cubic meter of the space you intend to occupy. You need to understand them.

- **They are not aggressive, but they are indifferent, and indifference at their scale is dangerous.** A creature the size of a small building that moves with geological authority and has never encountered a warm, soft, fragile organism will not adjust its path for you. You will need to adjust yours. Study the movement patterns described in the location data: the drainage channels that double as digestive systems, the corridors that migrate slowly across decades, the layered density of populations shifting across hours. You need to predict where the fauna will be, not react to where they are.

- **They fill every space you are not occupying.** This means that the moment you leave a space, it is likely to be occupied by fauna almost immediately. You cannot leave gear unattended. You cannot rest in a location and expect it to remain available when you return. Every stop is temporary. Plan your movements in continuous sequences with no expectation of returning to a previous position.

- **Their bodies are lethally cold-adapted and armored in mineral chitin.** Contact with their skin or shell would damage yours — not through intent but through the fundamental incompatibility of your thermal and material properties. Their surface temperature is adapted to an environment that would freeze your flesh on contact. Their armor is hard, sharp-edged, and mineral. Do not touch them. Do not let them touch you if avoidance is possible. If it is not — and in the density of this place, it will not always be possible — ensure your suit's outer shell is between you and them at all points of contact. Accept the sliding, scraping passage of mineral bodies across your suit as a constant. This is what being here is like.

- **The sound they produce is information.** The clicks, scrapes, resonant hums, and percussive strikes are not random. They are communication, or feeding, or simply the acoustic signature of mass in motion. Learn the difference between a feeding click and a movement scrape. Learn what the deep hums mean — they seem to be population-level signals, the sound of the collective rather than the individual. The percussive strikes may indicate structural interaction — fauna meeting architecture, or fauna meeting fauna, or fauna meeting the emerging stone that is still pushing upward through the city. These sounds will tell you where the active geological zones are. Listen.

## Movement and Navigation: Traversing a World Without Edges

The city extends in all directions. You cannot see its edges. There is no sky for reference. The ground is wet, sloping, irregular, and still forming. Corridors migrate. Rooms have drainage as their founding principle, which means everything slopes toward channels, which means your path is constantly angled, never level.

- **Move with the water.** The drainage system is the city's circulatory system, and it follows the path of least resistance — which is, in a very real sense, the path of truth about the landscape's shape. If you need to go deeper, follow the water. If you need to go to the surface, move against the water's flow. This will not always be precise, but it will keep you oriented in the vertical axis, which is the axis most likely to kill you if you lose it. Falling into a drainage shaft that extends downward for unknown distances, lined with grazing fauna and faint phosphorescence, is a scenario you want to avoid.

- **Mark your path, but accept that the city may change it.** The architectural gestation described in the location data means that walls trail off into rough stone, that structural anchors are placed decades before the buildings they support arrive, that districts exist in a state of becoming. Your route may be open today and partially obstructed by a new basaltic eruption next week. Carry marking materials that will adhere to wet, mineral-coated surfaces — fluorescent mineral chalk, perhaps, or UV-reactive compounds that you can detect with a filtered light source but that won't be visible to the fauna.

- **The electromagnetic fields that guide the water are your invisible architecture.** The city uses subtle electromagnetic pulses through the mineral content of the rock to direct water flow. These fields are, in a sense, the city's nervous system — they are how it knows itself. If you carry a sensitive magnetometer, you may be able to map these fields and use them as navigation references. The fields would be consistent where the architecture is established and absent where it is still gestating. They would show you the city's shape the way blood vessels show you the shape of a body.

- **Respect the geological cadence.** The engineers of this place time their constructions to arrive when new stone emerges. You should time your movements similarly. When you feel the deep subsonic vibration intensify — the city's heartbeat, the sound of new stone pushing upward — stop moving. Find a stable position. Let the eruption happen. New basaltic columns push through existing structures. You do not want to be in the path of one. The vibration is your early warning system. Learn its patterns. When it fades, move again.

## Psychological Preparation: What This Place Does to a Mind

This is the section most travelers neglect, and it is the one that may matter most.

**You will feel watched.** The fauna are everywhere, and while they are not watching you — they are processing their own existence in the mineral darkness — their sheer density creates a persistent sense of surveillance that human psychology is not equipped to rationalize away. You will feel observed from every direction, from above and below, from behind every surface, because you are. Not by consciousness. By *presence*. Thousands of mineral-skinned organisms moving around you, over you, through every space you are not occupying. This is what it is like. Prepare for the sensation and do not let it drive your behavior. Do not make sudden movements to "escape" the feeling of being surrounded. You *are* surrounded. That is the baseline condition of being here.

**The sound will wear on you.** Constant, omnipresent, multi-layered noise with no respite and no silence will erode your cognitive function over time. Research the effects of continuous noise exposure: impaired concentration, irritability, sleep disruption (not that sleep will be easy in this environment), elevated cortisol, decision-making degradation. Plan for shorter exposure times than you think you need. Build rest periods into your itinerary — not rest in the sense of sleep, but rest in the sense of stillness in a sheltered alcove with your hearing protection engaged and your breathing controlled. The city has rooms. Some of them may be quieter than others. Seek them out.

**The darkness is not empty.** This is stated explicitly and it bears repeating in the psychological context. The black vault overhead has *texture* and *pressure*. It is not the absence of something; it is the presence of something. The geological striations of indigo, the sense of vastness still depositing itself layer by layer — this will register on your hindbrain as a sky that is *too close* and *too heavy* at the same time. There is no celestial distance here. There is only depth without limit pressing down from above while depth without limit drops away below. The psychological effect is a compression of spatial perception that can induce panic in unprepared minds. You are on a ridgeline between two infinities, both of which are dark and wet and full of slow, mineral life.

**The indifference of the place will hurt.** Not physically. Psychologically. You are a warm, fragile, temporary arrangement of carbon and water in a world made of stone and cold and ancient patience. The city does not notice you. The fauna do not notice you. The water does not care. The geological processes that are still assembling this world will continue long after you leave, and they were proceeding long before you arrived, and your presence changes nothing. This is not a place that will welcome you or reject you. It will simply fail to register you. For a human being — a social animal, a meaning-making animal, an animal that looks for signs of acknowledgment in every environment it enters — this total indifference is a form of existential confrontation that should not be underestimated.

Prepare for it by adjusting your expectations before you arrive. You are not visiting a place. You are *witnessing* a process. The process does not require a witness. Your role is observer, not participant. Hold onto that distinction.

**You may grieve.** The name of this place is The Lachrymatory — the weeping room, the vessel for tears. It is possible that the place itself induces a response in the humans who visit it that has no analogue in their prior experience. The combination of overwhelming sensory input, existential smallness, the beauty of a world still being born, and the profound loneliness of witnessing something that will never know you witnessed it — this is a cocktail for emotional states that you may not have language for. Let the response come. Do not suppress it. The water is weeping. You may weep. This is appropriate.

## The Final Preparation

Before you go, understand this: The Lachrymatory is not a destination. It is a condition. You do not *visit* a place where the sky is made of still-depositing obsidian and the water weeps from every surface and the fauna are indistinguishable from the walls and the ground is not finished being born. You *enter* it. You submit to it. You allow it to be what it is, which is everything you are not — cold, dark, loud, vast, indifferent, ancient, and new.

The suit will keep you alive. The equipment will keep you functional. But the only preparation that matters is the preparation of your mind: the understanding that you are going somewhere that was not made for you, that was not made for anything like you, that may in fact not have been *made* at all but is instead *happening*, continuously, in a process that does not distinguish between architecture and organism, between erosion and emergence, between the weeping of stone and the weeping of something that might, if you are very quiet and very still in the green phosphorescent dark, be grief.

Go warm. Go slowly. Go knowing that the water will find every seam you have, and that the sound will find every quiet place in your mind, and that when you leave — if you leave — you will carry the weight of having witnessed a world that does not need you and never did, and that this weight is not a burden but a gift: the experience of being, briefly, in the presence of something that is still arriving.
