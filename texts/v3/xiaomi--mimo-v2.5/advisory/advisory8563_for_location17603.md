# Preparing for the Resinwarrens

---

## I. First Principles: Understand What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are being absorbed into a *medium* — one that was built by hands that measured in generations, carved from living rock, and then abandoned by whatever walked upright inside it. You will not remember how you entered. There is no door behind you. From the moment you arrive, the only direction that matters is forward, downward, or inward, and the architecture does not distinguish between them.

The Resinwarrens are a compressed city, folded into stone the way a hand folds into a fist. Corridors are narrow — deliberately, consistently, almost ritualistically narrow: a man's shoulders with a fist's clearance on either side. Ceilings are low enough that you will stand with your knuckles brushing rock. Rooms are small enough that sitting means drawing your knees to your chest. This is not cruelty. This is *philosophy expressed in stone*. The builders understood space as something to be conserved, divided, shared. Every corridor leads to doorways — six, eight, a dozen on each side, stacked vertically through levels connected by grooved stone ladders. The entire structure is a grid, three-dimensional, and you are inside it with no map, no legend, no way to know which corridor duplicates which.

You must accept this before you go: **you will be disoriented.** Not lost — you can move, you can climb, you can trace your path back by counting ladder-holes and doorway intervals. But the sameness of the corridors is not monotony. It is a principle. Every passage looks like every other passage because they were carved to a single, obsessive standard. Your mind will search for landmarks and find none. Your sense of verticality will blur. Up and down become conventions, not feelings. Prepare for this not as a problem to solve but as a condition to inhabit.

## II. Clothing and Gear

### The Wetness

Everything is wet. The walls weep constantly — thin trickles of mineral-heavy water that migrate through capillary networks in the stone and emerge on every surface. Press your hand flat against any wall and it comes away slick. The floor is slick. The ladder-rungs are slick. The rims of the shallow basins in every room are slick. The air itself is heavy with moisture, not like fog or rain but like the exhalation of the stone, a warm breath in a cool place that coats your skin and never dries.

**You need to dress for permanent wetness.**

- **Base layer:** Merino wool, or a synthetic equivalent that retains warmth when soaked. Do not wear cotton. Cotton absorbs the weeping water and never releases it, and in a place of constant cool (deep-earth temperature, stable and unchanging, the coolness of water that has never seen sunlight), wet cotton will pull heat from your body with the slow patience of the stone itself. Merino wicks. It regulates. It keeps you alive.

- **Mid layer:** A thin fleece or synthetic insulation, ideally something with a hydrophobic treatment. The temperature is not freezing — you will not shiver violently — but it is the kind of cool that seeps. It asks for stillness. If you sit in a room and do not move, you will feel the stone's coolth through the floor and through the wall behind you, two fronts closing on your core. A mid layer gives you a buffer.

- **Outer layer:** A breathable waterproof shell. Not heavy-duty rain gear — you need to move through corridors a man's shoulders wide, and bulky fabric will catch on every carved surface. Think light, think slim, think a shell that sheds water but doesn't trap sweat. The humidity is immense. Your body generates heat when it moves. You will sweat under the shell. The shell will keep the weeping water off you. The sweat will be the lesser evil.

- **Footwear:** This is critical. You need shoes with exceptional grip on wet, mineral-smoothed stone. Approach shoes with sticky rubber soles, or climbing shoes designed for damp rock. The corridors are not wide enough for graceful movement — you will shuffle, press, and sometimes brace yourself with your back against one wall and your chest against the other. Your feet need to hold on when every surface is lubricated by millennia of capillary seepage. Ankle support is advisable; the grooved stone ladders are climbable but slick, and a twisted ankle in a place where every step echoes to every chamber on every level would be both painful and demoralizing.

- **Hands:** Gloves with tactile sensitivity. You will be touching walls constantly — for balance, for warmth-seeking, for navigation in the dim light. Wet stone is abrasive. The tool-marks left by the builders' hand-axes and abrasive cords create a texture that will rasp your skin raw within an hour of contact. But the gloves must be thin enough to feel the stone's pulse — that slow, sub-tectonic thrum of water migrating through mineral substrate. You will want to feel it. It is one of the few confirmations that you are in a physical place and not in a dream.

### The Acoustics

This deserves its own section because it will shape your entire experience.

The Resinwarrens are *loud.* Not in the way of a storm or a crowd — loud in the way that every hard surface in a dense stone grid reflects and amplifies every discrete sound into a cumulative roar. Dripping water — and there are hundreds of drips at any moment, thousands if you count the corridors and chambers you cannot see — creates a constant white noise made of tiny, distinct sounds. Your breathing reverberates. A footstep becomes a report. A cough becomes a rolling thunder that takes seconds to decay.

**Your gear must account for this.**

- **Ear protection:** Bring soft, reusable earplugs — the kind that reduce volume by 20-30 decibels without eliminating sound entirely. You do not want silence; you want *reduction*. The full-spectrum roar of the Resinwarrens will fatigue your auditory system within hours. Tinnitus is a real risk. The constant mid-range din — neither the low boom of machinery nor the high whine of electronics, but a chaotic, organic white noise composed of water-on-stone in ten thousand variations — will grind against the structures of your inner ear until you feel a pressure behind your eyes that never fully resolves. Earplugs are not optional. They are medical equipment.

- **Do not bring anything that makes sound.** No jangling keys, no clips on your pack that rattle, no zipper pulls that clink. Every sound you introduce will be taken by the architecture, reflected, multiplied, and returned to you multiplied. In a place where the inhabitants lived knowing that every utterance was shared by the walls themselves, your rattling water bottle will announce your position to every corridor within acoustic range — and in a place this dense and this reflective, "acoustic range" means *everywhere.*

### The Light

There is no darkness, but there is no real light either. The veins of pale blue-white crystalline mineral embedded in every surface emit a constant phosphorescence — barely more than a luminous watch-dial, diffuse, directionless, without flicker or variation. It will never brighten. It will never dim. It will never respond to your presence. It is not a light source so much as a *condition* of the place, like temperature or gravity.

**You need artificial light. But use it sparingly.**

- **A single headlamp with a red-light mode.** Red light preserves your dark adaptation, which you will need when (not if) you descend to lower levels where the crystal veins may be thinner. Bring spare batteries — lithium, not alkaline, which perform poorly in cool, damp conditions. Seal all electronics in waterproof bags. The humidity will find every unprotected circuit.

- **Do not use the headlamp unless necessary.** Your eyes will adapt to the crystal light within thirty to sixty minutes. It is enough to see the corridor ahead, the ladder-run below, the doorway to your left. It is not enough to read expressions or make out fine detail. This is by design — the light rewards intimacy. To see anything, you must move your face close. To see the scratches on a chamber floor — the claw-marks of whatever lives here — you must press your nose inches from the stone. The headlamp violates this contract. It floods the corridor with a harsh white cone that flattens everything, and when you turn it off, you will be temporarily blind in a place where blindness means stumbling into a ladder-hole.

- **Chemical light sticks as backups.** Break one, hang it in a niche, and it gives you a fixed reference point in a place that is otherwise a repeating grid of identical corridors. Use them to mark your trail — not as a guarantee of return (you may not be able to retrace your steps in a place this uniform) but as a way of asserting your presence. *You were here. You lit this room. The glow will fade, and the crystal veins will remain, and the distinction between your visit and the absence that preceded it will become as thin as the mineral deposit over the claw-marks.*

## III. Navigation

### Accept That You Will Not Navigate in Any Traditional Sense

There is no sun. There are no stars. There is no compass bearing that matters when the corridors run in all directions and the levels stack above and below you in a lattice of stone. You are not on a map. You are *inside* a structure that was built without regard for the concept of "up" in the surface-dwelling sense.

**What you can do:**

- **Count ladder-descent.** Every vertical transition between levels is marked by a square hole cut in the floor or ceiling, with a grooved stone ladder. These ladders are your altitude markers. If you descend twelve ladders, you are twelve levels below where you started. You do not know the total number of levels, but you know your position *relative* to where you began, and that is something.

- **Mark junctions.** Carry a small bag of chalk or a wax crayon and mark the walls at every intersection. Not words — the acoustics make prolonged writing sessions an exercise in self-torture, and the marks will be hard to read in the dim light. Simple marks: a number for each junction, a directional arrow for the path you came from. These will accumulate. In a place where the builders left no writing, your marks will be the only semiotic layer on the walls. They will not last — the mineral weeping will cover them in weeks, months, years — but they will last long enough.

- **Track ladder-intervals.** In a grid of consistent dimensions — every corridor the same width, every room the same relative size — your best friend is rhythm. The number of doorways between ladder-holes is your horizontal metric. The number of ladder-holes between corridors is your vertical metric. Think of it as walking through a three-dimensional musical score: each ladder is a bar line, each doorway is a note, and the pattern of intervals between them is your melody. Learn the melody. Hum it if you must. The acoustics will carry it, and for a moment the Resinwarrens will contain not only the sound of water but the sound of *you* — a brief, fragile, human voice against the indifferent eternity of wet stone.

- **Bring a low-tech compass.** A magnetic compass, the simplest possible device. It will not tell you north (there is no "north" underground), but it will tell you *consistency* — if all corridors run in four cardinal directions (and the grid regularity suggests they do), the compass will confirm whether you are walking parallel to a corridor you've been down before, or perpendicular to it. This is not useful for finding your way out. It is useful for *understanding the geometry of the place*, and in a place this disorienting, understanding geometry is the closest thing to safety.

### Do Not Expect to Find an Exit

You entered without a door. The description of the place — the way it was written, the way you received it — implies that entry is a fait accompli and exit is... not guaranteed. There may be a way out. There may be a ladder that leads to a corridor that leads to a passage that leads to something that is not the Resinwarrens. Or there may not. You should prepare to be here longer than you planned, indefinitely, and you should prepare psychologically for the possibility that "longer than you planned" means *the rest of your life.*

This is not a reason not to go. It is a reason to go *prepared.*

## IV. Provisions

### Water

There is water everywhere — weeping from every surface, collecting in the shallow basins carved into the floors of rooms. The water is mineral-heavy; you can feel it on your skin, a slickness that goes beyond simple wetness, as if the water carries a dissolved version of the stone it moved through. **Do not drink it without treatment.** Mineral-heavy water can carry dissolved elements that are toxic in quantity — heavy metals, sulfates, calcium in concentrations that will wreck your kidneys over days. It is water, yes. It is also rock that has given up its solidity. Bring a water filter and a means of chemical purification (iodine tablets, chlorine dioxide drops). Filter first for particulates, then purify for biological and chemical contaminants. The water is clean in the biological sense — there are no spores, no organisms, nothing alive in the Resinwarrens' hydrological cycle — but "clean" and "safe to drink" are not the same thing. The mineral load alone makes this water a slow poison.

**Carry at least five liters of sealed, purified water for your first day.** You will not find surface water here. You will not find springs or streams. You will find stone that sweats. If your supply runs out, you will drink the stone-sweat and accept the consequences.

### Food

The Resinwarrens offer nothing to eat. There is no plant life, no fungal growth, no insects, no animals visible. The absence of organic matter is one of the most unsettling features of the place — in a damp, sheltered, temperature-stable environment, life should be everywhere. It is not. Something keeps this place immaculate.

**Bring calorie-dense, non-perishable food.** Energy bars, dried fruit, nuts, jerky, hard cheese. Pack for a week even if you plan to stay for a day. The cool temperature will slow the rate at which food spoils, but it will not stop it — the humidity is relentless, and mold (if spores can enter with you) will find anything organic within days. Seal food in waterproof, airtight containers.

**Eat regularly, even if you are not hungry.** The place suppresses appetite. The constant sound, the disorientation, the cool that rewards stillness — all of these conspire to make you want to sit in a room and not move, not eat, not do anything but listen to the water and feel the stone's pulse through the wall against your back. This is the beginning of a surrender that the Resinwarrens are very good at encouraging. Resist it. Keep your blood sugar up. Keep your body's engines running. You are a warm thing in a cool place, a living thing in a place that has forgotten life. The least you can do is remind your body that it is alive by feeding it.

### Waste

**Pack it out.** There is no sanitation infrastructure. There is no biological breakdown happening in the Resinwarrens — nothing decomposes here, because nothing *grows* here. Your waste will remain exactly as you leave it, in the exact state you produced it, until mineral deposits from the weeping walls slowly encrust it. In a place that has maintained its equilibrium for an unknowable span of time, your biological refuse is the most transgressive thing you can introduce.

Bring waste bags. Bring a small trowel, not for burial (the stone will not allow it) but for scooping and sealing. Leave no trace. The builders left no bones. You should leave no evidence that you were ever here.

## V. The Psychological Dimension

This is the most important section. Everything else is logistics. This is *survival.*

### The Sound Will Change You

You cannot acclimate to the sound because the sound is not one thing — it is a composite of thousands of discrete, asynchronous events (drips, trickles, seepages, the sub-bass hum of the rock itself settling in frequencies below conscious hearing) that never repeat in the same pattern. Your brain will try to find pattern in it. It will fail. It will try again. It will fail again. Eventually, if you stay long enough, it will stop trying, and this cessation will feel like peace, and this peace is the Resinwarrens doing to your mind what the mineral water does to your kidneys — slowly, gently, without malice, wearing you down to a shape that fits.

**Countermeasures:**

- **Earplugs.** Already mentioned. Worth mentioning again. Wear them during rest periods. Reduce the volume for at least two hours out of every twelve.

- **Self-generated sound.** Talk to yourself. Not constantly — the acoustics will make it feel like you are lecturing an invisible audience — but in bursts. Name what you see. "I am in corridor twelve. I have passed three doorways on the left. The walls are weeping. The light is steady." This anchors you to a narrative. It reminds your brain that it is *you* hearing the sound, not the other way around. You are the subject. The Resinwarrens are the object. Keep the relationship straight.

- **Music, if you can manage it.** Hum. Sing. Not loudly — even a quiet voice reverberates here, and the sound of your own singing echoing back at you from six corridors away is either comforting or deeply uncanny, depending on the hour. If you have a small instrument — a harmonica, a kalimba, a mouth harp — bring it. The acoustics will turn it into something magnificent. For a moment, the Resinwarrens will sound like a cathedral.

### The Claustrophobia Will Come in Waves

You are in a place where the corridors are narrow enough that you can touch both walls simultaneously. The ceilings are low enough that a tall man cannot stand straight. The rooms are small enough that you must fold yourself to fit. This is not a single claustrophobic event — it is a continuous, ambient condition. It cannot be escaped by leaving the room, because the next room is the same, and the corridor between them is narrower.

**For most people, this will not be a crisis.** It will be an irritation that sharpens occasionally into genuine distress — usually in the middle of the night (if you can determine when "night" is; you cannot), usually when you are lying on a stone platform with your knees drawn up and the ceiling a foot above your face and the sound pressing against you from all sides. This is the moment when the full implication of your situation — that you are *inside* a thing, that you cannot see the sky, that the door you came through does not exist — will crystallize with a force that makes the stone feel like it is closing.

**Countermeasures:**

- **Controlled breathing.** Box breathing: four counts in, four counts hold, four counts out, four counts hold. The rhythm is foreign to the Resinwarrens' acoustics, and your brain will seize on it as something *yours* — something generated internally, not reflected from the walls. It is an anchor.

- **Touch the walls.** This sounds counterintuitive, but claustrophobia is the fear of enclosure, and enclosure is a cognitive frame. When you touch the walls, you redefine them — they become surfaces, textures, materials. They become *things you are in contact with* rather than *things that are closing on you.* Feel the tool-marks. Feel the mineral deposit over the claw-marks. Feel the slow pulse of water migrating through capillary networks. These are fascinating. These are *specific.* Specificity defeats panic.

- **Look at the crystals.** The phosphorescent veins in the walls are beautiful, in a way that is difficult to articulate — pale, steady, everywhere, the light of a place that has no concept of illumination because it has never known darkness. There is no "light" here and no "dark" here — there is only the same dim glow, always, in every direction. This is either terrifying or profound, depending on where your mind is. When claustrophobia peaks, focus on the crystals. They are your night sky. They are your stars, buried in rock, shining for no one, and the fact that they exist at all is proof that the universe does things for no reason, which is either the most claustrophobic thought imaginable or the most liberating.

### The Sameness Will Test Your Identity

The Resinwarrens are built to a single standard. Every corridor the same. Every room the same. Every doorway the same curve, the same height, the same obsessive repetition of a form language that began with a thumb pressed into wet clay and became an architectural grammar spanning levels and corridors and hundreds of identical chambers. You will walk through this place and see your own reflection in the repetition — you, moving through a grid, making choices that lead to the same result, turning corners that open onto more corners, climbing ladders that lead to corridors that lead to ladders.

This is not a metaphor the place is *making.* It is a metaphor the place *is.* And if you let it, it will make you question the nature of your own movements, your own choices, your own sense that you are going somewhere rather than simply repeating a pattern.

**Countermeasures:**

- **Keep a journal.** Not a detailed one — the dim light makes fine writing impossible — but a rough log. Date every entry (even if "date" is a fiction; use elapsed time). Record what you see, what you feel, what you eat, how far you've traveled. This is not navigation. This is *documentation of self.* It reminds you that you are a person having an experience, not a variable moving through a system. The Resinwarrens do not care that you are here. Your journal cares.

- **Make decisions.** Not just "which corridor" — those are trivial in a grid. Make real decisions. Which room will you sleep in? Will you descend another level or stay here? Will you spend an hour studying a niche or move on? The Resinwarrens offer no guidance, no direction, no preference. Every choice is entirely yours. Exercise this deliberately. The place will not push you. It will not reward you. It will simply contain the consequences of your decisions with perfect, mineral indifference. This is freedom in its most absolute and most terrifying form. Use it.

### You Will Feel Watched

Not by eyes. There are no eyes in the Resinwarrens — or rather, there are no eyes you will see. But the place has a *quality of attendance* that is difficult to shake. You are in a structure that was built, occupied, and abandoned, and the abandonment left no trace, and the maintenance — that inexplicable, immaculate absence of biological growth — suggests that *something* still tends this place, or that the place tends itself with a precision that borders on awareness.

The claw-marks confirm that something lives here. You will not see it. You may hear it — a dry, skittering friction at the edge of perception, something with chitin or claws drawing itself along a surface just beyond the crystal light's reach. It will stop when you stop listening. It will resume when you resume moving.

**Countermeasures:**

- **Accept the feeling.** Do not fight it. Do not try to rationalize it away. The Resinwarrens *are* aware of you, in the way a dream is aware of the dreamer — not with intention, but with total, uncritical inclusion. You are here. The place knows. It does not care, but it knows. This is the most honest relationship you can have with a place: to be known without being judged. Let it be.

- **Do not seek the fauna.** Whatever made those claw-marks, whatever moves through the corridors just outside the light — it has a right to this place that you do not. You are a visitor in a structure that was built for inhabitants, and you are neither. The fauna, whatever it is, stays. You may not. Treat its territory with the respect of a guest, not the curiosity of a conqueror. If it approaches, stay still. If it retreats, let it. You are not here to catalog. You are here to *be.*

## VI. The Emotional Arc

Here is what will happen to you, roughly, in the order it will happen:

**Hours 1-3: Fascination.** The place is extraordinary. The craftsmanship, the density, the crystals, the sound, the wetness, the sheer *otherness* of it — it will fill you with a wonder that is almost giddy. You will touch every surface. You will peer into every niche. You will climb every ladder. You will think: *I am the first person in however long to see this.* You will be wrong — others have been here, or at least, the description you received implies that you are not the first. But the feeling of discovery will be real, and it will carry you.

**Hours 4-12: Fatigue.** The sound will wear you down. The cool will seep into your bones. The narrowness of the corridors will begin to press. You will eat, drink, and seek a room to rest in, and you will find that every room is the same, and the sameness will not bother you yet, but you will notice it. You will sleep on a stone platform with your knees drawn up and the ceiling a foot above your face, and the dripping will not stop, and the light will not dim, and you will sleep in the exact same light you woke in, and this will be strange.

**Days 2-4: Integration.** This is the dangerous part, and also the beautiful part. You will begin to *fit.* Your body will learn the corridors — the width, the height, the rhythm of doorways and ladders. Your ears will stop cataloging the sound and start *hearing* it — not as noise but as the place's voice, its only voice, the monologue of water speaking to stone. You will stop feeling claustrophobic. You will stop feeling watched. You will begin to feel *at home*, and this feeling will be real and it will be a lie, because this is not your home, and the place that built these corridors is gone, and whatever remains is not waiting for you. But the feeling will be real, and it will be enough, and you will begin to understand why the builders carved their domestic life into stone rather than wood, rather than fabric, rather than anything that could rot or burn or be forgotten: they wanted something that would *endure*, and they got it, and you are inside it, and it is enduring, and you are not.

**Day 5 and beyond: The Choice.** You will reach a point — and you will know it when you reach it — where you either commit to leaving or commit to staying. Not because the Resinwarrens give you an ultimatum, but because your body will. You will have eaten through a portion of your food. You will have climbed levels you cannot count. You will have seen every variation of the same corridor, the same room, the same niche. You will have heard every permutation of the sound. And you will ask yourself: *Do I go deeper, or do I go back?* The answer depends on who you are. There is no wrong answer. The Resinwarrens will contain either choice with equal indifference.

## VII. Final Advice

**Go alone.** The acoustics make companionship acoustically punishing — every conversation is a broadcast, every whisper a shout, every laugh a detonation that will echo for minutes. But more than that: the Resinwarrens are a place that rewards interiority. They are built for solitude — rooms for one, corridors for one, ladders for one. To bring another person is to introduce a variable that the architecture was not designed for. You will spend more time managing your companion's experience than having your own. Go alone. Let the place do what it does to a single human mind, unmediated.

**Tell someone where you are going.** Not because they can reach you — there is no entrance, no address, no coordinate. But because the act of telling someone is the act of maintaining your connection to a world outside the stone. It is a thread. It may not pull you out. But it will remind you that "out" exists.

**Bring a mirror.** A small one. Flat. You will go days without seeing your own face — the light is too dim to use reflective surfaces for anything other than close inspection, and the only close inspection the place rewards is of the walls, the crystals, the niches, the claw-marks. A mirror will remind you that you are a face, a body, a person, not just a set of footsteps echoing through a grid. Look at yourself in the crystal light. You will look different — the blue-white phosphorescence will wash the color from your skin and make your eyes look dark and deep and slightly alien. You will look like you belong here. You do not. Remember that.

**Know when to leave.** The Resinwarrens are not hostile. They are not dangerous, in the way that a predator or a trap is dangerous. They are dangerous the way *comfort* is dangerous — they offer a kind of peace that is indistinguishable from surrender. The cool, the sound, the sameness, the dim light, the wet stone against your back — these things will, over time, begin to feel not like conditions to be endured but like conditions to be *accepted.* And acceptance, in this place, means stasis. It means sitting in a room on a stone platform with your knees drawn up and the ceiling a foot above your face and the water dripping and the crystals glowing and the sound pressing against you from all sides, and not moving, and not wanting to move, and being content.

This is beautiful. This is also the Resinwarrens completing their function: enclosing you, absorbing you, making you part of the occupancy that the stone remembers. You are not the first person to sit in this room. You will not be the last. But if you stay, you will be the most recent, and the stone will learn your shape the way it learned the shape of whoever sat here before you, and when you leave — *if* you leave — the shape will remain, slowly filling with mineral deposit, slowly becoming indistinguishable from the walls.

Leave before the room learns you too well.

Leave before the sound becomes silence.

Leave before the light feels bright.

**Leave while the world outside is still louder than the world inside you.**

---

*Pack light. Move slow. Listen to everything. Touch the walls. Climb the ladders. Count the doorways. Breathe. Sleep. Eat. Write. And then, when you are ready — not when the place tells you to go, because it will never tell you to go, but when you remember that you are someone who leaves — climb the last ladder, walk the last corridor, and let the Resinwarrens recede behind you like a held breath finally released.*

*You will not remember how you entered. You will remember how you left.*
