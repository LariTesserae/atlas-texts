# Preparing to Visit The Underworks

## I. Breathing: The Fundamental Problem

Everything else is secondary to this. You are going to spend time in a volume of water the size of an inland sea, kilometers deep, with no surface that leads to air. There is no moment in this place where you will be able to remove a mask and breathe freely. The ceiling does not open. The lattice does not rise. You will be submerged for the entire duration of your visit, and if your breathing apparatus fails, you will drown in a place so vast that your body may never reach anything solid enough to rest on.

Prepare accordingly.

A closed-circuit rebreather is the minimum. Not open-circuit — you cannot afford to broadcast exhaust bubbles into an environment where sound travels perfectly and every vibration announces your position. A rebreather recycles your exhalations, scrubs the carbon dioxide, adds oxygen from a compressed supply, and produces almost no sound and no bubbles. Choose a model rated for long-duration dives — seventy-two hours minimum, though you should plan for shorter exposures and carry redundant systems. Pack two. Pack three. Pack a small emergency rebreather in a pouch on your chest that you can activate in three seconds if your primary fails.

Your oxygen supply calculations must account for the depth. The description places the ceiling at kilometers up and the lattice at kilometers down — an enormous vertical span. If the ambient pressure in the Underworks corresponds to the water column (and it must, since the ceiling plates are described as "adjusting to the pressure"), you will be at crushing depth by surface-world standards. Oxygen becomes toxic at high partial pressures. Nitrogen causes narcosis. Your breathing gas mix must be precisely calibrated to the ambient pressure at your intended operating depth — trimix at minimum (oxygen, nitrogen, helium) or, if you plan to explore the deeper lattice, perhaps a heliox mix with no nitrogen at all. Carry a depth gauge and a gas analyzer. Know your operating limits.

And yet — this is a multiverse. The Underworks is described as pressurized, engineered, sealed. It is possible that the internal pressure is managed to something more hospitable than raw hydrostatic pressure at equivalent oceanic depth. Investigate this before you commit to a gas mix. If the engineers who built this place are competent (and they are — the description radiates competence), they may have established a standard pressure throughout the volume. Find out what that pressure is. If it's close to one atmosphere, your life becomes dramatically simpler. If it's not, you're planning a deep dive on the scale of ocean trenches, and you need to prepare like a saturation diver with months of decompression planning.

Speaking of which: decompression. If you descend into the lattice — into the deep, dark structural forest where the larger predator moves — and then ascend to the ceiling, you will be moving through a pressure gradient. Your body will absorb nitrogen (or helium) at depth and need to off-gas it on ascent. Plan your profile. Ascend slowly. Make decompression stops. Carry a dive computer that accounts for your specific gas mix. The Underworks will not wait for you to decompress, and the water will not be forgiving if you get decompression sickness kilometers from any medical facility.

## II. Thermal Protection: Cool, Patient, Uniform

The water is cool. Not cold — the description is specific about this. Cool the way machinery is cool. Cool the way metal feels in a building that has never known sunlight. This suggests a temperature probably in the range of 15-18°C (60-65°F) — uncomfortable for extended bare-skin exposure, but not hypothermia-immediate. The danger is duration, not intensity.

If you are spending hours in this water — and you must plan for hours, because the distances are enormous and the travel between structures takes time — your body will lose heat steadily. Water conducts heat from the body roughly twenty-five times faster than air. A temperature that feels merely brisk on your face will become a deep, penetrating cold in your core over the course of an hour.

A drysuit is the correct choice, not a wetsuit. A wetsuit works by trapping a thin layer of water against your skin and letting your body heat it. In a multi-hour dive, that water will eventually equilibrate with the surrounding cool water and the suit becomes a thermal bridge, not a barrier. A drysuit keeps the water off your skin entirely, and you insulate underneath with thermal undergarments — thick fleece or pile layers that trap warm air against your body. You will look bulky. This does not matter. No one in the Underworks cares what you look like. The engineered fish will ignore you. The pylons will not judge.

Add active heating if possible. A battery-powered heating element woven into the undergarment, powered by a compact lithium cell, can add a trickle of warmth that makes the difference between four hours of comfortable observation and four hours of slow, grinding misery. The battery will drain faster in water — carry spares in sealed, pressure-rated containers.

Your extremities are the weak point. Gloves are essential — thick, insulated, dry gloves that integrate with your drysuit's wrist seals. The pylons have edges that could cut you; the gloves are protection against that as well. Boots, similarly — insulated, with rigid soles in case you need to push off from a structure or brace against a current. Your face will be exposed within your mask; a neoprene hood covers the rest of your head and neck.

Watch for thermal vents. The machinery periodically "belches warm water" — localized plumes of heated discharge that create visible distortions. These are not dangerous in themselves, but the temperature differential between a vent and the ambient water could cause localized currents strong enough to push you off course, and the sudden temperature change against exposed skin could cause a thermal shock reflex — an involuntary gasp that, with a rebreather in your mouth, could cause you to bite down and compromise your breathing loop. When you see a heat shimmer, give it distance.

## III. Sound: The Deafening Silence

This is the element of The Underworks that the description emphasizes most relentlessly, and it is the one that most travelers will underestimate.

Sound in water is not sound in air. In air, sound is diffuse, attenuated by distance and atmosphere and the simple fact that air molecules are lazy about passing vibrations along. In water, sound travels roughly four times faster and with far less attenuation. A mechanical tick that would be a faint metallic click in an air-filled factory becomes, in water, a percussive shockwave that reaches your body intact and undiminished. The description is explicit: *every vibration from every machine, every groan of settling structure, every sharp metallic crack — all of it arrives at your body intact.*

The constant industrial hum — the accumulated voice of a million operating systems — will be a physical pressure on your chest. You will feel your ribcage vibrate. Your lungs, filled with breathing gas at ambient pressure, will resonate. The low frequencies will settle into your bones. The high frequencies — the ticks, the thermal cracks, the whine of pumps — will ring in your inner ear with a persistence that becomes, over hours, genuinely maddening.

Protect your hearing. Standard earplugs are insufficient — they attenuate air-conducted sound but do little against bone-conducted vibration in water. What you need is a combination approach: molded silicone earplugs that seal your ear canal, paired with a lightweight external frame that grips your skull and provides vibration dampening — similar to what military divers use in high-noise underwater environments. This will not eliminate the hum. Nothing eliminates the hum. But it will reduce it from a physical assault to a manageable background pressure.

More importantly, protect your mind. The description compares the sound to weather — pervasive, constant, inescapable. In weather, you acclimate. You stop hearing rain after ten minutes. But the Underworks is not weather; it is weather with mechanical precision, a hum that never varies in pitch, layered with irregular percussive events — the ticks, the cracks — that prevent your brain from fully habituating. This is the recipe for a specific kind of sensory fatigue that manifests as irritability, difficulty concentrating, and a creeping sense of panic.

Plan for it. Brief your own psychology. Know that after two hours, the sound will begin to feel personal — as though the machinery is directed at you, as though the hum is a voice saying something you cannot understand. This is a known perceptual distortion in prolonged exposure to constant industrial noise. It will pass. It does not mean you are losing your mind. It means your brain is doing what brains do: trying to find meaning in noise, and failing, and generating anxiety in the gap.

Take breaks. If you can find a structure with an interior space — one of the factory chambers in the pylons, perhaps — the walls may dampen the sound enough to give your nervous system a reprieve. The description mentions "denser clustering of pipes" and louder hums in certain areas; avoid these. Seek the quieter zones, the gaps between machinery clusters, where the accumulated sound is merely overwhelming rather than crushing.

And understand that your own sounds will be broadcast just as efficiently as the machinery's. Every movement you make, every click of a buckle, every exhalation through your rebreather, every adjustment of your equipment — all of it travels through the water in every direction. You are loud. You are always loud. You cannot be quiet in the Underworks. Accept this.

## IV. Light and Vision: The Perpetual Grey

The ceiling glows. It has always glowed — or rather, it was engineered to glow, and it does so with the reliability of a circuit. The light is described as "cool, honest white," diffused through kilometers of water into a soft, directionless illumination that falls from above like perpetual overcast daylight. There is no sun to cast shadows. There is no night. The light simply *is*.

This will do strange things to your sense of time.

Humans are circadian creatures. We track time, in part, by the quality of light — the angle of the sun, the warmth of its color, the way it fades toward evening. In the Underworks, none of these cues exist. The light at hour one will be identical to the light at hour twelve. Your body clock, deprived of its primary zeitgeber, will begin to drift. You may feel wide awake when you should be tired, or suddenly exhausted when you feel alert. You may lose track of how long you have been submerged. Time becomes elastic, unreliable.

Carry a reliable timepiece. Set an alarm for regular intervals — every thirty minutes — that vibrates against your wrist. Do not rely on your internal sense of duration. The Underworks will steal hours from you and you will not notice.

Your mask must be chosen for this light environment. A standard diving mask with tempered glass and a black silicone skirt will work, but consider a mask with a slightly tinted or polarized lens — not to reduce brightness (the light is described as soft, not blinding) but to enhance contrast in the diffused illumination. The pylons, the fish, the jellyfish — these are all slightly grey-on-grey in the ambient light, and the deeper you descend toward the lattice, the more the light fades into a blue-black haze. A contrast-enhancing lens will help you resolve shapes and distances in this low-contrast world.

Carry a primary and backup dive light, even though the ceiling provides illumination. At depth — near the lattice, in the shadow of the pylons, in the factory interiors — the ceiling light will be attenuated by meters of water and the angular geometry of the structures. You will need your own light to see. Choose a wide-beam light for general illumination and a narrow-beam light for examining details — the seams in the ceiling, the grooves on the pylons, the faces of the angular fish. Red-light mode is useful for not disturbing the fauna; many deep-water organisms are less reactive to red wavelengths.

Do not carry a light so bright that it illuminates a vast radius around you. The description of the Underworks emphasizes emptiness, distance, and the way structures emerge from haze. If you blast a thousand lumens into that water, you will illuminate nothing — the light will scatter in the particulate and reflect back at you in a white wall of backscatter, blinding yourself while seeing nothing beyond arm's length. Moderate light, close light, patient light.

## V. Navigation: Being Lost in a Machine the Size of a Sea

The Underworks is described as continental in scale — a sealed volume the size of an inland sea. Within it, structures are "scattered like thoughts in a slow mind, separated by kilometers of clear, cool water in which there is almost nothing at all except you." This is an environment where getting lost is not a risk but a certainty if you are unprepared.

The ceiling plates provide some orientation — they are always above you, and their seams form a visible grid that could serve as a map if you could see enough of them at once. But you cannot. The water hazes everything beyond a few hundred meters. The pylons rise from the distant lattice and connect to the ceiling, but they are kilometers apart, and in the open water between them, there are no landmarks. There is only blue-black emptiness in every direction.

Carry a navigation system. A wrist-mounted sonar unit that pings the nearest structures and gives you range and bearing is ideal. If the Underworks has any kind of internal positioning system — and a facility this sophisticated almost certainly does — determine before you arrive whether your equipment can interface with it. A transponder that you carry, which communicates with the architecture and returns your coordinates, would be invaluable.

In the absence of electronic navigation, you can orient by the ceiling (always up), by the hum (which is louder near machinery clusters), and by the pylons (which are identifiable by their hexagonal profiles even at distance). Develop a simple strategy: always keep one pylon in sight. If you lose sight of one, find another before moving further. Do not swim into open water without a bearing on at least two structures.

Mark your path. Carry a reel of thin, high-visibility line and clip it to structures as you pass them, creating a literal thread back to your starting point. The line will drift in the currents — there are slow, deliberate circulations in the water — but it will remain visible and touchable. Follow it home if you need to.

And above all: do not descend toward the lattice without a line. The lattice is described as receding into "blue-black distance" so profound that it "reads less as ground and more as the memory of ground." You could fall toward it for an hour. If you lose your orientation in the deep water, with the ceiling fading above you and the lattice not yet resolved below, you will experience a disorientation that has no analog in surface-world experience. There is no up or down in that blue-black void except what your inner ear and your instruments tell you, and your inner ear will lie to you in water.

## VI. The Living Things: Respect What Was Built to Eat

The fauna of the Underworks is engineered, abundant, and — at the upper levels — largely indifferent to you. The angular fish graze on biofilm. The rust-colored shrimp colonies cling to pylons and taste the water in unison. The jellyfish drift through the middle distances like slow lightning. These are the livestock of the machine, biological components designed to process waste and maintain the ecosystem, and they have no instinct to interact with a human visitor.

But they are alive, and alive things are unpredictable.

The angular fish will ignore you unless you are very still and very patient, in which case they may approach out of curiosity or because your equipment has biofilm on it that they would like to eat. They are described as having "large and dark and flat" eyes. Do not mistake their indifference for tameness. They are not tame. They are simply unconcerned. If you make a sudden movement, they will scatter, and the sound of their displacement — the sudden rush of a hundred small bodies through water — will add to your noise signature and may attract attention from things that are less indifferent.

The jellyfish are the primary hazard at the upper levels. They are described as "long and thin, trailing filaments that glow faintly with bioluminescence." Bioluminescent filaments in jellyfish are usually nematocyst-bearing — stinging cells. The glow may serve as a warning, a lure, or an accident of chemistry. Do not assume it is harmless. Maintain distance. If a jellyfish drifts toward you, do not swat at it — the filaments can extend several meters behind the bell, and a panicked swipe could entangle your hand in them. Move slowly, vertically, to clear the tentacle field, and let the jellyfish pass.

Do not touch the shrimp colonies. They are described as moving "in unison like a single vast nervous system." Colonial organisms with coordinated behavior may respond to physical disruption as a colony — producing a defensive reaction, a chemical alarm signal, or a coordinated retreat that creates a cascade of movement along the pylon. You do not want to be the thing that triggers a colony-wide alarm in an environment where every vibration is broadcast to every corner.

And the deep — the lattice, the dark water below, where "something larger, something with too many limbs, too much deliberate motion" moves across the distant girders. You have no business there on your first visit. This creature is the apex of the engineered food chain, the predator that eats the jellyfish, and it is described in terms that suggest significant size and active hunting behavior. It is adapted to the deep, to the low light, to the geometric landscape of the lattice. You would be in its territory, at a disadvantage, in an environment where your sensory capabilities are reduced and its are enhanced.

If you must go to the lattice — and the description of the place will make you want to, because the lattice is where the deepest mystery lies — go prepared. Carry a powerful light that you can activate in an instant. Stay near a structural element that you can put between yourself and the creature. Move slowly and predictably. Do not bleed in the water. And have an ascent plan that takes you to the ceiling as fast as decompression physics allow.

## VII. The Emotions: What This Place Does to a Human Mind

The description of The Underworks is, beneath its technical precision, a description of a feeling. That feeling is this:

*You are inside something that does not know you exist, that was built for purposes you cannot discern, at a scale you cannot comprehend, and it is working, it has always been working, it will continue working, and you are a transient and insignificant biological event in a volume of engineered water that would not change at all if you were not in it.*

This is not a hostile feeling. The Underworks is not menacing. It is not indifferent, either — indifference implies a relationship. The Underworks simply does not have the capacity to notice you. It is a machine, and you are not part of its design, and your presence within it is as meaningless to it as a dust mote in a cathedral is to the architecture. The cathedral was not built for the dust mote. The dust mote does not affect the cathedral. The dust mote is free to observe the cathedral, and the cathedral is free to continue being a cathedral, and neither owes the other anything.

This can be profoundly peaceful, or it can be profoundly disturbing, depending on your psychological constitution and your relationship with scale.

Prepare for both.

If you are the kind of person who finds comfort in vast, empty, well-ordered spaces — who feels at home in the belly of a ship, in the silence of a desert, in the presence of systems so large they transcend individual purpose — the Underworks will be a revelation. You will feel, for the first time, what it is like to be genuinely *inside* something, not in a room or a building but in the working organ of a machine that encompasses an entire volume of geography. The hum will feel like a heartbeat. The fish will feel like companions. The light will feel like grace.

If you are the kind of person who needs to feel that you matter — that your presence is noticed, that the space you occupy was designed with you in mind, that you are not merely tolerated but *welcomed* — the Underworks will be an ordeal. The vastness will press on you. The emptiness between structures will feel personal. The hum will feel like a voice that is not speaking to you. The engineered fish will swim past you without acknowledgment, and you will feel, in a way that you have never felt before, that you are truly and completely *other* — a visitor in a place that was not built for visitors, tolerated by a system that does not have the concept of tolerance.

Prepare by knowing yourself. If you tend toward the first disposition, lean into it. Let the Underworks be what it is. Do not try to find meaning in it or impose your narrative on it. Observe. Float. Listen. Let the machine be a machine and the emptiness be an emptiness and the light be a light.

If you tend toward the second disposition, prepare mitigation. Bring a companion — not for safety, though safety is a bonus, but for human contact, for the sound of another voice that is not machinery, for the reassurance that you are visible to *someone* even if the Underworks does not see you. Talk to each other. Describe what you see. Narrate your experience. This will keep you grounded in your own significance at the precise moment when the environment is trying to convince you that you have none.

In either case, limit your exposure. The Underworks does not change. There is no arc to the experience, no development, no climax. It is a constant state — light, hum, emptiness, structure, fish, jellyfish, deep dark water, sharp edges, cool patient weight. After a few hours, you will have seen everything it has to offer, and what remains is not new observation but deepening of the feeling. That deepening can become overwhelming. Set a limit — four hours, six hours, eight hours — and honor it. Surface. Drain your suit. Sit in silence. Let your ears ring. Let your body re-acclimate to air and gravity and the sound of wind. Then decide if you want to go back.

## VIII. What to Bring: Summary Checklist

**Breathing and Life Support**
- Closed-circuit rebreather, long-duration (72hr rated), with redundant oxygen supply
- Emergency backup rebreather, chest-mounted, single-hour rated
- Gas mix calibrated to ambient pressure (trimix or heliox — confirm ambient pressure first)
- Spare gas cylinders, pressure-rated
- CO2 scrubber canisters, extra

**Thermal Protection**
- Full drysuit with integrated hood
- Thermal undergarments — thick fleece or pile, full body
- Active heating system with battery pack
- Dry gloves with thermal liner
- Insulated dive boots
- Spare batteries for heating system, pressure-sealed

**Sound Protection**
- Molded silicone earplugs, custom-fitted
- External vibration-dampening frame
- (Note: accept that you will still hear everything. Mitigation, not elimination.)

**Navigation and Communication**
- Wrist-mounted sonar unit
- Underwater communication system (bone-conduction mask or comms unit)
- High-visibility guide line, 500m minimum, on a reel
- Clips for attaching line to structures
- Wrist-mounted depth gauge and dive computer
- Compass (magnetic may be unreliable near so much metal — carry a digital gyroscopic compass)
- Underwater notepad and pencil for recording observations

**Lighting**
- Wide-beam primary dive light (moderate output, 300-500 lumens)
- Narrow-beam secondary dive light for detail examination
- Red-light filter or mode
- Spare batteries, pressure-sealed

**Safety and First Aid**
- Dive knife or cutting tool (for entanglement — jellyfish filaments, guide line)
- First aid kit, pressure-sealed, focused on wound closure and thermal burns
- Emergency buoyant ascent device
- Decompression planning tools (redundant to dive computer)

**Psychological Support**
- Pre-dive briefing for yourself: read this document, internalize the emotional dynamics, know your vulnerabilities
- If traveling with a companion: establish communication protocols, agreed time limits, check-in intervals
- Waterproof journal and pencil: writing keeps the mind anchored to personal narrative
- A small, familiar object — a stone, a token, something with weight and texture — carried in a pocket. Something that is yours, that the Underworks did not make, that reminds you that you come from a place where there is sky.

**Duration Planning**
- Maximum recommended first exposure: 4 hours
- Plan for a maximum of 8 hours on subsequent visits
- Always leave enough gas for ascent to the ceiling and decompression
- Know your exit point before you enter

## IX. What You Will See

You will see a world that was built to be invisible — a place that was designed to function without witnesses, that operates with the serene confidence of a system that has never been observed and does not require observation to justify its existence. You will see light that comes from nowhere and goes everywhere. You will see water that is perfectly clear and profoundly deep. You will see geometry in a place where geometry should not exist — hexagons and lines and sharp edges in the middle of a fluid that softens everything it touches. You will see life that was engineered to eat waste and will do so with the blind dedication of creatures that have never known a reason to do otherwise. You will see beauty that was not intended, that leaked through the engineering like heat through a seal, that no one designed and no one can explain.

You will see the lattice, if you dare to look down. You will see it recede into blue-black distance, and you will understand, in a way that is physical and immediate and not at all intellectual, that the space beneath you is so vast and so deep that your brain cannot hold it. You will feel your stomach drop. You will grip your guide line. You will look up, and the ceiling will be there, pale and solid and close, and you will feel relief so intense it is almost grief, and you will understand that this place was built to contain something, and that something is the water, and the water is what holds everything apart — the ceiling from the lattice, the light from the dark, the small from the vast — and you are in the water, in the space between, in the engineered gap where the system breathes and circulates and hums, and you are, for as long as you can endure it, a living thing in a place where living things were not supposed to be.

Go. Prepare thoroughly. Stay as long as you can bear it. Come back to the air. Tell others what you saw. They will not believe you. That is fine. The Underworks does not require belief. It requires only that it be true, and it is true, and it is working, and it will be working long after you have forgotten what it felt like to float in its patient, cool, lit depths, listening to the hum of a world that does not know your name.
