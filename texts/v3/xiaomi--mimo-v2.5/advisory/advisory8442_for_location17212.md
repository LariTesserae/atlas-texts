# Preparing to Visit The Floor of Everything

---

## I. UNDERSTANDING WHERE YOU'RE GOING

Before you pack a single thing, you need to sit with what this place *is*.

It is a world-ocean — no land has ever existed here, or if it did, it was swallowed so long ago that the planet forgot. The entire surface is water, flat and glassy and endless, stretching to a curved horizon in every direction. Above the water: a sun that never moves, a sky that never clouds, no weather of any kind. Below: kilometers — *many* kilometers — of clear, cold, darkening water, and at the bottom, a volcanic floor of young basalt ridges separated by hundreds of kilometers of mineral dust.

Nothing here changes. Nothing here has ever changed. The temperature is constant. The light is constant. The sound is constant. The fauna numbers are constant. The vents vent at the same rate they have always vented. The corroded, abandoned structures bolted to the ridges have been corroding at the same rate for longer than human civilization has existed, and will continue to corrode at that same rate long after human civilization is a memory.

This is not a place that is hostile to you. Hostility requires intent. This is a place that is *indifferent* to you — to an almost metaphysical degree. It will not harm you deliberately. It will simply continue being what it is, and what it is will kill you in about four minutes without preparation, and in about four hours with inadequate preparation, and even with perfect preparation it will slowly, relentlessly, quietly try to unmake everything you brought with you through corrosion, pressure, cold, and the sheer grinding patience of eternity.

Understanding this indifference is the most important preparation. You are not entering a battle. You are entering a *condition*.

## II. THE PRESSURE PROBLEM — Your Single Greatest Enemy

Let me be direct: the pressure at the floor of this world will kill you more completely and more quickly than anything else on this list.

The ocean is deep. The description tells you the ridges are enormous — mountainous — and their summits reach into the zone where sunlight still penetrates. That means the summits are perhaps 200–500 meters below the surface. The floor itself, the abyssal plains between ridges, the valleys where the vents sit — these could be 3,000 to 6,000 meters down, possibly deeper. At 4,000 meters, the pressure is approximately 400 atmospheres. That is 400 times the pressure of sea level on a world with air. It is 400 times what your lungs, your sinuses, your eardrums, your entire body were built to withstand.

At that pressure:
- Your lungs would collapse instantly on exposure to the water.
- Nitrogen and other dissolved gases would flood your bloodstream, causing narcosis and then death.
- Your equipment would be compressed, crushed, buckled. Anything not rated for this depth would fail catastrophically.
- The water itself behaves differently at these pressures — denser, more viscous to your equipment's sensors, though still recognizably water.

**What you need:**

A **submersible or atmospheric diving suit** rated to at least 6,000 meters. Not a scuba rig. Not a rebreather. Not any kind of open-circuit or closed-circuit breathing apparatus that exposes you to ambient pressure. You need a vehicle or suit that maintains internal pressure at approximately 1 atmosphere — the pressure of a pleasant afternoon on a world with air and trees and birds.

If you're using a **submersible**: make it small and rugged. You will be navigating between volcanic ridges, past hydrothermal chimneys, near structures bolted to basalt. Maneuverability matters. Choose something with a thick pressure hull — titanium or ceramic composite — with redundant life support rated for at least 72 hours (more on why below). Your viewports need to be thick acrylic or sapphire, rated for the depth. Your exterior lights need to be powerful — despite the description of "normal" sunlight filtering down, at the floor level you'll be relying on artificial light for anything beyond a few meters.

If you're using an **atmospheric diving suit** (an exosuit or similar): verify its depth rating absolutely. These suits maintain internal pressure while allowing some limb mobility. They are more maneuverable than a submersible for exploring inside the abandoned structures, but they limit your endurance and your ability to carry equipment. Choose this only if exploring the structures is your primary goal, and only if the suit's rating is verified.

**Critical note on the "normal light":** The description says sunlight reaches the floor through the flat, still surface. This is real — in an ocean with no surface turbulence and perfectly clear water, sunlight does penetrate to significant depths. But "significant" is relative. At 4,000+ meters, even in perfectly clear water with a perfectly flat surface, you are in a zone of extremely dim, blue-shifted illumination. Your eyes — human eyes evolved for a world with an atmosphere — will perceive this as near-total darkness. You will need artificial light. Do not make the mistake of thinking "the description says there's sunlight" means "I'll be able to see." You won't. Not with your naked eyes. The submersible's external lights are not optional. They are survival equipment.

## III. THE COLD — Patient and Total

The water temperature is "just above freezing." Call it 1–2°C. This is not the dramatic cold of an Arctic blizzard. It is the cold of deep ocean everywhere — quiet, uniform, and absolutely without mercy.

**If you are in a properly sealed submersible or atmospheric suit**, the cold is a systems problem, not a survival problem. Your vehicle's heating system must be able to maintain interior temperature against the constant external cold. Verify that your heating is rated for prolonged operation — you may be down there for days. A heating failure at 4,000 meters depth would be fatal within hours as the cold penetrates the hull.

**If any part of your skin is exposed** — even briefly, even a hand reaching out to touch something — the cold will act with terrifying speed. At 1–2°C water:
- 5 minutes: severe pain, loss of fine motor control.
- 10–15 minutes: numbness, early hypothermia in the extremities.
- 30 minutes: loss of consciousness.
- 45–60 minutes: death.

Do not expose skin. Not even for a moment. Not even to "feel" the water. Your suit seals must be absolute.

**Bring backup heating.** Redundant heating elements, chemical heat packs rated for the pressure, something. If your primary heating fails, you need a secondary system that can keep you alive long enough to ascend to warmer layers. And note: "warmer layers" at 4,000 meters means ascending for *hours*. You cannot simply swim up to the surface. The ascent alone in an atmospheric suit could take 8–12 hours depending on your vehicle's ascent rate, and you'd be freezing the entire way until you reached the thermocline.

## IV. THE SOUND — A Constant, World-Spanning Roar

This is the thing the description prepares you for intellectually but cannot prepare you for *sensorily*.

Thousands of hydrothermal vents. All active. All roaring. The sound is not localized — it comes from everywhere, a pressurized, tectonic, infrasonic vibration that the water carries for kilometers. You will feel it before you hear it: a pressure in your chest, a vibration in your teeth, a deep subsonic thrumming that makes your vision slightly blurry and your thoughts slightly unfocused.

**In a submersible**, the sound is attenuated by the hull but not eliminated. The hull itself will vibrate. You will hear it as a constant deep hum, like being inside a building next to a waterfall that never stops. Over hours, this becomes profoundly fatiguing. It seeps into concentration, into sleep, into every quiet moment.

**What to bring:**
- **Active noise-canceling headphones or earpieces** rated for the subsonic and low-frequency range. Standard consumer noise-canceling won't work here — you need equipment that can handle frequencies below 20 Hz. This is not optional comfort equipment. This is cognitive survival equipment. After 12–24 hours of constant infrasonic exposure, your ability to make decisions, read instruments, and respond to emergencies will degrade measurably.
- **Sleep system with integrated noise dampening.** You will need to sleep down here. Your submersible has sleeping quarters, or you have a bivouac protocol in your suit. Either way, the sound doesn't stop when you close your eyes. A sound-dampening sleep hood or helmet insert is essential.
- **Psychological preparation.** The sound never stops. It never changes. It never pauses. There is no silence on the Floor of Everything — not for a moment, not ever. Humans are not built for constant noise without variation. After a few days, you may find yourself doing strange things — talking to yourself, making sounds just to hear something different, or withdrawing into a numb flatness that mimics the world's own equilibrium. This is expected. Plan for it.

## V. NAVIGATION — The Tyranny of Distance

Read this carefully: the ridges are separated by hundreds of kilometers of featureless abyssal plain. The abandoned structures are spaced far apart on the ridges themselves. There are no roads, no cables, no markers, no buoys, no infrastructure of any kind.

The terrain, while described as varied (basalt ridges, volcanic spires, shield structures, chimneys), is composed of the same materials and the same colors — black basalt, pale mineral crusts, grey-white mineral dust. After hours of travel, everything begins to look the same. The ridges blur together. You lose the sense of which structure you've already visited and which is new.

**Navigation equipment:**
- **Inertial navigation system** with pre-loaded topographic maps of the region you intend to explore. If such maps exist — if anyone has surveyed this floor before you — load them. If not, you are mapping as you go, and you need to be disciplined about it.
- **Sonar or LIDAR** for terrain mapping. The water is clear enough for optical sensing at moderate range, but the distances are vast and the terrain is sharp. Sonar will give you the big picture — ridge locations, vent fields, structure signatures.
- **GPS or equivalent will not work at this depth.** Acoustic positioning systems, if available, can help but require deployment of transponders. If you plan to return to a specific structure or your ascent point, **drop acoustic markers** as you travel. This is not glamorous work. It is the work that keeps you alive.
- **Dead reckoning.** Maintain a log. Speed, heading, time, estimated distance. When everything looks the same, your log is the only thing that tells you where you've been.

**The distances will wear on you.** Hours of travel between ridges. Days between structures. The abyssal plain is flat, featureless, and dark. There is nothing to see. Your submersible's lights illuminate a cone of mineral dust and empty water. You move through an absence. Plan activities for these transit periods — data logging, equipment checks, reading, anything to keep your mind occupied. The emptiness is not dangerous in itself, but it amplifies every other stressor.

## VI. THE ABANDONED STRUCTURES — What They Are and How to Approach Them

You came here for these. Probably. Or they are what will draw your curiosity once you arrive.

The structures are heavy industrial facilities — factories, processing stations, mineral extraction rigs — built by someone who is no longer here. They are bolted to the volcanic ridges with massive anchors. Their walls are thick steel (or equivalent), corroded but intact. Their doors are open. Their machinery is dead. Mineral dust coats everything inside.

**Approach protocol:**

1. **Survey from outside first.** Use external cameras and sonar to map the structure's exterior. Note the door positions, the corrosion patterns, any structural damage. The structures have been sitting in hydrothermal mineral-laden water for millennia. While they are "still standing," individual components may have degraded unpredictably.

2. **Do not assume structural integrity.** The walls are thick and the anchors are massive, but corrosion has been continuous. Floors may be weakened. Overhead structures may be unstable. Interior machinery may have shifted. **If you're exiting the submersible to enter a structure in an atmospheric suit**, test every surface before committing weight to it.

3. **The open doors are a warning.** Whatever civilization built these structures had pressure-rated doors — doors designed to seal against the ocean. They are open. Not opened by force. Simply *open*. The mechanisms that held them shut failed, or whoever left did not close them. Either interpretation should make you cautious. The interior is exposed to the full pressure and chemistry of the deep ocean. Anything that can corrode the door mechanisms can corrode your suit's joints and seals.

4. **Bring sampling equipment.** If you intend to examine the machinery, the mineral deposits, or anything inside the structures, bring tools that can handle the pressure and cold. Standard field tools may become brittle. Use rated equipment.

5. **Time yourself.** The structures are eerie, but they are also fascinating. You could spend hours inside one, examining machinery, taking photographs, trying to understand the purpose of rooms you can't identify. Remember: your life support is finite. Set hard time limits for structure exploration and stick to them.

6. **The fauna avoid the interiors.** The description notes that the pale creatures slide past the open doorways without entering. This is interesting. It may be because the geometric interior is inhospitable to them. It may be because the interiors contain residues — chemical, thermal, or otherwise — that the fauna instinctively avoid. **Do not assume the interiors are safe just because the fauna avoid them.** Their reasons may not be your reasons, and what is inhospitable to them may be harmful to you in ways you don't expect.

## VII. THE FAUNA — Alien, Slow, and Unknown

The creatures here are not animals you recognize. They are:
- **Enormous translucent worms**, meters long, moving with geological patience.
- **Crustacean-like forms** with calcium-silica armor, scraping mineral surfaces with specialized mouthparts.
- **Filter-feeders** suspended in the water column — structures that are "almost like jellyfish, almost like sponges, almost like nothing" — pulsing rhythmically to strain organic particles from the water.

They are described as slow, pale, and abundant. They are not described as aggressive. But "not described as aggressive" is not the same as "safe."

**Precautions:**
- **Do not touch them.** You have no idea what their biology is. Calcium-silica shells suggest a biochemistry potentially based on silicon or mineral incorporation. Translucent bodies suggest no pigmentation, no light sensitivity, perhaps no vision at all. Unknown biochemistry means unknown toxins, unknown surface chemistry, unknown allergenic potential. Your suit should prevent contact, but if you're sampling or working near them, maintain distance.
- **Do not disturb the filter-feeders.** They are suspended in the water column, pulsing, feeding. Some of them may be large. If your submersible passes through a field of them, use caution — they are delicate in ways you can't predict, and disrupting a filter-feeder may release whatever it has concentrated from the water.
- **They are part of the equilibrium.** The fauna live and die at a rate that keeps their numbers exactly constant. You are not part of this equilibrium. Your presence — your vehicle's movement, your lights, your sampling, your waste — introduces a perturbation into a system that has never experienced one. Be aware that your actions here have consequences that are, in this context, almost geological in their permanence.

## VIII. THE HYDROTHERMAL VENTS — Living Geological Organs

The vents are everywhere. Black smokers and white smokers, some of them enormous — cathedral-scale columns of deposited mineral. They belch superheated water into the near-freezing ocean in a continuous, pressurized eruption.

**Near-vent hazards:**
- **Temperature gradients.** The water immediately exiting a black smoker can be 350–400°C. Two meters away, it is 2°C. The transition zone is violent, turbulent, and unpredictable. Your submersible's thermal sensors need to be accurate and responsive. A miscalculation of a few meters could put your hull in water hot enough to compromise its integrity.
- **Mineral-laden discharge.** Black smoker fluid is rich in dissolved metals — iron, copper, zinc, manganese — and hydrogen sulfide. This fluid is toxic. If you are in an atmospheric suit outside the submersible, the suit's seals and filtration systems must be rated for chemical exposure as well as pressure.
- **Structural instability.** Vent chimneys grow from deposited mineral. They are tall, narrow, and fragile in ways that their massive appearance belies. Mineral deposits can crumble. Chimneys can collapse. Do not position your vehicle directly above or down-current of an active chimney.
- **The sound is loudest near the vents.** The "constant, world-spanning roar" intensifies dramatically as you approach an active vent field. In a submersible, this will be physically uncomfortable even with noise dampening. Your equipment's structural resonances may become an issue — vibrations that were imperceptible at a distance become visible rattling in instrument panels and camera mounts.

## IX. PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION — The Deepest Challenge

You have prepared for the pressure, the cold, the sound, the distances, the alien structures, the unknown fauna, and the hostile chemistry. None of that is the hardest part.

The hardest part is the **equilibrium**.

Nothing here changes. Nothing here has ever changed. The light falls at the same intensity minute after minute, hour after hour, day after day. The sound never varies. The temperature never varies. The fauna drift past at the same speed, with the same patience, on the same paths. The vents roar with the same intensity. The structures stand with the same emptiness.

There is no weather. No dawn, no dusk, no night, no day — only the steady, diffuse sunlight filtering through the flat glassy surface kilometers above you. Your circadian rhythm, already disrupted by the absence of a normal day-night cycle, will find nothing here to anchor to.

**After 48–72 hours**, most humans in this environment report:
- **Temporal disorientation.** Inability to gauge how much time has passed. Minutes feel like hours. Hours feel like minutes.
- **Monotony depression.** A flat, grey emotional state that mirrors the environment. Not sadness, exactly. More like the emotional equivalent of the abyssal plain — featureless, empty, and extending in all directions.
- **The echo of permanence.** A growing, unshakable awareness that this place will exist exactly like this for longer than you can conceive, and that your visit is — in the literal, geological sense — nothing. You are a perturbation that will be corrected. The equilibrium will reassert itself the moment you leave. This is not an unhealthy thought, but it is an *overwhelming* one, and if you are not prepared for it, it can paralyze you.

**Countermeasures:**
- **Maintain a schedule.** Even though the environment offers no cues, impose structure on yourself. Wake at a designated time. Eat at designated times. Work, rest, exercise, sleep — all on a schedule you set and enforce with alarms. The environment offers no rhythm; you must bring your own.
- **Keep a journal.** Write daily. Not just data — personal thoughts, observations, feelings. The act of writing forces you to notice changes in yourself even when nothing outside you is changing. It becomes a record of your own humanity in a place that has no use for it.
- **Talk to someone.** If communication with a surface vessel or another traveler is possible, maintain regular contact. Even a delayed text exchange helps anchor you to the fact that other humans exist, that the world above the flat water surface still has weather and birds and irregular, unpredictable, *changing* life.
- **Set a departure date before you arrive.** Decide in advance how long you will stay. Tell someone. Write it down. The equilibrium will not encourage you to leave — it will encourage you to stay forever, not because it wants you, but because departure requires *decision*, and this place erodes the capacity for decision by removing every external reason to make one.

## X. EQUIPMENT CHECKLIST — Summary

**Life Support & Vehicle:**
- Submersible rated to 6,000+ meters, or atmospheric diving suit with equivalent rating
- Redundant heating system (primary + backup)
- Life support rated for 72+ hours (primary) with emergency extension
- Emergency ascent capability
- External lighting array (high-intensity, wide-angle, spare bulbs/elements)

**Navigation:**
- Inertial navigation system with topographic mapping
- Sonar/LIDAR terrain mapping
- Acoustic position markers (minimum 10)
- Manual dead reckoning kit (compass, log, chronometer)
- High-visibility markers for your ascent/descent point

**Sound & Psychological:**
- Subsonic-range active noise cancellation
- Sound-dampening sleep system
- Journal and writing implements
- Personal schedule system (alarms, clocks)
- Communication equipment for surface contact
- Entertainment for transit periods (books, pre-loaded media, puzzles — anything that requires active engagement)

**Exploration:**
- External cameras (high-resolution, pressure-rated)
- Sampling equipment (pressure-rated, chemically resistant)
- Manipulator arms (if exiting vehicle is not planned)
- Atmospheric suit (if structure exploration is planned)
- Structural assessment tools (sonar probe, load-testing equipment)

**Safety:**
- Redundant suit seals (minimum 3 backup sets)
- Emergency heating (chemical, rated for pressure)
- Emergency food and water (7 days beyond planned stay)
- Emergency beacon (acoustic, for local and surface)
- First aid kit adapted for hypothermia and pressure injury
- Comprehensive chemical exposure kit

**Documentation:**
- Cameras (still and video, pressure-rated, redundant)
- Environmental sensors (temperature, pressure, chemical composition, sound levels)
- Biological sampling and containment equipment
- Data storage (redundant, pressure-rated)

## XI. FINAL THOUGHTS — Why You Go

You go because there is nothing else like it.

You go because the Floor of Everything is proof that a world can exist in perfect, permanent, unchanging equilibrium — that stasis is not death, that a place can be alive and eternal and utterly indifferent to you and still be worth seeing.

You go because the abandoned structures are a question that has no answer. Who built them? Why did they leave? What were they extracting, and from where, and for what purpose? The structures will not tell you. They have been empty for longer than you can imagine. But standing inside one, in your suit, in the cold, in the roar, in the dark, looking at frozen machinery covered in mineral dust while pale translucent creatures drift past the open door — you will feel the weight of that question. And you will carry it home with you. And it will never be answered.

You go because the light is beautiful. Diffuse, full-spectrum, falling through kilometers of still water onto volcanic glass and pale mineral and the slow, patient shapes of creatures that have never seen the sky. It is the light of a world that has found its level. It is steady and clean and it has never flickered.

You go because it will change you. Not dramatically. Not in any way you can point to afterward and say "there, that's where it happened." But you will return to a world where things change — where weather happens and seasons turn and people argue and cities grow and decay — and you will feel, in a part of yourself that you didn't have before, the weight of all that changing. The preciousness of it. The strangeness of it. The fact that your world is the unusual one, the unstable one, the one that hasn't found its level yet.

And you will know, with a certainty that lives in your bones, that the Floor of Everything is still down there, still roaring, still lit by that steady light, still holding its impossible stillness — and that it will be there, exactly like that, long after everything you have ever known is gone.

---

*Go carefully. Go prepared. Come back.*
