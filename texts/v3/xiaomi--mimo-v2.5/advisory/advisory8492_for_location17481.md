# Preparing for the Mireworks

## First, Reconcile Your Expectations

The most important preparation is mental. You are not visiting a town. You are entering the digestive system of something alive. The Mireworks doesn't sit *on* the landscape — it has become the landscape, and the landscape has become it. If you arrive expecting streets, rooftops, and the familiar geometry of human settlement, you will be disoriented for your entire visit. Arrive expecting a forest that has learned to carry on a conversation, and you'll be closer to ready.

The pace here is geological by human standards. Your internal clock will rebel. You will feel the urge to hurry, to accomplish, to move through this place efficiently. Abandon that urge before you cross the basin's edge. The Mireworks does not operate on your schedule. It doesn't operate on anyone's schedule. It operates on the schedule of root growth and seasonal saturation, and if you can't synchronize with that rhythm even slightly, you will find the experience maddening rather than revelatory.

## Clothing: Your Most Critical Decision

Pack more changes of clothes than you think you could possibly need, and then add half again more.

**Nothing dries here.** That's the foundational fact that governs everything else about what you wear. The humidity approaches saturation. The condensation weeps from every surface during the cooler hours — which is to say, briefly in the very early morning before the basin's trapped warmth brings everything back to a uniform, dripping warmth. Your clothes will be damp when you put them on in the morning and damper when you take them off at night. They will smell faintly sweet and green within two days no matter what you do.

Choose **synthetic, moisture-wicking base layers** — polypropylene or merino wool, both of which will resist the mildew that cotton surrenders to within forty-eight hours. Avoid cotton entirely. It will never dry, and in this environment it will begin to grow things. You will literally feel a t-shirt start to go stiff and faintly tacky against your skin by the third day. This is not metaphor. This is biochemistry.

**Bring a mildew-resistant treatment** for all your fabrics. Spray everything before departure. Reapply when you can. Local people will have their own solutions — ask, and accept whatever they offer. They know the chemistry of keeping cloth wearable in a place that wants to compost everything.

**Outer layers** should be lightweight and water-resistant but not sealed — you need airflow against your skin or you'll develop heat rash within days. A loose, treated linen or thin synthetic shell that sheds water on contact is ideal. Zip-off sleeves are useful. Ventilation panels are useful. Anything that lets you adjust micro-ventilation without removing a layer is worth its weight in gold.

**Headwear** matters more than you expect. The canopy filters light into a perpetual diffuse green glow, but water still drips from above — constantly, irregularly, from surfaces and roots and leaf edges that you can't predict. A wide-brimmed, quick-drying hat will become your most prized possession. Treat it with waterproofing. Some visitors adopt the local habit of wearing a shallow woven basket on their head, which doubles as a carrying vessel. This is practical, not quaint.

## Footwear: Non-Negotiable

Your feet are the front line of your survival here.

The ground outside maintained paths is spongy, warm, and can swallow your foot to the ankle without warning. Even on the paths — which are raised root-mat and stone rubble — the surface is perpetually damp and slippery with a fine algae film. Inside buildings, the floors are packed root-mat, which has good grip when dry but becomes treacherous when the condensation cycle is at its peak.

You need **high-topped waterproof boots with aggressive tread** — something in the vein of modern jungle boots with drainage ports. The ports are critical: water will get in no matter what, and you need it to get *out* just as quickly. Standing water trapped against your feet for hours creates maceration, which creates fissures, which creates infection, which in a place this warm and wet becomes a medical emergency faster than you'd believe.

**Carry at least three pairs of boots** so you can rotate them. When one pair is being worn, the other two should be drying — which they won't, not really, but having them off your feet and in the warmest, most ventilated spot you can find gives them a fighting chance. Pack boot dryers if you have access to them. Some visitors bring small chemical heat packs and stuff them into their boots overnight. This is not paranoid. This is wise.

**Bring foot powder** in industrial quantities. Zinc oxide–based. Apply it obsessively. Check your feet nightly for any sign of maceration, fungal infection, or the faintly waxy skin that signals the beginning of immersion foot. Treat anything you find immediately. The locals will have their own herbal remedies — some of which, honestly, work better than anything you brought. Be humble about this.

**Bring sandals or wading shoes** for use inside buildings and on the drier platforms, where heavy boots are cumbersome and your host will quietly wish you'd taken them off. Something with drainage and grip that you can slip on and off easily.

## Respiratory Preparation

You are going to breathe in plant matter. Constantly. The air carries spore-motes, pollen, fine fragments of leaf tissue, and a faint green-tinged haze of evaporated plant exudates. For most people this is not dangerous — the body handles particulate matter well enough in moderate concentrations — but for anyone with asthma, allergies, or respiratory sensitivities, this is a serious concern.

**Bring antihistamines.** Take them preventively for the first few days even if you don't think you need them. Your body's mucous membranes will be working overtime to filter the air, and the resulting mucus production can feel alarming if you're not expecting it. It's normal here. You are clearing plant material from your lungs. It's what lungs do.

If you have any respiratory condition at all, bring a **portable air filter or a well-fitted particulate mask** for sleeping. You can wear it in the streets if you need to — no one will judge you, though they'll wonder at the unfamiliarity. Visitors from dry countries occasionally bring respirators for the first few days until they acclimate. The locals find this endearing.

The industrial zones amplify this significantly. The fiber-processing factories beat, shred, and press plant material, and the air in those areas carries concentrated particulate. If you're visiting the factory edge, **a cloth or mask over your nose and mouth is strongly recommended**, not just for particulate but because the steam from the processing vats carries mineral compounds — iron particulates, trace copper — that taste metallic and leave a film on your teeth.

## Hydration and Nutrition

**Do not drink the water untreated.** The basin water carries dissolved iron and copper sulfate in concentrations that are tolerable for long-term residents who have adapted, but which will give a visitor severe gastrointestinal distress. The locals themselves drink from deep wells or filtered collection points, not directly from the channels. Follow their lead exactly.

**Bring a robust water filter** — something rated for heavy mineral content, not just biological contaminants. Chemical treatment (iodine, chlorine) will handle pathogens but not the mineral load. A ceramic or activated-carbon filter is what you want. Some visitors bring their own water purification tablets *and* a filter and use both in sequence. This is appropriate paranoia.

The food situation is actually one of the easier adaptation points. The surrounding plain is enormously fertile, and the town's diet is rich in root vegetables, grain crops, and a variety of green leafy vegetables that grow prolifically in the basin conditions. The food is good — dense, mineral-rich, slightly sweet from the mineral content of the water used to cook it. Protein is available but less abundant; there are few animals here, and what livestock exists is raised on the drier fringes and brought in on the barges. You will eat a mostly plant-based diet while here, and you will not suffer for it, but if you're accustomed to heavy protein intake, **bring dried protein supplements** for the first week while your palate adjusts.

**Bring your own coffee, tea, or preferred stimulant.** The Mireworks produces no stimulant beverages. They drink a mildly astringent tisane made from a local bark that tastes like weak tea steeped in iron water. It is not terrible. It is not a substitute for what you're used to.

## Protecting Your Gear

This is where the Mireworks gets genuinely frustrating for a visitor.

**Everything organic will begin to grow things.** Leather gets fuzzy. Wood swells and warps. Paper develops a faint green patina within days. Your book covers will go soft and tacky. If you bring a wooden instrument, it will be unplayable within a week without constant maintenance.

**Everything metal will corrode.** The humidity and mineral content of the air accelerate oxidation with frightening speed. Your belt buckle will develop rust freckles overnight. Zippers will stiffen. Knife blades will develop a film. Bring **silicone-based lubricant** for anything mechanical and wipe everything down with an oily cloth every evening.

**Pack everything in waterproof dry bags.** Not just your clothes — everything. Your documents, your electronics, your spare glasses, your journal. Assume that at some point you will be splashed, or that the condensation cycle will find the one crack in your bag you forgot to seal, and plan accordingly.

**Bring a small repair kit:** needle, thread, fabric tape, waterproof adhesive, metal polish, a multi-tool. You will need all of it.

If you bring **electronics**, which I'd recommend limiting severely, they must be sealed. The humidity will find circuit boards and kill them. A phone in a sealed waterproof case, used sparingly, will survive. A laptop left open on a table overnight may not. Some visitors bring a small battery-powered dehumidifier and create a sealed "dry zone" in their luggage where sensitive items live. This is clever and worth doing.

**Leave anything irreplaceable at home.** If it can't survive being damp for a month, don't bring it. If it can't survive being colonized by aggressive plant growth, don't bring it. If it has deep sentimental value, the Mireworks doesn't care. It will compost it.

## Understanding the Social Contract

The Mireworks is a small, enclosed community where everyone knows everyone and has known everyone for generations. You are not a novelty — barges bring the occasional outsider — but you are an outsider, and that distinction matters.

**You are a guest in someone's living body.** The structures are alive. The walls grow. The roofs breathe. When your host shows you to a room, they are showing you a section of a tree they have been guiding for longer than you've been alive. Treat the space with the respect due to something that is simultaneously architecture and organism. Do not carve into the walls. Do not pull at the moss packing. Do not strip bark. Do not break a branch for firewood — there is no firewood; the boilers run on coal, and everything else runs on water power, and taking living wood for fuel is somewhere between theft and assault.

**The pace of social interaction matches the pace of the town.** Don't push for quick answers, fast decisions, or efficient resolutions. When someone says "we'll need to think about that," they mean it in a way that involves consulting neighbors, considering root systems, and possibly bringing it to the next monthly council meeting. Meet this with patience. Your impatience will be noticed and it will mark you as someone who doesn't understand the fundamental rhythm of this place.

**Learn the taboo around the boilers.** Don't swear near them. Don't spit near them. Don't touch them without asking. The boilers are sacred, or at least treated as sacred, and they are the only stone structures in town — the only things here that aren't alive, which gives them a particular and complex status. A visitor who disrespects the boilers will be remembered, and not kindly.

**Bring gifts** — practical ones. Metal tools are valued here (the Mireworks has limited metallurgy and imports most metal). Dried goods from other regions. Seeds, if you know what you're doing. Medicines. Information about the outside world is itself a valued gift; people here are curious and well-informed but infrequently updated. Bring stories.

## Acclimatization and Timing

**Arrive in the coolest season** if you have any choice in the matter. The basin holds warmth; even in the "cool" season, temperatures hover around 25–30°C with near-total humidity. In the hot months, the combination of heat, humidity, and the thermal mass of all that wet vegetation creates conditions that can cause heat exhaustion in unacclimatized visitors within days. Your body cannot sweat effectively when the air is already saturated with moisture. You will feel it as a creeping, suffocating weight — the sensation of being slowly submerged in warm water while standing in open air.

**Spend the first three days doing almost nothing.** Let your body adjust. Sleep as much as you can. Drink filtered water constantly, even when you don't feel thirsty — you are losing moisture through your skin and lungs in ways your thirst mechanism may not register properly. Eat lightly. Let the place inure itself to you and you to it.

**Plan for a minimum stay of two to three weeks** if you want any meaningful experience. A week will feel like suffocation in a beautiful terrarium. A month will begin to feel like inhabiting a place. Three months will change the way you think about the relationship between humans and their environment. Many visitors who stay longer than intended report difficulty readjusting to dry, open landscapes afterward — a kind of reverse culture shock where the sky feels obscene in its emptiness.

## Mental Health Preparation

This is the part most preparation guides skip, and it's the part that matters most.

**You will not see the sky.** Not really. The canopy seals you in. On rare, clear mornings, you might catch a sliver of pale brightness above the leaf ceiling, but it's not the sky as you know it. It's a rumor of sky. If you are someone who needs open horizons, wide views, unobstructed light — this place will press on you.

**You will feel enclosed.** Perpetually. The rooms glow with diffuse green light that is beautiful for the first three days and claustrophobic by the seventh. The walls are alive. The ceilings are alive. The ground under your raised platform is water and root and soft, warm earth. There is no escape from the organism. You are inside it, and it is inside you — in your lungs, in the mineral taste of the water, in the greenish cast of your own skin after a week under the filtered light.

**Bring something that connects you to open space.** A photograph of a horizon. A memory practice. Something. Some visitors find the experience profoundly peaceful once they surrender to it — the perpetual green twilight, the living walls, the slow breathing rhythm of a town that is also an organism. Others find it oppressive. You won't know which you are until you're in it.

**Talk to people.** The community is small and tight, but they are not unkind. They understand that visitors struggle. They've seen it before. There is no shame in saying "I need to sit quietly for a while" or "the walls are getting close." They'll bring you tisane and tell you stories, and the walls will still be close, but you'll feel less alone in it.

## What to Bring: A Summary

**Essential:**
- Waterproof boots (3+ pairs), high-topped, with drainage
- Synthetic moisture-wicking clothing (abundant)
- Waterproof dry bags for everything
- Water filter rated for heavy minerals
- Antihistamines, foot powder, basic medical kit
- Mildew treatment for fabrics and gear
- Silicone lubricant and metal polish
- Headlamp (the green twilight is dimmer than it reads on the page)
- Gifts: metal tools, dried goods, information
- Ear protection for industrial zones
- Hat, wide-brimmed, waterproofed

**Strongly Recommended:**
- Portable dehumidifier for a gear-drying zone
- Respirator or particulate mask (for industrial zones and sleeping)
- Sandals/wading shoes for indoor use
- Protein supplements
- Caffeine supply
- Repair kit (needle, thread, adhesive, multi-tool)
- Photographs or objects that connect you to open sky

**Leave Behind:**
- Anything irreplaceable
- Leather goods you're attached to
- Expectations of punctuality, efficiency, or dryness
- The belief that a town and a forest are different things

---

The Mireworks will not be comfortable. It will be extraordinary. You will come away understanding, in your body and not just your mind, that human habitation doesn't have to mean domination of the landscape — that you can live *inside* a growing thing, negotiate with it daily, and build a civilization on the schedule of roots and seasons. You will come away smelling faintly of green and iron, with a persistent dampness in your joints that takes weeks to fully leave, and with a very specific and hard-wear memory of what it feels like to be *inside* the process of photosynthesis.

Bring good boots. Bring patience. Bring the willingness to be changed by a place that changes on its own terms, at its own pace, indifferent to yours.
