# Preparing for the Mill-Heights

---

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

The Mill-Heights are not hostile. Nothing in the description suggests malice, predation, or active danger. What the Mill-Heights offer is something more insidious than danger: *irrelevance*. You are entering a place that was built for purposes no one recalls, that continues to operate at full capacity, that contains every surface a human body might touch but was not designed for human presence. It does not want you there. It does not want you gone. It does not think about you at all.

This matters because most travel preparation assumes a negotiation — with weather, with terrain, with inhabitants. The Mill-Heights require no negotiation. They are simply what they are, and you must be adapted to *them*, not the other way around. Everything below is organized around that principle.

---

## The Light Problem

This is the single greatest challenge of the Mill-Heights and the one most likely to end your visit early.

The ambient light is described as a persistent oversaturation — not blinding, not painful, but *relentless*. It comes from every surface simultaneously. There is no shade, no darkness to rest your eyes in, no direction you can face to reduce the input. It is the visual equivalent of a high-pitched tone that never stops: individually tolerable, cumulatively devastating.

**What this will do to you:**
- Eye strain within the first hour. The muscles of your iris, designed to constantly adjust between light and shadow, will have nothing to adjust *to*. They'll lock up. You'll get a headache that starts behind your eyes and migrates forward across your temples.
- Pupils will constrict and stay constricted. Your retinas are taking in far more light than normal ambient conditions warrant. Over hours and days, this causes fatigue, irritability, and a peculiar emotional flatness — the same glazed, overstimulated feeling you get from staring at a bright screen for too long, but with no screen to look away from.
- In the deepest interior zones, where the light has no identifiable source and simply *exists as a property of the volume*, the effect intensifies. Your visual system, trying to identify where the light is coming from, will fail repeatedly. This triggers a low-grade, persistent anxiety — not panic, just the feeling of a gear that won't engage.

**What to bring:**

*Polarized sunglasses* — quality ones, with a medium tint (not dark). You don't want to block the light; you want to reduce its intensity while preserving your ability to see detail. Dark lenses would make the spatial distortions worse by reducing your depth cues. Polarization cuts the diffuse glare bouncing off metal and concrete surfaces while letting you still read the environment.

*Photochromic lenses* as a secondary option. These adapt automatically, which matters because the light shifts subtly with the time of day and dramatically as you move between exterior basins and interior corridors. You won't always be able to stop and swap lenses.

*A brimmed hat* — wide-brimmed, light-colored. This won't help much (the light is ambient, not directional), but it provides a small cone of slightly-reduced intensity directly above your eyes, enough to give your iris a partial rest during long walks.

*Do not bring:* mirrored lenses. The Mill-Heights are already a place of infinite reflection. Mirroring your own eyes back at yourself in every surface will accelerate the psychological disorientation.

**Coping strategy:** Every thirty to forty-five minutes, find an interior room — one of the smaller ones, with dark walls or heavy equipment — and close your eyes for two to three minutes. Let your irises dilate. Let the photoreceptors in your retinas recover. This is not optional. Budget it into your schedule the way you'd budget rest stops on a long hike. If you skip them, you'll be functionally visually impaired within half a day.

## The Altitude

The air is described as cool and thin — the kind that makes your lungs feel hollow. This suggests significant elevation, likely above 3,000 meters and possibly higher. The thinness of the air means reduced oxygen saturation with every breath.

**What this will do to you:**
- Shortness of breath during exertion. Climbing the ladders and staircases that connect the Mill-Heights' vertical tiers will feel disproportionately hard. You'll huff and puff on flights of stairs that would be easy at sea level.
- Headache, nausea, fatigue, and disrupted sleep — classic altitude sickness symptoms, likely to begin within the first 12–24 hours.
- The *thin* quality of the air compounds the light problem. There's less atmosphere to scatter light, which may contribute to the overbright, washed-out quality of the visual environment.
- The cool temperature and reduced oxygen together will make you feel cold more intensely than the actual temperature warrants.

**Preparation:**

*Acclimatize before you arrive.* If you have any control over your approach, spend at least two days at moderate altitude before ascending to the Mill-Heights. The general rule is to gain no more than 300–500 meters of sleeping altitude per day above 2,500 meters. If the Mill-Heights sit at, say, 4,000 meters, you need a staged approach.

*Hydrate aggressively.* The dry, thin air and cool temperatures will dehydrate you faster than you realize. The condensation-damp environment is misleading — you'll feel moist air on your skin and assume you're fine, but the actual humidity fluctuates and the cool temperature suppresses your thirst response. Drink water on a schedule, not when you feel thirsty. Aim for three to four liters per day minimum.

*Diamox (acetazolamide)* — discuss with a physician before travel. It's the standard prophylactic for altitude sickness and may be worth carrying even if you don't plan to take it. Have it available in case symptoms escalate beyond what rest and hydration can manage.

*Portable oxygen* — consider a small, lightweight canister with a nasal cannula. This isn't for normal walking; it's for emergencies. If you develop symptoms of high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) — persistent cough, extreme breathlessness at rest, gurgling sounds when breathing — you need to descend immediately. Having supplemental oxygen buys time during that descent.

**Physical fitness matters more here than at most destinations.** You will be walking for hours on hard surfaces, climbing vertical ladders, navigating uneven floors, all while your body receives less oxygen per breath. Train for endurance before you go. Your cardiovascular system is your most important piece of equipment.

## Water and Hydration

The Mill-Heights have water — condensation, puddles, small streams running through forgotten drainage channels, still pools in the lower depressions. The description is almost seductive: clean, cool, catching the bright light.

**Do not drink it.**

The water tastes of iron, calcium, and "something else — something mineral and unplaceable." This is condensation that has been collecting on industrial surfaces for potentially decades. It has been in contact with rusting steel, old concrete, machine oil, electrical insulation, and whatever the machines beneath the surface are producing. The "something else" in the taste is a red flag — unidentified mineral content in an environment where industrial processes have been running无人监督 for an unknowable duration.

**Water strategy:**

*Bring all the water you need.* Calculate your daily requirement (3–4 liters minimum given the altitude and exertion level) multiplied by your intended duration, plus a generous safety margin. The Mill-Heights are vast. Rescue, if it were possible, would take a long time. You cannot count on resupply.

*Water purification as backup only.* If you run out, the condensation water could be filtered and chemically treated as a last resort, but it would taste terrible and the chemical contaminants (heavy metals from steel and copper, potentially industrial solvents or byproducts) may not be removed by standard purification. A quality activated carbon filter is the minimum; reverse osmosis would be better but is heavy to carry.

*A reusable water bottle with volume markings.* Measure what you drink. At altitude, you lose more water than you think — through respiration, through the cool dry air wicking moisture from your skin, through the physical exertion. The markings help you track intake and catch dehydration before it becomes debilitating.

**For drinking water:**
- Avoid caffeine (it accelerates dehydration and worsens altitude effects)
- Avoid alcohol completely (altitude + alcohol is dangerous and impairs judgment you will need badly)
- Consider electrolyte tablets to add to your water — the exertion and altitude will deplete sodium and potassium

## Clothing and Footwear

The environment is cool, damp, and unforgivingly hard. The floor is concrete and steel. The air carries intermittent moisture. The temperature is stable but cool, and the thin air makes it feel colder.

**Footwear — this is critical:**

You will be walking for hours on poured concrete and riveted steel plate. Your feet, knees, and lower back will bear the punishment. The surfaces are hard enough to cause joint pain within a single day and stress fractures over multiple days.

- *Quality hiking boots with thick, cushioned soles.* Vibram or similar compound. The sole is your primary defense against the relentless hardness of the floor. Look for maximum cushioning and arch support. Break them in thoroughly before the trip — you cannot afford blisters here.
- *Moisture-wicking socks, at least two pairs per day.* Your feet will sweat in the boots, and the ambient dampness means they won't dry quickly. Rotate socks to prevent maceration (softening of the skin from prolonged dampness, which leads to blisters and fungal infections).
- *Gaiters* — lightweight, for keeping condensation drips off your lower legs. The water comes from above constantly. Running down your pants and into your boots, it will make your feet wet and cold and miserable.

**Clothing layers:**

*Base layer:* Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking. Not cotton — cotton absorbs moisture and holds it against your skin, which is miserable in cool, damp conditions and dangerous if temperatures drop.

*Mid layer:* Fleece or light insulated jacket. The Mill-Heights are cool but not freezing. You want warmth without bulk — you'll be climbing ladders and squeezing through narrow gaps between structures.

*Outer layer:* Lightweight, waterproof, and breathable shell. You need protection from the constant condensation without creating sweat. A hardshell jacket with pit zips is ideal — waterproof when closed, ventilated when you're exerting.

*Pants:* Quick-drying, durable. Reinforced knees if possible — you may find yourself crawling through gaps or kneeling on concrete. Avoid loose fabric that could snag on the industrial infrastructure.

*Gloves:* Thin, grippy work gloves. The steel and concrete surfaces will be cold and potentially sharp-edged (rust, rough concrete, burrs on metal). You'll be grabbing railings, climbing ladders, bracing yourself against walls. Your hands need protection and grip.

**Additional gear:**

*A headlamp* — with a red-light mode. Yes, the Mill-Heights are always bright. But the headlamp is *yours* — a light source you control, that comes from where you're looking, that you can turn off. In a place where light comes from everywhere and nowhere, having a controllable point source is psychologically grounding. The red-light mode preserves your night vision (such as it is) and is less disruptive to the fauna. More importantly, shining your own light on a surface gives you a reliable depth cue when the ambient light flattens everything.

*High-visibility tape or chalk.* This is not optional. It is your primary survival tool.

## Navigation — The Most Dangerous Aspect

Let me be direct: **the Mill-Heights are designed to get you lost.** Not intentionally — there is no intention here, no mind, no malice. But the effect is the same. The architecture shifts. The proportions distort. Sounds arrive out of sequence. Rooms repeat. Corridors appear to change width. Your perception — the fundamental tool you use to navigate any space — becomes unreliable within hours of entering.

And the place is *vast*. "You can walk for hours in any direction and not reach an edge." You are inside a constructed basin measured in kilometers, with no landmarks, no horizon, no cardinal directions visible through the dense infrastructure.

**This is how people die in the Mill-Heights.** Not from violence, not from falls, not from exposure. They die because they can't find their way out. They walk in circles they don't recognize as circles. They pass the same stain on the same floor and feel the vertigo of false recognition but can't act on it. They follow corridors that seemed to lead somewhere and find themselves deeper. The place doesn't trap them. It just fails, persistently and gently, to help them leave.

**Navigation equipment:**

*Physical line — heavy cord, 100+ meters.* Tie one end to your entry point. Unspool it as you walk. This is your lifeline. No amount of high-tech navigation replaces a physical connection to a known location. The Mill-Heights' spatial distortions cannot move your cord. It will be there, where you left it, leading back.

*Chalk or removable tape.* Mark every junction. Every ladder. Every corridor entrance. Mark them with sequential numbers, not just arrows — arrows assume you know which direction to go, numbers let you retrace your exact path. The marks will last longer than you'd expect; the air is dry enough in many zones, and no one is cleaning.

*A laser rangefinder.* When a corridor *feels* like it's narrowing, measure it. When a room *feels* too large for its exterior dimensions, measure the walls. Your perception will lie to you here. The rangefinder will not. Keep a written log of measurements. If you measure a corridor at 2.1 meters wide and then measure it again and get 2.3 meters, either you're in a different corridor (which means you're lost) or the space is changing (which means you're lost in a different way). Either way, the information is valuable.

*A compass — but understand its limitations.* The Mill-Heights are saturated with steel and electrical systems. A magnetic compass will be unreliable in many zones, pulled toward structural steel and power cables. Use it in open areas and cross-reference with other navigation methods. It's a data point, not a solution.

*GPS — unlikely to work.* The dense metal construction will block satellite signals in most interior spaces. If you can get a signal in the upper open basins, take a fix and mark it, but don't depend on it.

*An audio recording device.* Record yourself narrating your path as you walk. "Entering corridor B-17, turning left at the junction with the rust stain, ascending ladder 22..." Later, when your memory of the route becomes confused (and it will), you can play back the recording and verify your actual path against what you remember. The Mill-Heights mess with your sense of time and sequence; the recording doesn't.

**Navigation discipline:**

- **Never leave your line.** The cord tied to your entry point is not a suggestion. It is the difference between walking back out and wandering until you collapse.
- **Mark every decision point.** Every time you turn left or right, mark the junction. Every time you ascend or descend, mark the ladder or staircase. Assume you will need to retrace your path.
- **Set time limits for exploration.** Decide in advance: "I will walk for two hours from my entry point, then return." The cord gives you the means to return; the time limit gives you the discipline to actually do it.
- **Never follow a corridor because it feels right.** The Mill-Heights generate false familiarity. "This feels like it leads somewhere" is your least reliable signal. Follow your cord. Follow your markings. Follow your rangefinder measurements. Ignore your instincts about direction.
- **Carry a physical map and draw it as you go.** Even a rough sketch of junctions, ladder positions, and room shapes, annotated with measurements and timestamps, gives you an external representation of the space that your brain can reference when perception fails. Update it constantly.

## Food and Nutrition

No food source exists in the Mill-Heights. The fauna — birds, rats, mice, moths, insects — has adapted to an industrial environment and is almost certainly contaminated with heavy metals, chemical residues, and whatever the machines are producing. Do not eat them. Do not eat the lichen-like growths on the walls. Do not eat anything you find.

**Bring all food you need:**

- *Calorie-dense, lightweight, non-perishable.* Dehydrated meals, energy bars, nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese. You'll be burning more calories than usual due to the altitude, the cold, and the sustained walking. Budget 3,000–4,000 calories per day.
- *Resealable packaging.* The Mill-Heights are damp. Open a package and the moisture will soften and spoil the contents within hours. Reseal everything tightly.
- *A small, lightweight stove and fuel* if you'll be there more than a couple of days. Hot food and hot water for tea are not luxuries — they're psychological anchors. In a place this alien and disorienting, the ritual of making and consuming a hot meal is a grounding practice that keeps you tethered to your own reality.
- *Salt packets.* The cool, thin air and sustained exertion will deplete sodium. Add salt to your food and water as needed.

## Fauna and Biological Hazards

The Mill-Heights have a thriving ecosystem, but it's an ecosystem that evolved in an industrial environment. The animals are adapted to concrete, steel, condensation, and whatever chemical signatures the machines produce.

**Insects:**

- *Mosquitoes* near standing water. They carry whatever pathogens the condensation water harbors. Bring insect repellent (DEET or picaridin-based) and consider a permethrin treatment on your clothing. A head net is lightweight insurance.
- *Beetles, silverfish, flies* — generally harmless but potentially carrying contaminants from their environment. Don't touch them. Wash your hands after touching surfaces where they congregate.
- *The metallic green flies drawn to running machinery* — avoid breathing near them in tight spaces. Insects near industrial machinery can pick up and aerosolize lubricant particles, metal dust, and chemical residues.

**Rats and larger fauna:**

- The rats are rats. They bite, they carry disease, they get into food stores. Keep all food in sealed containers, ideally suspended from a cord or stored in a hard-sided case. Set up a clean sleeping area away from walls and floor-level openings.
- The pale, long-legged mice in the open spaces are probably harmless but avoid handling. They live in an environment soaked in industrial chemicals.
- The *thing in the deepest corridors* — the one you hear dragging and shifting — is the reason you don't go deep. The oldest zones, where the geometry is least trustworthy, are where the largest fauna lives. Respect this boundary absolutely. Your line, your time limits, and your rangefinder measurements are your defense against accidentally wandering into the deep zones. Use them.

**Biological growth:**

- The pale green lichen or mineral deposit on damp surfaces — do not touch, inhale near, or disturb. In an industrial environment, biological growth on concrete and metal is often fed by unusual mineral and chemical inputs. Some species produce toxic compounds. Assume all biological growth in the Mill-Heights is suspect.
- The roots reaching up through floor cracks — interesting but irrelevant to your survival. Do not eat them. Do not assume that because something is growing here, the environment is benign. Plants in contaminated soil accumulate heavy metals. These roots are probably saturated with them.

**Hygiene:**

- Bring hand sanitizer and use it frequently. Your hands will touch surfaces covered in decades of accumulated industrial residue, condensation minerals, and biological growth.
- Wet wipes for body cleaning. Showers don't exist here.
- A small first aid kit with antiseptic, bandages, antibiotic ointment, and blister treatment. In a place this isolated, even a small infected cut is a serious problem.

## The Sound Environment — Psychological and Physical Effects

The Mill-Heights are profoundly quiet, but the quiet is constructed from imprisoned sound rather than absence of it. A vast machinery hum operates beneath everything — you feel it in your floor, your chest, your teeth. The walls are too dense for sound to escape, so it compresses, becomes pressure rather than noise.

**What this does to you:**

- *Infrasound effects.* Machinery operating at scale produces low-frequency vibration, much of it below the threshold of hearing but within the range of physical perception. Infrasound at certain frequencies causes anxiety, unease, a sense of being watched or followed, nausea, and in extreme cases, visual disturbances (peripheral shadow movement, difficulty focusing). The Mill-Heights are full of this. The feeling of spatial weirdness — the corridors that seem to narrow, the rooms that feel too large — may be partially caused by infrasound interacting with your vestibular system and visual processing.
- *Sound displacement.* Sounds arriving before their causes, footsteps sounding ahead of you, drips appearing to be in the wrong room. This is deeply disorienting. It disrupts your ability to build a spatial model of your environment. You can't use echo or sound direction to orient yourself, because the acoustics are actively misleading.
- *The silence between sounds.* When the machines aren't producing infrasound, or when you move into a zone with less activity, the silence is enormous. It presses. Humans are not built for the kind of silence the Mill-Heights produce — the absence of wind, traffic, voices, life. It causes psychological stress that accumulates slowly and manifests as irritability, paranoia, and a creeping sense of unreality.

**Coping strategies:**

- *Noise-cancelling headphones* — not to play music (though that's an option) but to *reduce* the infrasound pressure. Active noise cancellation is designed to counteract low-frequency ambient noise. It won't block the hum entirely, but it can reduce the physical pressure enough to prevent the worst of the infrasound effects. Use them periodically, not constantly — you need to remain aware of your acoustic environment for safety.
- *Your own voice.* Talk to yourself. Narrate what you see. "I am walking north through corridor C-12. The walls are smooth concrete. The corridor is 2.1 meters wide. I can hear water dripping to my left." This serves double duty: it grounds you in reality, and it provides an external sound source that you can use to verify the acoustics. If your voice sounds like it's coming from the wrong direction, you know the sound displacement is active in this zone.
- *A personal sound source* — a small speaker playing ambient sound (rain, conversation, music) at low volume. Something constant, something predictable, something that responds the way sound is supposed to respond. It's an anchor.

## The Psychological Challenge — This Is What Will Actually End Your Visit

Everything above — the light, the altitude, the water, the navigation, the fauna — is manageable with preparation and equipment. The thing that will actually drive you out of the Mill-Heights, or deeper into them, is the psychological effect of the place itself.

The description is very clear about this: *"If you stay long enough, you begin to remember them too — rooms you have never entered that feel familiar, corridors you have never walked that feel like returning, a place you have never been that feels, with a strange and quiet certainty, like somewhere you have always known."*

This is not metaphor. This is a real effect, and it is the most dangerous thing in the Mill-Heights. The place generates false familiarity. It *rhymes with itself* — emergent patterns of space that your brain, pattern-hungry and desperate for orientation, latches onto and misinterprets as memory. You start feeling at home. You start recognizing things. And that recognition pulls you deeper.

**Protective practices:**

- *Time limits, enforced ruthlessly.* Decide before you enter: "I will spend X hours in the Mill-Heights, no more." Set an alarm. When it goes off, you leave, following your line back to your entry point. No exceptions. "I'll just go a little further" is the thought the Mill-Heights want you to have.
- *A physical talisman.* Something from outside, something distinctly *not* of the Mill-Heights. A photograph of someone you love. A stone from a riverbank. A letter. Something that connects you to the world beyond these walls and reminds you that you are a visitor here, not a resident. Touch it when the false familiarity rises.
- *A journal.* Write in it regularly. Record what you've seen, what you've felt, what time it is, what your plan is. Review what you wrote an hour ago. The Mill-Heights distort your sense of time and sequence; the journal is your external memory, harder to corrupt than the internal kind.
- *A firm identity practice.* Remind yourself regularly of your name, your home, your reasons for being here, your plan for leaving. This sounds absurdly simple, but in an environment that generates false memories and false recognition, having a clear, externally-anchored sense of who you are is a critical defense.
- *Recognize the moment to leave.* There will be a moment — it might come subtly — when you feel more comfortable in the Mill-Heights than you should. When the noise of your own equipment and the sight of your own gear starts to feel like an intrusion. When the quiet starts to feel like peace rather than oppression. When the light stops bothering you. *That is the moment to leave.* You have begun to adapt to something you should not adapt to. Follow your line. Exit. Don't come back for at least a week, and when you do, assess honestly whether you want to.

## Final Thought

The Mill-Heights will not try to keep you. They will not try to release you. They will do neither, because they are not aware of you. You are a small, warm, temporary thing walking through a vast machine that has been running since before you arrived and will continue long after you leave. Your preparation is not about conquering this place or surviving its dangers — it is about maintaining, against the gentle, patient, overwhelming pressure of the environment, the simple fact that you are *you*, that you are *here temporarily*, and that you intend to leave.

The Mill-Heights do not hurry. Make sure you do.
