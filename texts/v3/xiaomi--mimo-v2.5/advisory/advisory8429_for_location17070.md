# Preparing for The Breathing Ditch

## First Principles: What You're Actually Walking Into

Before you pack a single thing, understand what The Breathing Ditch is. It is not a cave. It is not a jungle. It is not a volcano. It is the exposed interior of a world still in the act of being born, and it has no accommodation for you whatsoever. The place is not hostile — hostility implies intention. The Ditch is *indifferent* with the completeness of a geological process. It does not hate you. It does not know you exist. It is too busy becoming itself.

Everything below is organized by the question: **What will kill you fastest, and how do you prevent it?**

---

## I. GROUND AND FEET

**The Threat:** The basalt is fractured into angular plates with edges sharp enough to slice through standard boot leather. The ground radiates heat — in some places warm as a fever, in others hot enough to blister exposed skin. New fumaroles open without warning, meaning solid ground can become a vent between one step and the next. Pools are thermally unpredictable: some are comfortably warm, others are near-boiling mineral acid, and there is no visual distinction between them until contact.

**The Preparation:**

Your footwear is the single most critical piece of equipment. You need a boot that does three contradictory things simultaneously: insulates against ground heat, resists penetration by razor-edged basalt, and seals against water and mineral liquid.

The traders from the upland settlements use layered constructions — inner wraps of a fibrous plant material for insulation, outer shells of hardened bone plates lashed together. This is elegant and well-adapted to their environment, but a multiverse traveler can do better with modern materials. You want:

- **A rigid-soled boot** with a puncture-resistant sole plate — think Kevlar or ceramic-composite, rated for volcanic terrain. The sole must be thick enough to provide meaningful thermal insulation from ground temps that could range from 40°C to well over 100°C in vent-proximate areas. A Vibram-style sole will not be enough. You need something purpose-built for volcanic approach work.
- **Ankle-high to mid-calf height**, sealed with a waterproof gasket or integrated gaiter. The mineral pools will splash onto your feet. Some of these pools are acidic, loaded with dissolved minerals that will eat through untreated leather or nylon in hours.
- **An outer磨-resistant layer**. The basalt will grind against your boot with every step. Standard synthetic fabrics will be shredded within the first mile. Consider a boot with metal or ceramic-infused panels on the outer surfaces, particularly around the toe and the inner ankle where rock strikes are most frequent.
- **A spare pair.** Boots will be destroyed. Bring at least two pairs. The traders make do with what they can craft; you have the luxury of preparation, so use it.

**A walking stick or probe** is essential — not for balance, but for testing ground ahead. A telescoping pole with a thermal-sensing tip (even a simple thermochromic marking that changes color at specific temperature thresholds) will let you probe the ground before committing weight. You are testing for two things: thin crust over a void (a fumarole about to open), and liquid pools disguised by mineral film on the surface.

Move slowly. Move deliberately. Every step is a negotiation with ground that is rearranging itself.

## II. HEARING AND SOUND

**The Threat:** The sound wall described in the documentation is not metaphorical. Sustained exposure to multi-tonal noise at the levels described — with components spanning from subsonic bass to high shrieks — will cause physiological damage. Tinnitus. Disorientation. Nausea. Cognitive degradation. The subsonic component in particular is insidious: frequencies below 20 Hz are felt more than heard, and prolonged exposure affects equilibrium, causes anxiety and dread responses, and can produce visual disturbances. The traders report violent dreams; this may partly be auditory trauma manifesting during sleep.

There is also the echolocation problem. Creatures in the Ditch navigate by sound. Your own noise — your footsteps, your breathing, your equipment — broadcasts your position and nature to everything in earshot. The Ditch's fauna have never encountered a human. They will investigate. Some will be curious. Some will be defensive. At minimum, your noise profile will shape every encounter.

**The Preparation:**

You need **active noise filtering**, not passive blocking. Total silence is dangerous — you need to hear approaching fauna, the hiss of a pool, the crack of calving rock. What you want is a system that:

- **Suppresses sustained ambient noise** (the roar, the hum, the drone) by 20–30 dB, bringing it to a manageable background level.
- **Passes transient sounds** (clicks, movements, cracking, hissing) at full fidelity or even amplified. You need to hear the difference between a benign pool and one about to boil over.
- **Filters the subsonic component** specifically. A tuned mechanical or electronic damper that reduces subsonic transmission through bone conduction. This is the frequency range that will unmake your sleep and your sanity. If you can only bring one modification to your hearing protection, make it this one.

Modern options include active noise-cancelling earbuds with customizable frequency profiles. However, electronics are vulnerable here — the humidity is extreme, the mineral-laden moisture is corrosive, and the electromagnetic environment around a geologically active valley is unpredictable. If you bring electronic hearing protection, waterproof it. Harshly. Put it in sealed housings with external microphones. Bring backups. Bring non-electronic alternatives.

A cruder but reliable option: molded ear inserts (like musician's earplugs) with tuned dampening filters, plus a separate throat-conduction audio system for communication with any travel companions. Bone conduction bypasses the ear canal and may be less affected by the sound environment.

**Communication with companions** is severely impaired. You will not be able to speak at normal volume. Shouting will not work — the Ditch absorbs shouts into its roar. Plan for hand signals, light signals, or short-range radio (waterproofed, shielded). Establish your communication protocol before entering the valley. Not after.

## III. AIR AND BREATHING

**The Threat:** The atmosphere in The Breathing Ditch is a layered chemical soup. You are dealing with:

- **Volcanic gases:** Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) are near-certain given the active vents and fumaroles. Both are acutely toxic. H₂S is insidious — at low concentrations it smells like rotten eggs; at higher concentrations it deadens the olfactory nerve, so you stop smelling it right when it's about to kill you. SO₂ is an immediate respiratory irritant.
- **Ash particles:** The perpetual ceiling is composed of volcanic ash and condensed steam. Fine particulate matter in the air — abrasive, irritating to lung tissue, potentially damaging with prolonged exposure.
- **Biological material:** This is the one the documentation emphasizes. You are breathing the Ditch's reproductive ecosystem. Spores, pollen, fungal fragments, bacterial aerosols, the microscopic detritus of organisms that are propagating with "frantic generosity." Some of this will be allergenic. Some may be infectious. Some may be biologically active in ways you don't want — the fungal-spirals are noted as "mildly psychoactive" when eaten; inhaled fungal spores concentrated enough could have more systemic effects.
- **Heat and humidity:** The air is warm, saturated with moisture from the condensation process. Breathing warm, humid air is physiologically taxing — it reduces the body's ability to thermoregulate through respiration and increases perceived exertion.

**The Preparation:**

You need a **respirator with chemical and particulate filtration.** Not a dust mask. Not a bandana. A proper respirator — half-face minimum, ideally full-face to protect eyes from irritant gases.

- **The filter cartridge** must handle both particulate (ash, spores) and chemical (SO₂, H₂S) threats. A combination cartridge rated for acid gas and particulate (look for multi-gas/P100 combination filters) is the baseline.
- **A full-face respirator** has the added benefit of protecting your eyes, which will thank you. The mineral moisture is acidic; getting it in your eyes repeatedly will cause chemical irritation that compounds over time.
- **Filter life:** You are breathing heavily in a chemically aggressive environment. Filters will clog faster than rated. Bring many spares. Mark the installation date and time on each filter. If you start breathing harder or tasting sulfur, change the filter immediately.
- **Hydration:** A respirator makes drinking difficult. Plan your hydration system around it — a hydration bladder with a bite valve that can be used without removing the mask, or pre-portioned water in containers you can access quickly during rest stops.

**If you cannot obtain a proper respirator** (some universes simply don't have the industrial base), then your fallback is a wet cloth treated with a basic chemical neutralizer — baking soda solution helps with SO₂; a thin layer of activated charcoal (crushed and packed into fabric) provides some general-purpose filtration. This is inferior. This is survival, not comfort. But it is better than nothing, and people have entered hostile atmospheres with less.

**Do not sleep in the valley without filtration.** Eight hours of breathing that air with a resting respiratory rate will expose you to vastly more biological and chemical material than a few hours of active traversal. If you must sleep in the Ditch — and you should plan not to — wear your respirator.

## IV. LIGHT AND DARKNESS

**The Threat:** Perpetual geological darkness is disorienting in ways that go beyond simply not seeing. Human proprioception, spatial reasoning, and emotional regulation all depend partially on visual information. Prolonged darkness degrades all of these. You will become clumsy. You will become anxious. Your sense of time will distort. This is well-documented in cave exploration and polar night research.

Additionally, the Ditch is not uniformly dark — the bioluminescence creates a false, shifting, organism-generated light that moves and pulses. This is potentially more disorienting than pure darkness, because your visual system will be constantly trying to process and interpret light patterns that have no stable source or consistent behavior. The coordinated pulsing that observers report may be a genuine phenomenon; standing in it at ground level, surrounded by it, may produce perceptual effects that are difficult to predict.

**The Preparation:**

Bring a light source, but use it sparingly and strategically. The Ditch's fauna have evolved in this darkness; their visual systems (where they have them) are adapted to bioluminescent wavelengths. A white LED flashlight will be blinding to them and conspicuous to everything within visual range. You are an intrusion of alien light in a system that has none.

- **Red-spectrum light** is your primary tool. Red wavelengths are the least disruptive to both your own dark-adapted vision and the vision of most fauna. A dim red headlamp, worn on low, gives you enough light to read the ground immediately ahead without announcing yourself to the entire valley.
- **UV or blue-spectrum light** should be carried but used only for specific purposes — identifying bacterial mat species by fluorescence, or checking pool temperatures if UV-reactive indicators are present. The bioluminescence in the Ditch peaks in blue-green; your own blue-green light source will blend with the environment and make you harder to distinguish from the background glow, which may be advantageous or dangerous depending on what you're trying to avoid.
- **Do not use white light casually.** Every time you fire a bright white light, you are creating a momentary disruption — blinding yourself, blinding nearby fauna, casting sharp shadows that make the fractured terrain momentarily harder to read. Use it only when you need to make a fast assessment of a wide area (approaching a pool, scanning for fumaroles, checking your route).
- **Dark adaptation:** Your eyes will take 30–45 minutes to fully adapt to the Ditch's darkness. During this period you will be severely limited. Plan to enter the Ditch during a rest period — find a relatively stable spot, sit, let your vision adapt, listen to the environment, and begin processing the sound landscape before you start moving. Rushing into the dark while your eyes are still adjusting is how you step into a fumarole or a pool.

The bioluminescent glow will be, honestly, beautiful. Allow yourself to see it. But understand that the beauty is a byproduct of an ecosystem's reproductive machinery, not a welcome. It is not for you.

## V. HEAT AND THERMOREGULATION

**The Threat:** The ground radiates heat. The air is warm and saturated with moisture. Geothermal vents produce localized zones of extreme heat. The combination of ambient warmth, high humidity, and physical exertion (navigating fractured terrain is exhausting) creates a high risk of hyperthermia — heat exhaustion and heat stroke. Your body's primary cooling mechanism (evaporative sweating) is severely degraded in saturated air. You will sweat, but the sweat will not evaporate, and you will overheat despite it.

Conversely, the ash ceiling is cool — that is the entire reason condensation forms. If you spend time near the ceiling's lowest points (unlikely on the valley floor, but possible on the ridgeline approach), there may be cool air layers that create confusing microclimate variations.

**The Preparation:**

- **Clothing:** Lightweight, loose-fitting, breathable fabric in light colors. The Ditch is dark, so color doesn't matter for visibility, but light-colored fabric absorbs less radiant heat from below. Natural fibers or advanced synthetics with moisture-wicking properties. Avoid rubber or plastic anything — they trap heat and moisture against your skin.
- **No full-body sealed suits.** You need air circulation against your skin. A chemical-resistant apron or chaps over breathable base layers gives you protection where you need it (lower body, where splash from pools is a risk) without creating a heat trap.
- **Active cooling:** If you have access to phase-change cooling vests or ice-pack systems, use them. They will not last long in this environment, but even a few hours of assisted cooling during the most physically demanding portions of your traverse could prevent heat injury.
- **Electrolytes:** You will lose salt and minerals through sweat at an accelerated rate. Bring electrolyte supplements — tablets, powders, or pre-mixed solutions. Plain water is not enough. Dehydration combined with electrolyte imbalance in a geologically active environment where you need to think clearly and move precisely is a fast path to a fatal mistake.
- **Rest cycles:** Plan to stop every 30–45 minutes for active cooling and hydration. Sit still. Breathe through your respirator. Let your heart rate come down. The Ditch is not going anywhere. You are the fragile thing here.

## VI. BIOLOGICAL HAZARDS AND FAUNA

**The Threat:** The Ditch's fauna is abundant, diverse, and has never encountered a human being. It does not know what you are. This means every interaction is an experiment for both parties, and some experiments end badly.

Specific concerns:

- **Insects with eight-inch wingspans** that navigate by echolocation and "crash into things frequently and without apparent consequence." They will crash into you. Repeatedly. Their wings may have spines, hooks, or abrasive surfaces. Their impact, at wing-size and flight speed, is not trivial.
- **Six-legged mammals with translucent skin** — these are likely the most ecologically significant fauna. They are glowing. They are everywhere. Some will be predators. A predator adapted to ambush in near-darkness, with bioluminescent camouflage matching its prey, is a serious threat if you are perceived as either prey or obstacle.
- **Eyeless wall-clingers** ranging from coin-sized to table-sized. These are ambush specialists that have abandoned vision entirely. They detect vibration, heat, or chemical trails. Your footsteps, your warmth, your exhaled CO₂ — these are beacons announcing your position to everything that hunts by non-visual means. Which is most things in the Ditch.
- **The ape-analogues with four arms and tool use.** These are the most cognitively complex organisms in the valley. They use sticks and stones. They have not encountered you. They may be curious, defensive, or predatory. They may also be the closest thing to a potential ally or informant in the Ditch — the traders harvest from the valley but do they *interact* with its fauna? The ape-analogues' tool-use suggests a threshold intelligence that could, in theory, communicate in rudimentary ways. This is also the most dangerous possibility: a species smart enough to plan, armed with stones, in a system where they have no natural reason to fear you.
- **The mucilage and spore environment.** Even without direct fauna contact, you are wading through a biological medium. Spores will land on your skin, in your hair, on your equipment. Some will germinate. Some will produce chemical effects. The bacterial mats "shift color" based on temperature — this is metabolic activity, and the organisms producing it may be doing things to the chemical composition of the water and soil that affect you on contact.

**The Preparation:**

- **Full-coverage clothing:** Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, hood, face protection. You want to minimize exposed skin. Not because of cold (there is no cold here) but because every square centimeter of exposed skin is a surface for biological colonization. The Ditch's organisms will settle on you. You want to make that as difficult as possible.
- **Smooth outer layers.** Spines, hooks, and adhesive organisms have an easier time gripping rough fabric. A smooth-surfaced outer layer — think tightly woven synthetic or a treated shell — lets things slide off rather than catch.
- **Gloves with grip.** You will be placing your hands on basalt to steady yourself. Sharp basalt. Hot basalt. Mucilage-covered basalt. Your gloves need to protect against all three while still giving you enough tactile feedback to read the rock surface. This is a hard engineering problem. Leather palms with ceramic-fiber reinforcement on the back, sealed seams, waterproof liner. Bring four pairs. They will be shredded.
- **No exposed food or waste.** Biological material from your body — food crumbs, sweat, urine, blood — will attract fauna. Everything in the Ditch is tuned to chemical signals. You are a walking chemical signal of something the ecosystem has never encountered. Pack out all waste. Seal all food containers. If you cut yourself (and on this terrain, you will), clean and seal the wound immediately. Blood in the Ditch is a dinner bell.
- **Do not touch the fauna.** This should be obvious, but when something with translucent skin and internal bioluminescence floats past your face, the instinct to reach out is strong. Don't. You don't know what it's carrying, what it will do if touched, or what its defensive mechanisms are. Observe. Record. Do not interact unless interaction is forced on you, and if it is, your first response should be to withdraw calmly and quickly, not to fight.
- **Sleeping security:** If you sleep in the Ditch (again, plan not to), you need a sealed sleeping environment. A tent will be shredded by the basalt terrain and colonized by fauna within hours. A suspended hammock or platform, raised above the ground and sealed with fine mesh, is more viable — it gets you off the warm ground (where crawling things are most concentrated) and behind a physical barrier. But the mesh must be fine enough to exclude the smallest fauna and strong enough to resist the larger ones. And you will hear everything through it. Every click, every drone, every shriek, every subsonic groan. Sleep will be difficult.

## VII. GROUND INSTABILITY AND NAVIGATION

**The Threat:** The valley floor rises and falls. Fumaroles open without warning. The eastern wall calves in sheets. Pools appear and vanish. There are no trails, no markers, no landmarks in the conventional sense — the darkness precludes visual wayfinding, and the sound environment precludes auditory wayfinding (every direction sounds the same). The ground beneath you is not a surface; it is a membrane over a magma system, and it is in constant flux.

You can become lost in a space that is 10 miles across. This is not a joke or an exaggeration. In perpetual darkness, with no visual reference points and a sound environment that eliminates directional cues, ten miles is a lifetime.

**The Preparation:**

- **Inertial navigation or GPS equivalent.** Whatever your universe's technology provides for dead-reckoning navigation — a compass will not work here (too much magnetic interference from geological activity). Inertial navigation systems that track your movements from a known starting point are your best bet. If you have nothing electronic, then you need a physical method: a line. A rope or wire paid out from your entry point, marking your path. This is how cave explorers prevent getting lost in unmapped systems. Bring hundreds of meters of high-visibility (to you, not to the fauna) line and secure it to the basalt as you go — wedged into cracks, tied to rock projections. It is ugly, primitive, and it may save your life.
- **Thermal awareness.** The ground temperature will tell you things. Warmer ground means you are closer to a vent or thermal feature. A sudden temperature increase underfoot means you should stop and probe ahead before continuing. If you have thermal imaging capability, use it to scan the ground ahead for hot spots, vent precursors, and pool boundaries.
- **Route planning based on the geological description.** The eastern wall is basalt that is still actively cooling and calving. The western wall is not described — it may be more stable. The valley is 40 miles long. You do not need to cross it. You need to reach your objective (whatever that is — fungal-spiral harvest site, biological sampling point, documentation site) and return. Plan a route that minimizes distance, avoids known vent clusters (if any are mapped by the traders), and maintains proximity to the wall for orientation. In the darkness, a wall is a wall — you can follow it by touch.
- **Time tracking.** Without celestial cues or light cycles, you have no natural sense of time. Bring a reliable timekeeping device. Check it regularly. The Ditch will distort your sense of duration; the traders' reports of persistent perceptual effects suggest the valley influences cognition over time. Knowing how long you have been inside is a tether to reality.

## VIII. PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION

**The Threat:** This is the one that the documentation circles without naming directly. The Ditch is not just physically dangerous. It is *cognitively invasive*.

The traders report: violent dreams. Persistent afterimages. The feeling of the ground deciding beneath you. The pulsing bioluminescence that may or may not be real. The sound that you feel in your sternum and your teeth. The subsonic frequency that triggers primal anxiety responses. The sheer *alienness* of a system that has no frame of reference for your existence.

You will be in a place where everything is alive, everything is growing, everything is glowing, everything is screaming, and none of it is about you. Human beings are not built for this kind of irrelevance. We are narrators. We tell stories in which we are characters. The Ditch does not have a story in which you are a character. You are a brief chemical anomaly passing through a system that will not remember you.

This realization, if it arrives fully, is destabilizing. Not in a dramatic way. In a quiet way. In a way that makes you stop moving and stand still and feel the ground deciding, and wonder why you are here, and wonder whether "here" is a place or a process or a mind or nothing at all.

The coordinated bioluminescent pulse — the whole valley glowing in unison — is the part that keeps scientists from fully committing to models. If it is real, it implies coordination across species at a scale that suggests something like... not consciousness, perhaps, but *coherence*. A system aware of itself at a level that makes your individual awareness seem like a candle in a cathedral.

**The Preparation:**

- **Time limits.** Set a hard maximum for your visit and enforce it absolutely. The traders say three days. I would say less for a first visit — 36 to 48 hours maximum. The psychological effects are dose-dependent. The longer you stay, the more the environment colonizes your cognition. Set a timer. When it goes off, you leave. No matter what you haven't finished.
- **A tether to outside reality.** Bring something that reminds you of who you are and where you came from. A photograph. A recording of familiar sounds — birdsong, rain on a roof, human voices. Something you can access during rest periods that re-grounds you in a human context. This sounds soft. It is not soft. Cavers and polar explorers use these techniques routinely. Sensory deprivation and alienation produce real psychological harm.
- **A buddy, if at all possible.** The Ditch is not a solo environment. Two people can monitor each other's cognition, catch each other's disorientation, share the cognitive load of staying focused. Solo travel in the Ditch is possible but exponentially more dangerous, because the first sign of the place getting into your head is that you stop noticing it getting into your head.
- **Accept the fear.** You will be afraid. The sound, the darkness, the heat, the crawling, the glow, the feeling of geological indifference — these produce a fear response that is older than language. You cannot think your way out of it. You can only acknowledge it, let it be present, and keep moving. The fear is appropriate. The Ditch is genuinely dangerous. Fear is your early warning system. Do not suppress it. Listen to it. It will tell you when to stop, when to turn back, when to change your route. Ignore it and the Ditch will teach you why you shouldn't have.

## IX. SUPPLIES AND EQUIPMENT — THE COMPLETE KIT

A suggested packing list, organized by priority:

**Tier 1 — Survival (you do not enter without these):**
- Respirator with chemical/particulate combination filters (minimum 6 filter changes)
- Armored, insulated, waterproof boots (2 pairs)
- Thermal-probing walking stick
- Sealed water supply with electrolyte supplements (minimum 4 liters/day)
- Timekeeping device
- Navigation line (500m minimum, high-visibility to user)
- First aid kit with emphasis on: wound sealing (butterfly closures, liquid bandage), burn treatment, antihistamines, eye wash (saline), activated charcoal tablets (in case of ingestion of toxic material)
- Red-spectrum headlamp with backup batteries (sealed in waterproof container)
- Full-coverage clothing: long-sleeve base layer, reinforced outer layer, sealed gloves (4 pairs), hood, gaiters

**Tier 2 — Function (you will be severely limited without these):**
- Active noise filtering ear protection with subsonic dampening
- Full-face respirator upgrade
- Thermal imaging device (handheld, waterproofed)
- Communication system for companions (short-range radio or bone-conduction headsets, waterproofed)
- Suspended sleeping platform with fine-mesh enclosure
- Sealed waste bags
- Spare clothing (2 changes minimum — everything will be wet with mineral moisture within hours)

**Tier 3 — Sanity (you will wish you had these):**
- Psychological tether items (photograph, audio recording, familiar object)
- Notebook and writing implement (for documentation, for self-monitoring, for maintaining a sense of linear time and continuous self)
- A second notebook for recording observations, because the Ditch will try to make you forget what you're there for, and writing is how you remind yourself

## X. WHAT IT WILL ACTUALLY BE LIKE

I want to be honest about this, because the preparation is the practical layer and what follows is the experiential one.

**The first hour** will be overwhelming. You will be adjusting to darkness, adjusting to sound, adjusting to heat, adjusting to the feel of fractured rock under your feet and the pressure of a respirator on your face and the alien weight of this place pressing in from all sides. You will not see far. You will hear everything. The ground will be warm through your boots. The air will be thick and wet and taste of sulfur even through the filter. You will hear things you cannot identify, and you will feel things watching you that you cannot see, and you will wonder — seriously, genuinely wonder — whether you should turn around.

This is normal. Everyone who enters the Ditch feels this. The traders feel it, and they grew up in a world where the Ditch exists and is known. You are from somewhere else entirely.

**The third hour,** if you are still moving, you will begin to adjust. Not comfortably — you will never be comfortable here — but functionally. Your dark-adapted vision will start to resolve the bioluminescent glow into structure. You will begin to see the layers: the canopy's blue-green veins, the understory's amber spirals, the pools' shifting bacterial colors. The sound will separate into components. You will learn to pick out the clicking of specific insects, the hum of the six-legged mammals, the distant shrieks of the ape-analogues. You will start to read the Ditch the way a sailor reads the sea — not by understanding every element, but by sensing the overall pattern and knowing when the pattern shifts.

**The sixth to twelfth hour,** you will enter the Ditch's rhythm. This is the most dangerous period, because you will feel like you are adapting when what is actually happening is that the Ditch is absorbing you. The sound becomes a kind of music. The darkness becomes a kind of space. The ground's warmth becomes a kind of presence. You will stop thinking of the fauna as threats and start thinking of them as neighbors, which is a cognitive shift that the traders experience and that the documentation warns about. You are not a neighbor. You are a visitor. Maintain the distinction.

**The overnight period** — if you remain — will be the hardest. The traders are right: the sound makes dreams violent. Even if you sleep (unlikely), the Ditch will come with you into your unconscious. You will dream of basalt and glow and things with too many legs, and you will wake not knowing where you are or what year it is, and for a span of seconds that feels like minutes, you will be utterly lost. Have your tether item ready. Hold it. Read it. Remind yourself.

**The departure** will feel like surfacing from deep water. The Ditch does not want you to leave — not in a conscious sense, but in the way that any dense, total environment resists the removal of its elements. You are part of the system now, however briefly. Your biological material is in the air. Your footprints are on the basalt. Your warmth is in the soil. You are decomposing, slowly, into the Ditch's memory.

Step out onto the eastern ridge. Feel the steam from the cooling basalt on your skin. Look back into the valley — if the ash ceiling shifts, if lightning catches it at the right moment, you might see the pulse. The slow, rolling wave of light from one end to the other, the whole system breathing in unison.

Then walk away. Do not go back. Not immediately. Let the Ditch settle back into its own story, the one you were never part of.

Carry what you learned. The Ditch will not carry you.

---

*Last note: if you find yourself standing very still in the deepest part of the valley, and you feel the ground deciding beneath you — and you will, the traders all describe it, and it is real — do not wait to find out what it decided. Move. Whatever the ground chose, it chose without you in mind.*
