# Preparing for the Col of Shattered Pillars

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, you need to internalize what this place actually is: a small, exposed saddle between two high ridges at an altitude where the air itself becomes hostile. It is not a mountain pass you walk *through*. It is a room you enter and must navigate on its terms — every surface is stone, every surface is cold, every surface reflects light back at you with the indifference of a mirror that doesn't know you're there. There are no trees. No soil. No animals. No windbreaks. The col has filled itself completely with geology, and you will be a trespasser in a place that was never designed for the human body.

This matters because most mountain preparation assumes you're traveling *toward* something — a summit, a camp, a valley on the other side. The col rewards no such purpose. It is itself. You will spend your time there doing nothing more ambitious than standing, looking, touching stone, listening, and trying not to die. The preparation for that is different from what you might expect.

## Breathing: The First and Most Critical Problem

The passage states it plainly: the air is *thin enough to taste its absence*. This is not a poetic flourish. At the altitudes implied by permanent frost, year-round snow streaks, and the absence of all animal life, you are looking at a place where atmospheric pressure may be half or less of sea level. Your blood oxygen saturation will drop. Your thinking will slow. Your body will begin to fail in quiet, insidious ways before you feel dramatic symptoms.

**What to bring:**

- **Supplemental oxygen with a regulator and mask**, if your multiverse travel technology permits it. This is not optional for an ordinary human. You are not a Sherpa with decades of high-altitude adaptation. You are sea-level flesh in a thin-air world. A lightweight compressed oxygen cylinder with a demand valve regulator will give you enough reserve to spend meaningful time in the col without your brain beginning to swell inside your skull. Calculate your expected time at altitude and carry at least fifty percent more oxygen than you think you need, because navigation through the basalt columns will be slower than you expect, and cold increases oxygen consumption.

- If supplemental oxygen is unavailable, you must **acclimatize at progressively higher altitudes for weeks** before attempting the col. This means spending days at moderate altitude, then higher, then higher still, allowing your body to produce more red blood cells and adjust its chemoreceptor responses. Even with acclimatization, you should plan for **very limited time in the col itself** — hours, not days.

- Carry **acetazolamide (Diamox)** as a prophylactic against acute mountain sickness. It helps your kidneys excrete bicarbonate, compensating for the respiratory alkalosis that occurs when you hyperventilate in thin air. It will not make you immune to altitude sickness, but it buys you margin.

- **Know the symptoms of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) and high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE).** HACE presents as confusion, ataxia (loss of coordination), altered consciousness. HAPE presents as breathlessness at rest, a gurgling or rattling sound in the chest, a persistent cough producing frothy or pink-tinged sputum. Both are fatal without descent. There is no descent from the col that is fast enough to treat these conditions if they develop. Prevention is everything.

## Thermal Protection: Fighting a Cold That Thinks in Geological Time

The col's cold is not the dramatic, wind-driven cold of a blizzard. It is worse in some ways. It is *ambient*, total, and patient. It doesn't assault you; it simply never relents. The passage describes the rock as cold "in a way that feels less like temperature and more like absence, as though the mineral is drawing something out of the hand that touches it." This is the cold you must prepare for — not the cold that burns your exposed skin in minutes, but the cold that seeps into your joints, your bones, your core temperature over hours of exposure, steadily and without warning.

Because you will be **placing your hands on rock constantly** — the terrain demands it — your extremities are your greatest vulnerability. You cannot wear thick insulated mittens. You need grip. You need dexterity. You need to feel the stone beneath your fingers to judge whether a surface is frost-slick or dry, whether a slab is stable or merely wedged.

**Layering system:**

- **Base layer:** Merino wool or a synthetic moisture-wicking fabric. Not cotton. Cotton kills at altitude — it absorbs condensation and frost and holds it against your skin, wicking heat away with ruthless efficiency. The condensation in the col will coat your clothing in fine moisture throughout the morning hours. Merino wool retains warmth even when damp and does not transmit cold as aggressively as wet synthetics.

- **Mid layer:** A thin fleece or synthetic insulated jacket, something with a high warmth-to-weight ratio. This layer's job is to trap the heat your body generates and create a buffer of warm air around your core. It should be breathable enough that you don't overheat during the physical exertion of scrambling through basalt columns, but insulating enough that when you stop — and you will stop often, to look, to listen, to study the mineral patterns — you don't lose core temperature rapidly.

- **Outer shell:** A windproof, waterproof hardshell jacket and pants. Not for rain — there is no rain in the col — but for wind and frost. The hardshell blocks the thin, glassy gusts that move through the columnar joints and prevents the condensation that forms on your outer layers from penetrating inward. Look for Gore-Tex or equivalent — something with a membrane that blocks liquid water while allowing water vapor to escape, so your sweat doesn't accumulate inside the shell.

- **Hands:** This is the critical decision. You need **liner gloves** — thin merino wool or silk — that you can wear while gripping rock, combined with **overmitts** that you can slip on during pauses. The liner gloves give you tactile feedback and basic protection. The overmitts provide insulation during rest. When you're scrambling, the overmitts go in a pocket or clipped to your harness. When you stop, they go on immediately. Bring at least two pairs of liner gloves. They will get wet from condensation on the rock. You need dry backup.

- **Feet:** Insulated, waterproof mountaineering boots with aggressive tread. The basalt columns are slick with condensation and frost, and the shattered granite slabs are angled unpredictably. Your boots need to grip cold, wet rock. Fit them with thick merino wool socks — again, not cotton, not even once. Consider vapor-barrier liner socks if your boots' insulation is inadequate; they prevent moisture from reaching the insulating layer by trapping your foot sweat against your skin, which is unpleasant but thermally effective in extreme cold.

- **Head and face:** A balaclava or Buff that covers your neck, ears, and lower face. A brimmed hat or the hood of your hardshell to keep frost and reflected light off your face. Exposed skin on the face will not frostbite quickly — the col's cold is not that severe — but over hours, wind chill from even thin gusts across the exposed ridgeline will erode comfort steadily.

**Critical principle:** You must avoid sweating. Sweat is your enemy at this altitude and temperature. During the scramble through the columns, move slowly enough that you don't build up heat. Vent your shell by unzipping before you begin to feel warm. If your base layer becomes damp with sweat, you have made a mistake — that moisture will chill you dangerously the moment you stop moving. Pace yourself as if your life depends on not overheating. At altitude, in this cold, it does.

## Vision: Surviving the Light

The light in the col is described as something that *strikes*. Every surface is a mirror or a blade. The brightness comes from above and below simultaneously — from the sky and from the mineral surfaces reflecting upward. There is no shade. There is no canopy. There is no shadow deeper than a fracture in the rock. If you close your eyes, you still see the afterimage burning.

This is a snow-blindness scenario even without snow. The combination of high-altitude UV radiation (the thin atmosphere filters far less ultraviolet light) and the reflective mineral surfaces — quartz, feldspar, mica — creates an environment that can damage your corneas in under an hour without protection.

**What to bring:**

- **Category 4 glacier sunglasses** with side shields. Not fashion sunglasses. Not sport shades. Glacier glasses have dark lenses (Category 4, blocking 92–97% of visible light), wraparound frames, and side shields that prevent light from entering from the periphery. The side shields are essential here because the light comes from every direction — from the rock beneath your feet, from the basalt columns beside you, from the sky above. Without side shields, reflected light enters from the sides and causes the same damage as direct exposure.

- **A spare pair of sunglasses.** If you drop your primary pair on the rock and they crack or are lost into one of the crevices between basalt columns, you are done. You will not be able to see. Carry a backup, secured to your person.

- Consider also carrying a **face shield or wraparound goggles** for the brightest periods. The passage notes that at peak brightness, the col is "almost painful to inhabit" — white light on white frost on grey-white stone, a bleached and directionless glow. In these conditions, even Category 4 lenses may not be sufficient. Goggles with an anti-fog coating will protect the entire eye area and reduce the relentless assault of reflected light.

- **Be aware of twilight.** When the light fades to the "brief, deep-blue twilight" described in the passage, the iron-oxide patterns on the basalt seem to darken and pulse. This is your visual cortex straining for pattern in failing light — it is not real. Do not let the illusion draw you toward the rock face. In low light, the frost-slick surfaces become even more treacherous, and the depth-deceptive pools become invisible. Reduce your movement in the col during twilight. Find a stable position and wait for either full darkness (in which case you need a headlamp, see below) or the return of light.

- **Headlamp with red-light mode.** If the col does experience anything resembling night — and the passage notes the ambiguity of whether it does, depending on season and latitude — you need illumination. A headlamp frees your hands for scrambling. The red-light mode preserves your night vision and minimizes the disorientation that bright artificial light can cause at altitude. Carry spare batteries, kept warm inside your jacket — cold drains batteries rapidly.

## Terrain: Navigating a Landscape of Almost-Architecture

The col is only about two hundred paces across at its widest. This sounds small. It is small. But the terrain within it makes those two hundred paces a genuine obstacle course. The floor is not flat. It is not clear. It is a packed and cluttered field of columnar basalt standing in clusters, with shattered granite slabs wedged between them at every angle. There is no path. There is no trail. Every step requires judgment, and every handhold requires trust.

**Footwear and movement:**

- Your boots (described above) must grip wet, cold, mineral surfaces. Test them before you go. Walk on wet granite in your chosen boots and verify that the tread pattern holds on slick rock. Vibram Megagrip or equivalent compound. Aggressive, deep lugs.

- **Move slowly and deliberately.** This is not a place to rush. Test every foothold before committing your weight. Rock that has been fractured by millennia of freeze-thaw can be deceptively unstable — a slab that looks solid may be resting on ice-filled cracks and shift when you step on it. Place your foot flat rather than on an edge, to maximize contact area and friction.

- **Use your hands on the rock constantly.** The passage says it directly: "There is no walking here that does not involve placing a hand on rock." Treat every handhold the same way you treat footholds — test it, grip it firmly, and be prepared for it to be slick with condensation or frost. Your liner gloves must provide enough grip that your hand doesn't slide off a wet basalt face. Consider applying a thin layer of climbing chalk to your gloves to improve friction, though be aware that chalk will absorb condensation and become useless if it gets wet. Carry a chalk bag and re-chalk frequently.

- **Beware the shattered granite slabs.** They are stacked at every angle, "wedged by millennia of freeze-thaw into configurations that suggest intention without delivering it." Some of these slabs will hold your weight. Some will not. A slab wedged at an angle between two basalt columns may look like a step but may pivot when loaded from one side. Test with weight before committing.

## Water: The Deceptive Pools

The passage describes small pools of meltwater in sheltered hollows between basalt columns — extraordinarily clear, magnifying and distorting the rock beneath them, appearing a hand's breadth deep but actually far deeper. These pools are both a hydration resource and a trap.

**What to know:**

- **Do not assume depth.** The refraction of the water creates powerful visual illusions. A pool that looks shallow enough to reach into may be deep enough to submerge your arm to the shoulder. Reaching into a deep pool in these temperatures will soak your sleeve, and a wet sleeve in this cold is a medical emergency waiting to happen. Use a trekking pole or a stick to probe depth before inserting any part of your body.

- **The water is drinkable** — there are no animals, no biological contamination, no organic matter. But it is *cold*. Hypothermia-risk cold. Drinking it directly will lower your core temperature. If you need to hydrate from these pools, use a lightweight insulated bottle. Fill it, seal it immediately, and keep it inside your jacket against your body heat. Sip, don't gulp.

- **Collect water during the warmest part of the day** (if such a thing exists here) when some of the frost has sublimated and the pools are most accessible. In early morning, the needle-thin ice crystals dusting the pool surfaces will need to be cleared, and the cold is at its most intense.

- **Carry a water purification method regardless.** Even though the water appears biologically sterile, you don't know what mineral content it carries. High-altitude meltwater can carry dissolved metals — iron, manganese, potentially others — in concentrations that cause gastrointestinal distress. A simple carbon filter or purification tablets are light and could save you from spending your limited time in the col doubled over with cramps.

- **Hydrate proactively.** At altitude, you lose moisture rapidly through respiration — the thin, dry air strips moisture from your lungs with every breath. You will also perspire from exertion during scrambling. Dehydration at altitude accelerates altitude sickness and impairs judgment. Drink before you feel thirsty. Thirst is a lagging indicator at altitude.

## The Frost: Managing Moisture

The col's condensation cycle is specific: moisture condenses directly onto cold surfaces as fine granular frost, forming in the hours around dawn, persisting into midday on shaded faces, and sublimating with a faint hiss in the brighter hours. This means that for a significant portion of your time in the col, surfaces will be coated in a fine layer of frost that is invisible or nearly so on pale grey rock.

**Implications:**

- **Every surface you touch in the morning hours will be frost-coated.** The basalt columns will feel like gripping a cold, slightly gritty metal. The frost adds a micro-layer of slippery granules between your glove and the rock. Your grip will be reduced. Compensate by gripping harder and choosing holds with more positive features — edges, cracks, depressions.

- **The sublimation hiss is not dangerous but it is disorienting.** The faint, pervasive sound of frost converting directly to vapor creates a sense that the landscape is alive and whispering. In combination with the resonant tones produced by wind moving through narrow columnar gaps, the col's soundscape can feel overwhelming in its strangeness. Prepare yourself psychologically for an environment where the ground is audibly exhaling beneath your feet.

- **Pack everything in waterproof bags inside your pack.** The condensation will coat your pack, your straps, your exterior gear. Anything not protected by a waterproof barrier will become damp. Use dry bags or even heavy-duty zip-lock bags for your spare clothing, electronics, and oxygen supplies.

## Navigation and Orientation

The col is small — two hundred paces across — but within that space, the dense basalt columns create a maze-like environment with limited visibility. You can see the ridgelines above you, but between the columns, sightlines are restricted. The bright, directionless light makes it harder to use shadows for orientation, and the sun's position is genuinely ambiguous — the reflected light comes from everywhere.

**Practical measures:**

- **Mark your entry point.** When you enter the col from the ridgeline, note distinctive landmarks — a particular cluster of basalt columns, a specific slab angle, a unique iron-oxide pattern. In the dense terrain, it is easy to wander in a small area and lose track of where you came in. At altitude, with impaired cognition from low oxygen, spatial disorientation is a real risk.

- **Carry a compass.** The magnetic field at high altitude is typically functional (unless this is a world where it isn't — check before you go). Even a simple compass, oriented to a known ridgeline, will prevent you from becoming disoriented among the columns.

- **A GPS device is advisable** if your multiverse travel technology is compatible with the local electromagnetic environment. Program the coordinates of your entry point and set waypoints for any significant features — the cairn, the largest pools, the flat-topped boulder. At altitude, with cold fingers and thin air impairing your fine motor skills and cognition, simple navigation tools beat complex ones.

- **Consider marking your path with small cairns or thread.** As you move through the columns, stack two or three small stones at junctions where you've turned, or tie short lengths of bright thread to column edges (non-permanent, low-impact). This gives you a trail back to your entry point without permanently altering the landscape.

## The Cairn and the Marks: Psychological Preparation

This deserves its own section because it is, in many ways, the most challenging aspect of visiting the col.

You will arrive at a place that has never supported animal life, that is hostile to biological existence in every conceivable way, that is defined entirely by geological process and mineral logic — and you will find evidence that someone else was here. Someone who carried seven river-worn stones up to this altitude and stacked them deliberately. Someone who scratched parallel marks into a flat boulder, possibly counting, possibly recording, possibly doing something you have no framework to interpret.

The cairn and the marks will affect you. They should. They mean that you are not the first conscious being to stand in this place and feel its pull. Someone else came here with purpose — not survival purpose, not resource-gathering purpose, but something that looks like *devotion* or *curiosity* or *obsession*. They hauled stones from a river valley you cannot see, stones that were wrong for this place — too round, too smooth, belonging to a gentler landscape — and they built something that has stood for longer than you can determine, knocked down and rebuilt so many times that the contact surfaces are worn smooth and concave.

**Prepare for this emotionally.** At altitude, with your cognition dulled and your senses heightened by cold and light and thin air, the cairn will hit you harder than you expect. You may feel a profound loneliness. You may feel a connection to whoever built it. You may feel an irrational urge to add a stone, to continue the pattern, to participate in whatever ritual has been happening here across time. All of these responses are normal. None of them are dangerous. But they will be *intense*, and you should expect them.

The mineral patterns — the concentric rings, the branching lines, the spirals — will also affect you. Your brain is a pattern-recognition machine, and it will fire relentlessly in a place where mineral deposits hover at the threshold of recognizable forms. You will see faces in the basalt. You will see hands. You will see eyes. You will know, intellectually, that these are iron-oxide precipitates following the logic of crystallization. You will also feel, viscerally, that the stone is looking back at you. This is pareidolia, the same mechanism that makes you see faces in clouds and electrical outlets. But knowing the mechanism does not diminish the experience. Let yourself feel it. Just don't act on it — don't press your face to the rock, don't try to communicate with it, don't become so absorbed in the patterns that you lose track of time, temperature, and your oxygen supply.

## What to Carry: A Checklist

Here is a consolidated list, organized by priority:

**Survival (non-negotiable):**

1. Supplemental oxygen system (cylinder, regulator, mask, spare)
2. Category 4 glacier sunglasses with side shields + spare pair
3. Full cold-weather layering system (base, mid, outer shell, hardshell pants)
4. Merino wool liner gloves (2 pairs) + overmitts
5. Insulated waterproof mountaineering boots (broken in)
6. Thick merino wool socks (2 pairs)
7. Balaclava / face protection
8. Headlamp with spare batteries (kept warm)
9. Insulated water bottle
10. High-calorie food (you will burn energy at altitude even at rest — the body works harder to maintain temperature and oxygenate blood; carry 2-3x the calories you think you'll need)

**Navigation:**

11. Compass
12. GPS device (if compatible)
13. Bright thread or flagging tape for marking routes

**Medical:**

14. Acetazolamide (Diamox) — altitude sickness prophylaxis
15. Ibuprofen — for headache management at altitude (a symptom of AMS, but also just miserable)
16. Any personal medications
17. First aid kit focused on hand injuries — you will be gripping rough stone constantly, and cuts on cold, dry skin heal poorly and bleed profusely

**Practical:**

18. Trekking pole (collapsible) — for probing pool depth and testing foothold stability
19. Chalk bag with climbing chalk — for hand grip on wet rock
20. Waterproof dry bags for internal pack organization
21. Repair tape (duct tape wrapped around a trekking pole) — for field repairs to gloves, shells, boots
22. A small journal and pencil (not pen — ink freezes; pencils don't). If you are visiting a place this strange and this old, you will want to record what you see. The cairn's builder scratched marks in the rock; you can do better.

## Time Management

This is perhaps the most important practical consideration. The col is not a place you linger. It is a place you *visit briefly and deliberately*.

Your oxygen supply limits you. The cold limits you. The altitude limits you. The psychological intensity limits you. Plan to spend **no more than two to four hours in the col proper**, depending on your oxygen supply and acclimatization level. Use that time to:

1. Enter slowly, letting your senses adjust to the light.
2. Move to the flat-tacked boulder and the cairn. These are the human artifacts. Spend time here.
3. Study the mineral patterns on at least three or four basalt faces. Look closely. Look at the concentric rings, the branching lines. Notice how they suggest without resolving.
4. Listen. Stand still and listen to the col's soundscape — the sublimation hiss, the frost-cracks, the glassy wind-whisper, the occasional resonant tone. Let the silence between sounds tell you something.
5. Touch the stone. Place your gloved hand on a basalt column and feel the cold that is "less like temperature and more like absence." Hold it there for thirty seconds. This is the col's most honest communication.
6. Drink water from a pool, carefully, observing the depth illusion.
7. Leave. Retrace your marked route to the ridgeline. Descend to where the air is thicker and the light is forgiving.

Do not attempt to climb the ridgelines unless you have specific mountaineering skills and equipment. They are described as steep, with sharp crests and unseen drop-offs on the far side. The col is accessible from at least one direction. Enter and exit through that direction.

## After You Leave

The col will stay with you. Not in the dramatic way that a summit or a battlefield stays with you — not as a story of achievement or horror. It will stay the way a word stays when you've been repeating it until it loses meaning and then becomes meaning itself. The brightness. The cold. The stone patterns that almost speak. The cairn that has been rebuilt so many times that someone else's devotion is worn smooth into the rock.

At altitude, in the days after your visit, you may find that your dreams are unusually vivid and strange. This is common after high-altitude exposure — the brain, deprived of oxygen for hours, sometimes overcompensates in recovery with intense neurological activity. You may dream of the mineral patterns. You may dream of the pools and their false depths. You may dream of stacking stones.

This is normal. It passes.

What does not pass is the knowledge that the col is still there, still weathering, still producing its almost-alive patterns grain by grain, frost-crack by frost-crack, in its slow and ceaseless mineral way. It was there before you arrived. It will be there after you leave. It does not need you. It does not need anyone. And yet someone carried seven river stones to its floor and stacked them, and scratched marks into its rock, and left — and you, too, will leave something, if only footprints that the frost will erase by noon and breath-moisture that sublimates back to the thin, bright air within minutes of your departure.

Go anyway. Go carefully, go briefly, and go knowing that the col's deepest lesson is the one written in its geology: that even in a place where nothing living can survive, the patterns of life will emerge from the stone, patient and unmistakable, if you give them enough time and enough silence to speak.
