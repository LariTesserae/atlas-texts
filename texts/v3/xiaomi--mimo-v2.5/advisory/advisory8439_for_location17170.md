# Preparing for the Seamount Cities

---

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

You are going to the tops of undersea mountains. That is the core fact, and it shapes everything. There are no continents here, no lowlands, no hinterland. There are just volcanic peaks jutting above a warm, hazy ocean, and people have built their lives on the narrow ridgelines and summits where rock breaks the surface. You will be arriving at a place that is, in geological terms, barely finished being born. The stone beneath your feet is new enough to still remember being liquid. The ecosystem is young enough that there are no trees, no birdsong that would sound familiar to you, no sense of deep ecological history. Everything here is provisional, assembling, still deciding what it is.

Think of it less like arriving at a country and more like arriving at a verb — a place that is actively *becoming* something and hasn't stopped yet. If you can hold that frame, you'll be less disoriented than if you expect stasis.

## Physical Preparation

**Your body is not built for this place. Let's be honest about that.**

### The Heat

The air is warm and saturated with moisture. Not warm the way a tropical beach is warm, where there's a breeze and the sun moves the air around — warm the way the inside of a mouth is warm. The haze means the sun never fully burns through, so there's no radiant heat that dries your skin, but there's no cooling either. The humidity is so high that your sweat has nowhere to evaporate. You will feel perpetually damp, slick, as though you've been lightly oiled. Within hours, your clothes will be soaked through and stay soaked. Your skin will prickle. Your joints may ache from the constant low-grade thermal load.

Before you go, spend time in the hottest, most humid environments you can access — saunas, steam rooms, tropical greenhouses — and get your body accustomed to the sensation of being unable to cool itself. It won't fully prepare you, but it will keep the first few days from being a misery spiral of panic and profuse sweating.

Drink an enormous amount of water. More than you think. Then more. The Seamount Cities' inhabitants have bodies adapted over generations to this environment; yours is not. You will lose fluids faster than you can sense. Bring electrolytes — salt, potassium, magnesium — in portable form. The local people will have dietary sources of these (the root vegetables, the shellfish, the fish), but you won't have their gut flora or their metabolic adaptations at first.

### The Humidity and Your Lungs

Breathing this air will feel like breathing soup. It's thick, warm, wet, and carries mineral smells — sulfur, salt, wet basalt, and beneath all of it, something faintly hot and metallic that you won't be able to identify. If you have any respiratory sensitivities, asthma, chronic bronchitis, or even just sensitive sinuses, take this seriously. The moisture-laden air will irritate and inflame. Pack antihistamines, nasal saline, and if you need them, corticosteroid inhalers. The local people breathe this air because they were born into it. Your lungs need time to adjust.

You may also notice that the air has a faintly sulfuric quality near certain areas — vents, or places where the rock is still actively degassing. The people know where these spots are and avoid them. You will not. Learn to recognize the smell of hydrogen sulfide (rotten eggs) and treat it as an absolute danger signal. Even low concentrations can cause headaches, nausea, and disorientation. Higher concentrations can kill you quickly, and because the air is so thick, the gas doesn't disperse — it hangs.

### The Terrain

Your feet and ankles are going to take a beating. The rock is basalt — hard, rough, and uneven. It has not been smoothed by time or by water in most of the above-surface areas. It is raw volcanic rock: sharp in places, glassy and slick in others, and the geometry of the flows means the surfaces are never what you expect. What looks like a flat ledge may be a tilted plane. What looks like a step may be a half-formed joint in a flow that will give way under your weight.

Bring boots with aggressive tread and good ankle support — something with a rigid sole, because thin-soled shoes will transmit the sharpness of the rock through to your feet painfully and won't protect you from the occasional still-warm patch. Yes, still-warm. Some areas of rock, especially in hollows or near seams, may be hot enough to feel through boot leather. Not hot enough to ignite anything, but hot enough to burn bare skin. Do not sit down on rock without testing it first. Do not lie down on rock at all.

The settlements are vertical. Ladders, stairs cut into rock, narrow terraces with no railings — and you will be climbing these while dehydrated and heat-exhausted and possibly nauseous from the sulfur. Take it slow. Rest often. The people move through this terrain with a sure-footedness that comes from a lifetime of practice. You do not have that. Assume you will be the clumsiest person in any settlement you visit, and plan accordingly.

### Diving

The local people dive frequently and without apparent effort, descending into the warm shallows to gather shellfish, to harvest the living film of algae and not-coral from the submerged flanks, to fish in the green-brown water where visibility is measured in arm lengths. You will want to do this too. The underwater world of the Seamount Cities is extraordinary — the branching, luminous structures of the not-coral, the schools of small fish that move like thought through the shallows, the way the black rock drops away into blue-green murk that grows warmer the deeper it goes.

But.

The warmth of the water is not normal. The people know this. The deeper you go, the warmer it gets, and the gradient is wrong — it increases faster than solar heating can account for. There is something beneath, some source of heat, and it is the thing the people fear most quietly. They do not dive below a certain depth. Learn that depth from them. Respect it absolutely. You do not have their knowledge of where the thermoclines shift, where the currents pull, where the luminous structures grow thick enough to snag a limb.

Also consider: the warm water, the haze-shrouded visibility, the unfamiliar structures of the not-coral — these combine to create a diving environment of extreme disorientation. If you are not an experienced diver in unfamiliar waters, do not dive alone. Do not dive at all without a local guide who can read the water the way you might read a familiar room. The underwater world here is not hostile — it is simply alien in a way that your proprioception and spatial reasoning will struggle with. You will lose track of up. You will lose track of the surface. In the murk, with the haze above, you may not be able to see the sky even when you break the surface.

## Gear and Supplies

### Clothing

Forget cotton. It will never dry. Wear lightweight, quick-drying synthetics that breathe as much as anything can breathe in air that is basically water. You need loose clothing — airflow matters even if the air is warm, because even a warm breeze will help your sweat do some evaporative work. Long sleeves and long pants are advisable despite the heat, because the basalt will scrape your skin if you brush against it, and there are insects — beetles, mostly — that are small but persistent.

Bring a hat with a brim. The haze means the sun is diffuse, but it is still there, and hours of exposure through haze is still hours of UV exposure.

You will need swimwear for diving, obviously, but you'll also want something to wear over it while you're above water, because you'll be climbing and scrambling on rock, and wet skin on basalt is a recipe for abrasion.

Expect to go through clothes. Bring more than you think you need, and accept that some of it will be stained permanently by volcanic mineral deposits, greened by algae, or worn through by the rock. The local people wear clothing woven from the fiber of their cultivated creeping plant — a rough, durable textile that breathes well and dries fast. If the local people offer you some, take it. It is better suited to this environment than anything you brought.

### Water and Food

Bring purification capability. The water here is seawater everywhere around you, and the rain — when it comes through the haze, more as a perpetual fine mist than as actual rain — is not reliable enough to collect. The local people drink water they've collected in stone basins where rainwater pools in hollows on the summits. They know where these natural cisterns are. They guard some of them carefully.

The local diet is straightforward: root vegetables from the terraces, shellfish, fish, the fiber crop (which is apparently edible when prepared a certain way, though not particularly nutritious for humans — more a filler than a staple). The food is mineral-rich, faintly salty from the air, and not what you'd call varied. If you have specific dietary needs, bring supplementation. If you are vegetarian or vegan, you'll be fine on local food. If you rely on grains or dairy, you will not find them here.

Cooking is done with minimal fuel, because there is minimal fuel. No trees. The tallest woody plants are knee-high. The local people apparently cook over small fires fed by dried plant material, driftwood (which is rare — the currents and the geography don't produce much), and whatever else they can burn. Do not expect hot meals frequently. Many things are eaten raw or simply prepared. Accept this.

### Tools and Navigation

There are no roads. There are no signs. There are no maps in any form you'd recognize. The settlements are connected by paths worn into rock by generations of bare feet (and your boots will find these paths differently than their feet do — the grip is different, the rhythm is different). The haze means that even from a high point, you can see only a limited distance. Landmarks disappear into grey quickly.

If you have any kind of GPS or satellite navigation, it may not function here — this is the multiverse, and the relationship to orbital infrastructure may be nonexistent. Compasses will behave strangely near certain areas where the volcanic rock is strongly magnetized (basalt often is, especially new basalt; it records the magnetic field at the time of cooling). Bring a compass anyway — it will work most of the time, and when it doesn't, that itself is useful information.

The most reliable navigation tool is a local person. Befriend one early. The social dynamics of this will be discussed below, but from a purely practical standpoint, you need someone who can read the haze, who knows which summit is which when everything looks grey and similar, who can tell from the vibration in the rock underfoot which way the deeper channels run.

### Medical

This is not a place with medical infrastructure. There are no healers trained in anything you'd recognize as medicine. The local people have their own knowledge — herbal, practical, and presumably effective for their own bodies and their own environment — but it is not your medicine, and your body is not their body.

Pack:

- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (for infection from cuts on volcanic rock, which are common and slow to heal in the constant damp)
- Antifungal medications (the humidity will colonize your skin, your feet, your groin, any fold or crease — and it will be aggressive about it)
- Anti-nausea medication (for the heat, the sulfur, the adjustment period)
- Painkillers (for the foot and ankle trauma of basalt navigation, for the headaches from humidity and possible sulfur exposure)
- Wound care supplies — antiseptic, bandages, something waterproof to keep wounds dry in a place where nothing is dry
- Sunscreen (yes, even through the haze)
- Insect repellent (for the beetles and whatever else is in the ecosystem — it's young, but it's there, and it will find you)

Bring more than you think you need. You cannot resupply.

## Cultural and Social Preparation

### These Are People, Not Curiosities

This is the most important section, and the one most likely to be neglected by a traveler focused on the spectacle of the place.

The inhabitants of the Seamount Cities are human beings who have built a civilization in an environment that most humans would find uninhabitable. They have done this through ingenuity, patience, and a deep knowledge of their world that you do not have. They stack stone without mortar and it stands for generations. They dive into waters that get wrong-warm at depth. They cultivate crops in soil that barely qualifies as soil, on terraces they have built by hand from the living rock of mountains that are still cooling.

They are not primitive. They are adapted. The distinction matters enormously, and if you walk into their settlements thinking that copper chisels and stone architecture represent some lesser stage of development, you will be wrong, and they will know you are wrong, and you will have lost any chance of being welcomed.

Their knowledge is deep and specific. They know the rock — its joints, its flows, its temperamental spots where it will fracture unpredictably. They know the water — its currents, its depths, its dangers. They know the vegetation — what grows where, when to harvest, how to process it. They know the not-coral, and the fish, and the tides, and the way sound moves through the haze. This knowledge is not written down. It is held in practice, in memory, in the calluses on hands and the sureness of feet on wet basalt. It is as sophisticated as any written tradition, and in this environment, more effective.

Treat them as the experts they are. Ask before you touch anything. Ask before you go anywhere. Ask before you dive. Do not assume you understand how anything works until you have watched it work and had it explained to you — and even then, assume you understand it less than you think.

### Communication

Language will obviously be a barrier, and the specific characteristics of this place will make communication harder than usual even with gestures and good will. The haze dampens sound. Voices don't carry. You may need to be closer to someone than feels comfortable to have a conversation. Speak slowly, clearly, and not loudly — loudness doesn't help here; the moisture in the air absorbs it. The sub-audible vibration of the mountain may interfere with your ability to hear low frequencies. You may find yourself speaking in a higher register than normal simply to be heard.

Bring patience. Communication will be slow, imperfect, and frustrating. Accept this as the price of admission.

### Social Integration

The settlements are small. Everyone knows everyone. You will be immediately, inescapably visible as an outsider. There is no way to blend in — your clothes, your skin (probably), your movements on the rock, your inability to dive, your confusion about everything — all of it marks you. You are the most interesting and most useless person any of them have ever seen.

This can go two ways. It can make you a curiosity that people enjoy showing around, explaining things to, feeding (out of pity or hospitality), and eventually tolerating. Or it can make you a disruption — someone who takes up space in a place where habitable space is the scarcest resource. The summits are narrow. The terraces are carefully managed. There is not room for someone who doesn't contribute.

Find a way to contribute. Learn quickly. Carry things. Help with the harvest. Offer whatever skills you have — and be honest about what those skills are, because they may not map onto anything useful here. If you're a software engineer, you have nothing to offer. If you can tie knots, or carry heavy loads, or keep watch, or cook (even poorly), or simply be quiet and helpful and respectful, you have something.

The younger people — the ones who look at the script-texture on the rock when no one is watching — are your best allies. They are curious, and they are less invested in the careful balance of the settlement's social order. They will want to know where you came from, what the world outside is like, what things look like beyond the haze. Meet that curiosity with honesty and genuine engagement, and you will find friends.

The elders are your gatekeepers. They have seen the mountain cool. They have measured the quarry-faces deepen. They have watched the water rise, imperceptibly but measurably, over the course of their lives. They carry the knowledge of the place in their bodies and their memories. Respect them, listen to them, and do not dismiss their fears — the slow warmth, the not-coral, the geometry of the rock — as superstition. They may be more right than you think.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Haze

This is the thing that will get to you first, and it will not let go.

You are going to a place where you can never see clearly. The horizon is always obscured. The sky is always grey or pale amber. You cannot see the stars — the haze is too thick, too constant. You cannot see the ocean clearly from the shore; it dissolves into mist a short distance out. The settlements are partially obscured even from within them. Everything is soft-edged, indistinct, muted.

This will affect your sense of time, your mood, your sense of direction, and your sense of reality. The psychological impact of perpetual overcast, perpetual haze, is well-documented in humans: it leads to disorientation, depression, and a kind of temporal dislocation where days blur together. In the Seamount Cities, this is compounded by the lack of clear dawn and dusk — the shift from grey to pale amber and back is subtle enough that you may not notice it, especially in your first days. You will lose track of what day it is. You will lose track of how long you've been there. The cycle of days is measured by tides and crop growth, not by light, because the light barely changes.

Bring something to mark time with. A watch, a journal, anything that gives you an external anchor. Keep a daily record — not just of events, but of your emotional state, your sleep quality, your sense of connection to the ground. If you start to feel untethered, the journal will be your evidence that time is still passing and you are still yourself.

### The Silence

The settlements are quiet. Not the quiet of a forest or a desert — the quiet of a place where sound is swallowed. The haze takes voices. The water is warm and moves slowly, so waves don't crash; they lap, or they don't move at all. The tool-marks are patient and rhythmic — scrape, chip, scrape — and then silence again. Condensation drips. The mountain vibrates, low and steady, felt more than heard.

You will hear your own body more than anything else: your breathing (loud in the humidity, labored if you're not acclimated), your heartbeat, the creak of your joints as you climb, the slap of your feet on rock. This internal soundscape, unmasked by external noise, can be profoundly unsettling. You may feel exposed, as though you're the only real thing in a faded world. You may feel watched — not by people, but by the rock, by the mountain, by the haze.

This is the vibration. The sub-audible hum that the people call the mountain breathing. You will feel it in your feet, in your chest, in your teeth if you press them together. It is constant. It does not stop. It is always there beneath everything else, and once you notice it, you cannot un-notice it. Some people find it comforting — a heartbeat, a proof of life. Some people find it maddening — a presence that won't leave, a pressure that builds.

If the vibration starts to feel like it's inside your head, like it's pressing against the back of your eyes, get above it. Climb to the highest point you can. The vibration is strongest in the lower reaches of the settlements, in the valleys between summits. At the very peaks, it fades somewhat. If it doesn't fade, if it intensifies — well, that would be worth paying attention to.

### The Scale of Time

Everything here is slow. The rock cools over centuries. The vegetation creeps outward over years. The terraces expand by a few courses of stone per generation. The water rises in amounts so small that you'd need decades of measurement to confirm it. The people's lives are shaped by this slowness — they build for their grandchildren, not for themselves. They measure progress in the deepening of quarry-faces, in the height of a terrace wall that their parents started and their children will finish.

You will not be here long enough to see any of this change. And that gap — between your brief visit and the deep time of the place — will be its own kind of alienation. You are a mayfly on a mountain that is still becoming. Your concerns will feel absurdly urgent and temporary here. The people are planting root vegetables that will be harvested next season. You are worried about whether your boots are the right boots. The dissonance is real.

Let it teach you something. Slow down. Not just physically — mentally. Stop measuring your visit in days. Start measuring it in tides, in the rhythm of the settlement, in the growth of the fiber crop. You won't fully achieve this. But even a partial alignment with the local sense of time will make you a better visitor and a more welcome one.

### The Fear

The text mentions, almost in passing, what the people are afraid of: the slow warmth climbing up from below, the not-coral growing in geometries it shouldn't know, the fact that the mountain is still young and the water is still rising. These fears are not dramatic. They are not urgent. They are the fears of people who have watched the world very carefully for a very long time and noticed things that don't add up.

You will notice them too, if you pay attention. The rock with its too-regular hexagons. The cavities that are almost too symmetrical. The script that is not writing. The warmth that increases with depth when it should decrease. The not-coral that builds structures no one recognizes, structures that are faintly luminous in the murk.

There is a geometry here. Something underneath — not the water, not the heat, but the *pattern* — that is trying to be recognized. The people have decided not to name it. They call it cooling patterns. They go look at it again when no one is watching.

You will look at it too. Everyone does. And you will feel, standing before some surface of too-regular basalt columns or some cavity that opens into a chamber of almost deliberate symmetry, that you are in the presence of something that is not alive but is not inert either. Something that is, like the rock itself, still deciding what it is.

Do not try to understand it quickly. Do not try to explain it. Stand in front of it, feel the vibration through your feet, feel the warm humid air on your skin, and let it be what it is: strange, patient, slow, and probably not finished yet.

## What to Bring, Consolidated

**Essential:**
- Quick-drying clothing (multiple sets)
- Sturdy boots with rigid soles and ankle support
- Swimwear
- Hat with brim
- Water purification (tablets or portable filter)
- Electrolyte supplements
- Comprehensive first aid kit (antibiotics, antifungals, antihistamines, painkillers, wound care)
- Sunscreen and insect repellent
- Headlamp (the haze makes dusk-to-dawn transitions unclear; you'll want your own light)
- Journal and writing implement (for timekeeping and mental health)
- Compact mirror or signal device (for communication across haze)

**Strongly Recommended:**
- Quick-dry towels (you will be perpetually wet)
- Snorkel and mask (if you want to observe the shallow underwater environment without full diving)
- Extra boots (the rock will destroy one pair; you need a backup)
- Ear protection (for sleeping near the vibration if it disrupts you)
- Sealed containers for keeping gear dry
- A gift — something from your world, something handmade, something that shows care. The people here build everything by hand from the rock beneath them. They understand labor and intention. Bring something that shows you do too.

**Do Not Bring:**
- Expectations of comfort
- A sense of your own superiority
- Heavy gear (you will be climbing, constantly)
- Synthetic materials you can't afford to lose (the rock will eat them)
- A plan that assumes things will go the way you expect

## Final Thought

The Seamount Cities are not a place you visit. They are a place you enter into — a slow, warm, grey-green world where stone is still becoming stone, where the mountain breathes beneath your feet, and where human beings have done something extraordinary in the most quiet, patient, unglamorous way imaginable. They stacked rock on rock and grew food in ash and dove into water that shouldn't be warm and looked at things that shouldn't have patterns and chose to stay.

You are visiting their world. It was not built for you. The rock is not yet finished deciding what it is. Neither, probably, will you be — not by the time you leave.

Go slowly. Breathe the thick air. Feel the vibration. Let the haze have you. And when you come back out of it — when you leave the warm water and the black rock and the grey sky and return to a world with trees and stars and edges — you will carry the hum of the mountain in your chest for longer than you expect. Much longer.

That's not a bad thing to carry.
