# Preparing for the Breathing Vault

---

## Before You Go In: What This Place Actually Demands

The Breathing Vault is, at its core, a place that will kill you through seven or eight different mechanisms simultaneously, all of them slow, all of them patient, none of them dramatic. It will not drop a ceiling on you or flood you with poison gas in a cinematic burst. It will simply *pull* — pull the moisture from your tissues, pull the oxygen from your lungs, pull the heat from your body, pull the certainty from your mind — and it will do all of this with the same quiet, metronomic persistence with which its crystal shelves extend themselves molecule by molecule into the dark.

You need to understand this before you pack a single thing: **the Vault does not accommodate visitors.** It is not hostile. Hostility implies awareness, intent, the possibility of negotiation. The Vault is simply *complete*, and you are an anomaly in a system that has no mechanism for absorbing anomalies. Every piece of equipment you bring, every breath you take, every step you make is an intrusion into a state of equilibrium that has held since before your species walked upright. The Vault will not fight you. It will simply continue being what it is, and what it is will unmake you over the course of hours if you are not absolutely, obsessively prepared.

---

## THE AIR

**This is priority one. Non-negotiable. The atmosphere will kill you faster than anything else.**

The Vault's atmosphere is a cocktail of hydrogen sulfide, carbonyl sulfide, carbon dioxide, and various trace gases seeping from the deep rock. There is no mention — and you should assume — of free oxygen. Not a whiff. Not a trace. The chemosynthetic growths are feeding on these gases, metabolizing them through processes that have no need for oxygen at all. You, on the other hand, are an oxygen-dependent organism visiting a place that has never produced or needed oxygen in its entire multi-million-year history.

**You need a closed-circuit breathing apparatus.**

Not a simple gas mask. Not a filter. Filters work by removing contaminants from ambient air. There is no ambient air here that is useful to you. You need a system that carries its own oxygen supply and scrubs your exhaled CO2, recycling it or venting it and replacing it with fresh oxygen from a tank.

A rebreather is ideal. Military-grade closed-circuit rebreathers — the kind used by special operations divers — are the closest surface-world analogue. These systems recycle your exhaled breath through a CO2 scrubber (typically a lithium hydroxide or soda lime canister), add fresh oxygen from a small high-pressure tank to replace what you've consumed, and recirculate the mixture. They are quiet, efficient, and they produce no exhaust bubbles (or, in this case, no exhaust plumes that might disturb the gas equilibrium the growths depend on).

**Specifications:**

- **Oxygen supply:** Carry enough for at least 3× your planned time in the Vault, plus emergency reserve. If you plan to spend four hours inside, carry twelve hours of oxygen. The Vault is a place where exit could be delayed — a dislodged crystal shelf blocking your path, a moment of spatial disorientation in the dim light, a simple need to stop and process the overwhelming sensory input. You do not want to be calculating remaining oxygen while standing in a place that makes your hindbrain scream *tomb.*

- **CO2 scrubber capacity:** Match it to your oxygen supply or exceed it. Hypercapnia (CO2 buildup in the blood) will disorient you, cause headaches, rapid breathing, confusion, and eventually unconsciousness. In the Vault's already-syrupy light and disorienting acoustics, the early symptoms of CO2 poisoning would be nearly indistinguishable from the ambient sensory overload. By the time you realized what was happening, you might not be able to retrace your steps.

- **H2S filtration as backup:** Even with a closed-circuit system, seals fail, gaskets degrade, connections loosen. The system should include a secondary hydrogen sulfide filter in the breathing loop, capable of neutralizing H2S that might leak in. Hydrogen sulfide is insidious: at low concentrations it smells like rotten eggs (your nose adapts quickly and stops warning you), at moderate concentrations it causes respiratory irritation and pulmonary edema, and at high concentrations it deadens your sense of smell entirely while simultaneously destroying your olfactory nerve — meaning you lose the ability to detect *any* airborne threat. Your breathing apparatus should not rely on your nose for quality assurance. It should be engineered to be safe even if every seal has failed, with independent O2 and CO2 monitoring in the breathing loop and an automatic switch to emergency supply if contamination is detected.

**Test your system on the surface.** Test it in a sealed room with a hydrogen sulfide source. Test it until you trust it with your life, because you will be trusting it with your life.

## THE DRYNESS

The Vault's air is described as "aggressively, comprehensively dry" — dry enough to crack your lips within minutes. This is not ordinary arid-climate dryness. This is the dryness of mineral chemistry, of gases that have never contained water vapor, of an atmosphere that actively pulls moisture from anything wet. In a closed-circuit breathing apparatus, your exhaled moisture is captured and recycled within the breathing loop, which helps enormously — but every other square centimeter of exposed tissue is vulnerable.

**Skin protection:**

- **Full body coverage.** Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, hood. Every inch of skin should be covered. The material should be tightly woven — a dense cotton or a synthetic blend with low vapor permeability. You want a barrier, not ventilation. Loose clothing will allow air circulation against your skin, accelerating moisture loss. Fit should be close but not restrictive.

- **Face coverage.** A balaclava or similar head covering that leaves only the area around your eyes exposed, and that area should be sealed against the breathing apparatus's mask interface. The skin around your eyes — eyelids, the thin tissue at the corners — is particularly vulnerable to desiccation. Consider applying a thick, occlusive barrier cream (petroleum jelly, or a medical-grade moisture barrier) to any exposed skin before suiting up, and carry more to reapply.

- **Hands.** Thin gloves are not enough. The Vault's growths are crystalline, mineral, and they will be at arm's reach. You will want to touch them — every human instinct will drive you to reach out and feel the shelves, the needle-crystals, the porous lung-like formations. The gloves need to be thick enough to protect against abrasion from mineral surfaces while still allowing enough dexterity to operate your breathing apparatus, handle your light source, and maintain your footing on an uneven floor. Leather work gloves with a tight fit are a reasonable compromise. Bring two pairs — the dryness may crack or stiffen the leather over time.

- **Eyes.** Moisture-wicking is the enemy here. Your eyes will try to protect themselves by producing tears, and the ambient dryness will evaporate those tears almost instantly, leaving your eyes progressively more exposed and irritated. Carry sealed, sterile saline eye drops and plan to use them every twenty to thirty minutes. If you wear contacts, leave them out — the Vault will glue them to your corneas. Prescription glasses are fine but will fog at the breathing apparatus interface; anti-fog coating or a pinhole in the seal may help.

**Hydration:**

- Drink aggressively before entering. Two liters of water in the hour before descent. Your body should be at maximum hydration when you cross the threshold.

- Carry water in a sealed container within your suit, accessible through a drinking tube that interfaces with your breathing mask's oral port. Sip regularly — not because you'll feel thirsty (the Vault's sensory overload will drown out thirst signals), but on a timer. Set an alarm on a wrist-mounted device. Every fifteen minutes, drink.

- Electrolytes. The dryness will pull moisture from your body through your skin as well as your respiratory system. Sweat you didn't realize you were producing will evaporate instantly, leaving behind salt without the water. Add electrolyte tablets to your water supply. Sodium, potassium, magnesium.

## THE LIGHT (AND ITS ABSENCE)

The Vault is dim. "Syrupy" is the word the description uses, and it's exactly right — the light moves through the air as though the air were thicker than it should be, and maybe it is, weighted with gases denser than the nitrogen-oxygen mix your lungs expect. You can see, but only in the immediate vicinity. Distances compress. Edges blur. The vault curves and you cannot tell if it is the vault or your perception.

**Lighting equipment:**

- **Primary: A head-mounted light with red and white modes.** Red light preserves your dark adaptation, which your eyes will desperately try to develop in the Vault's dim chemiluminescent glow. White light is for when you need to see detail — examine a growth formation, check your equipment, read your instruments. The head mount keeps both hands free. Choose a model with a long battery life and carry spares; the cool 17°C temperature will be kind to batteries, but you don't want to gamble.

- **Chemical glow sticks as backup.** Non-electric, non-heat-producing, reliable in any condition. Carry several in different colors. Drop them as you go — this is your thread through the labyrinth. The Vault has no landmarks except the brighter chemiluminescent nodes, and in a disoriented state, those nodes will all look the same. Glow sticks on the ground behind you create a visible trail back to your entry point. Use a consistent color for your outbound trail. Use a different color if you need to mark a secondary route.

- **Do NOT use a flashlight with a powerful focused beam.** This seems counterintuitive — bring the brightest light you can, right? Wrong. In the Vault's packed crystalline environment, a powerful beam will reflect, refract, and scatter through millions of mineral surfaces, creating a blinding, disorienting glare that will make the surrounding darkness seem even darker by comparison. You'll be flash-blind in the center and blind at the periphery. The Vault's own ambient chemiluminescence, augmented by a moderate headlamp, will serve you far better. You need to see *enough*, not *everything*.

- **Mark your path.** Beyond glow sticks, carry a ball of thin, high-visibility cord. Tie it to a secure point at your entry and unreel it as you move deeper. The cord is your lifeline — literally. In the Vault's disorienting, horizon-less space, with the walls pressing close and the ceiling lost in murk, your sense of direction will degrade faster than you expect. The cord does not lie. The cord does not compress distances or blur edges. The cord goes where you went, and it leads back to where you came from.

## THE SOUND

This is the part most travelers will underestimate, and it is the part most likely to compromise your mission before the atmosphere or the dryness or the darkness have a chance to.

The Vault's soundscape is described as a "collective, resonant, subsonic drone" — a vibration felt in the jaw, the soles of the feet, the soft tissue behind the eyes. This is not noise in the way that a waterfall is noise or a crowd is noise. This is *infrasound*, low-frequency vibration at or below the threshold of human hearing, and it does terrible things to the human body and mind.

**Known effects of sustained infrasound exposure:**

- **Anxiety, dread, and panic.** Infrasound at frequencies around 18-19 Hz — very close to the resonant frequency of the human eyeball — can cause visual disturbances, feelings of unease, and irrational fear. The Vault's drone is described as subsonic, meaning it sits in exactly this range. You *will* feel afraid. You *will* feel watched. You *will* feel that the walls are closing in, even when you know they are not. This is not intuition. This is physics acting on your eyeballs and your inner ear. Recognize it for what it is.

- **Disorientation and nausea.** Infrasound disrupts the vestibular system — the fluid-filled semicircular canals in your inner ear that tell your brain which way is up. In the Vault's already-disorienting environment, with no horizon and compressed distances, vestibular disruption could make navigation genuinely dangerous.

- **Fatigue and depression.** Sustained infrasound exposure, even at low levels, causes fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and a pervasive sense of hopelessness. In a place as existentially overwhelming as the Vault — where you are acutely aware of your own insignificance against geological time — these effects will amplify the psychological weight exponentially.

- **Headaches.** The physical vibration of your skull at infrasonic frequencies, sustained over hours, will produce headaches. Not mild ones.

**Mitigation:**

- **Ear protection with selective filtering.** You need to reduce the infrasonic component while preserving enough auditory awareness to hear higher-frequency cues — the tink of crystal growth (which tells you a formation is actively extending and might be structurally stressed), the rustle of gas through the shelf-formations (which indicates proximity to active vent points and thus to thicker growth and potentially warmer floor zones), and, critically, the sound of your own footsteps and movement. Industrial-grade hearing protection with active noise cancellation tuned to sub-30 Hz frequencies is the ideal solution. If that technology is not available, even simple foam earplugs (NRR 33) will attenuate the low frequencies significantly, though they will also muffle the higher sounds you want to hear.

- **Time limits.** Do not stay in the Vault longer than four hours on your first visit. Infrasonic effects are cumulative and, critically, *they persist after exposure ends*. You may leave the Vault feeling fine, only to experience disorientation, anxiety, and visual disturbances hours later as your vestibular system and brain chemistry slowly normalize. Plan for a rest period of at least six hours after exiting before you attempt any activity requiring clear judgment.

- **A companion.** If at all possible, do not enter the Vault alone. The psychological effects of sustained infrasound in an alien, claustrophobic environment are severe, and having another person — someone whose voice you can hear, whose presence you can feel, who can physically grip your arm and confirm that you are both real and both still functional — is an anchor that no amount of equipment can replace. If you must go alone, carry a two-way radio and maintain contact with someone on the surface at all times. Their voice — a human voice, speaking human words about human things — will be a lifeline against the Vault's relentless, mechanical drone.

## THE GROWTHS

Everything is covered. Everything is at arm's reach. And the growths are mineral — calcium and barium sulfates, gypsum needles, crystalline structures that range from spongy-soft to sharp-hard.

**Physical protection:**

- Your gloves (described above) are the primary defense. But understand that the growths are fragile and the space is narrow. You will brush against them, lean against them, push through them. Some will crumble. Some will not. The needle-crystals of gypsum, described as growing in radiating fans, will be at face height and shoulder height in places. They will be invisible in the dim light until you are already among them.

- **Eye protection is mandatory.** Sealed goggles that conform to your face and interface with your breathing mask. Not safety glasses — goggles, with a full seal. A gypsum needle in the eye in a place with no running water, no sterile environment, and no evacuation route for hours is a potentially blinding injury.

- **Do not take samples.** This is not a prohibition born of respect for the environment (though respect is warranted). It is practical. The growths have been building themselves for millions of years in perfect equilibrium. They are chemically bonded to each other and to the rock beneath. Attempting to remove a piece will not result in a clean break. It will result in a cascade of fractures through adjacent formations, creating dust (in an environment where you're already breathing through a filter that wasn't designed for mineral particulate), sharp fragments, and, potentially, a structural shift in the local growth mat that could destabilize the formation you're standing next to. Look. Touch gently, if you must. Do not take.

## THE MACHINERY

The old infrastructure — the corroded pipes, the frozen valves, the skeletal frames of pump-housings — is both an asset and a hazard.

**As an asset:**

- The old pipes, with their crystalline interiors and fixed gas flows, are part of the Vault's circulatory system. They will be warmer than the ambient rock (gas from deep vents carries thermal energy). If you need to warm your hands or check the temperature of your equipment against a reference, a pipe-section carrying active gas flow will be a few degrees warmer than the surrounding growth.

- The machinery's right angles and straight lines are, in a place that has "forgotten" both, *navigational landmarks*. The Vault's organic curves and compressed distances make spatial orientation nearly impossible. A pipe running horizontally across your path, or a valve wheel jutting from a wall of growth, is something your brain can latch onto, measure distance and direction against. Note them. Use them. Reference them when describing your position to the surface team on radio.

**As a hazard:**

- Corroded metal in a chemically aggressive environment is unpredictable. The pipe-sections are described as corroded "to lace-work" — meaning their structural integrity is compromised. Do not lean on them. Do not hang from them. Do not trust them to support weight. The fact that they have persisted for millennia in equilibrium does not mean they can bear the load of a human hand, let alone a human body.

- Mineral deposits have seized every mechanism. Valves are frozen. Pump housings are solid. Do not attempt to operate anything. You will not succeed, and the force you apply might shatter a corroded fitting, releasing a concentrated plume of gas from a pipe that has been channeling it for eons. Given that you are dependent on a breathing apparatus with finite filters, a sudden high-concentration H2S release is not a scenario you want to engineer.

## THE PSYCHOLOGY

This is the most important section, and the one most travelers will not prepare for.

The Vault will affect your mind. Not just through infrasound and claustrophobia and sensory deprivation, but through something deeper and harder to name — the sheer, crushing *weight* of deep time made physical.

You will stand in a place that has been exactly as it is for longer than your species has existed. Every crystal shelf, every needle-fan, every frozen valve, every photon of chemiluminescent light has been in its precise state for millennia, and it will be in that same state for millennia after you leave, and it was in that state for millennia before you arrived, and your entire life — your birth, your childhood, your memories, your loves, your fears, every moment that has ever mattered to you — occupies less than an eyeblink on the timescale against which this place operates.

You will feel small. Not in the way that standing at the edge of the Grand Canyon makes you feel small, or watching a rocket launch, or reading about the size of the galaxy. Those are abstract smallnesses, intellectual ones. This will be *visceral*. You will feel small in your body — in the thinness of your skin, the fragility of your bones, the absurdity of your soft, wet, warm, *temporary* flesh in a place of mineral permanence. You will feel small in your mind — in the brevity of your thoughts, the poverty of your perceptions, the laughable inadequacy of your senses to take in even a fraction of what the Vault contains.

And you will feel — the description says it with terrible precision — that you are "the only unfinished thing in it." The Vault is complete. It is sufficient. It does not need you, does not want you, will not remember you. You are a perturbation. A fluctuation. A brief and violent deviation from a baseline that has held since before your ancestors descended from the trees.

**This feeling is real. Do not try to suppress it. Do not try to fight it with bravado or philosophy or the comforting narrative that "I am a conscious being experiencing something beautiful." All of those things are true and none of them will help.**

What will help:

- **Acknowledge it.** Name it. Say it aloud, into your radio, to your companion, to yourself: "I feel very small right now. This is normal. This is the correct response to standing inside deep time. It will pass. I will leave. The Vault will continue. This is okay."

- **Keep your task in mind.** You are there for a reason — to observe, to document, to measure, to map. Give your conscious mind a job. Catalog the growth forms. Measure the width of the passage. Count the brighter nodes you pass. The Vault is overwhelming in its totality; it is manageable in its details. Focus on the next ten meters. Focus on the shelf at eye level. Focus on the reading on your instrument. The Vault's permanence becomes less crushing when you reduce it to a series of discrete, completable tasks.

- **Breathe.** Your breathing apparatus makes this literal — each breath is a controlled, measured act within a closed system. Match your breathing to a steady rhythm. Four counts in. Four counts hold. Four counts out. This is not meditation. This is mechanical regulation of your autonomic nervous system in an environment that is actively trying to dysregulate it.

- **Set a firm turnaround time.** Decide before you enter exactly how long you will stay. Write it on your wrist, where you can see it under your headlamp. When the time comes, *go*. Do not stay for "just a few more minutes." The Vault's psychological effects intensify non-linearly — the second hour is worse than twice the first, the third worse than three times the first. Your judgment will degrade. Your sense of time will distort. The Vault's eternal patience will begin to feel like a seduction, a pull to stay, to sit down, to stop moving and let the drone and the dim light and the crushing weight of permanence just... hold you. This is the infrasound talking. This is your vestibular system degrading. This is not wisdom. Set the time. Keep the time. Leave.

## PRACTICAL PACKING LIST

Summarized:

1. **Closed-circuit rebreather** with O2 supply for 3× planned duration, CO2 scrubber matched to supply, independent gas monitoring (O2, CO2, H2S), automatic emergency switch
2. **Sealed goggles** interfacing with breathing mask, anti-fog treated
3. **Full-body clothing** — tightly woven, close-fitting, covering all skin
4. **Thick leather gloves** (two pairs)
5. **Balaclava** or full head covering
6. **Head-mounted lamp** with red and white modes, long battery life
7. **Chemical glow sticks** (two colors — one for outbound trail, one for secondary markers)
8. **High-visibility cord** on a reel, minimum 200 meters
9. **Sterile saline eye drops** in sealed, single-use vials (minimum 10)
10. **Petroleum jelly** or medical-grade moisture barrier cream
11. **Hydration system** with drinking tube, minimum 2 liters of electrolyte water
12. **Selectively-filtering ear protection** (infrasonic attenuation)
13. **Two-way radio** with spare batteries, maintained contact with surface team
14. **Timer** (wrist-mounted, visible under headlamp)
15. **Notebook and pencil** (pencils work in any temperature; pens may not in the cool dry conditions; paper will not get damp here — if anything, it will become brittle)
16. **Compact instrument kit** (as appropriate to mission: thermometer, barometer, gas sampler, camera with flash capability for documentation)
17. **A second pair of everything that might fail** — especially batteries and rebreather scrubber cartridges

## THE FINAL PREPARATION: LEAVING ROOM

The hardest part of preparing for the Vault is not the equipment. It is accepting what the place will show you about the nature of time, permanence, and your own exquisite impermanence.

The Vault is not a metaphor. It is a place. A real, physical, measurable place with a temperature of seventeen degrees and an atmosphere of hydrogen sulfide and a soundscape of ten billion simultaneous chemical reactions. You can go there. You can stand in it. You can see its dim light and feel its dry air and hear its ancient, mechanical heartbeat.

But you cannot stay. That is the rule — not the Vault's rule, which has no rules, only processes, but *your* rule, the rule of your biology, your oxygen, your water, your soft and temporary flesh. You are a visitor in a place that does not visit. You are a fluctuation in a system of permanence. You will enter, and you will change nothing, and you will leave, and the Vault will settle, and in an hour there will be no evidence you were there at all.

Prepare for that. Not just the gear and the logistics and the safety protocols, but the *feeling* of it — the experience of mattering less than you have ever mattered before, in a place that does not measure matter in human units.

Then go. See it. Come back. Tell the rest of us what deep time looks like from the inside.
