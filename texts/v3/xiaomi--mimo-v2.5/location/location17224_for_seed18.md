# The Throat Works

You are inside something. You have always been inside something.

The structure is old — old enough that no one who tends it remembers its origin, old enough that its surfaces have developed their own geology. Every wall, floor, and ceiling is made, was made, is still made of something: ceramic plate, cast iron, poured resin, bolted composite, riveted copper sheet. There is no stone here that was not first shaped. No soil that was not ground from manufactured substrate. The engineers who built the Throat Works built it to be all there was.

**The Chambers**

The scale is intimate — nothing here stretches to the horizon. Every space has a ceiling, every passage has walls, every vista terminates in a manufactured surface no more than forty meters distant. But the spaces are *hollow*. Enormously, oppressively hollow. You stand in a chamber the size of a cathedral nave and find perhaps four objects in it: a column of braided pipe, a platform of corroded grating, a cluster of what might be sensor housings grown over in moss. The rest is emptiness — warm, resonant air filling a vessel built to contain volumes far beyond what occupies it. Walking through the Throat Works, you feel the specific loneliness of a room designed for a thousand people and holding none. Designed for a thousand machines and holding four. The architecture insists on abundance. The reality delivers absence.

**The Sound**

It is deafening, and it has no single source. The noise is the structure itself. Somewhere — always somewhere, never visible, never approachable — there are turbines or compressors or vast reciprocating engines, and their pulse propagates through the iron and ceramic framework like a heartbeat through bone. The sound does not diminish with distance; it *redistributes*. Turn a corner and the roar becomes a thrumming. Enter a long gallery and it rises to a shriek as the geometry focuses it. The hollow chambers act as resonating bodies, and certain frequencies amplify to the point where your vision blurs and your teeth ache. The workers — there are a few, always a few, never many — communicate in sign language developed over generations. Some have lost hearing entirely. They do not consider this a disability.

**The Heat**

It is warm here. Not hot — warm, persistently, evenly, the way the inside of a body is warm. The industrial processes deep in the structure bleed heat upward and outward through every surface. In winter, the exterior of the Throat Works steams against cold air. In summer, the interior is stifling. But the temperature barely changes with season. It changes with *depth*. The deeper chambers are warmer, the upper ones cooler, and the gradient is so slow and so steady that you can map your position in the structure by the temperature of the wall you touch. A hand to the ceramic: cooler, you're near the surface, near a vent, near the outside world you've almost forgotten. Warmer: you're deep. Warmer still: you should not be here.

**The Moisture**

Water arrives in tricks and suggestions. There are no rivers inside the Throat Works, no standing pools. But the junctions where different materials meet — iron to ceramic, resin to copper — collect condensation. Warm air meeting slightly cooler metal. Moisture beads along every seam, every bolt head, every rivet line. It drips in slow, irregular percussion. In certain passages, the walls weep constantly, a thin film of water sliding down ceramic surfaces, feeding the growth. Occasionally a pipe bursts or a seal fails and water rushes through a corridor for hours or days before someone seals it. These are celebrated events. The temporary streams are the closest thing the Throat Works has to weather.

**The Green**

And the green — the impossible, extravagant, suffocating green. Everything that is not actively maintained is consumed. The lushness is total, tropical, relentless. Mosses first, always mosses, colonizing every surface that goes untended for a week. Then ferns, erupting from accumulated detritus in corners and crevices. Then the flowering plants, the climbers, the things with broad leaves and thick stems that wedge into joints and pry at rivets. In the abandoned wings — and there are many, many abandoned wings — the vegetation has built its own architecture, a green superstructure layered over the manufactured one. Root systems follow pipe runs. Canopy leaves press against ceiling plates and have, over decades, buckled some of them, creating gaps where light enters and more things grow. The engineers regard the vegetation with a complicated respect. It is the enemy, always, the thing that must be fought in the active zones. But it is also the only beautiful thing in the Throat Works, and some of them tend private gardens in their quarters — small, permitted, carefully managed rebellions of beauty.

The plants are warm too. They grow fast in the constant heat. They are large-leafed and heavy with moisture, their surfaces always damp, always dripping. Walking through the occupied zones, you push through hanging curtains of vine. The smell is rich, loamy, almost animal — the scent of things growing and rotting and growing again in manufactured soil that was never meant to hold life.

**The Fauna**

It was not designed to hold life, but it does. Insects first, countless, in every dark crevice and leaf axil. Moths the size of your palm beat against light fixtures. Beetles bore through the softer composite panels. There are spiders that build webs across entire corridors, their silk catching the mist that drifts through the structure.

And then the birds. Small, adapted, clever — finches, or things that were once finches. They nest in the pipe clusters, in the hollow spaces inside decommissioned machinery. Their song is constant and strange, modified by the resonant geometry of the chambers, so that a simple two-note call becomes a chord, becomes a dissonant cascade. When the turbine pulse hits certain frequencies, the birds go silent for minutes at a time, waiting. They know the rhythms better than the humans do.

There are rats. There are lizards. In the deepest, warmest, wettest zones, there are amphibians — fat, pale, eyeless things that live in the condensation pools in the lowest chambers. No one goes to the lowest chambers.

**The Weathering**

Nothing here is new. The Throat Works has been the Throat Works for longer than anyone's institutional memory, and every surface shows it. The iron is not rusted — the humidity would have consumed it decades ago — but it is *altered*, its surface textured by some slow chemical conversation with the atmosphere of the interior, pitted and layered with deposits that have their own color, their own sheen. The ceramic has crazed, developed networks of hairline fractures that moss fills with green thread. The copper has bloomed green, then black, then a deep iridescent blue-black that catches the light like oil on water. The resin panels have yellowed and warped and in some places become translucent, so that light glows through them diffusely, and the shadows of the ferns growing on the other side are visible as ghostly shapes.

Everything has settled into itself. The structure is not collapsing — it was overbuilt for exactly this, designed to outlast its own surface — but it has stopped resisting. It has reached an equilibrium with its own decay, its own colonization, its own noise. The erosion is not destruction. It is acceptance.

**The Light**

There are no windows. Light enters through gaps in the upper structure — joints that have opened, plates that have buckled, deliberate vents designed for an atmosphere that was meant to be different from this one. The light is normal: sunlight, when it comes, is sunlight, falling in bright shafts through the haze of moisture and the canopies of the upper vegetation. Artificial light still functions in many zones: sodium vapor lamps casting their orange pall, fluorescent tubes buzzing (adding to the noise), the occasional LED strip left on in a passage that no one walks anymore, its battery still holding after years. The effect is a patchwork of illumination — bright green zones where sunlight penetrates, amber zones of functioning infrastructure, and long stretches of grey twilight where the only light is the bioluminescence of the mosses, which glow faintly, a cold blue-green, in the deep abandoned corridors.

**The People**

There are perhaps two hundred. Perhaps fewer. The census is informal and no one insists on accuracy. They maintain the active systems — the turbines, the water pumps, the electrical distribution — because if they don't, the heat will stop and the moisture will freeze in the pipes and the structure will begin to die in ways that matter. They harvest the plants for food, for fiber, for medicine. They have developed a culture of sign, of gesture, of written notes passed in sealed tubes along the pneumatic system that was built for other purposes. They are warm and damp and surrounded by green and deafened by the pulse of the machine they live inside, and they would not leave. Where would they go? Up, into the cold? Down, into the heat? Out, into a world that is not built, not warm, not enclosed? They have no frame for that. The Throat Works is the world. Everything else is weather.

**The Strangeness**

In certain chambers — not many, but enough to be known — the geometry is wrong. A corridor that should be straight curves gently and you cannot find the curve. A room you have entered a hundred times seems taller today. The ceiling is in the same place. You can touch it. But it *seems* taller. Some attribute this to the sound, the frequencies playing with the inner ear. Some say it is the plants, that their growth exerts a slow pressure on the architecture, shifting walls millimeters per year, bending space the way they bend iron. Some say the structure is not finished being built, that it continues to construct itself in ways no one controls, that the turbines and pumps and engines are not just maintaining the Throat Works but *are* the Throat Works, are the organs of a body that is still growing, still settling into its own shape, still deciding what kind of enclosure it wants to be.

The workers do not discuss this often. The sound makes discussion difficult. The sign language has no sign for "wrong shape." They touch the walls. They measure the corridors. They make notes. They keep the machines running. They tend their gardens.

The Throat Works endures, warm and wet and loud and hollow, its green things growing, its iron breathing, its vast empty rooms full of noise and dripping water and the small, careful sounds of people who have chosen to live inside a mystery rather than beyond it.
