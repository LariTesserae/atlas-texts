# The Steaming Galleries

You descend. The air thickens with each step, and not only in humidity — in *presence*, as though the walls themselves lean closer the deeper you go. The passage was cut recently. You can tell by the edges: every surface bears the geometry of extraction. Bore-holes gape in spiral rows along the ceiling like blind eyes. The stone is a paleite, calciumite something — pale, granular, still releasing a faint chalky dust that settles on nothing because the updraft carries it back up. The walls have not yet softened. There is no patina, no mineral staining, no lichen. Every corner is an angle. Every surface remembers the machine that shaped it.

The heat comes from below — not fire, not industry exactly, but the *fact* of depth. The stone itself radiates. You press your palm to a wall and it presses back with warmth, steady as blood. The galleries sit above a thermal anomaly, some shallow intrusion of mantle heat that makes the rock sweat. And it does sweat: moisture beads on every surface that isn't actively vented, gathering in the pores of the cut stone, sliding in slow rivulets along walls that haven't been sealed. The air is a hot towel pressed to the face. You breathe it and feel the water enter you from the inside.

The space is *small*. Not cramped — there is a certain grandeur to the proportions, a cathedral logic in the vaulting — but it closes. Every gallery leads to another gallery, and each is perhaps forty meters long, perhaps thirty across, perhaps fifteen to the crown of its arched ceiling. You can always see a wall. You can always see the next passage. The settlement — if that is what it was, what it still is — was not built for grand vistas. It was built for *function packed into volume*. Every cubic meter has a purpose, or had one, or is being assigned one now by processes you cannot quite identify.

And there are many presences here. Dense. Layered. Not in the sense of crowds — there are no crowds, there are no *creatures at all* — but in the sense of infrastructure. The galleries are threaded with infrastructure. Pipes run along the walls in bundles, some copper-gone-green, some coated in a ceramic insulation that glows faintly amber where internal heat bleeds through. They carry water — you hear it sometimes, a slow pulse, a gurgle in the wall — but the water is not for drinking. It is process water. It is heat-exchange water. It moves in closed loops, and where it leaks, the leaks steam. Small white plumes that rise and dissipate almost before you register them, like the building is breathing in its sleep.

The machinery is old but not ancient. Industrial-age. You recognize the grammar of it: flywheels behind grating, belt-drives running on ceramic pulleys, a piston assembly in one gallery that still moves — or *would* still move, if the power were constant. The power is not constant. It surges and fades. Somewhere, a generator turns. Somewhere, a turbine catches the updraft from the thermal anomaly and converts heat to rotation. The system works. It has always worked. It is currently working. But it works in the way a heart works in a fever — irregularly, with effort, with a wet sound beneath the regular rhythm that you try not to think about.

No animals. Not one. Not a beetle, not a spider, not a rat, not a bacterium that you can smell the way you'd smell bacterial life in a warm cellar. The heat keeps the galleries sterile. Or something keeps them sterile. The sparse vegetation that exists — a handful of pale, waxy growths in joints where water pools — is not quite plant life in any recognizable sense. It grows in crystalline fractals. It is translucent. It does not photosynthesize because there is almost no light to photosynthesize by. What it feeds on, you do not know. It is rooted in mineral deposits at the base of walls where condensation gathers, and it seems to feed on the minerals themselves, on the iron in the stone, because its edges are faintly rust-colored and its core is green as old copper.

Light comes from nowhere and everywhere. There are no lamps you can find, no bulbs, no filaments. But the galleries are not dark — they are *dim*, lit the way a mouth is lit from within, a warm ochre glow that has no source you can trace. It seems to come from the walls themselves, from the pipes, from the thermal bleed of the rock. It is enough to see by. It is not enough to see *well*. Shapes hold at the edge of recognition. A flywheel might be a column. A column might be a figure. You stop trusting your peripheral vision early, and you learn to look directly at everything, and you learn that looking directly at everything makes you feel watched, because direct attention is a kind of confrontation and something in these galleries does not like confrontation — or does, in a way you don't want to examine.

The sound. Mostly quiet. But not silent — silence would be honest, and these galleries are not honest. There is a low vibration, more felt than heard, that runs through the floor into the soles of your feet and up through the skeleton. It is the thermal anomaly. The earth *hums* here, a tectonic frequency that makes your vision blur slightly at the edges if you stand still too long. Layered on that: the drip of condensation, the slow pulse of process water in the walls, the ticking of cooling metal as galleries shift temperature across cycles you cannot perceive. And occasionally — rarely, but with a clarity that makes you stop — a sound from deeper in. A metallic *ring*, as though something vast and precise has just finished a task. A chime. A completion tone. It reverberates for a long time, bouncing between the angled surfaces, multiplying, and then it is gone and the hum returns and you wonder if you imagined it.

The weirdness here is subtle. It is not the weirdness of wrong physics or impossible geometry. It is the weirdness of *purpose without agents*. Everything in these galleries is working. The pipes carry water. The turbines turn. The thermal exchange continues. The crystalline growths mineralize. But no one tends it. No one is here. The city runs itself, or rather the city *runs*, full stop, as a process rather than a place. It is active in the way a river is active — it does not need inhabitants to continue its work. The architecture suggests it was built for inhabitants, once. The ergonomics of certain galleries — a bench-height shelf, a passage at walking pace, a doorway scaled for a body — imply that someone designed this for occupation. But the occupation has ended, and the machine has not noticed, and it continues to hum and drip and glow with the steady, thoughtless commitment of a system that does not know it has been abandoned.

You notice, finally, that the passages slope gently upward as you move deeper. Not enough to feel. But the condensation changes — it gathers more thickly ahead of you, drips more frequently, pools in the channels cut into the floor. And the air, though hot, changes character. It becomes *richer*. More mineral. More wet. As though something ahead is producing moisture at scale, feeding the closed loop that the pipes carry, that the system circulates, that sustains the crystalline growths and the faint light and the hum.

You do not go toward it. Not because you are afraid, though you are, but because the passage ahead is too narrow — it was built for pipes, not for bodies — and the steam that rises from it shimmers with a faint greenish cast that matches the growths, and you understand, with the certainty that comes not from knowledge but from the body's older senses, that whatever process you would find at the heart of the system is not something a body should witness from close range.

You stand in the warm, dim, wet, breathing dark of a city that works without workers, that runs without reason, that hums in frequencies that rearrange thought, and you feel the stone radiate against your skin, and you listen to the water pulse, and you watch the crystalline growths catch the lightless light at their edges, and you understand that you are standing inside a *verb* — not a noun, not a place, but a process that has learned to make walls around itself — and that it will continue, and that it does not need you, and that it does not know you are here, and that it never will.
