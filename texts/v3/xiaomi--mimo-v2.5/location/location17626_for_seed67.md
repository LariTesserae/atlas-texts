# The Plate of Perpetual Industry

You stand on a surface that was never ground. Below your feet: poured syntheticite, a warm resin-bondite aggregate layered in sheets meters thick, still bearing the faint geometric scoring of the machines that laid it. Every surface you can see — flat, low, stretching to every horizon — is made. There is no substrate that was not placed here by some process, at some time, and every trace of that placement has been preserved with absolute fidelity, because nothing here erodes. Nothing decays. The temperature holds at a constant warmth, like the inside of a machine that has been running for a very long time and will never stop.

The scale is continental. The built plain extends without interruption to every horizon, broken only by structures that are not buildings in any familiar sense — vast low-profile housings, rectangular and trapezoidal, some kilometers across, their surfaces studded with vents, hatches, and mechanical protuberances that catch the ordinary light in dull metallic ways. Nothing rises more than a few stories. The elevation is flat to the point of geological suspicion, as though the entire surface were a single poured slab the size of a small continent, which — in fact — it is.

---

**The water is everywhere, and it goes nowhere.**

It condenses from the warm, thick atmosphere in a fine ceaseless mist that clings to every surface. It beads on metal housings. It gathers in shallow, perfectly still pools in every depression of the constructed floor — pools that never deepen, never evaporate, never overflow. They hold at a precise depth, millimeter-perfect, as though each one were calibrated. Water weeps from the seams of structures in steady, silent threads and is caught in channelized grooves cut into the syntheticite, grooves that lead to reservoirs already full, already balanced. The entire hydrological system is an open loop running at perfect equilibrium. Rain falls sometimes — warm, heavy, slow drops — and when it does, it simply replenishes what the condensation maintains. The air smells of warm mineral and wet metal, a clean industrial smell, like the inside of a cooling tower after shutdown. Except nothing has shut down. Everything is running. Nothing changes.

There is a haze to every direction, not fog exactly but a permanent softening of the distance, the accumulated weight of moisture suspended in warm air. You could walk in a straight line for weeks and see only the same: flat constructed ground, low industrial housings, wet surfaces gleaming dully, the haze.

---

**There are no plants. Not one.**

No moss on the metal. No algae in the pools. No root systems, no spore prints, no organic filaments threading through cracks. The chemistry of this place does not support it. Whatever substrate the syntheticite is composed of — and its composition varies in subtle ways across the plain, suggesting different production runs over vast distances — it produces no nutrients that any photosynthetic or chemosynthetic life-form has ever colonized. The surfaces are bare. The pools are clear. The air carries no pollen, no decomposing matter, no bacterial bloom. This is a mineral world in the fullest sense: metal, resin, processed stone, chemical compounds, and water.

And yet.

---

**The fauna is overwhelming.**

They are *everywhere*, in numbers that defy easy comprehension, and they are not animals in any evolutionary sense. They are, as nearly as anyone can determine, constructed alongside everything else — products of the same vast industrial process that poured the ground and raised the housings. They come in forms that range from the mechanical to the biological to states that sit uneasily between: jointed metallic organisms with too many limbs, moving on precise, silent articulations across the surfaces. Soft-bodied presences, translucent and warm to the touch, filling the spaces between hatch-frames and vent-grilles in dense, layered aggregations. Things like insects but larger, with exoskeletons of hammered sheet metal and compound eyes made of polished quartz. Things like worms but translucent, their interiors visible — fluid systems, mechanical pumps, tiny clockwork visible through their skins.

They pack every niche. They fill the grooves and channels. They cluster on the undersides of structures, stacked in dense aggregations, touching one another. They gather in the pools — wading, standing, half-submerged — in numbers that seem impossible for the volume of water, yet each pool holds exactly the density it can sustain, and no more, and nothing shifts. The density of living — or functioning, or *operating* — presences per cubic meter of space, in the zones where they are most concentrated, approaches something like saturation. You could reach into the mass of them and feel limbs, carapaces, soft bodies, mechanical joints, all pressing together, all warm, all subtly moving, and yet the mass does not displace. It is already as dense as it can be.

And yet the world is **quiet**.

---

That is the strangest thing. This is the part that makes the place feel like a held breath, a moment that has been stretched across geological time without ever releasing. The fauna are present in crushing abundance and they are *nearly silent*. You hear things — a faint clicking of metal on metal, rhythmic and precise, like a clockwork heart. A low hum that might be mechanical, might be biological, might be resonance from the structures themselves. The soft sound of water moving in channels, almost subliminal. Occasionally a sound like a large intake of breath from somewhere far away, sustained and unchanging in pitch. But these sounds do not vary. They do not crescendo or fade. They do not respond to your presence. They are the acoustic signature of a system in perfect equilibrium, and they remain at precisely the level of amplitude they have always been at.

Nothing startles. Nothing flees. Nothing hunts, in any visible sense. The fauna operate — they move, slowly and ceaselessly, along predetermined paths across the constructed surfaces, into and out of the housings, through channels and over hatches — but they operate without urgency, without competition, without interaction that looks like anything other than coordinated maintenance. They are the moving parts of the machine that is the world, and the machine is vast and old and complete, and it runs at a constant rate, and it will never stop, and nothing about it will ever change.

---

**The strangeness of it — and the strangeness is real, a low hum beneath everything — is the total absence of history.**

The features are young. Sharp. The scored lines on the syntheticite are as crisp as the day they were cut. The hatches have no wear on their edges. The fauna show no signs of age, injury, adaptation. There are no gradients — no zones of abundance and zones of scarcity, no places where water is more or less, where life is denser or thinner, where temperature shifts. The entire continental surface is uniform in its condition. It is as though the whole thing was placed here fully formed, already in equilibrium, already populated, already running, and has been running for an unknown length of time in a state of perfect, absolute, unchanging stability.

Nothing grows here. Nothing dies here. Nothing is born. Nothing erodes. Nothing accumulates. The water pools and does not rise. The fauna move and do not arrive. The machinery hums and does nothing, or does everything, and you cannot tell the difference.

You could set down a marker — a scratch on the ground, a stone placed in a channel — and it would be there forever, unchanged, because nothing would move it, nothing would wear it, nothing would disturb the exact and permanent arrangement of things. The scratch would join the other marks on the surface, the original ones, from the original construction, and it would look exactly as new as they do, because there is no aging here.

There is only the warm light, the constant water, the vast flat made-world, the overwhelming and silent fauna doing their eternal work, and the profound, total, unchanging quiet of a place that was finished before it began.
