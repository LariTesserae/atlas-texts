# The Settled Town

You come upon it from above, which is how you come upon most things at this elevation — the ground falls away less than it opens outward, spreading into a shallow shelf of rock that has been worn, over ages, into something almost hospitable. The shelf is not flat. It undulates in long, low waves of granite or whatever the local stone is — you'd have to chip it to know — and in the troughs between those waves, someone has built.

Or something has built. The distinction matters and you're not sure it has an answer.

The buildings are stone. Everything is stone. It has to be, up here — there is not enough soil for timber posts, not enough forest for beams, and whatever trees once grew or whatever equivalent of trees once stood have long since surrendered their substance to the damp and the cold and the slow chemical patience of the rock. The structures are low. One story, mostly. Some are half-buried in the natural troughs of the shelf, so that their roofs are level with the ground behind them and you could walk across a ridge and unknowingly pass over someone's head. The walls are thick. Two cubits minimum, three in the lower courses where the weight bears down. They are built of shaped stone — not finely dressed, but *shaped*, with edges that know what they're doing, with courses that settle into each other with the quiet competence of work done by hands that learned from hands that learned from hands.

The faces of the stones are smooth where the water has found them, polished by mineral deposit and mineral removal, the eternal negotiation of water and silicate played out in millimeter increments over centuries. At the base of every wall, the stone is darker. This is where the condensation lives.

Because the air here is always damp. Not wet — you will not see standing water, not in any quantity, not anywhere you look. But the moisture is *present*. It is in the air as a taste, a weight on the tongue, a faint film on the inside of the mouth. It gathers on every surface that is cooler than the air around it, which is every surface, because the temperature here is low and steady and has no reason to change. In the morning — if there is a morning distinct from any other time — the stone walls are dark with absorbed water. By what passes for afternoon, the surfaces have lightened a shade as the moisture migrates inward or evaporates into the cool air, only to return again as the air itself cools by some imperceptible fraction and the stone surrenders its warmth and the water comes back to its surface. It is a breathing. The walls breathe dampness. The ground breathes dampness. You breathe dampness.

The ground between the buildings is paved. Not uniformly — some stretches are bare rock, naturally exposed, worn smooth by foot traffic or by nothing at all. Others are cobbled with stones set into a packed matrix of grit and clay and whateveriteiteite organic residue has accumulated over time. The cobblestones are rounded. Not just weathered — *rounded*, as though each one has been individually held in a palm and worried smooth, though of course that is not what has happened. What has happened is simply *time*, and enough of it will do to any stone what a hand can do in an afternoon. Moss grows between the cobbles. This is the main vegetation. A low, grey-green, persistent moss that exists in the crevices and the shaded gaps and the places where foot traffic does not quite reach. It is not lush. It is not spreading. It is simply *there*, as it has always been there, as it will always be, because the conditions that produce it do not change and the conditions that would destroy it do not arrive.

There are a few other plants. A sparse, tough grass that grows in the thin soil of the shelf's edge, where the rock gives way to a little more ground and a little more ground gives way to nothing. A lichen on the north-facing walls — pale, almost white, in irregular patches that look like the stone itself is dreaming. No trees. No shrubs. The air is too cool, the soil too thin, the wind too constant for anything with ambitions greater than a handspan above the ground.

The sky is the color it always is here: a pale, uniform grey-white that sits just above the buildings like a low ceiling. It does not brighten or darken in any dramatic way. The light is normal — you can see, clearly, everything there is to see — but it has no direction. There are no shadows, or the shadows are so soft and so diffuse that they are merely areas of slightly less illumination, more a suggestion than a shape. You could lose track of time here easily. You already have.

The town — and it *is* a town, not a ruin, not an encampment, but a settled, structured, organized town — occupies perhaps two hundred paces along the shelf and fifty or sixty paces deep. It is not large. It is small in the way that places are small when they have found exactly the size they need to be and have no reason to grow or shrink. The buildings number perhaps forty or fifty, arranged along a single main path that follows the long axis of the shelf, with smaller paths branching off at intervals and terminating at individual doors or at the shelf's edge where you can look out over a grey expanse of nothing — the slope falling away below the level of cloud and mist, the valley or the plain or whatever is down there hidden by the white.

You hear the town before you see it fully. Not sounds, exactly — the town is mostly quiet, a quiet so established and so deep that it has the texture of a material, something you could reach out and touch. But the quiet is not total. There is the sound of your own movement, which seems loud and foreign and which you find yourself trying to soften. There is the faint, persistent sound of moisture — not dripping, not flowing, but *present*, a sound like the memory of water, like the frequency that water would produce if it were a little more itself. Occasionally a stone settles somewhere, a tiny adjustment, the sound of gravity completing a sentence it began centuries ago. A bird — a single bird, small, grey — makes a sound from somewhere you cannot locate, a brief, questioning note, and then does not make it again.

The fauna is rare. The bird is the only animal you have seen in an hour of walking, and it did not stay. The town supports nothing that you can see — no chickens, no dogs, no livestock. No insects, or so few that they do not announce themselves. The moss does not teem. The lichen does not crawl. Everything that lives here does so slowly and quietly and with a minimum of fuss, the way things live when they have concluded that the world is not going to offer them more than this, and have decided that this is enough.

The built environment is layered. You can see it in the walls — where a lower course of stone has been set, and then a later course added above it, and then a repair made to that repair, and then a doorway narrowed by a few fingers' width because the lintel shifted, and then a window opened where there was no window before, and then that window sealed again with smaller, rougher stone. Each layer is distinct if you look closely, and each layer is *old* — not old the way ruins are old, which is a surrender, but old the way a scar is old, which is a persistence. The town has been modified. It has been maintained. It has been changed in small ways over long periods, and each change has settled into the whole the way sediment settles into rock, until the whole thing is as much a natural formation as the shelf it sits on.

You do not see the people who built it. You do not see their tools or their works-in-progress or the evidence of recent activity. But you do not see abandonment either — no collapsed roofs, no choked doorways, no signs of decay that has been allowed to proceed unchecked. The town is in a state. It is in its state. It is in the only state it has ever been in or will ever be in, and the distinction between habitation and artifact has long since stopped mattering to anyone, including the town itself.

If there are people here — and the town implies them, requires them, demands them by its very tidiness — then they are as quiet as everything else. They move through the stone paths with the same unhurried permanence as the moisture on the walls, present and undeniable and completely without urgency. They have tasks. Those tasks have always been the same tasks. The tasks will always be the same tasks. There is water to carry and stone to shape and moss to clear from the cobbles and a bird to listen for, and each of these things is done in its time, and its time is always now.

You stand on the shelf and the damp air touches your skin and the grey sky hangs above you and the town extends in both directions along the rock, small and old and perfectly itself, and you understand that nothing here is going to happen. Not nothing in the sense of emptiness — there is too much *being* here for emptiness — but nothing in the sense of *change*. The place has arrived. It has completed its journey. What you are looking at is the destination, not the road.

The stone is cool under your hand when you touch it. The moisture is there. It is always there.
