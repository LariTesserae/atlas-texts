# The Keelworks

You are standing at the bottom of a crack in the world that should not exist this far underground.

The space is small — no larger than a cathedral from end to end — but it drops upward and downward in ways that make the inner ear suspicious. Above, a fissure in the basalt ceiling climbs perhaps four hundred meters to a surface no one here has seen, the walls narrowing and widening as they go, choked with iron infrastructure: catwalks, pressure conduits, cable bundles the thickness of a man's torso, all bolted directly into fresh-looking rock with the sharp edges of stone that was split open only decades ago, perhaps less. Below, the floor is not a floor but a series of descending platforms — some natural, some poured concrete, some steel plate — stepping down into a shaft of indeterminate depth, from which warm updrafts rise carrying a faint metallic taste. The whole volume feels *squeezed*, as though the rock is still deciding whether to keep this space open.

The rock itself is dark: a basalt-adjacent volcanic glass, almost black, with fracture surfaces that catch the dim light in planes of dull violet. It is young stone. Everything here is young. The edges of every outcrop are knife-clean, crystalline, unsoftened by time or weather or the patient work of water that doesn't exist in this place. There has been no water here — perhaps never. The atmosphere is bone-dry, cool to the point where breath becomes faintly visible, carrying a stillness that borders on mineral. The air tastes filtered, recycled through something industrial and thorough. Every surface has a fine grit ofite dust on it — not quite pollen, not quite spore, not quite sand. Something between mineral deposit and biological trace. It accumulates in the corners of welded joints and along cable runs, a pale roseate film.

The Keelworks is — was — is still — a settlement. It clings to the walls of this crevice like a shipwreck caught in a slot canyon. The construction is entirely industrial in character: heavy steel framing, riveted and welded, bolted into the living rock with expansion anchors the size of a forearm. There are levels — six or seven main platforms, perhaps more in the upper reaches where the fissure narrows beyond comfortable passage — and each level is a dense lattice of machinery, housing, conduit, and compressed human infrastructure. Walkways barely a meter wide thread between boiler housings and what appear to be geological sampling stations. Lights are sparse: amber industrial fixtures in heavy glass enclosures, some powered, some dark, casting pools of warm color against the black glass walls. The overall illumination is dim — a perpetual amber twilight, with areas of deep shadow where the infrastructure blocks the few light sources from each other.

The machinery is heavy, purposeful, and running. The Keelworks was built to extract something from this fissure — the rock itself, perhaps, or something trapped in the formation — and it has not stopped. You can feel it more than hear it: a subsonic thrumming through every surface, a vibration in the handrails and the deck plates that never quite resolves into sound. But there are sounds. They are quiet, specific, and strange. The tick and creak of cooling metal. A rhythmic hydraulic exhale somewhere below, like the breathing of something with too many lungs. The occasional sharp *ping* of thermal contraction in an overhead cable. And underneath all of this, a faint skittering — rapid, irregular, close.

The fauna is abundant, and it is not what you expected.

They are not exactly animals. They are not exactly machines. They range in size from insects to small dogs, and they occupy every surface of the Keelworks with the confidence of organisms that consider this place native. Most are arthropodal — segmented, chitinous, with exoskeletons that have the same dark glassy sheen as the walls, as if they are made of the same material or have been coated in it over generations. They move along the cable runs and infrastructure with mechanical precision, pausing occasionally to do something with their mouthparts against the rock or the metal, leaving behind traces of that roseate dust. Some are larger: centipede-like creatures, a meter long or more, that navigate the vertical shafts between levels with an unsettling fluidity, their legs finding purchase on surfaces that would defeat a human climber. They are quiet. Almost all of them are quiet. The skittering you hear is the collective sound of hundreds of small bodies moving across metal and glass, a sound like static, like the background noise of a city viewed from very far away.

There are no plants. There is nothing green. The closest analogue is a growth on the north wall — a spreading, branching mineral deposit that might be fungal in origin or might be a chemical precipitation, pale and warm to the touch, forming fan-shaped shelves across a ten-meter stretch of rock. Small fauna congregate on and around it. Whatever it is, it is alive, or adjacent to alive, in a way that this place rewards.

The turbulence here is geological. The Keelworks is still *forming*. The fissure is not stable — it shifts. Not often, and not dramatically in any given moment, but the monitoring equipment mounted on every wall tells the story: slow pressure changes in the rock, micro-fracture events detected by seismographs bolted to the ceiling, the gradual creep of the walls at rates measured in millimeters per year. The infrastructure accounts for this. Every joint has play in it. Every platform is mounted on sliding brackets. The pipes are not rigid but articulated, with expansion loops and flexible couplings at every level change. The entire Keelworks is designed to *breathe with the rock*, to accommodate the slow and sometimes not-so-slow reshaping of the space it occupies. There are scars on the walls — old bolt holes, sheared brackets, the ghost of a platform that was consumed by a rockfall or a wall-shift and had to be abandoned and rebuilt half a meter to the east.

The people — there are people here, or there were recently, because there are signs of human habitation that are too fresh to be archaeological: a jacket hanging on a hook, a mug on a shelf, a schematic pinned to a bulkhead with handwritten notes in the margins — are not present now. The Keelworks is operating, but its operators are elsewhere, or between shifts, or up in the narrow upper reaches where the fissure thins to a passage barely wide enough for a person, where the infrastructure becomes more cramped and personal, where you might find beds and cookstoves and the small domestic machinery of lives lived in a space that is fundamentally inhospitable to life. The absence of people is not eerie. It feels scheduled. It feels like a quiet hour in a working plant, when the machines hold down the fort and the humans sleep.

The unusual quality of this place — its *weirdness*, registered but not overwhelming — lives in the details. The way the roseate dust moves on its own, in slow drifts across surfaces, when no air current accounts for it. The way the larger fauna seem to be *working* — not erratically, not with the blank purpose of instinct, but with something that approaches coordination, assembling or disassembling material on the lower platforms in patterns that repeat with slight variation. The way the rock, in certain lights, shows veins of something that glows — not luminously, not with any light of its own, but with a color that the amber fixtures cannot account for, a deep blue-green that appears and disappears as you shift your viewing angle, as though the mineral structure of the stone is performing some optical trick that requires exactly the right geometry to become visible. The way the monitoring equipment on the lower levels has been repositioned — recently, by hand — to point not at the walls or the ceiling but at the shaft below, as though something down there is now more interesting than the geology.

The Keelworks is small, enclosed, dry, dim, and full of things that move in the dark. It is a pocket universe of industry and strange biology, caught in a crack that is still opening, and it will not last forever. Nothing here lasts forever. The brackets slide. The walls shift. The rock reforms itself in slow motion, and the infrastructure bends to accommodate it, and the fauna adapt, and whatever the people came here for continues to justify the effort of maintaining a city in a wound in the earth that is slowly, patiently, trying to heal itself shut.
