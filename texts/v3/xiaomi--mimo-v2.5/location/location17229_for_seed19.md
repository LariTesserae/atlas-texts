# The Paralogues

The mountains are older than water.

This is not metaphor. Before any ocean touched this stone, before the first rain fell and found a channel, these formations already existed in something like their present shape — not carved, but *precipitated*, as though the rock itself exuded upward from a planetary fever that broke and cooled. The erosion index is a misnomer. Nothing has been removed. Everything has been *finished*. The peaks and mesas and knife-edged ridgelines arrived in their final form the way a bone arrives in its joint, and have remained so for periods that dwarf the invention of geology as a discipline.

The color is the color of old teeth and moonless predawn. Tan, grey-white, a mineral pink where iron concentrations bled upward through the strata during the settling. The stone is dense — not merely geologically, but *ontologically*. Stand on a ledge and feel the pressure of it beneath your feet: this is rock that has been thinking about itself for a very long time and has arrived at conclusions. The surfaces are not smooth from wind or water but from something else, some settling-into-final-form that rounds edges the way grief rounds a person. Everything is finished. Everything is deliberate.

And the altitude. The scale of it defies the low horizon. You are always on something — a shelf, a ridge, a bridge of stone spanning a chasm that drops into a geometry your eyes refuse to parse. The chasms are the main sign of the fracture. Reality, here, broke along clean lines, the way glass breaks when scored first. The canyons do not follow river logic. They follow *crack* logic — sudden, angular, plunging in directions that have nothing to do with gravity's preferences. You look down and see a passage that curves sideways, then upward, then simply *terminates* in a flat wall of stone that shouldn't exist at that depth but does, and has for longer than anything has had eyes.

This is the weirdness: not a shimmer, not an aura, but a settled wrongness in the architecture of space. The fractures have healed in the wrong order. Distance works here, but it works *differently* — a ledge that looks forty meters away might be four hundred, or four, and there is no consistent rule. You learn to judge by the fauna.

---

The fauna.

The fauna is everywhere and it is the most silent thing you have ever encountered.

They cling to every surface — packed into the density of the stone like barnacles on a hull, but alive, or something adjacent to alive. In the crevices, on the undersides of bridges, in the deep vertical fractures where no light should reach, clustered in numbers that make your vision swim. They are the size of your fist, or your head, or your torso, or much larger — the deeper you look, the bigger they get, and the density does not decrease. A cliff face is not a cliff face. A cliff face is a *colony*, a mosaic of bodies fitted together with the same ancient settledness as the stone itself, and they are watching, and they are utterly, absolutely quiet.

Some have limbs. Some have shells or plates or protrusions that might be limbs or might be the same mineral as the rock they're pressed against, and it is often impossible to tell where the creature ends and the stone begins, because the process that settled the landscape settled them too. They are in equilibrium. They are *part* of the equilibrium. The rock grows around their legs and they grow around the rock and neither minds, and neither has for eons.

They do not move.

You will stand on a ledge for an hour — a day — and you will not see a single one move. Their eyes, if they are eyes, are fixed open. Their mouths, if they are mouths, are fixed in positions that are not quite expressions. A few of the larger specimens have postures that suggest interrupted action — a limb raised, a head turned — but the interruption happened so long ago that the gesture has fossilized into the creature's anatomy. It is part of their shape now. They are frozen mid-thought in a sentence they will never finish, and the silence they produce is not the silence of absence but the silence of a held breath that has lasted since before your species existed and will last long after it doesn't.

The sound parameter of this place is not zero but it might as well be. What you hear is the creak of stone settling under its own ancient weight — not often, and not dramatically, but in long, slow, sub-audible groans that you feel in your jaw before your ears. And beneath that, something that might be the collective respiration of ten thousand (a hundred thousand? a million?) still creatures breathing in unison so slowly that each breath lasts a season. But you can't be sure. It might be tectonic. It might be nothing.

---

There are traces of hands.

Not many. A carving on a sheltered wall, half-swallowed by the creeping mineral growth of the stone — a spiral, or a map, or a counting-system. A stack of flat rocks arranged with deliberate asymmetry on a high shelf where no creature rests, as though whoever built it was careful not to disturb the locals. A tool: a shaped piece of bone, or a shaped piece of bone-shaped stone, left in a crevice as though its owner set it down meaningfully and never came back.

The tool is old. But it is not ancient in the way the mountains are ancient. It is merely *very* old. A few thousand years, maybe. A blink. Someone was here recently enough to have hands, to have purpose, to have the capacity to leave a thing behind rather than carry it forward, and they did their work in silence among the silent watchers, and then they were gone, and the landscape did not notice, because the landscape has been in equilibrium since before the concept of *change* applied to it, and one species more or less is a rounding error on the density of what lives here.

The creatures did not eat them. The creatures do not eat anything, as far as you can tell. They sit, and they watch, and they are packed so tightly onto every surface that the term *sparse vegetation* is a lie the way you've understood it — there is almost no plant life, almost no photosynthesizer, almost no base of any food chain you'd recognize, and yet this place is *dense* with animal presence, teeming, saturated, every cubic meter of space near the stone occupied by something that is alive or *almost*-alive and committed to staying exactly where it is.

They feed on something you can't see. The weirdness, maybe. The fracture. Reality, here, leaks — not visibly, not in any way you could capture with equipment — but *something* seeps from the wrong geometries, from the sideways canyons and the impossible walls and the spaces where distance lies, and the fauna drinks it, or breathes it, or simply exists in its proximity the way a plant exists in sunlight, and this is why there are so many of them and so little of anything else.

---

You could walk here for a long time and see the same thing, and it would never become familiar, because the wrongness of the space ensures that your perception is always recalculating, always uncertain, always slightly nauseated. The scale is open — you can see for kilometers in every direction, across canyons and along ridgelines and up to the impossible flat tops of the highest mesas, where the largest creatures sit in arrangements that might be social or might be geological, and every sightline is populated by the packed, watching, silent density of the colony.

Nothing changes here.

This is the deepest rule. The dynamic index is not merely low; it is *metaphysical*. This place arrived at its present state and stopped. The creatures stopped. The stone stopped. The fractures stopped spreading. Reality broke, found its broken shape, and *hardened into it*, and now it holds, the way a cast holds — permanent, rigid, finished. You could return in a thousand years and see the same creature in the same raised-limb posture on the same ledge, and the only difference would be that the mineral growth had thickened slightly around its joints, which is itself a form of not-changing, just slower.

The cool air sits still. It has no moisture to offer, no weather to speak of. It is simply *there*, at a temperature that suggests the ghost of comfort, a climate that might have been habitable if anything here still used the word *habitable* in a way that implied choice. The light is normal — a flat, directionless illumination that comes from everywhere and nowhere, as though the sky itself has reached equilibrium and no longer bothers with the drama of sun angle or cloud cover. There are no clouds. There is no water to become clouds. There is only the light, and the stone, and the watchers, and the silence, and the settled wrongness of a reality that broke open and discovered it preferred the open position and closed the latch behind it.

You pick up the tool. You set it down again, carefully, exactly where it was. You do this because the silence asks it of you. You do this because the ten thousand eyes on the cliff face have not blinked and will not blink and you do not want to be the thing that makes this place *change*, because you understand, with the deep animal certainty of a species that still has things to lose, that this place is *not to be changed*, that the equilibrium is the only law, and that the last creature who tried to disturb it left only a tool and a carving and then was absorbed into the same ancient stillness, and their hands are somewhere in the stone now, in the same place as everything else, at rest, at last, in the terrible silence of a world that finished.
