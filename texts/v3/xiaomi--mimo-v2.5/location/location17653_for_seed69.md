# The Heightherne

You are standing on a plateau that has no sky.

The darkness above is not cloud cover, not canopy, not the absence of a sun — it is the plain and total absence of any surface that light could strike. Look up and there is nothing to look up *toward*. The plateau exists at high elevation within a geological structure so vast that its own ceiling has weather into a kind of geology: inverted mountains of deposited mineral, hanging formations grown over epochs, their bases lost so far above that sound cannot carry the distance. The scale of the enclosure is continental. You cannot see the far wall. You cannot hear it.

But you can hear everything else.

---

## The Water

The Heightherne weeps. That is the simplest and most accurate word. Water moves through this landscape the way blood moves through a body — constantly, silently in some places, deafeningly in others, but always. It is warm. The ambient temperature holds steady around the heat of a body left in shade, and the water carries that warmth with it. It seeps from fractures in the rock with a persistence that suggests the fractures are old and deep, fed by some immense reservoir far below. Condensation beads on every exposed surface: stone, metal, the broad surfaces of fungal growths. In places where the air moves — and it moves often, in slow convective currents driven by geothermal gradients — the condensation forms a low mist that never quite settles, never quite clears, drifting at knee height like a living thing with no ambition.

The water collects in channels that look like they were carved by a patient hand over millions of years. The erosion parameters here are deeply established. Every surface is rounded. Every edge has been found and softened. The drainage basins are smooth-bottomed bowls ofite-colored stone, their floors covered in a thin, constant sheet of warm water that barely moves. In other places the water falls — not far, rarely more than a few meters — but the sound of it compounds. Multiply a small waterfall by ten thousand, spread them across a basin the size of a continent, and you begin to understand the baseline roar of this place. It is the sound of the Heightherne breathing.

Where the water pools more deeply, it is black and still and warm as bathwater. The surface tension is high — something in the mineral content, perhaps, or something else — and things move beneath it without breaking the skin. Ripples propagate outward in geometric patterns that seem almost too regular. Something in the chemistry of this water preserves, too. Organic matter doesn't decay so much as *integrate* — leaves and shed carapaces and old fungal tissue sink and become part of the substrate, layering over millennia into a soft, dark sediment that is part soil, part mineral deposit, part compressed archive of everything this landscape has ever been.

## The Living Dark

There is no photosynthesis here. There has never been photosynthesis here. And yet the Heightherne is *lush* — obscenely, almost aggressively lush — in a way that challenges the assumptions of any biology shaped by surface light.

The primary producers are chemosynthetic organisms, and they are everywhere. Great shelving formations of fungal tissue grow from the wet rock faces, some of them meters across, their surfaces slick with condensation and faintly warm to the touch. They grow in tiers, dozens deep, layering over each other like geological strata made of living matter. Their color is irrelevant — everything here is irrelevant to color — but their texture ranges from smooth and cartilaginous to rough and fibrous, almost woody. They metabolize sulfur compounds, iron, manganese, whatever the mineral-rich water brings them. They are the foundation.

Above them — or rather, *around* them, orientation is complicated in total darkness — the plant-like organisms have evolved toward a different logic. They grow toward chemical gradients rather than light. They are thick-stemmed, bulbous, their tissues dense with stored water and dissolved minerals. Some have evolved hollow, resonant chambers — bladders of tissue that vibrate with the movement of air and water through the landscape, producing low, droning tones. Others have developed vast root networks that extend laterally for tens of meters, interlocking with their neighbors into mats of such density that the ground itself becomes a living substrate. Walk across it and you feel the give of it, the way it absorbs your weight and slowly, gently, pushes back.

The canopy — if it can be called that — is composed of enormous fungal spires, some reaching fifteen or twenty meters high, their upper surfaces broad and flat, catching condensation from the mist layer and channeling it downward along grooved stalks. They are the trees of this place, and they creak with a sound like ship timbers in a slow swell. Between them, hanging curtains of filamentous organisms drift in the convective currents, trailing sticky threads that catch airborne spores and small flying creatures.

The ecology is dense. Every cubic meter of air between the spires contains life: drifting spore clouds, small winged organisms that navigate by echolocation or vibration-sense, larger things that move through the mist layer with slow, deliberate purpose. The fauna is *abundant*. It has to be — in a system this dense, with this many trophic layers, the biomass requirements are enormous.

## The Fauna

Nothing here has eyes. Or rather, nothing here has eyes in the way you would recognize them. Some organisms have developed patches of light-sensitive tissue — not for vision, but for detecting the faint bioluminescent traces that certain fungi produce. These patches are dim and imprecise, more thermometer than camera, useful for locating warm patches of active fungal growth in the cold distances between spires.

The larger fauna navigate primarily by sound. The Heightherne is loud — the water, the wind through the fungal forest, the resonant drones of the plant-like organisms — and the animals have evolved to read that noise the way a surface dweller reads a landscape by sight. They sense vibration through their feet, through their bodies, through water-filled chambers in their tissues that act as resonating organs. A large herbivorous organism — six-limbed, low-slung, its body armored in plates of mineral-encrusted chitin — can detect the footfalls of a predator from three hundred meters by the way its vibrations propagate through the living substrate of the ground.

The predators are patient. They are ambush hunters, and they have evolved mimicry that goes beyond appearance into the acoustic domain. They produce sounds that match the ambient noise of the water, the fungal drones, the wind. They sit motionless for hours, days, and when something walks close enough they strike with limbs that are built for power rather than speed. The prey species, in turn, have developed the ability to go silent — to stop breathing, to arrest the movement of their internal fluids, to become effectively acoustically invisible for short periods. It is a landscape-scale arms race played out in sound.

Smaller fauna fill every niche. Things that crawl along the wet rock surfaces, scraping fungal growth with mineral-hardened mouthparts. Things that swim in the warm pools, filter-feeding on the suspended organic matter. Things that hang from the fungal spires in clusters, dormant during periods of low water flow and active when the rains — or rather, the condensation surges — come. The diversity is staggering, not in spite of the darkness but because of it. Without light as a shaping force, evolution here has explored dimensions of adaptation that have no equivalent on the surface: vibration, chemical gradient, thermal signature, acoustic profile. The niche space is carved along axes that have nothing to do with seeing.

## The Industry

Scattered across this basin, half-sunk in the living substrate, draped in fungal growth and trailing curtains of filamentous organisms, are the structures.

They are industrial. They were built by someone — something — with access to metallurgy, to engineering, to the ability to shape stone and fabricate metal at scale. And they are old. Not as old as the erosion patterns, not as old as the landscape itself, but old enough that the Heightherne has begun the long process of incorporating them. Some structures are barely recognizable — a wall here, a foundation there, a shape in the terrain that is too geometric to be natural. Others are more intact, their metal surfaces corroded but structurally sound, their doors still functional on heavy hinges that grind when moved.

The ones that still run are the strangest things here.

They are *loud*. Pumping stations, primarily — vast mechanical assemblies that draw water from the deep fractures and push it through pipe networks that run along the basin floor like artificial rivers. The pipes are enormous, some of them three or four meters in diameter, and they carry water that has been heated by whatever geothermal source feeds the system. Steam escapes from joints and valves, adding to the mist layer, and the sound of the pumps is a rhythmic, pounding heartbeat that underlies everything else. The machines are powered by — this is the part that takes effort to accept — by the water itself. The geothermal pressure is sufficient to drive turbines, and the turbines drive pumps, and the pumps move water that drives turbines. It is a self-sustaining system, or close enough to one that the distinction has ceased to matter. It has been running for a very long time. It will run for longer still.

Other structures are smaller. Maintenance shelters. Monitoring stations with instruments that have long since lost their function — analog dials frozen at readings that no longer correspond to anything, wiring chewed through by burrowing organisms, screens cracked and dark. Occasional artifacts sit in odd places: a heavy wrench on a shelf, a coil of wire in a corner, a ceramic insulator cracked in half. They feel like the possessions of someone who left in a hurry and never came back.

And there are larger structures, too — factories, perhaps, or processing plants — whose interiors have become ecosystems of their own. The walls are thick with fungal growth. The floors are covered in standing water and organic sediment. Machinery stands in the midst of it all, silent now, its surfaces colonized, its moving parts seized by corrosion and biological encrustation. In some of these structures the fauna has moved in: large organisms resting in the sheltered spaces between machine components, using the residual warmth of the geothermal feed pipes as heating. They are comfortable there. The structures belong to them now.

The question of who built these things, and why, and what they were processing, is one that the Heightherne does not answer readily. The structures predate living memory of any organism in the basin. They are artifacts of a civilization that left only its infrastructure, and the infrastructure has become part of the landscape, and the landscape has forgotten its purpose.

## The Atmosphere

The air is thick. Not with pollution — though the industrial systems contribute chemical compounds to the atmosphere — but with moisture, with spores, with the volatile metabolic products of a billion organisms going about their business in the dark. Breathing it is like breathing warm broth. It carries scent the way water carries sediment: constantly, heavily, in layers. There are sharp mineral smells, sweet fungal smells, the acrid tang of sulfur compounds, the warm animal smell of fauna moving through the mist.

The sound is the defining feature of the atmosphere. It is not one sound but a composition of thousands: the percussive roar of waterfalls, the rhythmic thud of industrial pumps, the droning of resonant plant-chambers, the sharp cracking of mineral deposits forming on exposed surfaces, the calls of fauna communicating across the dark distances. It is loud. It has always been loud. Silence, in the Heightherne, would be a catastrophe — the sound is how the ecosystem reads itself, how predators and prey locate each other, how the fungal networks coordinate their growth. The noise is information. It is the nearest thing this place has to light.

The mist layer shifts and flows, driven by thermal currents, and within it the air moves in patterns that are almost but not quite predictable. There are updrafts where geothermal vents warm the air and it rises, and downdrafts where it cools and sinks, and lateral flows along the basin floor that carry spores and small organisms on slow, patient journeys across the landscape. The air is itself an ecology. Things live in it permanently — drifting, drifting, feeding on what the air carries, reproducing in the mist, dying and becoming what the air carries.

---

## The Shape of the Place

The basin is not flat. It rises and falls in long, weathered undulations — ancient geological features that have been eroded and re-eroded over timescales that make the industrial structures look ephemeral. There are ridges of harder rock that stand above the general elevation like the spines of buried creatures, their surfaces polished smooth by eons of water flow. There are depressions where water collects in deep, warm pools. There are slopes — gentle, long, covered in fungal growth — that lead up to higher ground where the air is slightly thinner and slightly cooler and the mist layer sits lower.

The edges of the basin are lost in the dark. The scale is so large that the far rim, if there is a far rim, might as well not exist. What you know of this place is what you can hear and what you can touch and what you can smell. The darkness has no gradient — it is equally total at arm's length and at the theoretical horizon. You exist in a bubble of sensory information, and beyond that bubble is the Heightherne's true scale: vast, dark, warm, wet, loud, alive, and utterly indifferent to your ability to comprehend it.

The erosion tells the only history. Water and time have shaped this place into its current state — a state of equilibrium, or close to it. The rock is old. The water is older. The life is new by comparison but has had long enough to fill every available niche with a density of adaptation that borders on the excessive. The industry is the newest layer, and it is already being absorbed. Given enough time, the Heightherne will digest it completely, and the next thing that comes will find only subtle geometric irregularities in the terrain and wonder, briefly, before continuing with the business of survival in the warm, loud, total dark.
