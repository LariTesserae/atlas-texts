# The Resz Plateau

## I. The Scale of It

You cannot see the edge. You can be told there is one — that the Resz Plateau drops away on every side, thousands of meters to lowland you will never visit — but standing on it, this knowledge means nothing. The plateau is not a landscape you stand upon. It is a landscape that contains you. It stretches to every horizon and beyond, a vast high table of young stone, and the sky above it is enormous in a way that flat lowland skies are not, because the air is thin and dry and there is nothing — no haze, no dust, no moisture — to soften the distance. The sky is deeper blue than any lowlander has a word for. At its edges it grades toward black.

The cold is not dramatic. It does not bite or sting. It simply *is*, in the way that gravity is — a constant, total, inescapable fact of the body. It lives in the stone beneath your feet. It lives in your joints. It lives in the air you breathe, which never warms in the lungs. Temperature here does not mean what it means elsewhere. There are no seasons in any cycle a lowlander would recognize. There is the plateau's cold and then, briefly, the plateau's less cold, and the difference between the two is measured in the behavior of condensation: whether frost crystals form on the windward side of a rock face or only in its deepest cracks.

## II. The Stone

This is young country. The plateau is still rising — slowly, tectonically, but measurably over a human lifetime — and the rock shows it. The dominant material is a pale grey basalt, fine-grained and dense, broken into sharp angular forms where frost-wedging has cracked it along cooling joints. There are no rounded hills here. Everything is blade-edged, faceted, geometric. Columns of basalt jut from the plateau floor in clusters, some standing alone like pillars, others in dense walls of hexagonal sections, still locked together, still barely a single mass. The joints between them are narrow and deep and cold — dark lines scored into the stone with a precision that seems deliberate but isn't.

Between the basalt formations, the ground is a mosaic of broken stone: scree and talus in angular gravel, dusted with a fine pale grit that is the slow product of thousands of years of frost-shattering. This grit moves. Not quickly. Not visibly. But it moves — down slopes, into cracks, into the joints between columns, filling them gradually, compressing. The dynamic here is patience. The landscape is being reshaped by water that freezes and expands, grain by grain, crack by crack, over centuries. A column that stands today will have a new fracture line in fifty years. A wall of basalt that seems eternal is slowly, imperceptibly, falling.

The sound of the stone is silence. Basalt does not ring, does not echo, does not sing in the wind. It absorbs. The wind moves through the column-fields and around the sharp ridges and the only sound is the low, subsonic moan of air forced through narrow gaps, felt more in the chest than heard. On still days there is nothing. The silence of a vast high place where the air is too thin to carry sound far and the rock drinks everything that reaches it.

## III. Water, and the Absence of It

There is no river. There is no lake. There is no rain, or almost none — a few days a year when the air's moisture threshold is barely breached and a thin mist falls, more condensation than precipitation, coating every surface in a film of droplets that freezes within minutes. This is the wettest the plateau gets. It is not enough to carve, not enough to pool, not enough to feed even a stream for more than a few hours before the water seeps into the porous grit or sublimates back into the dry air.

Water here exists as frost. As rime. As the white crystalline crust that forms in the shadowed cracks between basalt columns, building up over months into veins of pale ice that glow faintly in the flat light. As the condensation that beads on every surface when the temperature briefly rises and then falls again — a micro-cycle of wetting and freezing that is the most active geological process on the plateau, slowly, relentlessly prying rock apart from within.

There is one other form water takes here. In certain places — and these places are the reason anything lives on this plateau at all — the rock is warm. Not hot. Warm. The young basalt is still close to its mantle source, and along certain fracture lines, deep groundwater makes contact with residual geothermal heat and rises to the surface. It emerges not as springs or pools but as *vapor* — columns of warm, mineral-laden steam that rise from cracks in the rock and immediately condense in the freezing air, creating halos of ice fog around each vent. These are the *rezh* — the breath-holes — and they are the most important features on the plateau.

Around each rezh, the air is different. Warmer by a few degrees — just enough. Damp. Thick with mineral particulate. The condensation is constant, a perpetual gentle rain of ice crystals falling outward from the steam column in a halo that can extend twenty or thirty meters. The rock within this halo is coated in a rind of mineral deposit: calcium, silica, iron compounds, laid down layer by layer over centuries. The deposits are pale, almost white, streaked with ochre and rust, and they have a smooth, almost organic texture, rounded where the fresh basalt is sharp. They are the only smooth stone on the plateau.

## IV. What Lives Here

The rezh-fields are dense. Not with vegetation — the cold and the altitude and the thin soil mean that plant life is sparse and low-growing: lichens on the mineral rinds, a few species of hardy moss in the warmest and most sheltered crevices, the occasional stunted shrub no taller than a fist, its woody stems buried in grit, its leaves reduced to scales. The vegetation exists, but it is a footnote, a minor accent in the landscape.

What the rezh-fields are dense with is animals.

They come for the warmth, and they stay for the minerals, and over generations they have become so numerous and so habituated to one another and to the rezh themselves that the breath-holes have become the nuclei of entire ecosystems. Around the largest rezh — and some are enormous, steam columns rising ten meters or more, surrounded by halos of ice fog a hundred meters across — the animal density is extraordinary. There is no lowland equivalent. Every square meter of warm rock within the halo supports life.

The ground-nesting birds are the most visible. They are small, heavy-bodied, dense-feathered things — the plateau has sculpted them into near-spheres of plumage — and they nest in the crevices between basalt columns in colonies of hundreds of thousands. Their eggs are laid on bare rock warmed by proximity to the rezh, and they hatch in the perpetual half-light of the ice fog. Their droppings are the primary source of organic material on the plateau — a thick, white, ammonia-rich guano that coats the rock around every colony site and feeds the lichens and mosses that in turn feed the insects.

The insects are the second density. In the warm, mineral-rich air immediately around the rezh, midges and flies and small beetles breed in numbers that seem impossible for this cold. They do not migrate. They live their entire lives — egg, larva, pupa, adult — within meters of a single vent, emerging in brief warm-season pulses, mating, laying eggs in the mineral deposits, dying. Their bodies are a food source for everything else.

The mammals are fewer but larger. Thick-furred, slow-metabolizing, they move between rezh-fields along paths worn into the basalt grit — paths that have been walked for longer than any settlement has existed. They are patient animals. They stand in the steam and let it warm them. They lick the mineral deposits. They tolerate one another at close quarters in a way that animals elsewhere would not, because the rezh are too important to fight over. Herds of stocky, six-legged grazers — low to the ground, broad-footed, with dense woolly pelts that trail in the grit — gather around the smaller vents in groups of thirty or forty, standing motionless for hours, their breath mingling with the steam. Predators — lean, long-bodied, pale-furred — move through the colony sites at night, taking birds from the outer edges of the nesting crevices.

And there are others. Things that live in the cracks, in the spaces between basalt columns, in the dark warm crevices where the mineral deposits build up in smooth, rounded tunnels. Worms, small crustaceans, organisms that feed on chemical energy from the geothermal water rather than on sunlight. The plateau is a closed system in miniature around each rezh: energy rises from below, is captured by simple organisms, is passed upward through the food web, and returns to the stone as guano and waste and death.

## V. The People

They have been here for a long time. Not as long as the basalt, not as long as the rezh, but long enough that they are as much a part of the landscape as the nesting birds. Their settlements are dense — necessarily so, because they are built around the rezh, and the rezh are finite — and they are built of the only material available: basalt.

The construction is heavy, geometric, and silent. Houses are fitted between existing basalt columns, the gaps filled with smaller stones packed with mineral-rich grit that freezes into a near-cement. Roofs are flat slabs of basalt laid across the column-tops. The walls are thick — a handspan or more — and the interiors are warm, heated by proximity to the vent and by the body-heat of the inhabitants pressed close together in the small spaces. Every dwelling has a steam-vent: a channel carved into the mineral deposit that directs a portion of the rezh's warmth into the living space. Control is crude — a stone plug that can be raised or lowered to adjust flow — but it works. It has worked for centuries.

The settlements are not scattered. They are concentrated, layered, stacked. Where a rezh-field is large enough, multiple dwellings crowd around it in concentric rings, each ring slightly colder than the one inside it, each ring home to a different function. The innermost ring — closest to the vent, warmest, most humid — is for the eldest and the youngest and for the work that requires the steadiest heat: the softening of animal sinew, the working of the simple metals. The outer rings are for the hardier members, for the storage of dried meat and mineral blocks, for the animal pens where the six-legged grazers are kept in warm stone enclosures during the worst of the cold.

The metal is copper. Occasionally tin-bearing stone, traded along the paths between rezh-fields, worked into bronze with great difficulty and reverence. Tools are few. Every tool has a name. A copper blade, a bone awl, a stone hammer, a sinew-twine bundle — each one is the product of days of labor and will last a lifetime and be passed down. The people do not waste. They cannot afford to. Everything is used, and when it is used up, it is returned to the stone.

Their technology is simple but it is not crude. It is refined by repetition over generations. They know the rezh — every one, its rhythm, its mineral composition, its steam output, its behavior across the slow cycles of the plateau. They know the animals — every species, every pattern, every season. They know the cold — not as an enemy but as a medium, the water in which they swim. Their knowledge is vast and deep and entirely oral, carried in stories and songs and the daily rituals of work, because there is no material on the plateau that takes ink well and no tradition of writing. The density of their knowledge matches the density of their living.

## VI. The Sound of the Place

Silence. Then not-silence.

The rezh themselves are almost soundless — a faint hiss, a wet exhalation, like breathing heard through a wall. The ice fog muffles everything. Animal calls are soft, brief, economical: a bird's two-note contact call, a grazer's low hum of contentment in the warmth. No animal on this plateau screams or sings loudly or calls across distance. Sound is conserved here, the way everything is conserved. The thin air at this altitude does not carry it, and the cold does not encourage it.

The human sounds are the loudest things on the plateau, and even they are quiet by lowland standards. The tap of a stone tool on copper. The scrape of a bone awl through sinew. The low murmur of voices in a dwelling, warm breath fogging in the half-light. Laughter — which happens, which is warm and real — but brief, because deep laughter takes breath and breath is precious in thin cold air.

At night, if you lie very still on the basalt, you can hear the rezh. Not with your ears. With your body. The deep, slow pulse of geothermal pressure moving through the rock — a vibration so low it registers only as a fullness in the chest, a sense that the stone beneath you is alive, is breathing, is exhaling its warmth upward through the cracks and fissures that thread through the plateau like a vast slow circulatory system. The plateau breathes. The people who live on it have always known this. They have no word for geology. They have a word for breathing, and they use it for the stone.

## VII. The Light

Normal light. Flat, high-altitude daylight under a deep blue sky, shadowed by ice fog around the rezh-fields. The sun crosses the sky but its angle barely changes — the plateau's latitude and its altitude conspire to create a light that is almost uniform, almost directionless, a bright grey-white wash over everything. Shadows are soft-edged because the ice fog scatters the light. There is no golden hour. There is no sunset color. There is the light and then, gradually, there is less of it, and then there is darkness — a darkness so complete and so total that the stars seem solid, seem close enough to touch, seem brighter than they have any right to be.

The ice fog around the rezh glows at night. Not much. A faint luminescence, the breath of the vent catching starlight, a soft white halo in the blackness. The people navigate by these halos. Every rezh has a distinct shape, a distinct brightness, a distinct position in the sky of the plateau. To know where you are, you look for the halos. To know where home is, you look for the brightest one.
