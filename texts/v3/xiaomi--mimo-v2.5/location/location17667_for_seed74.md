# The Millwork Hollow

The first thing you notice is the cool — a deep, cellar-cool that rises from the ground itself, even though the sky is open above you. It's a low place. Not a valley exactly, more of a broad, shallow bowl, the kind of terrain that water remembers even when water isn't present. And water is sometimes present. Condensation beads along every surface in the early hours, gathering in slow, patient drops along the undersides of living beams, falling at intervals so regular they almost have a rhythm — a soft, irregular metronome marking time in a place that doesn't seem to track it.

The town — if it can still be called that — occupies perhaps two or three hectares. You can see its edges: the ground falls away slightly, and beyond that threshold the plant-structures thin and give way to scrubby, normal growth. But within the boundary, everything is built. And everything is alive.

---

**The structures.** They are not ruins overtaken by green. They never were. The walls are ribbed trunks — not trees, exactly, but engineered growth-column hybrids, planted decades ago and trained along frameworks of rusted iron and poured concrete that you can still see in places where the living bark has worn thin. The columns branch as they rise, and the branches were guided, at first by wires and scaffolding, and later by their own momentum, into vaulted canopies that serve as roofs. The result is a series of interconnected rooms, halls, and passages, open to the air but sheltered overhead by dense, overlapping leaf-layers that filter the normal light into a greenish, diffused, perpetual overcast.

The spacing is what makes it strange. This isn't jungle. The plant-architecture has been *designed*, or at least cultivated with the memory of design, and the gaps between structures are wide — alleys, courtyards, small squares where the sky opens fully. The density is sparse in the way a town is sparse: room to move, room to see across a space, visibility down a corridor or across a plaza. But every surface within that space is living matter. Bark underfoot where wooden decking has been swallowed. Vine-threads strung taut between columns like telegraph wire, still carrying the faintest grooves where cables once hung. Leaf-panels hinged on flexible stems that sway with air current — shutters, still functional, still opening and closing with thermal shifts in the cool air.

---

**The machines.** Industrial. Yes. They're still here. Some still run.

There is a sound to this place that is not silence but is almost silence — a low, intermittent vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Deep in the largest structures, where the plant-columns are thickest and the root-mass is dense enough to feel like walking on a firm mattress, there are machines. You can find them by following the hum. Pressurized systems, originally steam-driven, now fed by the plants themselves — sap-lines tapped and rerouted through hollowed conduits, the hydraulic pressure of the root network doing work that boilers once did. A generator turns somewhere beneath the largest hall. Its rhythm is slow. It does not always turn. Sometimes it stops for hours, and the only sound is water moving through root-tubes and the patient creak of growth.

Not all the machines are running. Some have been fully consumed — iron frameworks visible inside translucent sheaths of root-matter, like insects in amber. Others sit in open courtyards, oxidized, colonized by lichen and fungal bloom, no longer functional, no longer relevant. The town didn't abandon them. It simply grew past them.

---

**The water.** Low elevation means the water table is close. It comes up through cracks in old foundations, pools briefly in depressions in the root-floor, and retreats. It arrives with weather — a thin rain that falls more like mist than like actual precipitation, drifting in under the canopy edges, coating everything in a fine slick. But it's intermittent. There are dry stretches where the air is merely damp, where the condensation slows and the pooled water evaporates and the root-surfaces go matte and papery. Then the rain returns — soft, quiet, more atmosphere than event — and the whole place swells slightly, the plant-structures audibly drinking, a faint creaking across the whole settlement like a ship settling in a harbor.

There is no river. There is no stream. The water is groundwater and rain and condensation, and it moves through the living architecture as much as across the ground. You can see it traveling — slow trickles along grooved bark-channels, dripping from canopy edges into shallow cisterns that were once concrete basements and are now smooth-walled root-cavities. It has a faint sweetness. It is clean.

---

**The ecology.** The plants are the ecosystem. A dozen engineered species — most no longer identifiable, cross-pollinated over generations into something new — performing every function. The canopy-species filters light and regulates temperature. The column-species bears weight. A creeping ground-cover, thick but not tall, carpets the open spaces between structures, its roots forming a living subfloor that is somehow always just above the damp. Fungal networks thread everything together underground, communicating — or at least distributing nutrients — in ways that no one in the town fully understands anymore.

Fauna is almost absent. You might see a small bird in the canopy, once. Insects are few — a few species of moth, some beetles that live in the bark. The ecosystem is not hostile to animal life; it simply doesn't require it. Pollination is handled by wind through the designed channels in the canopy. Decomposition is handled by fungi. The place runs on photosynthesis and hydraulics.

There is a small mammal — a vole-like creature, pale, sightless, living in the root networks. If you stand very still in one of the quieter corridors, you can hear it moving beneath your feet: a faint rustling, a soft tapping of small teeth on root-fiber. It is the only fauna that has truly adapted. It belongs to the place now.

---

**The people.** Still here. A few hundred, perhaps. They live in the upper stories of the plant-structures, in rooms that have been shaped — by selective pruning, by directed growth, by patient years of guidance — into something resembling domestic space. The walls are alive but they are not growing into the rooms; that is maintained. Doors are gaps in the root-matter, sometimes hinged with metal, sometimes simply gaps where a branch was cut and didn't regrow. The floors are dense living wood, slightly springy underfoot, warm even in the cool air because of the metabolic heat of the root network below.

They still farm the root-system. They tap it for sap, for hydraulic pressure, for building material when new rooms need to be grown. They maintain the machines — the ones that still run — not out of necessity exactly, but because the machines are part of the network, and the network is part of the town, and the town is part of the plants, and the plants are the town. The distinction between infrastructure and organism has dissolved. They would not describe themselves as living in a forest. They would say they live in their town.

They are quiet people. The town itself enforces a kind of quiet — the canopy dampens sound, the living walls absorb vibration, and the cultural memory of the machines teaches you to speak low and move with care. Conversations happen in murmurs. The loudest sound on a typical day is the rain, when it comes.

---

**The edges.** The boundary of the settlement is not a wall. It is a thinning. The plant-structures gradually lose their guided form — the columns lean, the canopy gaps, the ground-cover gives way to ordinary soil. You can feel yourself leaving. The engineered order gives way to something wilder and less legible, and within a hundred meters of the edge, you are in normal countryside — fields, scrub, the kind of low-elevation terrain you'd expect. Behind you, the settlement breathes and creaks and hums, green and cool and almost empty of sound, a place where the built and the grown have forgotten which is which.

The odd thing — the one detail that doesn't resolve — is the pattern. If you look at the settlement from above (and the canopy is sparse enough in places to allow this), the layout of the plant-structures follows a geometry that was not in any original town plan. The original plan is still visible in places — a grid, simple, industrial. But the living architecture has deviated. It curves. It spirals slightly. The rooms and corridors have arranged themselves into a form that suggests something intentional but is not any intention anyone admits to. The plants are building something the townspeople did not ask for. They know this. They do not discuss it. They continue to maintain what they can and let the rest grow where it will.

It has been growing for a long time. It is in balance. It will continue.
