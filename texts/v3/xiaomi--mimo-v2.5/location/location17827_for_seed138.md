# The Vatworks at Shallow

The town sits on land that has no right to exist yet. The ground is only decades old — flat, compacted ash andite andite and mineral sludge, deposited when the basin first began to weep. You can still see the sharpness of it: edges of stone that haven't been kissed by weather long enough to round off, gravel beds with grains still angular enough to cut skin, embankments that hold their slope at the exact angle of their settling and nothing less. Nothing here has softened. Nothing has earned a patina.

Above this young ground, steam.

Always steam. The basin floor is wet in a way that goes beyond weather — the water table sits a finger's width below the surface, and in the heat it rises. It rises through cracks in the substrate, through the foundations of buildings, through the grates set into every road. By midmorning the air is thick with condensation that never quite becomes rain. It hangs. It beads on surfaces. The atmosphere itself feels like it has weight, like you are wading through the lower inch of a warm bath. Your clothes never dry. Your skin never cools. The steam and the smoke from the factories merge at a certain height and form a ceiling — a low, grey-brown lid over the town that turns the sky into an accusation of light. Noon looks like dusk. The sun is a rumor, a bright smear behind the pall that gives heat without clarity. Shadows exist but are soft, directionless, more like areas of slightly less obscurity than actual shadows.

The heat is the first thing anyone mentions and the last thing anyone forgets. It is not the dry heat of desert or the oppressive heat of jungle. It is a wet heat, a *soaking* heat, heat that carries moisture into your lungs with every breath. The town is roughly the size of a small hamlet — you could walk across it in fifteen minutes if you didn't stop — but it is not cramped. That is the strange mercy of it. The buildings are spread out with an industrial logic that has nothing to do with human comfort and everything to do with process flow. There are gaps between structures — not gardens, not yards, but *intervals*, the empty spaces that machinery needs to breathe, that pipes need to route, that heat needs to dissipate (though it never dissipates enough). A single iron-pipe street runs the length of the settlement, branching into smaller access roads that lead to individual structures. The spaces between are filled with gravel and hardpan and the occasional puddle of mineral-bright water that will not evaporate because the air is already saturated.

The buildings are functional in a way that predates architecture and supersedes it. They are low, broad, mostly single-story with high ceilings to let the worst of the heat climb. Corrugated metal walls, riveted steel frames, concrete slab foundations poured directly onto the young earth. Many have open sides — or rather, sides that were designed to be open, fitted with louvers or screens that have been bent and corroded into permanence. Nothing is pristine. Everything works. The metal is dark with oxidation and condensation in equal measure, streaked with mineral deposits where water has run and dried and run again in exactly the same paths, leaving pale calcite veins on rust-brown surfaces. The pipes are the connective tissue of the place — they run above ground on trestles, along the ground on sleepers, and into the ground through maintenance pits. They carry steam, brine, slurry, waste heat. They hiss. They tick with thermal expansion. They are wrapped in insulation that is wrapped in sheet metal that is wrapped in condensation.

The sound is constant and layered. The base frequency is the deep, arrhythmic chugging of steam engines — there are a dozen of them, scattered through the buildings, each driving something: a pump, a press, a conveyor, a mill. They do not synchronize. They produce a rumble that is not quite mechanical and not quite geological, something closer to the sound of a large animal breathing in its sleep. Above that, the hiss of steam at every joint and valve and intentional vent. The clatter of metal on metal from wherever the presses are working — rhythmic when a run is steady, arrhythmic when a new piece is being set. The gurgle of water in open channels that run alongside every road, carrying runoff and condensation and waste back to the basin floor where it soaks in or sits. And beneath all of this, the fauna.

The fauna is the thing that surprises people. For a place this hot, this wet, this industrial, there is an astonishing amount of life. The insects are the first layer — enormous, heavy-bodied things with iridescent shells that thrive in the steam and the heat, clustering around the warm surfaces of pipes and machine housings, filling the air in slow, droning clouds that part when you walk through them and close again behind you. Their sound is constant, a high electric whine that sits just above the mechanical noise. Then the amphibians — fat, pale creatures that live in the gravel beds and drainage channels, feeding on the insects, calling to each other in low, almost subsonic pulses that you feel in your chest more than hear. Birds, too, though fewer — small, dark, quick things that nest in the rafters of the open buildings and feed on the insect swarms, darting through the steam like scraps of burnt paper. In the wettest margins, where the young ground meets standing water, there are mats of algae and cyanobacteria, bright green and rust-red, forming a kind of micro-ecology that supports everything else. The sparse vegetation — a few hardy grasses, the occasional pioneering shrub — grows in cracks in concrete and along the edges of access roads, always near a water source, always slightly yellowed by the mineral content of the water.

The strangest thing about the Vatworks is its permanence. For a place that shouldn't have existed long enough to erode, for a place built on land that is practically embryonic, everything is locked in. The machinery runs at the same speed it has always run. The pipes carry what they have always carried. The buildings settle but do not collapse, corrode but do not fail, leak but do not flood. No one can remember the town being built and no one can imagine it being abandoned. The work that is done here — the pressing, the refining, the extraction of mineral value from the basin's weeping earth — continues at a rate that matches the earth's own patient output. It is not a boomtown. It is not a dying town. It is a town that arrived at its equilibrium the moment it formed and has simply continued. The workers move through the steam and the heat with the unhurried pace of people who know that nothing will change whether they hurry or not. The fauna has adapted to the machinery and the machinery has adapted to the fauna. The water rises and the machines drink it and the waste returns to the ground and the ground gives it back. It is a closed loop, a small and wet and scalding world, humming to itself in the dim light under its own self-made sky.

Nothing changes here. The ground is new and the town is old and both of these things are true at the same time and neither is going anywhere.
