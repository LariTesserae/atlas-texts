# The Vauld of Keening

There is no surface here. There has never been a surface. The place is a cavity — a hollow worked into the deep basalt and mantle-stone of the world's floor, sealed imperfectly, perpetually pressurized, perpetually filled. Warm water occupies every cubic meter of the space, and the water never rests.

The chamber is small. If you could stand at its edge — which you cannot, because there is no edge unclaimed by machinery — you could probably throw a stone and hear it strike the far wall within a few seconds. Hundreds of meters across, perhaps. No more. But it feels infinite because almost nothing occupies it. The structures are sparse: towers and platforms and massive housings for engines that no longer have names for their purposes, rising from the floor or descending from the ceiling like stalactites in a cave made by hands, then abandoned by meaning. Between them, vast emptiness. Water. Just water, lit by no sky, carrying no silt, warm as blood.

---

The warmth is the first thing that would tell you this place is wrong.

Not geothermal — or not *only* geothermal. The machinery contributes. What machinery remains running — and much of it still runs, still turns, still grinds and hammers and drives — generates heat that the water cannot dissipate because the water has nowhere to go. The cavity is sealed. The heat builds and circles and builds again. The walls themselves are warm to whatever touch could reach them. The water temperature sits at something that would be comfortable for swimming if swimming were possible here, if the noise wouldn't kill you first.

The noise.

It is not loud in the way a storm is loud or a waterfall is loud. Those are broad, washed-out sounds, white with chaos. This is *specific*. The noise has shape and intention even though it has long since lost its audience. The engines — great piston-driven assemblies, centrifugal pumps the size of houses, turbines fitted with blades that scrape their housings by millimeters — produce a layered, rhythmic, *architectural* sound. It is the sound of a cathedral organ played by a machine that does not understand music but understands repetition absolutely. The lower frequencies are felt in the skeleton before they are heard: a thrumming that makes the water itself pulse with pressure waves. Above that, mid-range grinding, metal-on-metal at tolerances that were once precise and have since warped into something percussive and arrhythmic. Above *that*, a keening — hence the name, if anyone still uses names here — a whine at the threshold of pain, produced by something spinning too fast in a medium that cannot cool it.

You would go deaf in minutes. Then you would go mad. Then you would stop noticing either.

---

The walls and structures are built of something that resists the deep ocean's patient violence. Not stone, though there is stone — the original basalt of the cavity, ancient, colonized by no organism, stained by mineral deposits into patterns of rust and pale calcium. The built elements are metal: iron and stranger alloys, layered in the way of sedimentary rock but with human intention. Decades of construction have left strata. You can read the history of this place in cross-section where pipes have corroded through and exposed their interiors: early work, crude and overbuilt; middle work, more elegant, almost architectural in its care; late work, hurried, utilitarian, patched with whatever was at hand. Everything is covered in a fine patina of mineral growth — not biological, just chemistry, the slow marriage of warm saltwater and exposed metal. Crystalline deposits catch the dim light and glitter like something alive.

The light is dim and amber. It comes from no sun. Bioluminescence would be too generous a word — there is almost no biology here. Instead, certain of the machines produce light as a byproduct: heated elements behind thick glass portholes, electrical arcing from damaged conduits that someone once tried to seal and failed, and a pervasive phosphorescence in the water itself, a faint chemical glow, perhaps from the same processes that keep the temperature elevated. The effect is of a perpetual dusk, a sunset frozen in its amber moment and held there for centuries. Shadows are long and soft. The far reaches of the chamber dissolve into murk, and beyond the murk, into darkness that no light has ever touched.

---

There is almost nothing living here. The water is too mineral-heavy, too warm, too turbulent for most deep-sea organisms. The structures are too unstable for colonial growth. But *almost* is not *nothing*.

In the joints of machinery, in the spaces where pipes meet walls, in the crevices of corroded housings — there are organisms. Small. Translucent. They do not photosynthesize because there is nothing to photosynthesize. They feed on the mineral precipitates, on the thin films of bacterial mats that somehow persist in the warm water, on the electrical charge that bleeds from every damaged surface. They are not animals in any recognizable sense. They have no symmetry. They cling to surfaces and extend filaments into the current, and they catch particles the way a filter-feeder catches plankton, except the particles are metal dust and crystallized salt. They are rare. You could search for an hour and find none, and then round a corner and see a dozen of them clustered on a valve housing, their filaments waving in the turbulence like sea anemones in a tide pool, except they are not sea anemones and this is not a tide pool and there is no tide.

They are the only softness in this place. Everything else is metal, stone, water, heat, and noise.

---

The turbulence is constant. It is not the turbulence of weather — there is no weather here, no atmosphere, no sky — but of *process*. The engines move water. The turbines move water. The piston assemblies displace volume with every stroke, and in a sealed cavity, displacement has nowhere to go except *through*. Currents collide. Vortices form behind structural elements and persist for minutes before being torn apart by newer currents. The water does not flow in any direction. It *churns*. Standing at any point in the chamber, you would feel water moving past you from three or four directions simultaneously, warm and heavy, carrying the faint metallic taste of dissolved minerals and the faint electric charge of the machinery that saturates everything.

Sometimes — and this is where the place earns its slight weirdness — the turbulence *organizes*. Not completely. Not in any way that suggests intelligence or design. But occasionally, for a span of seconds or minutes, the currents align. They move together, all of them, in a single direction, as if something very large and very deep had drawn a slow breath. The water surges toward one wall, then releases, then surges again. The machinery does not cause this. The machinery is too small, too scattered. The turbulence comes from outside the chamber, from the deeper ocean beyond the walls, from pressures and currents that the engineers who built this place did not account for because they did not know they existed. During these surges, the filaments of the strange organisms extend further, as if feeding. Then the alignment breaks and chaos resumes.

No one has measured the interval between surges. No one remains who could.

---

What was this place?

The layered construction suggests it was built over a long period — centuries, perhaps — by a civilization that understood deep-ocean engineering intimately enough to seal a cavity in basalt, equip it with industrial machinery, and maintain it across generations. The scale suggests it was never a city in the open sense — never a place of homes and streets and gathering. It was a *function*. A factory, or a pump, or a processor. Something that needed to be deep, warm, enclosed, and loud. Something that needed to move water, or heat it, or pressure it, or filter something from it. The sparse arrangement of structures — no walls dividing rooms, no corridors, just isolated towers and housings scattered across the void — suggests that the water was the medium of work, that the machines were not meant to be accessed by people but to operate autonomously, maintained only periodically by visitors from a surface that no longer sends them.

Or the surface still exists, and simply does not care.

The place persists. It was built to endure, and it endures. The engines run. The turbines spin. The water churns. The rare organisms cling. The dim amber light glows. The surges come and go on their own unknowable schedule. Everything is in its own state, settled into the particular equilibrium of deep, warm, deafening emptiness. It is not dying. It is not growing. It is *operating* — continuing to perform its function past the point where anyone remembers what that function was, in a sealed room at the bottom of a world that has forgotten it is there.

You could listen at the walls and hear it working. The keening. The thrumming. The percussion of metal and water in a chamber too small for the ocean and too large for anything human. If you pressed your hand to the warm basalt, you would feel it in your bones.

The Vauld does not need you. The Vauld does not need anyone. The Vauld needs only power and pressure and time, and it has all three, in abundance, and it will continue long after the last organism in its crevices has clung to its last valve housing and released its last filament into the churning, amber, deafening dark.
