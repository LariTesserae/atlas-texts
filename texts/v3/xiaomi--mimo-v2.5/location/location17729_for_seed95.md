# The Flats of Yss

The first thing you notice, standing at the edge of the broken approach, is that it doesn't look like height. The ancient erosion has done its work so thoroughly over so long that the high country reads as horizon — a plane that simply *continues*, with only the faintest suggestion that you are above the world rather than beside it. Everything has been rounded. Everything has been softened. The ridgelines that remain are not sharp but blunt, like the knuckles of a buried hand.

The rock is old. Not old in the way that mountains are old — old in the way that *time itself* seems to have pooled here and evaporated, leaving behind mineral residue. The exposed stone across the flats is a deep rust-amber, striated with veins of chalky white where ancient water once cut channels that have long since sealed shut. In places the surface has a glassy quality, as though immense heat or pressure once fused the top layer, and that glaze persists even now — warm underfoot, faintly reflective in certain angles of light. You can see your own shadow doubled in the stone.

And it is warm. Not hot. The warmth here feels ambient, almost metabolic, as though the plateau itself exhales low heat. It settles on the skin and doesn't leave. The air carries moisture intermittently — not rain, exactly, but a condensation that forms on everything in the early hours, a dew that tastes faintly mineral, almost metallic. By midday it lifts. By evening it gathers again. There are no rivers, but in the shallow basins where the erosion pooled millennia ago, water sits in thin, still sheets — barely an inch deep, perfectly clear, lasting hours before evaporating or sinking back into the porous stone.

---

**The silence is wrong.**

This is the second thing, and it takes longer to register because you might spend the first hour telling yourself it's simply quiet. It isn't. The fauna density here is extraordinary — perhaps the highest concentration of animal life you will ever witness in a single place — and yet the volume of the landscape is barely above a whisper.

They are *everywhere.*

The lizards first. Dozens of species, though most are variants on a single ancestral form — squat, broad-bodied, with skin the color of the stone they press against. They cluster in groups of twenty, thirty, sixty. Their bellies are pressed to the warm glass-surface of the rock and they do not move when you pass. Their breathing is visible as a faint pulse in the flanks but makes no audible sound. Some of them have frills or crests, elaborate calcium deposits that fan out behind the head like dried flowers, and these are the strangest thing about them: they are not keratin or bone but something closer to coral — porous, delicate, faintly iridescent. You could, if you broke one free (and you would not want to), hold it to your ear and hear the faint whistle of air through its chambers. But the lizards themselves produce nothing.

Overhead, the birds — if they are birds — move in slow, deliberate patterns. They do not flock in the chaotic bursts you might expect from creatures this numerous. They orbit. They trace wide arcs across the open sky, sometimes dozens in a column, sometimes a single pale shape hanging motionless above a particular basin. Their wings do not beat in the way wings should. The motion is more like breathing — an expansion and contraction that barely disturbs the air. Their calls, when they come at all, are subsonic: you feel them in your sternum rather than hearing them. A pressure. A vibration that arrives and departs without crossing into anything the ear would recognize as sound.

The insects are the densest layer. They fill the air not as swarms but as *atmosphere* — a fine particulate presence, so numerous and so small that they are almost a texture of the air itself. You walk through them. They land on your skin and you feel only the faintest tickle. Their wings, when they beat, produce a frequency below your hearing. The overall effect is of a world that is teeming, layered, almost *saturated* with biological presence — and yet muffled. Wrapped in cotton. As though the density of life here has produced its own soundproofing, its own acoustic gravity, pulling all vibration inward and holding it.

---

There are things moving beneath the stone.

You can see the evidence in the shallow basins. The thin water shifts in patterns that have no wind-driven cause — smooth, circular ripples expanding from points where nothing breaks the surface. In the dried channels, the crust of mineral deposit has been pushed upward in long, slow undulations, like something vast is pressing against the underside of the world and the world is barely yielding. The dynamics here are slow. These movements happen over hours. You would have to sit in one place all day, all night, to see them fully — and some visitors do. Some visitors sit.

The strange quality of this place — the *wrongness* that you carry away if you leave — is not any single oddity. It is the conjunction. It is the overwhelming abundance of life paired with the almost-total silence. It is the ancient, final erosion — the place that has been worn to its essential form, its last meaningful shape — combined with the ceaseless, patient biological activity that fills it to the brim. The plateau has been finished by geology. The life here has not been told.

---

The remains are scattered and ambiguous.

In certain basins, half-submerged in the thin water or fused into the mineral crust, there are structures. Or fragments of structures. Low walls, barely rising above the stone surface, composed of the same material as the rock itself — same color, same grain — but with edges too regular to be natural. They might be natural. You could argue they are. The erosion patterns of deep time can produce startling regularity. But the walls, where they form enclosures, form *enclosures.* And in one basin toward the eastern edge of the flats, the enclosures form a pattern: concentric rings, diminishing in diameter, with a central depression that holds water even when the surrounding basins have dried.

Nothing built these with tools. There is no evidence of tools. The marks on the stone are not tool marks — they are the marks of growth. Of accretion. The walls look less like something constructed and more like something that *formed,* the way a coral reef forms, the way a termite mound forms — but at the speed of stone. At the speed of geology. Something biological and something geological worked together here over an immense span of time, and what they produced is a set of structures that serve a purpose you cannot determine and may never determine.

The fauna avoid the central basin. Not dramatically. Not with any visible fear. They simply route around it, the way water routes around a stone. The insects thin above it. The lizards press themselves flat on the far rim and do not look toward the center.

---

The light on the Flats of Yss is ordinary and that is part of the strangeness. No twilight persists longer than it should. No shadows fall in wrong directions. The sun crosses a normal sky and the sky is the color a sky should be. This is a landscape under plain light doing something that plain light should make visible and legible, and it doesn't. The clarity doesn't help. You see everything and understand nothing.

In the late afternoon, when the warmth has soaked fully into the stone and the condensation is beginning to gather on the cooler edges of the basins, there is a moment — brief, maybe fifteen minutes — when the glass-surface of the rock catches the low sun and the entire plateau turns the color of a deep bruise: purple-black and amber, with veins of white running through it like lightning frozen in the moment of striking. The lizards lift their heads. The birds slow their arcs. The subsonic pressure in your chest deepens for just a moment.

Then the sun drops further and the light goes normal again and the Flats continue as they have continued for longer than any name can reach — warm, damp, teeming, silent, finished and unfinished simultaneously, patient beyond any definition of patience, dense with a life that has learned to fill every volume without raising its voice.
