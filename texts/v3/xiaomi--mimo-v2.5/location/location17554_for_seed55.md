# The Drowned Platforms

There is no surface. There has not been a surface in longer than anyone remembers — if anyone ever did remember, which is doubtful, because there is almost nothing here that remembers.

What there is, in every direction, is the architecture.

It goes down and it goes out and it does not stop. Platform after platform, each one barely distinguishable from the last, separated by shallow steps or channels or grooves worn so smooth by time that they might be natural if they were not so precise, so repeating, so deliberate. The platforms are low. The whole world is low. There is no mountain here, no ridge, no meaningful horizon in any direction — only the next shelf, and the next, and the next, each one perhaps a body-length lower than the one behind it, descending in increments so gradual that to swim from one to the next feels like swimming nowhere at all.

The material is stone. Not dressed stone — not any longer. Whatever edges or faces or tool-marks once existed have been consumed by the long patient work of water and growth and silence. What remains is stone that has become itself again, rounded and pitted and softened, but still recognizably shaped. Still flat on top where it was meant to be flat. Still straight along edges that were meant to be straight. The channels between platforms still run in lines — not perfectly straight, not with any engineered precision that survives, but with a *directionality* that no natural formation holds for this long over this scale. You can swim for hours in a single direction along a channel and never once turn, and that is not something the sea does on its own.

The scale is incomprehensible. Not in the way a canyon is incomprehensible or a mountain range is incomprehensible, where there is a feature to be overwhelmed by. There is no feature here. There is only *extent*. The platforms stretch outward beyond what any light could reach, beyond what any mind could hold. In some directions they go so far that the water itself seems to change character — denser, older, less likely to move — as though the distance has become a kind of substance. The darkness out there is not empty. It is *full* of architecture, stacked and spread and layered, all of it at the same low elevation, all of it slowly sinking into its own silt.

And everywhere, on every surface that is not too current-swept to hold it, there is growth.

Not animals. Almost nothing moves here with intent. No fish flicker between the platforms. No crustaceans click in the grooves. The world is nearly empty of fauna — a few tiny, soft, translucent things that drift like debris and might be alive, might be merely shaped like life, and in either case contribute nothing to the feeling of the place except a faint sense that the boundary between living and not-living has become as blurred as everything else.

But the vegetation — the *vegetation* is something else entirely.

It is thick. It is everywhere. It grows in every direction the water allows, which in this place is nearly every direction, and it has had so long to grow that it has become the place. Great curtains of it hang from the edges of platforms like the fringes of something being unraveled. Mats of it lie across the flat surfaces, dense and layered, new growth atop old growth atop older growth, centuries or millennia of it compressed into mats that might be an arm's length thick and still look like a single surface from the outside. Strands of it drift upward from the mats into the dark water above, rising slowly, questing toward a light that does not exist, and finding nothing, and continuing to grow anyway because growth is what it does and it has long since stopped requiring a reason.

The vegetation is in no hurry. Everything here is in no hurry. The temperature is barely above freezing — cold water, cold stone, cold growth, all of it metabolic and deliberate, all of it moving on timescales that make geology look hasty. A mat that looks like a single season's growth might represent a century. A curtain of fronds that appears to sway with a current might be responding to a change in flow that began before the last time the platforms shifted, which was itself so long ago that the shift was not a shift at all but a settling, a final settling, the last adjustment the architecture would ever make to the weight of the water on top of it.

And the water *is* on top. It presses down from above with a heaviness that this place has internalized so completely that it no longer seems like pressure. It seems like gravity. The platforms were built low — were *made* low, deliberately, by hands that understood low, hands that worked with simple tools, stone on stone, hand-fitted, hand-shaped, hand-carried into position on a scale that should have been impossible for hands but was accomplished anyway through patience and time and the willingness of many generations to do the same work their parents did, on the same platforms their parents built, adding the next course, the next tier, the next channel, always at the same low elevation, always spreading outward rather than upward, as though the builders feared height or mistrusted it or simply could not conceive of a world that went up when it could go out.

They built it all at roughly the same level. That is the thing. Across the entire scope of the place — across distances that would take a fast swimmer days to cross — the architecture is *level*. Not perfectly level. There are undulations and sags and places where the foundation settled unevenly and the platforms tilt by a degree or two. But the ambition of levelness is everywhere, and the achievement of it, over such a vast area, with nothing but hand-tools and simple craft and the kind of organizational will that must have once animated a civilization, is the most impressive and the most melancholy thing about this place. Someone wanted a world that was flat. Someone spent lifetimes making it flat. And then the water came, or the builders left, or both, and the flat world sat in the dark and grew its patient gardens and forgot what it was for.

The quiet is the loudest thing here.

Not silence — there is no true silence in water. There is the faint, subsonic groan of the ocean itself, the sound of a body of water so vast that it has its own slow pulse. There is the occasional creak of stone settling, a sound so low and so soft that it is felt more than heard, a vibration that passes through the cold water and through the vegetation and through whatever else might be here and resolves into nothing. There is the soft, almost frictionless whisper of water moving through the channels — not a current, exactly, but a drift, a barely perceptible flow that has been following the same path for so long that the channels are worn slightly deeper on one side than the other.

But nothing calls. Nothing sings or chatters or groans or roars. The water absorbs sound and carries it away and gives back only the deepest frequencies, and at those frequencies there is almost nothing happening. The place is quiet the way a tomb is quiet, except it is not a tomb. It is not dead. The vegetation sees to that. The vegetation is alive and growing and utterly indifferent to the architecture it covers, and its indifference is a kind of life that does not require witnesses, does not require an audience, does not require light.

In the black water, in the utter absence of any source of illumination, the vegetation grows by chemistry — by the slow breakdown of stone, by the chemistry of the cold, by whatever minimal energy the deep water provides. It does not photosynthesize. It does not need to. It has found other ways, the way everything in this place has found other ways, the way the architecture found a way to endure and the water found a way to fill every space and the darkness found a way to be not quite empty.

If you were here — if something could be here, which is uncertain, because there is nothing that wants to be here — you would feel the cold as an absolute. Not sharp, not biting, just *present*, the way the water is present, the way the darkness is present, the way the platforms stretching out in every direction are present. You would feel the slight, constant pressure of the water above and around you. You would see nothing, or almost nothing — perhaps a faint luminescence in some of the vegetation, a pale chemical glow that does not illuminate so much as *suggest*, marking shapes in the dark that resolve into more shapes that resolve into more shapes until you understand that the architecture is not just under you but *around* you, above you, to every side, that the platforms are not all at one level, that there are tiers and sub-tiers and buried layers of older construction beneath the ones you can touch, and beneath those, more, and beneath those, more, the whole thing built and rebuilt and expanded over generations until the original purpose is buried under its own elaboration and all that remains is the impulse to build, the hand-fitted stone, the flat surface, the channel, the step, the line extending outward into the dark.

The water was always going to come. The builders might have known that. They built low enough that they probably knew. And they built anyway, or they built *because* of it, because the act of building was the thing, because the flatness was the thing, because in a world of chaos and current and depth the only sane response was to make a surface — to insist on a surface — and to make it so vast and so level and so enduring that even the ocean, given enough time, would learn to respect it.

The ocean did not learn to respect it. But it did, in its way, preserve it. The water is cold and still and heavy and it has held this place in place for longer than the memory of whatever made it. The vegetation has covered it, claimed it, made it into something the builders would not recognize, but the bones are still there. The platforms. The channels. The flat, flat, endless flat, stretching out into the dark like a sentence that was never finished, written in a language that no one speaks, in a world that has forgotten it was made.
