# The Still-Making

You are underground, but not underground in the way of caves. There are no ceilings. There are no walls. The space simply *goes* — a basin of raw, warm stone stretching in every direction as far as light allows, and light here allows quite a lot, because the rock itself glows. Not brightly. Not like a lamp. More like the way heated iron holds its color — a sullen amber luminance that bleeds from every surface, as though the ground is still cooling from something enormous and recent. You can see for kilometers. The land folds and buckles in low ridges and shallow troughs, nothing higher than a single story, nothing lower than a wading pool. It is warm the way a body is warm. It breathes. The ground is not quite finished. Mineral ridges curl upward like unopened ferns, and some of them are still *moving* — impossibly slowly, a centimeter per day, depositing themselves into existence through some pressure that has no name. Sheets ofite calcium hang like wet curtains from formations that look like they were going to become stalactites but changed their minds mid-gesture. The geology here has opinions. It resists coherence.

The water comes without warning. It seeps up through pores in the stone, collects in depressions that didn't exist an hour ago, and vanishes again into cracks that open and close like slow mouths. Some of the pools are warm — almost hot — mineral-bitter and the color of weak tea. Others are startlingly cold, fed by something deeper. Steam rises in ragged columns from the contact points where hot water meets cooler stone, and the whole basin smells of sulfur and wet iron and something underneath those two that is almost, but not quite, alive.

Nothing grows here in any way a botanist would recognize. But there are *structures*. Thick, waxy crusts of mineral-fungal hybrid spread across the stone in pale, irregular patches — somewhere between a lichen and a salt deposit, eating chemical energy from the rock, leaving behind a residue that crunches underfoot like thin ice. They pulse faintly. Not visibly — you feel it in your soles, a rhythmic vibration, as though the crusts have a slow heartbeat synchronized with whatever geological engine drives this place. In the wetter areas, the crusts grow tall — columns and shelves and arched ribs of the stuff, some reaching waist height, their surfaces slick and faintly iridescent. They smell of chalk and yeast. They are the closest thing to a forest this world has made.

And then there is the fauna.

God, the fauna.

It is *everywhere*. Insects — if you can call them insects — the size of your hand, armored in mineral shells that match the rock so perfectly they're invisible until they move. They move constantly. They are everywhere you step, everywhere you look, in the water, in the cracks, on the crusts, in the air in buzzing, whirring clouds that rise and fall like weather. Larger things, too. Eyeless, pale creatures with too many legs navigating by vibration, by chemical signature, by some sense that reads the ground itself. They range from centipedes the length of a forearm to squat, toad-shaped things that sit motionless in the warm pools, their frilled gill-folds fluttering. Some of them are luminous — blue-green, pale violet — and in the low moments between geological groans, you can see their lights moving through the basin like a scattered constellation viewed from the wrong direction. They are dense. Every cubic meter of air contains something flying. Every square meter of ground contains something crawling. The sound of them is constant — a layered, chittering, clicking, humming roar that rises and falls but never stops. It is the sound of a city at rush hour, translated into exoskeleton and mandible.

Among them, larger shapes move. Things with the mass of a large dog, armored and slow, browsing on the fungal crusts the way cattle graze grass. Things that are long and sinuous, navigating the warm pools with eel-like undulation, disturbing clouds of smaller creatures as they pass. Things overhead — gliding things, leathery, with wingspans that seem too generous for this depth. They skim the air in wide arcs, dropping into the clouds of flying insects and emerging with mandibles full. The basin churns with predation and avoidance, an ecosystem running at a furious tempo, all of it built on mineral and chemistry rather than sunlight.

And there are marks. Scattered across the warm stone, in places where the ground has been stable long enough to hold a shape, you find them — simple, crude. A line of stones arranged in a rough arc, half-buried by new mineral deposit but still legible. A hollowed-out depression in a rock shelf, worn smooth by sitting. Charred patches on stone where fires once burned — small ones, quick ones, the kind you make when you don't want to be seen. A pit, carefully dug, its walls lined with flat stones, still holding a cupful of the mineral water. Whoever made these things had hands and patience and very little material to work with. They shaped what the geology offered them and nothing more. No metal. No complex joinery. Just the most elemental acts of habitation — a place to sit, a place to store, a place to see from. The structures are half-swallowed by the place already. The mineral crusts grow over them. New stone deposits fill the fire-pits. The basin is generous with creation and indifferent to what was made within it.

The turbulence here is not weather. It is *the earth itself* — groaning, shifting, rearranging. A new ridge will thrust itself upward over the course of an afternoon, cracking through a fungal crust, displacing pools, sending the fauna scattering in waves of clicking panic. A pool will drain in minutes, its water swallowed by a new fissure, leaving behind a steaming, mineral-crusted bowl that something immediately colonizes. The sound of rock under stress — deep, resonant, almost subsonic — rolls through the basin in pulses, and when it comes, everything pauses. The insects dim their noise. The larger creatures go still. Then it passes, and the frenzy resumes, and you understand that this place is not settled. It is not old. It is *becoming*, continuously, and what you are standing on might not exist in a year — will be something else, somewhere else, shaped by pressures that have no interest in permanence.

You are warm. You are dry, despite the intermittent water — the heat wicks moisture from your skin as fast as it arrives. The air smells of minerals and life and the deep, clean scent of stone still forming. The noise is immense and constant. The ground beneath your feet is not finished. Nothing here is finished. You are standing in the middle of a world that is being made in real time, by forces that do not know they are making anything at all, and within it — between the growing ridges and the steaming pools, among the billions of clicking, crawling, gliding, pulsing things — there are traces of someone who sat here once and decided, for reasons the basin will never understand, to stack a few stones in a line.
