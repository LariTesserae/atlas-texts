# The Mireworks

The town sits in a shallow basin that never dries. You can see its edge from half a kilometer away — a dark mass of green-black canopy rising barely two stories against the flat, waterlogged plain — but you can't see any of the structures inside it. Nothing that a visitor from a drier country would call a building is visible. There are no rooflines, no gables, no brick facades. The whole settlement looks, from outside, like a single enormous copse that has grown too tall, too dense, too deliberately.

Then the smell reaches you. Warm, wet, vegetal. Not rot — that's important — but the hot, sweet-green smell of massive photosynthesis. Of transpiration on a civic scale. Of things growing so fast you can almost hear the cells dividing. Underneath it, something mineral and metallic. Iron. Copper sulfate. The smell of industry, but softened, swaddled in green.

## The Structures

The buildings are *grown*. This is not a metaphor or a quaint local tradition. The load-bearing walls are living hardwood — oak, mostly, though some older structures in the town center are built from a tighter-grained species that the locals simply call *pillarwood*, which doesn't occur naturally anywhere else. The pillars are planted as saplings and guided over years into the shapes required: arches, lintels, partition walls. Growth-rings record the seasons. Some of the oldest structures in the quarter have walls thirty centimeters thick, and the rings are so tight you need a lens to count them — decades of patient, guided living compression.

Between the structural members, everything is filled. Not with plaster or stone, but with layered growth: dense mats of moss and liverwort packed into cavities and allowed to root into a living, self-maintaining insulation. Vines trained into grid patterns form the equivalent of lath and plaster. In many buildings the distinction between wall and plant has been erased entirely — a wall might be a living hedge, trimmed on the inside to maintain a flat surface, allowed to grow wild on the outside where it merges with the canopy.

Roofs are almost universally canopied. In the older quarters, enormous broadleaf specimens — something like a tropical fig, though the leaves are waxy and thick as leather — are planted at the building corners and trained overhead. Their aerial roots drop down and thicken into secondary supports. In newer construction (which here means decades old, not years; the town changes on a slow clock), the roofs are engineered mats of interwoven bamboo and a fast-growing creeper that the inhabitants cultivate in scaffold-frameworks and then lower into place once the mat is thick enough to shed water.

The effect from inside is perpetual twilight. Even at midday, light filters through four or five layers of translucent green. The interiors glow with it — a warm, diffuse chartreuse that makes every room feel like the inside of a leaf. People who live here their whole lives have slightly greenish-white skin, though whether this is from the filtered light or from something in the water they drink is a matter of local debate.

## The Water

The basin collects everything. Rain falls on the plain and flows inward through a network of drainage channels — some natural, some clearly cut and maintained — that converge on the town like veins. The water doesn't pass through; it saturates. The water table sits half a meter below the surface almost everywhere. Step off the maintained paths and your feet sink. The ground is spongy, warm, a mix of decomposed plant matter and clay that has been wet so long it has lost all memory of being solid.

Inside the town, the water is managed but never fully controlled. Every structure sits on a raised platform of packed root-mat and stone rubble — just enough to keep the floor above the standing waterline. Between buildings, the ground is a maze of shallow channels, some covered with woven-grate walkways, others open, steaming faintly in the warm air. The channels carry runoff but also serve as the town's waste system, its coolant infrastructure, and, in the hot months, its only real relief from humidity. People sit on their doorsteps with their feet in the slow-moving flow and call it comfort.

The condensation is relentless. On cooler mornings — which are rare; the basin holds warmth the way it holds water — every surface weeps. The hardwood walls bead with moisture. Metal fittings drip. The air itself feels like a substance, thick and warm and barely distinguishable from a slow exhalation. Visitors from drier places describe a persistent feeling of being gently submerged.

## The Industry

This is not a pastoral village. It is loud.

The town's economy runs on plant-matter processing. The surrounding plain, though waterlogged, is enormously productive — the same warmth and moisture that makes the basin uncomfortable makes the surrounding flats into one of the most fertile growing regions anyone has mapped. Crops come in, are processed, and go out. The factories sit at the town's edges where the canopy thins enough to vent their heat.

The primary industry is fiber extraction. Pillarwood and bamboo are harvested from managed stands — the town maintains extensive plantations on the drier fringes of the basin — and broken down in a sequence of soaking, steaming, beating, and pressing. The machines are heavy, iron-framed, belt-driven, and ancient in design if not in manufacture. They run on a combination of water power (channeled from the basin's inlets through overshot wheels) and on steam raised from the same water in coal-fired boilers that sit on the driest ground the town owns, a slight rise at the eastern edge where the basin floor lifts by almost a full meter. The boilers are the only structures in town with stone walls. They are considered faintly sacred, or at least faintly taboo — you don't swear near them, you don't spit near them.

The sound is enormous. The hammers of the beating mills run on cams driven by the water wheels, and they strike in a polyrhythmic clatter that can be heard from the plain. The boilers hiss and tick. The presses groan. At peak production hours — morning, always morning, because the workers need the cool of afternoon for maintenance and recovery — the town is a wall of mechanical noise overlaid on the constant drip and trickle of water. Conversation happens by leaning close and shouting.

The output is a dense, resinous fiber board used for construction and waterproofing across the region. It ships out on flat barges that navigate the basin's larger channels and connect, eventually, to a river. The barges are the town's only contact with the outside world beyond the plain, and they are watched with the obsessive attention of people who know their survival depends on a single route.

## The Ecology

Fauna is sparse, and this is noticed by visitors but not remarked upon by the inhabitants. There are no birds singing in the canopy. No frogs in the channels. A few insect species — a particular beetle that bores into pillarwood and is considered the town's worst pest, some midges near the water, a moth that pollinates the broadleaf canopy trees — but the overwhelming impression is of a place where animal life has been quietly displaced by the sheer competitive dominance of plant growth.

The vegetation fills every volume. Not just the ground and the walls but the air itself feels occupied — spore-motes, pollen, fragments of leaf tissue, a fine green-tinged haze that you breathe in with every breath. The channels are lined with algae so thick they look like tubes of living jade. The air above the water surfaces shimmers with a faintly green fog of evaporated plant exudates. Even the stone of the boilers, when they're cold enough to inspect, has a film of something — not quite moss, not quite lichen, a flat photosynthetic skin that the workers scrape off weekly and which returns by Tuesday.

Nothing here is hostile. Nothing is predatory or parasitic in any dramatic sense. The plants simply grow, and they grow so well and so fast that there is very little room for anything else. The town exists in a kind of negotiated truce with the vegetation — it manages it, guides it, harvests it, builds with it, and in return the vegetation allows itself to be shaped. But the negotiation is daily, and it is work. Leave a wall untrimmed for a season and it will bud. Leave a channel uncleaned and it will choke. The boundary between "town" and "forest" is not a line but a gradient that must be actively maintained, every day, by every person who lives here.

## The Pace

Everything here is slow. The wood grows slowly, even pillarwood, even the engineered fast-growth stock. The channels shift over years, not weeks. The town's layout — its winding paths, its organic arrangement of structures, its absence of straight lines — is not the result of chaos or informality. It is the accumulated record of decades of micro-adjustments: a wall moved two degrees to avoid a root, a channel widened where the flow was strongest, a path rerouted when a new tree took hold in the old one's place.

Decisions take a long time here. Not because the people are slow but because the consequences of a decision are embedded in living material that will persist for a century. You don't decide to move a wall lightly when the wall is a tree. You don't reroute a channel when the channel is lined with root systems that are structurally integral to three adjacent buildings. The town's governance — a council of elders, though "elder" here means "person who has been maintaining their section of the town longest" — meets monthly and discusses issues that have been on the agenda for years. Some agenda items have been on the agenda since before the current council members were born.

This slowness is not stagnation. It is the pace at which living infrastructure can be altered without collapsing. The town is always changing — always growing, always shifting, always being renegotiated between human intent and botanical momentum — but the changes happen at the speed of seasons, not days. A visitor who stays a week will leave thinking the town is static. A visitor who stays a year will begin to see the differences. A visitor who stays a decade will be unable to recognize the town they first arrived in.

## What It Feels Like

It feels like being inside something alive, because you are.

The warm air on your skin has the quality of exhalation. The filtered green light makes everything feel close and intimate, even in the larger clearings where the waterwheels turn. The sound is constant — water, machinery, the creak of living wood under load, the drip-drip-drip that never stops. There is a humidity to the relationships here as well as the air: people know each other in the way of small, enclosed communities, which is to say thoroughly and sometimes unwillingly.

It smells good, mostly. Green and warm and slightly sweet, with the sharp mineral bite of the boilers and the faint sulfur tang of the processing vats. It smells like work. It smells like growth.

It is not a beautiful place in any conventional sense. It is too wet, too dense, too cluttered, too loud. There are no vistas. There is no skyline. Every sightline terminates in a wall of green or a curtain of dripping root. But there is a richness to it, a layered density of living texture, that rewards attention. The grain of the pillarwood walls. The way the light catches the water surface in the channels. The slow turn of a waterwheel half-submerged in a current of liquid jade. The sound of a town that is also, in every structural sense, an organism — growing, breathing, drinking, and very, very slowly, changing its mind.
