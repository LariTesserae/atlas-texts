# The Throat of Vael's Disappearance

You cannot tell, at first, whether you are standing or drowning.

The water is warm — not bath-warm, but blood-warm, the warmth of a living thing pressing against every surface of your skin. It comes from below as much as from above. It rises around your calves, your waist, your chest, and there is no surface to it. There is no surface at all. The sea has swallowed this place whole, and yet — impossibly — there is still air here. Pockets of it, clinging to hollows in the rock like trapped breath. You move through a medium that is neither fully liquid nor fully gaseous, a churning suspension in which water and air refuse to maintain their boundaries. Bubbles don't rise. They orbit. They cluster in slow helical chains around protrusions of stone, humming at frequencies you feel more than hear, though *hearing* is a word that barely applies here.

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing that breaks you.

It is not loud. *Loud* implies a scale, a comparison, a threshold that has been exceeded. This sound has no threshold. It is the condition of the place. It is the medium itself vibrating — every molecule of water and air and rock caught in a resonance that seems to originate from the mountain's core, as though the stone itself is a bell that has been struck and will never finish ringing. It is not a single tone. It is every tone, layered and at war, a chord that shifts and dissonates in patterns too complex to parse. Your bones pick up frequencies your ears cannot. Your teeth ache. Your vision blurs in time with the oscillations, and for a terrible moment you understand that your eyes are vibrating in their sockets, that the world is not shaking but that your *perception* of it is being modulated like a signal, like something is tuning you.

You press your palms against your ears and feel the sound transmitted through your skull, arriving at the inner ear from both directions at once. There is no silence beneath your hands. There is never silence. The sound does not diminish when you close your mouth. It reverberates through the water inside your lungs — the water you did not drink, the water that simply *is*, here, the water that has no respect for the boundary between *inside* and *outside*.

## The Terrain

You are on a mountaintop, or what was once a mountaintop. The elevation is high — absurdly high — yet the water extends above you in all directions, a ceiling of swirling particulate darkness that should be impossible. The pressure should have killed you. The depth should have killed you. The sheer fact of your lungs working — half-filled with warm seawater, half with trapped air that tastes of copper and ozone — should have killed you. Nothing about your survival here follows any rule you once knew.

The rock is young. That is the strangest thing about the geology. Everything here looks as though it emerged from the earth yesterday, or is emerging *still*. The stone is sharp, its fractures clean and glassy, unmarred by the patient work of millennia. Basalt columns jut from the substrate at erratic angles — not the orderly columns of cooled lava flows, but twisted, bent, as though they cooled while rotating. Some of them are spiraling. You can see the helical striations where they hardened mid-twist, each groove still holding the memory of torque. The rock is black and wet and faintly warm, and in places it is so smooth it behaves like a lens, bending the dim light into shapes that don't correspond to anything in the room you think you're standing in.

The ground — if it can be called ground — is a fractured plateau roughly the size of a large room. Not a natural amphitheater. Not a clearing. A *room*. The boundaries are definite. To the north, the rock drops away into a chasm so dark it seems to exhale. To the south, a wall of twisted basalt rises ten meters, its face crawling with a slow effervescence — not bubbles exactly, but globules of something heavier than water, clinging to the stone and drifting upward in defiance of gravity. To the east, a narrow passage, barely shoulder-width, through which water surges and recedes in rhythms that don't match any tide. To the west, nothing. Just the water, the endless churning dark-water, and the suggestion — felt rather than seen — of the mountain continuing downward forever beneath your feet.

## The Weirdness

Reality is not stable here. It is not that reality *breaks* — it is that reality *has not decided what it is*.

The basalt columns cast two shadows. One is dark, as shadows should be. The other is a pale luminous outline, a shadow made of light, as though the column exists in two states simultaneously and each state casts its own opposition. The two shadows never overlap. When you move, they rearrange themselves relative to you in ways that imply there is a light source somewhere behind your eyes, projecting outward.

The water does not flow. It *iterates*. It repeats a gesture — a surge, a curl, a eddy — and then it repeats it again, and again, with each repetition subtly different, as though the water is trying to perform an action it cannot quite remember the correct way to do. Watch long enough and you will see a wave crest, break, collapse, and then *un-collapse*, running backward up its own face, reversing its foam into un-foam, smoothing itself back into potential. Then it does it again. Slightly differently. As though it's learning.

There are places where the water and the air swap properties. You will reach forward into what looks like open water and your hand will emerge dry, passing through a region where the water is so thin and diffuse that it is functionally atmosphere — warm, saline, pressing against your skin with the gentle insistence of fog. Then you pull your hand back and it is dripping, the water thick and clinging again, and you can feel the weight of it between your fingers, the weight of *depth*, the terrible weight of knowing how many leagues of ocean are pressing down from above.

The temperature does not distribute evenly. There are veins of cold — not cool, but *cold*, arctic, crystallizing the water around them into fractal ice-structures that form and dissolve in seconds. These veins move through the space like living things, threading between the basalt columns, and where they pass, the air — the water — the *medium* — fills with a crackling so sharp it sounds like language. You have pressed your ear to one of these veins. The crackling resolves, at the edge of comprehension, into something that might be syntax.

## The Built

There are remnants.

On the highest basalt column — the one that spirals most tightly, the one that seems almost to reach toward the impossible ceiling of water above — there is a mark. Not a carving. A *correction*. Someone, at some point, has *corrected the rock*, smoothing a flat face into the natural spiral and inscribing it with lines that are too precise to be natural and too wrong to be any alphabet you recognize. The lines are shallow. They glow faintly, a dull amber, and when you look at them directly they seem to rearrange, to slide across the surface like insects under glass. Looking away and looking back never shows the same pattern twice.

Half-submerged in the substrate at the room's center — if it is a room — there is a stone. Not basalt. Granite, maybe, or something like granite: coarse-grained, pale, flecked with minerals that catch the dim light and return it slightly *wrong*, as though the light bounces back at a wavelength your retina interprets but does not trust. The stone is roughly rectangular, roughly waist-high, roughly the shape of an altar. It is not an altar. It is a stone that happens to have been shaped into a form that your pattern-seeking mind interprets as *altar* because the alternative — that it is a stone shaped into something you cannot categorize — is more than you can bear right now.

Around the base of this stone, the water behaves differently. It moves *slowly*. It moves with purpose. It eddies in patterns that look, from above, like writing, and this is the only place in the space where the iterative stutter of the water seems to resolve into something coherent, a single continuous flow that circles the stone three times before descending into a crack in the substrate and vanishing.

There is a second structure, half-buried in the eastern wall: a frame of some kind, made from a material that is not stone and not metal and not bone but has the properties of all three. It is rectangular. It is two meters tall. It holds nothing. It frames the narrow passage through which water surges, and when you stand before it, the sound — the deafening, omnipresent, structural sound — changes. Not in volume. In *kind*. For a moment, while you stand framed within its frame, the sound organizes itself into something that feels almost like music. Almost like a chord progression. Almost like it is trying to *accompany* something.

Then you step through, and the sound collapses back into its oceanic dissonance, and you are left with the conviction — strong, irrational, persistent — that you just stepped through something and cannot step back.

## The Ecology

There is almost nothing alive here. Almost.

The basalt columns are streaked with a growth that might be lichen but behaves like neither lichen nor any plant you know. It is dark red, almost maroon, and it *pulses*. Slowly. With the rhythm of a heartbeat that does not match yours. When you touch it — and you will touch it, because everything here demands touching — it is warm and yielding, like skin, and it contracts beneath your fingers, pulling away from your touch and then slowly, tentatively reaching back. It grows in fractal patterns that echo the fractal ice-structures formed by the cold veins. You are not certain it is a separate organism from the cold veins. You are not certain it is a separate organism from the rock.

In the water — the churning, iterative, impossible water — there are occasional shapes. They move too deep to see clearly, and they are too large to be fish but too irregular to be anything you have a name for. They do not approach. They do not flee. They *occur*, the way weather occurs: passing through the space as a condition rather than as a creature, altering the water's behavior as they move, leaving vortices in their wake that spin in the wrong direction and never decay.

There are no birds. There are no insects. There is the lichen-thing, the shapes in the deep, and there is *you*. You are the fauna here. You are the rare and improbable life-form that has washed up on this impossible plateau in this impossible ocean, and the space regards you with the indifference of a place that was not designed for anything but is nonetheless *full* — full of motion, full of sound, full of the furious iterative energy of a reality that has not yet committed to its own rules.

## The Experience

To be here is to be inside a process, not a place. The space is not finished. It is not *becoming* finished. It is in a state of perpetual generation, each moment a draft of itself, each draft slightly revised, the revisions accumulating without ever producing a final version. The water surges and un-surges. The shadows duplicate and separate. The sound modulates and modulates and never resolves. The lichen grows and contracts. The cold veins drift. The shapes in the deep transit and re-transit.

You stand in the center of this — on the fractured basalt, up to your calves in warm water, your lungs half-full of brine, your eyes adapting to the dimness, your bones humming in sympathy with the bell-tone of the mountain — and you understand that this place is not *dying*. It is not *breaking*. It is the opposite. It is generating itself so vigorously, so chaotically, with such enormous and deafening energy, that it cannot stabilize into the fixed state you call *real*. Reality here is not breaking down. It is *building up*, too fast, too much, a perpetual draft, a sentence that adds a new clause with every syllable, a structure that builds itself faster than it can stand.

You are warm. You are submerged. You are vibrating. You are alive in a place that is trying, with all its deafening, turbulent, iterative might, to be *everything at once*, and cannot, and will not stop trying, and will never stop, and the sound it makes while trying is the loudest thing you have ever heard, and it is the most beautiful thing you have ever heard, and you cannot tell the difference anymore, and you are not sure there is one.
