# The Gash

You are standing at the lip of something that should not be finished yet.

The ground drops away so sharply that the far wall of the gorge is visible only as a suggestion — a dark, wet glint of stone and hanging green somewhere below the threshold of sight. The cleft is narrow, perhaps sixty meters across at its widest, and deep enough that the light at the bottom is not light at all but merely a less committed form of darkness. You can hear it before you see any of it: the gorge *sounds* like a machine. Wind compresses through the throat of the fissure and emerges from the other end with a sustained, brassy roar that never quite modulates. Beneath that — always beneath that — there is the percussion of water striking stone at irregular intervals, and beneath *that*, a low, arrhythmic cracking, like knuckles, like something splitting under load.

The rock is young. You can tell because it is honest about itself. There is no soil, no patina, no softening. The walls are raw fracture planes — obsidian-dark basalt and something paler, a grey-green tuff — and their edges are still sharp enough to draw blood. They angle inward as they descend, then outward again, creating a profile like a cracked ribcage. In places, enormous slabs have detached and wedged themselves between the walls at odd angles, suspended, still settling, and from these you can hear that cracking — the slow groan of mass finding its equilibrium against mass. The rock faces are wet. Not flowing, not weeping in any generous sense, but damp: a film of condensation that catches what little light filters down and turns the stone into something that gleams like an animal's eye. In recesses and under overhangs, water has pooled into shallow, dark pockets that look like bruises in the rock. These do not overflow. They simply exist, fed by condensation and by the rare, thin rain that reaches this depth, and they give off no sound.

But there is green.

The green is the thing that makes the gorge feel impossible. It is everywhere — dense, layered, almost aggressive in its insistence. Ferns with fronds like open hands crowd every ledge and crevice, and they are not the pale, tentative ferns of caves. They are dark, saturated, *lush*, growing in thick mats that drape over the rock and hang downward in curtains. Between them, something like moss — though broader, fleshier — coats every available surface in a velvet so thick it muffles the sound of your own footsteps on the stone. In the deepest shadows, where you would swear no photosynthesis could occur, there are clusters of broad-leafed plants pressed flat against the wall, their surfaces black-green and slick, drinking condensation through their skins. The vegetation is layered — old growth underneath, new growth on top, dead matter decomposing in between — and it covers perhaps eighty percent of the accessible rock face. The gorge is not a wound in the earth. It is a garden that happens to be vertical and happened to form inside a fracture.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds in the upper reaches, even where the sky is a thin, pale line impossibly far above. The green exists without witness, without pollination that you can identify, without any obvious mechanism of reproduction. It simply *persists*, dense and total and silent in the way that only plants can be silent — which is not silent at all, but a kind of slow, green noise that your ears eventually learn to distinguish from the geological roar.

The temperature is cool. Not cold — not the teeth-clenching cold of altitude — but the steady, damp cool of deep stone. It presses against your skin uniformly. There is no warmth anywhere, no hint of solar gain, no thermocline as you descend. The gorge maintains its temperature like a cellar, like a vault, and the air is heavy with moisture that has nowhere to go.

And the gorge is *active*. That is the thing the parameters conspire to describe — the almost industrial quality of this place. It is not a static landscape. The dynamic is relentless. You can see it: a cascade of small debris — pebbles, chips of tuff — sliding down a fracture plane and disappearing into the green below. You can hear the settling. The wedged slabs shift. Water finds new paths. The wind changes direction without warning and the whole gorge inhales, and the ferns shudder in unison, and for a moment the cracking intensifies as the temperature differential causes the rock to contract, and then it subsides, and then it begins again. The place is manufacturing itself in real time. It is a factory of erosion, of deposition, of botanical colonization on stone that is still warm from its own formation. The processes are layered and overlapping and they never stop, and the sound they make — wind and water and cracking rock and the barely-perceptible rustle of ten thousand ferns — fills the gorge with a dense, industrial wall of noise that is not unpleasant but is *total*.

You realize, standing at the lip, that this is a place defined by what it is still becoming. The edges are sharp because they have not yet had time to soften. The vegetation is aggressive because it is colonizing territory that did not exist a century ago. The sound is loud because the stone is still finding its shape. Nothing here has settled. Nothing here is finished. The gorge is a process, not a place — a young fracture in the earth's crust, narrow enough to be intimate, deep enough to be dark, loud enough to remind you that the ground beneath you is not as stable as you have been pretending.
