# The Pore

---

You are inside something.

That is the first thing to understand — and the understanding does not come all at once. It comes in the way the walls, if they are walls, curve with intention. In the way the ceiling is not a ceiling but a surface that continues upward beyond the reach of any light you've seen, into a blackness that might be height or might be nothing. In the way the geometry repeats but never quite the same, as though whatever built this place was thinking in fractals before the word existed.

The heat is the second thing. It doesn't strike you. It *saturates* — a thermal hum that lives in the air and in the floor and in the thin, mineral surfaces you instinctively avoid touching. The ground beneath your feet is warm to the point of pain if you stand still too long in certain places, so you learn to keep moving, to follow the cooler veins — narrow channels where some faint circulation pulls air from deeper within the structure. These channels are the only wind here. They make almost no sound.

**The structure itself** is the landscape. There is no distinction between architecture and geology. The surfaces are mineral — crystalline, in places, with a fine-grained density that suggests ceramic but behaves like bone. They are young. The edges are sharp, the planes flat and unweathered, the fracture lines clean. Whatever process formed them — and it was not erosion, not time, not pressure in any geologic sense you'd recognize — happened recently. Possibly recently means something different here. The newest surfaces have a faint luminescence, a dim phosphorescence in shades of grey and amber, like light trapped behind frosted glass. This is the only illumination. It comes from the structure itself, from within the walls, cycling through slow pulses that take minutes to brighten and fade. You can feel time passing by counting the pulses. You will stop counting eventually. Everyone does.

**The voids** are the important feature. The structure is not solid. It is a lattice — no, a *foam* — of enormous, mostly empty chambers connected by passages that narrow and widen without logic. The chambers are vast. You stand at the lip of one and look down, and the far side is not visible. The phosphorescent walls recede into a haze of their own radiance, and the space between is simply *distance*, pure and unoccupied. The air is thin but breathable, and it carries a faint metallic taste, like licking a battery in a dream. Sound does not travel well here. A sharp clap of your hands dies within arm's reach, swallowed by the geometry. What remains is a low, near-subliminal vibration — not a sound so much as a pressure behind the sternum. The structure is doing something. It has always been doing something. It was doing it before you arrived, and it will continue, and the nature of the activity is not available to you.

**The water** appears without warning. It is not rain. There is no sky. It condenses on surfaces in the cooler hours — though whether the pulsing light cycle constitutes "hours" is a question — forming beads of extraordinary clarity that cling to the crystalline walls until they grow heavy enough to fall. When they fall, they strike the ground with a sound like a finger snapping in an empty hall, and then they are still, sitting in shallow depressions that the condensation has carved over... over how long? The depressions are perfectly round. Some are small enough to cup in your hands. Some are pools that stretch for meters across their still, hot surface. The water tastes of calcium and something else — something faintly sweet, almost organic, though there is nothing organic here. It is the sweetest thing in this place, and you learn to seek out the pools the way animals seek water in a desert, though there are no animals, and you are not sure you should be thinking of yourself as one.

**The settlement** — if it can be called that — exists in a pocket. This is the paradox of scale. The structure extends in all directions beyond comprehension, and the place where things are *inhabited* is a single chamber, perhaps thirty meters across, connected to the greater void by a corridor that narrows to a width you have to turn sideways to pass through. This is the urban element, and it is alien in a way that is difficult to articulate because it does not resemble any city you have known.

The surfaces of this chamber have been *reworked*. The sharp, young mineral edges have been smoothed in places — deliberately, precisely — and new geometries have been imposed on them. Niches have been carved and then filled with materials that seem to emit their own faint heat. Shelves, or what function as shelves, hold objects that are clearly manufactured but resist identification: smooth, dark, dense things that are warm to the touch and seem to shift in weight when you are not looking directly at them. Some are the size of your fist. Some are smaller than a grain of rice. Some appear to be smaller than that, but you can feel their presence, a slight displacement of air, a tactile whisper against your skin. These are the artifacts of the beyond-modern technology, and they are as much a part of the landscape as the crystal walls. They were not left here. They *grew* here, or were grown, in the same way the structure itself emerged — young, sharp-edged, purposeful.

There are flat surfaces arranged in patterns that suggest communal use — gathering, perhaps, or work, though no one is working, and the last time someone was here may have been a long time ago or may have been a moment ago. There is no dust. The heat and the condensation prevent it. Everything is clean in the way that a surgical instrument is clean: aggressively, almost hostilely clean. The emptiness of this chamber is not the emptiness of abandonment. It is the emptiness of a held breath. A space that is being waited in.

**The strangeness** accumulates. You notice that the geometry of the passage leading into this chamber is different each time you traverse it — not in a way you can photograph or measure, perhaps, but in the way it *feels*, the way the turns press against your shoulders, the way the passage seems to take slightly longer to cross each time. You notice that the condensation pools are never in the same locations. You notice that the dim phosphorescent light, when you stare at it long enough, resolves into patterns — not quite faces, not quite text, but structures of meaning that your visual cortex insists are *almost* interpretable. You notice that the low vibration behind your sternum has a rhythm. You notice that the rhythm matches your heartbeat, or your heartbeat matches the rhythm, and you cannot determine which came first. You notice that the objects on the shelves are arranged differently when you look away and look back, and you stop noticing this, because the not-noticing is a kind of sanity you cannot afford to lose.

**The activity** is everywhere and nowhere. The structure processes. The light cycles. The water condenses and falls. The air circulates through the channels, carrying its faint charge. The objects on the shelves sit in their configurations and do whatever it is they do. There is no weather, but there is change — slow, patient, relentless change, operating on a timescale that flirts with geology but is not geological. Something is being computed, or refined, or remembered, and you are inside the mechanism, and the mechanism does not require you, and the mechanism does not require anything at all. It is older than it looks. It is newer than it should be. It is hot, and quiet, and mostly empty, and you are in the pocket of it that has been shaped for purposes that are not yours.

The pools collect in their round depressions. The light dims and brightens. The passage narrows behind you and then widens. The crystal surfaces hold their sharp, young edges against a heat that should have softened them by now but hasn't.

You are inside something. You will not find the outside. The structure does not have an outside. It has *interior*, and it has *more interior*, and between these two states is a distinction that does not apply here.

The water beads on the wall. It swells. It falls. The sound it makes is the loudest thing in this place, and it is very, very quiet.
