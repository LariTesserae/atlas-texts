# The Vaskr Common

You are standing on a high plateau that has no bottom.

Not in the sense that it is tall — though it is, and the air is thin and cool enough to raise the breath into fog before your lips — but in the sense that the stone beneath your feet does not remember where it began. Eons have gnawed at this elevation. The edge of the Common is not a cliff so much as a *thinning*: the ground simply becomes less of itself, layer by layer, until you are standing on a membrane of compressed dust and mineral memory, and beneath it there is a grey suggestion of depth that your eyes refuse to resolve. The plateau is ancient beyond the language that might name its age. It has been eroded not downward but *inward*, settled into whatever final geometry the world's patience could produce. It is nearly flat. It is nearly nothing. It endures.

## The Structures

And then there are the **works**.

They rise from the plateau like the fingers of something buried alive — enormous, arboreal, but not trees. Not exactly. They are architecture, or they are organisms, or they are the thing that existed before the distinction between those two words was useful. Each one is a tower of living tissue: layered cambium and lignified vessels and membranes of root-mat that have been grown, trained, grafted, and *coaxed* across centuries — no, longer — into the shapes of human habitation. There are walls. There are rooms. There are corridors that branch and reconnect in patterns that correspond to no known building code but that serve, undeniably, as infrastructure. A great structure near the center of the Common — the one the workers here call the **Pulpit**, though no one preaches from it — stands perhaps eighty meters tall, its surface a dark green-grey hide of moss-plating and lichen crust, its interior a hollowed-out honeycomb of chambers, some of which still contain the iron armatures of the original scaffolding. Those armatures are rust-red and weeping mineral tears. They have not been structurally necessary for longer than anyone can remember.

Between the major works there are lesser things: low, spreading constructions like fungal platforms, their surfaces flat and hard enough to walk on, threaded with capillary channels that carry what moisture exists across the plateau's surface in slow, deliberate pulses. These are the roads. These are the plazas. The city — if it is a city, and by any functional definition it is — is not connected by built paths but by *grown* ones. The entire Common is a single contiguous organism wearing the shape of a town.

It is sparse. This is important to understand. For all its biological totality, the Common is *empty* in the way that only very old places can be. The works are separated by distances that seem to increase the longer you look at them — a hundred meters, two hundred, and then the eye loses the ability to measure because the ground between them is featureless and the air is hazy with altitude. You can walk for minutes between one structure and the next. The spaces between are not void, exactly. They are *pauses*.

## The Damp

It does not rain here, not in any way that would be recognizable as weather. The moisture arrives as a slow exhalation — condensation that beads on every surface with the regularity of a tide. The plant-structures weep. They are always weeping. Thin films of water trace channels along their bark-walls and pool in shallow basins that have been grown into the architecture at deliberate intervals, their rims curled like lips. These reservoirs are the Common's only water source, and they are enough, because the Common does not need much. The air itself carries enough to keep everything alive, which is a low bar when everything is already alive and has been for longer than there have been animals to notice.

The cool temperature keeps the moisture from evaporating. The plateau is a terrarium the size of a town, self-contained and breathing slowly. In the mornings — or what pass for mornings under the normal, undramatic light of this latitude — the fog is so dense that the works disappear into it like ships, and you navigate by sound and touch, running your hands along walls of living wood that are warm to the touch despite the cold air, because they are metabolizing, because they are *alive*, and alive things at this scale generate their own weather.

## The Machine

The industrial layer is not separate from the plant. This is the thing that a newcomer would find hardest to accept.

Engines turn inside the works. You can hear them — a deep, arrhythmic grinding, like a heartbeat with opinions. The original machinery was mechanical: steam-driven pumps, conveyor systems, fabrication rigs for the tools that once shaped this place before it learned to shape itself. All of it has been *colonized*. Belts of vascular tissue have grown around drive shafts and replaced the chains. Hydraulic systems have been supplanted by root-pressure differentials. But the old engines still run, or something like them still runs — there are pistons in the Pulpit's lower chambers that cycle with a wet, heavy percussion, pushing fluid through channels that are half-copper-pipe and half-xylem. The sound is enormous in the thin air. It reverberates off the walls of the hollowed works and comes back changed, lower, stranger, and then it hits the next structure and changes again.

There are factories here, if you can call them that. Fabrication halls where the plant-structures were once *made* — shaped, stitched, encouraged into their forms by workers who are now dust and memory. The halls are still occupied. Not by workers. By the process itself. The machines in those halls still operate, still cut and join and grow, still produce new structures that rise from the plateau's surface in the slow, patient way that teeth emerge from gums. No one has directed this production in a very long time. The system is self-sustaining. It is self-*interested*. It grows because growing is what it does, and the distinction between a city maintaining itself and an organism reproducing has ceased to be meaningful.

## The Sound

The Common is loud. This surprises people, or it would if there were still people here in sufficient numbers to be surprised.

The grinding of the embedded machinery is the base layer — a constant, subsonic rumble that you feel in your chest before you hear it. Layered above that are the creaks and groans of the plant-structures themselves, which are enormous and always growing and whose internal pressures produce sounds like ships' timber in a storm. Water trickles, drips, and occasionally gushes from the weeping walls. The fog moves through the hollow interiors of the works with a sound like breathing. And underneath all of this, if you press your ear to the ground — to the living, hard, calloused surface of the Common's floor — there is a vibration that might be geological or might be biological, a pulse that has no rhythm you can predict and no source you can locate.

The sounds change in the open spaces between structures. The plateau's surface is hard and flat and carries vibration efficiently, so you can hear the Pulpit's machinery from a kilometer away, distorted and stretched into something almost melodic. Sound here does not dissipate. It *travels*. It finds you. It is patient.

## The Fauna

Almost nothing lives here that is not plant.

The Common's ecology is self-referential: a world of producers without consumers, of architecture without inhabitants, of systems that maintain themselves with the indifference of weather. There are insects — or things that were insects once, before generations of isolation and the plateau's strange chemistry reshaped them. They are small, chitinous, and numerous in certain seasons, and they serve as pollinators for the less integrated plant-structures on the Common's periphery. They are not interesting. They are not charismatic. They are *functional*, and the Common functions because of them, and that is the end of the conversation about fauna.

Birds occasionally land on the plateau. They do not stay. The place is too loud and too strange and too empty of the kind of things that birds eat. They rest for an hour, look around with the flat irritation of animals that have arrived somewhere that has nothing to offer them, and leave. Their departures are the most animated events on the Common.

No mammals. No reptiles. No fish, though there are pools of water in the basins. The water is potable but faintly sweet and faintly *wrong* in some way that is difficult to articulate. It tastes of metabolism.

## The Break

And then there is the matter of the **break**.

Reality on the Vaskr Common is not reliable. This is not dramatic. There are no portals, no shimmering barriers, no sudden shifts in the color of the sky. The breakdown is *architectural* — it operates on the level of space and time and the basic assumption that things are where they appear to be.

Walk east from the Pulpit for twenty minutes and you will find yourself north of it. Not turned around — the light does not shift, the fog does not thicken — but *displaced*, as though the ground between two points is not the distance it appears to be. The plant-structures accommodate this. Their corridors curve in ways that make no geometric sense but that always arrive at the correct room. Their root-maps — the deep, embedded networks that connect the works — follow paths that would be absurd on a flat plane but that are perfectly efficient on whatever *folded* geometry the Common actually occupies.

Time is affected. Not uniformly. Certain rooms in the works are older than the structures that contain them — their walls predate their ceilings by years, which is not how building works. Certain corridors take longer to walk than their length justifies, and the delay is not constant, and the delay is not random, and the delay *means something* that no one has been able to decode. The machinery runs at speeds that fluctuate without cause. A piston that cycles once every four seconds today will cycle once every six seconds tomorrow and once every two seconds the day after, and the plant-tissue that has grown around it will adjust, will *absorb* the change, because the plant does not care about regularity. The plant cares about continuity.

The light is normal. This is perhaps the strangest thing. Everything else is wrong, and the light is fine. A flat, honest, high-altitude daylight that illuminates the Common with the even-handed thoroughness of a bureaucrat filing reports. It does not flicker. It does not shift. It lights the breakdown evenly, so that you can see it clearly, so that you cannot pretend it is a trick of shadow.

## What It Is

The Vaskr Common is a city that has become an organism, or an organism that became a city and then forgot the difference. It is ancient and industrial and alive and broken. It is loud and sparse and cool and damp. It grows, still, with no one to direct it and no one to live in the spaces it builds. The fog moves through its hollow towers like breath through lungs. The machinery cycles. The water weeps. The geometry folds.

You can leave. There is no wall, no gate, no border. The plateau simply ends, and the world resumes its usual shape beyond the thinning edge. But something in the sound of the Common — the grinding, the creaking, the patient hydraulic pulse of a city that no longer needs you — follows you for a long time afterward. A vibration in the sternum. A rhythm you did not agree to learn and cannot quite forget.
