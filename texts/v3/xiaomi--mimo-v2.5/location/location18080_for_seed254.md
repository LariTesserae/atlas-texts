# The Throat of Vaelund

You have to crouch to enter. The passage narrows after the first twenty paces — not from collapse, but from growth. Roots and runner-stems have thickened the original carved walls until they nearly kiss across the corridor, and you press through them wetly, feeling them yield and spring back behind you like a slow pulse. The ceiling is no higher than your bent back. The air hits you before anything else: warm, almost feverish, and so saturated with moisture that your first breath feels like drinking.

Then the passage opens, and you are inside.

---

**Vaelund** is the name still carved above the inner arch, though the letters have been swallowed to rounded nubs by decades of moss and creeping lichen-pulp. It is not a cave. It was built — every wall, every beam, every vaulted rib of the ceiling was placed by hands and machines — but that distinction means almost nothing now. The city, if it can be called that, is no larger than a town square. Perhaps sixty meters across at its widest, maybe thirty deep, and no more than four meters from floor to the lowest hanging cluster of root-mass overhead. The ceiling exists in theory. In practice, the upper meter and a half is a solid canopy of intertwined root systems, hanging water-roots, and the thick rope-stems of creeping plants that have fused into a secondary living ceiling below the original stone. You never see the carved rock above. You see its bones — the iron tie-beams and timber trusses — threading through green like a skeleton caught in kelp.

**The ground is wet.** Not damp, not moist — wet. Every surface below knee-height glistens. The original cobbles of the streets are invisible under a carpet of liverwort, marsh-moss, and the low rosettes of a plant that resembles nothing so much as a fleshy green tile. Water stands in thin films across every horizontal surface, and where the footways slope toward the old drainage channels — most of which are now choked — it pools in shallow, warm puddles that you feel through your boots as a faint give, a softness, as though the city is slowly sinking into itself. It is not flooding. It has never flooded, not catastrophically. The water simply *is*. It arrives from everywhere: seeping through the walls in slow curtains, dripping from every overhead root in a perpetual warm rain that never reaches the floor before being caught by intermediate layers of foliage, condensing in fat droplets on every metal surface, and welling up from cracks in the original foundation with a patient, geothermal insistence. There is no dry place in Vaelund. There has not been one in living memory.

**The warmth is constant.** Not the warmth of summer, which rises and falls, but the deep, even warmth of a body that never cools. The walls radiate it. The earth beneath the floor offers it up. The dense biological mass of the interior — all that respiring green, all that slow decomposition and regeneration — holds it in. You are aware of your own temperature beginning to equalize with the space around you within minutes. Stripping down to a layer feels appropriate. The humidity makes it impossible to sweat effectively, and there is a heaviness to the air, a thickness, that reminds you of the inside of a lung.

**Light comes from below and within.** There are no lamps that still function in the original sense — the wiring is buried under centimeters of growth, the fixtures consumed — but the plants themselves produce a dull, ambient luminance. Not true bioluminescence in the spectral sense, but a quality of the translucent green tissue of the dominant ground-cover and ceiling-draping species, which captures what little light exists and re-emits it as a diffuse, warm, almost yellowish glow. The overall effect is of a place lit from everywhere and nowhere. Shadows are soft and directionless. The far wall of the space — you can see it — is rendered in a greenish half-light that makes depth hard to judge. Thirty meters might be fifteen. You navigate by feel as much as sight: the gradient of the floor, the brush of stems against your arms, the increasing density of overhead growth that tells you you're approaching a wall or a structural partition.

**And the sound.** Vaelund is loud, and the loudness is the one thing that truly unsettles, because it is not the loudness of activity. There are no voices, no engines firing to life, no footsteps but your own. The sound is *geological and botanical simultaneously*: the steady, unvarying roar of water moving through a hundred hidden courses beneath and behind every wall, the creak of timber that has been swelling with moisture for decades and has never fully dried, the deeper groan of root growth physically displacing stone — you can hear it, a sound like a slow exhale, as a hairline crack in a wall you're passing visibly widens by a millimeter over the course of a minute. There is the drip-drip-drip of condensation, but not individual drips — a curtain of them, so many that they merge into a white-noise hiss that fills the lower frequencies. And underneath it all, a vibration you feel in your sternum: the slow, rhythmic pulse of water being absorbed and released by the massive biological infrastructure of the city. It has a beat. Not fast. Not alarming. But constant, like the city itself is breathing, and you are inside the chest.

**The buildings are still standing, mostly.** They were stone and timber and brick, built close together in the old urban pattern — shared walls, narrow alleys, second stories that overhang the street. Industrial infrastructure dominates: you can identify the shape of a pump-house by its iron framework, though the machinery within has been colonized by a thick, spongy fungal-plant hybrid that has filled every gear and piston with itself. Pipes run along walls and across ceilings, some still carrying water by gravity and pressure — you can hear it rushing through them — others burst open and trailing curtains of fern. The buildings have not collapsed so much as *incorporated*. Load-bearing walls still bear loads, but they are wrapped in living tissue so thick that the original masonry is visible only in chips and fragments, like bone through skin. Doors are impassable, sealed by growth. Windows have become archways of hanging stems. But the structures endure. The architecture is *there*, beneath the green, and the plants have not so much destroyed it as adopted it, using its angles and planes and hollows as scaffolding for their own slow ambitions.

**There is no animal life.** You notice this after a while, and the noticing is gradual — first a surprise, then an absence that deepens. No insects buzzing in the wet warmth. No frogs, which this place seems custom-built for. No worms in the exposed soil. No birds in the upper reaches. Nothing with eyes watches you from between the leaves. The ecosystem is entirely botanical: plants, fungi, and the microorganisms that sustain them. The silence of fauna is the one silence in the place, and it is conspicuous. Whatever keeps Vaelund free of animal life — the chemical signature of the water, the particular mineral content of the local stone, some atmospheric compound released by the dominant plant species — it has been effective for a very long time. The plants grow without interruption. They grow without browsing, without pollination by anything but water and wind, without disturbance. This is why they are everywhere. This is why they are so *thick*. A plant ecosystem with no herbivory, no predation, no animal-mediated dispersal — just slow, patient, vegetative expansion in a warm wet dark — reaches a saturation point that a surface ecosystem never would. Vaelund is past that point. It has been past it for years. Every cubic centimeter of air and surface that can support growth does support growth, and much that shouldn't, does anyway.

**The erosion is settled.** Nothing here is actively falling apart. The city has reached its equilibrium state — the balance between the built structure and the biological pressure that has been reshaping it — and it will hold this shape for a long time. The original engineers built deep and solid, and the plants, for all their power, are not demolition agents. They are *replacement agents*. They fill, they wrap, they hold. When a stone eventually crumbles under decades of root pressure, there is already three times its mass in plant tissue occupying the same space, and the transition is seamless. The erosion of Vaelund is not destructive. It is metamorphic. The city is becoming a different kind of structure — still architectural, still geometric in its bones, but organic in its flesh. Give it another century and the distinction will be meaningless.

**You walk the main street for perhaps ten minutes.** It curves gently — the original engineers followed the natural seam in the rock — and the passage narrows and widens as you go, because the growth is not uniform. In places where water collects, the foliage is so thick you must push through it chest-high, stems bending reluctantly around you. In drier stretches — relative term — the buildings assert themselves more clearly, and you can read the old city in the brickwork and beam-ends and the ghosts of signage painted on walls, the lettering bleeding and soft but occasionally legible: *HALLORAN PUMP CO. / EST. 19—*, the last digits lost. *STREET 4*. *WATER TREATMENT — AUTHORIZED ONLY*, beside a doorway now filled solid with a pillar of fused root-mass that reaches from floor to ceiling and looks like it was poured there.

You hear, always, the water. You feel, always, the warmth. The light never changes. The city breathes around you, slowly, in its own time, with a patience that has nothing to do with you and will continue long after you have left and forgotten the exact quality of the warm dark and the living walls.

Vaelund does not need you. Vaelund does not need anything. It is complete.
