# The Proliferant Heights

The first thing you notice is the sound. Not wind — there is wind, hot and wet and constant, moving in off no identifiable horizon — but beneath and through and above the wind, a deeper noise: the sound of *growing*. Not metaphorical. A low, fibrous creaking that rises and falls in surges, punctuated by sharp cracks like branches snapping in a storm, except no branch has snapped. Something has *opened*. A new surface, wet and pale, has just been exposed to air for the first time.

The second thing you notice is that nothing here is stone.

---

## The Substrate

The ground — if you can call it ground — is living tissue. Dense, interwoven root-mass, pale at the surface where new growth hasn't yet darkened it, ruddy and veined deeper down, compressed into something with the hardness of old wood but the structure of a sponge. Water moves through it visibly. You can see it: capillary channels beneath translucent membranes, the whole floor breathing moisture upward in a slow exhalation. The atmosphere condenses against your skin almost immediately — not rain, not quite, but a perpetual mist that beads and drips from every surface above you and weeps upward from every surface below.

This is a high place. The air confirms it — thinner than it should be for the heat, carrying a faintly mineral tang that suggests mineral content in the water cycling through the root-lattice below. The horizon, where you can see it between the great standing forms, is open and distant: a landscape of the same material extending in every direction, broken by ridges and hollows, with mist pooling in the low points like pale lakes. The scale is enormous. You could walk for hours and never leave this biome, because this *is* the biome — one continuous organism expressing itself across a high-altitude basin, its edges somewhere you cannot see.

## The Architecture

The standing forms — the *structures* — are plants, but the word feels inadequate. They rise from the living floor in configurations that have the deliberateness of buildings without the geometry. Some are columns: thick, buttressed at the base, their surfaces ridged and grooved with the vertical emphasis of tree trunks, except they aren't trees. They don't branch into canopies. They terminate in swollen, bulbous masses — some open, some sealed — that pulse faintly with internal hydraulic pressure. Others are walls: broad, thin membranes of woven vascular tissue stretched between anchor-points, their surfaces covered in a fine trichome-fur that catches the mist and channels it downward in rivulets toward the root-mass below. These walls don't stand still. You can watch them — if you watch long enough — *repositioning*. Not quickly. Over the course of minutes, the base of a wall-membrane will thicken on one side and thin on the other, and the whole structure will tilt, reorienting toward or away from something you can't identify. The whole landscape is in slow, constant rearrangement.

Then there are the **hollows**: vast, open chambers formed where multiple structures lean together and their tissues have fused at the contact points. The interiors of these hollows are smooth, almost polished, and they amplify sound. The creaking of growth becomes a roar inside them. Water gathers on their inner surfaces in heavy drops that fall with a rhythmic drumming — the landscape's heartbeat, audible from outside as a deep, resonant *thump-thump-thump* that comes from everywhere and nowhere.

## The Turbulence

This is not a peaceful place. The growth is *violent* in its energy. Structures are forming and collapsing on a timescale you can perceive in real time — a column reaching its peak over the course of an afternoon, its tissues hardening and darkening, then beginning to sag as the hydraulic pressure shifts elsewhere, folding slowly downward while new columns rise around it. The whole basin operates like a slow-motion eruption: material cycles from the root-floor up into standing structures, through their mature phase, and back down into collapse and reabsorption. Dead tissue doesn't accumulate. The living substrate beneath digests it almost as fast as it falls, pulling nutrients back down into the vascular network.

You can feel the ground shifting underfoot — not dramatically, not like an earthquake, but with the constant micro-adjustment of a surface that is still settling, still *forming*. This is a young landscape. The erosion is minimal because there isn't enough time for erosion to matter; formations rise and are consumed before weathering can round their edges. Everything has a sharpness to it — the ridges of new columns, the edges of wall-membranes, the lip of every hollow. Clean lines. Fresh surfaces. The whole place looks *recent*.

## The Wetness

Water is everywhere and it is not passive. The condensation is so constant that surfaces are never fully dry; a sheen of moisture coats everything, and the light — normal, diffuse, filtered through a perpetual haze of suspended droplets — catches it and turns the landscape into a shimmering, half-reflective field. You can see your own distorted reflection in the smooth interior walls of a hollow. You can see the sky — overcast, white, the color of steam — mirrored in the root-floor where water pools in shallow depressions between growth-masses.

But the water also *moves*. The capillary network beneath the surface is visibly pumping. You can see the flow-direction in the translucent membranes: tiny rivers running in every direction, feeding the standing structures, draining the collapsed ones, redistributing moisture with a purposefulness that approaches intention. In places where two vascular channels cross, the intersection swells into a node — a knot of activity, warm to the touch, where water from different parts of the organism is being mixed, tested, sorted. Some nodes weep. Some nodes hiss, releasing vapor under pressure. Some nodes are quiet, sealed, their contents unknown.

## The Absence

There are no animals. The silence of animal life is conspicuous — you don't notice it at first because the landscape is so loud, but once the thought occurs to you, it settles in like a pressure change. No insects. No birds circling the high structures. No worms in the root-mass when you press your hand into a soft patch and feel the wet, fibrous interior yield and then *push back*, closing around your fingers with a gentle, vegetal grip before releasing you. Nothing moves except the plant. Nothing breathes except the plant. The entire ecology — if that word still applies — is singular. One organism. One system. One mind, if there is a mind, and in the *intentionality* of the structural rearrangements, in the purposeful flow of the hydraulic network, in the way certain columns seem to orient toward you as you pass (you may be imagining this; you may not), the question of consciousness hovers unanswered.

## The Strange

There are formations you cannot categorize. Suspended masses that hang from nothing — no visible support, no connecting tissue — hovering two or three meters above the root-floor, their surfaces covered in a pattern of indentations that, if you stare too long, seem to resolve into something almost like script. They are warm. They vibrate at a frequency you feel in your teeth. They do not grow. They do not collapse. They are the oldest things in a young landscape, and they may not be part of the organism at all, or they may be its memory, or its dreaming.

The loud places — the hollows, the nodes, the collapse-zones — sometimes synchronize. For a span of thirty seconds, the drumming of water-drops, the creaking of shifting structures, the hiss of vapor-vents, and the deep subsonic groan of the root-floor all align into a single rhythmic pulse, a coordinated vibration that passes through the air and through your chest and through the ground beneath your feet. Then it breaks apart again into chaos. This happens irregularly, without warning. It has happened twice since you arrived. You don't know what triggers it. You don't know if it has a purpose.

You are standing in the middle of a valley-sized organism at high altitude, in heat and moisture and constant, turbulent growth, surrounded by architecture that builds and collapses around you in real time, on a young landscape that has not yet decided what it is. The light is white and flat. The mist is everywhere. The sound never stops.

You press your palm against the nearest column, and it is warm, and it is wet, and it is *moving* — not externally, but inside, the slow churn of fluid through living tissue, the pulse of something vast and singular and utterly indifferent to your presence, busy with its own becoming.
